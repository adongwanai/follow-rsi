Abstract
LLM agents for GPU kernel optimization sit at two ends of a structure–freedom tradeoff. Scripted agents fix what the model does at each step, via a learned policy, an optimization menu, or fixed roles, and are sample-efficient where the recipe applies and stall where it does not. Open-ended agents let a self-directed model mutate a population of kernels and pay for the freedom in trials: thousands of evaluations, repeated edits, and wrong candidates. Both respond to hard targets by equipping the agent with more: parsed profiler output, disassembly analysis, retrieval over documentation and libraries, and stores of lessons. We argue that both lack direction, a hypothesis about where the remaining gap is, and that a short question asked at the right moment supplies it without narrowing what the agent may do next. We present
KernelSeek
, a question-driven agent that makes an existing CUDA kernel faster with no operator-specific knowledge and no faster implementation to imitate: a general-purpose coding agent, a minimal harness that enforces a verifiable goal, and questions at decision points, first from a human, then from a bank the agent applies to itself. On the same task, evaluator, hardware, and week, a tool-rich autonomous agent built in the prevailing style plateaued at 0.50

FlashAttention-3 after 133 model-driven steps;
KernelSeek
reached 1.13

We release the kernels, harness, question bank, and all traces.

KernelSeek
: A Question-Driven Kernel Agent
KernelSeek
takes a working CUDA kernel, a set of scenarios, and a goal, and returns a faster kernel whose outputs match the seed on every scenario. It has three parts: a minimal harness that makes the goal verifiable, a general-purpose coding agent with no script around it, and a source of questions at the moments the agent is about to go wrong. The questions come from a human steerer or, once they recur, from a bank the agent applies to itself. Nothing in the method is specific to an operator, and nothing requires a faster implementation to exist.
2.1
Ask vs. Equip: A Common Frame
Kernel optimization by an agent is a search. A candidate is a kernel source; its fitness is a vector of per-scenario speedups over the seed, collapsed to zero if any scenario is incorrect; and the lineage is monotone, as in AVO
Chen et al. (2026)
. The agent is the variation operator: it proposes the next candidate conditioned on a context, and everything a designer can do is a choice of what enters that context and when. An
equipped
agent receives the task, tools whose output has been distilled for it (a profiler parser that names the bottleneck, a disassembly differ), knowledge retrieved for it (documentation sections, hints mined from reference libraries, a store of lessons), the lineage, and feedback on the last candidate; the designer improves it by adding tools and knowledge. Our baseline (§
3
) is a serious instance. An
asked
agent receives the task, the harness’s output, the lineage, and, at decision points, a short question. It still has every tool a developer machine offers, the compiler, profiler, disassembler, sanitizer, and debugger, but nothing is pre-digested, and direction arrives as a question rather than a menu. The designer improves it by asking better questions.
2.2
A Minimal Harness and a Verifiable Goal
The only component we build is the evaluator. One command builds a candidate, checks its outputs against the seed’s and an fp32 reference on every scenario, and times it against the seed back to back on the same GPU, reporting per-scenario ratios and their geometric mean. Other kernels the user cares about can be added as measured comparison columns, but none is required. Constraints are stated once and enforced mechanically: in the case study, the ban on CUTLASS, CuTe, cuBLAS, and cuDNN is checked by inspecting the built binary’s symbols.
The goal is a predicate over the evaluator’s output, enforced rather than requested. We install it as a Stop hook in the coding agent: whenever the agent attempts to end its turn, the hook evaluates the predicate and, if any clause is unmet, returns the agent to work with the unmet clauses in hand. A predicate such as “at least parity with the seed on every scenario” removes the most common failure of autonomous agents, declaring completion early. Two lessons shaped how we write predicates. They must quantify over every scenario rather than an aggregate, because an agent will otherwise satisfy the aggregate on the shapes it is good at. And they must not be judged by a model where they can be computed: the one time our hook delegated the verdict to a model judge, it accepted a premature claim that a human then overturned (§
5.4
).
2.3
Questions: What They Are and When to Ask
Across the traces, the questions that preceded the largest gains share six properties. They carry no answer: they name a class of hypothesis and leave the derivation to the agent. They demand a measurement the agent can make with the tools it has, so the answer is a number rather than an opinion. They move across layers of the system, from the kernel to the host launch path to the routing logic to the benchmark itself, where an agent left alone stays at the layer it started at. They refuse premature closure by asking whether a claimed limit is a property of the hardware or of the agent’s own design. They are cheap, tens of tokens against the tens of thousands that retrieved documentation costs
Liu et al. (2024)
;
LangChain (2025)
. And they counteract deference: an agent tends to keep the structure it started from and accept the framing it is given
Sharma et al. (2024)
, and a question asks it to justify that choice with evidence.
Eight categories cover the questions a human asked during the case study (Table
4
in Appendix
A
): measurement check (13 messages), bound differencing (10), benchmark validity (7), gap triage (7), agent process (6), challenge a limit (5), strategy options (5), and hypothesis check (3). The largest checks a measurement: whether a win is noise, whether a comparison is fair, whether the agent is timing what it thinks it is timing. The second pits the agent’s claimed ceiling against an independent estimate of what is achievable. In the case study that estimate was often the upstream kernel, but the pattern does not depend on one existing: a roofline, a speed-of-light figure
Lin and others (2026)
, or an A/B that swaps only the launcher around an identical binary serves the same role. Questions are asked at decision points, not continuously; the traces show five: at the start of a campaign, to fix the scope; at a plateau; when the agent claims a limit or completion; after a suspiciously large win; and when correctness breaks in a way the agent explains away. Between these points the agent works alone, about 71 tool calls per human message, where the median message is a sentence or two. The steerer contributes no code and no CUDA knowledge the agent lacks; it contributes doubt, at the right moment, about the right thing.
2.4
The Question Bank
After the campaigns we distilled the recurring questions into a bank. Its first version, taken from the traces alone, had 48 entries, and reading it back showed the hazard of distilling from one case study: 21 named the upstream kernel as the comparison and 13 named attention structure. The released bank removes both. A question is admitted only if it carries no answer, is answerable by a measurement with stock tools, is phrased against a bound (a roofline or a per-tile cycle budget) rather than a reference, names an architecture only as a parameter, and names no operator. To cover the aspects a single attention campaign never exercised, we also mined three public GEMM worklogs on Ampere, Hopper, and Blackwell
Boehm (2022)
;
Shankhdhar (2024)
;
Modular Inc. (2025)
and the FlashAttention-3 and -4 papers
Shah et al. (2024)
;
Zadouri and others (2026)
for the diagnostic that preceded each of their optimizations, and phrased it as a question. The result has 101 core entries in ten groups ordered by where the gap might be (scope and triage; the bound and the regime; harness and measurement; host, launch, and routing; memory hierarchy; compute issue; occupancy and registers; asynchrony and pipelining; scheduling; low-precision numerics), five that apply only when another implementation is available to measure, and eighteen per-architecture checklist entries that bind the generic mechanisms to Ampere, Hopper, or Blackwell instructions. Operator structure enters through a plug-in with five slots (regimes, irregular work, non-MMA units, reduction splits, indirect loads); the attention plug-in fills them with prefill and decode, causal masks, softmax, split-KV, and paged gathers. Appendix
A
lists the bank with provenance tags: 29 entries carry a trace tag, and the 72 from public sources alone are untested.
2.5
From Asking the Agent to the Agent Asking Itself
Because the questions recur, they can be handed to the agent as part of its instructions. We codify them as a skill with five parts: the goal, the hard rules the harness enforces, a loop, a list of questions to ask itself, and the commands that run the evaluator and profiler. The loop is the bank in procedural form: fix the scope; measure the seed; classify each gap as host, routing, or kernel before touching code; bound it; form a hypothesis; rewrite; gate. At each step the agent fans out subagents, one per hypothesis, each returning a hypothesis and the measurement that tests it rather than a patch. The skill is parameterized by the seed, the scenario set, and the goal; its questions were reused across the Hopper and Blackwell campaigns nearly verbatim.
2.6
Composing with Evolutionary Search
KernelSeek
is compatible with the evolutionary loops of the open-ended agents, because it changes the variation operator’s direction rather than the loop around it. We wrap the same agent in an AVO-style search
Chen et al. (2026)
: a monotone lineage, several diverse mutations per generation applied to isolated copies and scored by the evaluator, and a strategist that reviews the trajectory and writes the next generation’s directives. The one change is that the directives are questions, hypotheses to test rather than edits to make. As §
5.5
shows, evolution amplifies direction; it does not supply it.
2.7
Hypotheses
The evaluation tests five claims.
H1 (quality):
on the same task, evaluator, and hardware, the asked agent reaches higher performance at full correctness than the equipped one.
H2 (efficiency):
with fewer model-driven steps and GPU-hours, counting human time.
H3 (mechanism):
the largest gains follow questions that change the layer under consideration or reject a claimed limit, not questions that supply information.
H4 (complementarity):
evolutionary search amplifies the effect of questions and does not substitute for them.
H5 (generality):
the questions that moved the case study contain nothing specific to the operator or to a reference once the reference is replaced by a bound.

Baseline: A Tool-Rich Autonomous Evolutionary Agent
Before
KernelSeek
, we built the agent the prevailing recipe prescribes.
Arsenal
is an autonomous evolutionary agent for the Hopper half of the case study (§
4
), in the lineage of AVO, KDA, and CUCo
Chen et al. (2026)
;
MIT HAN Lab (2026)
;
Varadharajan et al. (2026)
: an orchestrated loop, a profiler whose output is distilled for the model, retrieval over documentation and reference libraries, a typed knowledge store, and no human in the loop by design. Its failure is the paper’s motivation, and its evaluator, unchanged, scored the first
KernelSeek
session in the head-to-head of §
5.3
.
Design.
The task was a single dependency-free CUDA file implementing vLLM’s FA3 operator for H200, with success defined as at least parity with FA3 on 26 configurations and token-for-token equality with FA3 on a greedy decode in vLLM. A deterministic Python orchestrator owns the loop. Each step generates one candidate, builds it, checks for forbidden dependencies by inspecting the binary’s symbols, analyzes the SASS instruction mix, verifies correctness on all 26 configurations, times it against FA3 interleaved across four GPUs, profiles the worst configuration with NCU, and, on promotion, A/B-tests it inside a live vLLM instance. It then writes a lesson to a memory file, a note to the knowledge store, and an entry to a lever ledger, checks for a plateau, and checkpoints. A Claude Opus model (
claude-opus-4-8
) running headless in Claude Code is the brain, invoked in a fresh session at every step. The orchestrator decides
what
to try and the brain decides
how
: sixteen hand-written levers, from tensor-core MMAs and TMA loads to warp specialization and persistent scheduling, are keyed to the bottleneck label the NCU parser assigns, and the chosen lever is placed in the prompt with the instruction to write the improved candidate. Everything else the brain sees is distilled by a component (Appendix
E
): a bottleneck diagnosis, retrieved documentation, hints extracted from DeepGEMM, ThunderKittens, and CUTLASS, and typed knowledge-store notes. The median step prompt was 22.6K characters, 37% of it pre-fetched reference material, plus the current best kernel re-read each step. Promotion is greedy on the worst-case configuration ratio with a 3% noise band, and a plateau detector opens rewrite branches. No human-authored text ever entered a step prompt.
Outcome.
The run kept 313 steps over 4.2 days, with a further 117 discarded by three human rollbacks. The model was active for steps 0 to 132, about 62 hours and $660 of API usage over 253 sessions; from step 133 the brain’s credentials had expired and the loop re-scored old kernels until stopped, so we report 133 model-driven steps as the budget. The best candidate reached 0.50
×
\times
FA3 in geometric mean with a worst case of 0.24
×
\times
; no configuration reached parity. All progress happened by step 35, in about 45 hours. The remaining 98 model-driven steps produced nothing beyond noise: the final champion, from step 112, is byte-identical to the candidate of step 104 and was promoted because one of 54 re-measurements of the same source landed above the noise band. Correctness was not the problem: 312 of 313 candidates were correct.
Why it stalled.
The traces show the failure modes of §
1
, each with a number attached.
Information was not the constraint
: with more than a third of every prompt devoted to reference material and 1.6 GB of reference code on disk, 82% of the characters the brain read through its own tool calls were its own kernel, and its 92 documentation lookups over 246 sessions mostly justified not making a change.
The menu bounded the search
: 36 of the 37 rewrite branches the plateau detector opened were seeded from the same step, and every candidate after step 35 was a local edit of the same design.
Memory hardened conclusions, not evidence
: the memory file recorded one configuration as “DEFINITIVE[ly] …walled by [the] 65536-reg file”, a wall FA3 does not hit, and the brain’s summary at step 112 was that “the only remaining escape is a clean-sheet 1-blk/SM warp-spec persistent seed, which is outside this branch’s mandate and measured to lose”; each fresh session inherited earlier verdicts without the reasoning behind them, and nothing in the loop was positioned to doubt them.
Every diagnostic lived at one layer
: NCU, the SASS analyzer, and the sanitizer all examine the kernel, while the gap
KernelSeek
later closed on this evaluator was in the host launch path (§
5.4
).
The objective rewarded caution
: promoting on the worst configuration inside a noise band accepted noise as progress and could not fund a restructuring that regresses before it pays off; 312 correct candidates out of 313 is the signature of a search that never took a risk.
Three lessons carried into
KernelSeek
: the mechanical parts of the harness were worth keeping, and
KernelSeek
’s harness is this evaluator with the orchestration removed; everything built to inform the brain could go, and the knowledge store was actively harmful; and the loop lacked one input, a reason to doubt a conclusion and a hypothesis about where else to look.

Case Study: Dependency-Free FlashAttention for Hopper and Blackwell
Task.
We asked
KernelSeek
to produce a FlashAttention forward kernel for Hopper and one for Blackwell, each a single CUDA file with no CUTLASS, CuTe, cuBLAS, or cuDNN, exposing the operator schema vLLM uses for FlashAttention-3 so that the result drops in for the upstream kernel and is timed against it from identical inputs. The scenario suites mirror the upstream kernels’ own test grids and add production shapes from SOL-ExecBench
Lin and others (2026)
: 182 scenarios per architecture spanning bf16, fp16, and fp8; paged and contiguous KV caches; MHA, GQA, and MQA; causal, sliding-window, softcapped, and attention-sink masking; ragged batches; prefill, chunked prefill, and split-KV decode; and unequal query and value head dimensions. The breadth is deliberate, since kernels scored on a few shapes report speedups that shrink on the production path
Oliaro et al. (2026)
. The goal was correctness on every scenario and at least parity with upstream in geometric mean. The seed in both cases was a slow but general kernel (a scalar one-warp-per-query kernel on Hopper, a one-warpgroup dense-only skeleton on Blackwell), and anything the fast paths did not yet cover fell back to it, so correctness held throughout. The upstream kernels, FA3 as shipped in vLLM and the CuteDSL FA4
Shah et al. (2024)
;
Zadouri and others (2026)
, were the correctness oracle and the yardstick the goal was stated against. The dependency-free constraint means the layouts, descriptors, pipelines, and register allocation that CUTLASS and CuteDSL derive for the upstream kernels had to be written by hand
Thakkar et al. (2023)
.
What the agent built.
On Hopper,
fa3_hopper.cu
(3.6K lines) is a warp-specialized kernel in raw CUDA and PTX: producer and consumer warpgroups connected by a TMA and mbarrier ring, asynchronous
wgmma
MMAs with
setmaxnreg
register rebalancing, persistent scheduling with inter-warpgroup ping-pong, split-KV decode with a combine pass, and a native fp8 path. Its one remaining limit sits where a library’s allocator would otherwise be: at head dimension 256 the hand-written swizzle composes only at a few tile widths and the output accumulator caps occupancy at one CTA per SM, so that scenario runs at 0.94
×
\times
. On Blackwell,
fa4_blackwell.cu
(10.2K lines) is a family with host-side routing, a structure the questions pushed the agent toward after a single kernel had stalled (§
5.4

tcgen05

μ
\mu
s per call, a stale routing gate, a TMA gather issued from divergent lanes, and ring state the compiler had placed in local memory. fp8 inputs and dense head dimensions of 192 and above are not yet built on Blackwell (28 of 182 scenarios).

5.4
RQ3: Anatomy of the Questions
What the human said.
Of the 162 human messages, directives are the largest class (58, 36%), followed by questions (44, 27%), approvals (30, 19%), corrections (19, 12%), and goals (11, 7%); 63 messages (39%) are interrogative in phrasing, and 12 of the 19 corrections are phrased as questions (Appendix
D
). The steering was interrogative, not mostly questions: directives and approvals keep the agent moving, and the questions are where its course changed. The agent also asked. Across the Claude Code sessions it posed 35 structured questions to the human, 24 of which offered an option to accept the current state or stop, and its messages assert a limit or an impossibility 126 times. The Stop hook returned it to work 59 times. Autonomous agents seek closure; the harness and the human refused it.

5.6
RQ5: Reuse
The question categories were populated mostly during the Hopper campaign, which came first. Five of the eight recur in the Blackwell campaign, and the Blackwell skill’s self-questions restate Hopper questions nearly verbatim. Of the 63 interrogative messages, 17 mention a concept specific to attention and 32 mention the upstream kernel; the rest name only measurements, layers, and process, and where a question names the upstream it uses it as a bound, a role a roofline fills when no upstream exists. This is the evidence for H5 that we have: necessary, since a bank that did not transfer across two architectures would not transfer further, but not sufficient, since both campaigns were attention and the 72 bank entries mined from public sources (§
2.4
) have not yet steered an agent. The experiment that would test both, and whether the bank can replace the human at decision points, is described in §
6
.
6
Discussion
Did a human expert do the work?
The human wrote no kernel code, and the messages name measurements, layers, and process rather than mechanisms: of the 63 interrogative ones, 17 mention an attention concept and none prescribes a CUDA construct (§
5.6
). The human did have systems expertise and used it to decide which measurement to demand and when; we claim only that the questions, once asked, are short, generic, and now written down. The agent wrote all 13.8K lines of the two kernels. Whether the bank can replace the human is the experiment this paper does not contain. Its design is fixed: start from the released Blackwell kernel on one of its remaining gaps (dense long context or head dimension 96), hold the model and budget constant, and compare four arms: the equipped loop, the bare agent with the harness alone, the bare agent with a questioner that draws from the bank at plateaus and claimed limits, and a human questioner.
Does
KernelSeek
need a reference implementation?
The method does not: the harness checks correctness against the seed and an fp32 reference, the goal is a predicate over the seed, and the bank phrases every bound as a roofline or a cycle budget. The case study had one, and used it in two roles. As a yardstick it set the goal and scored the result. As a bound it let a question overturn a claimed ceiling, and this role is the one that mattered; it is filled without a reference by a roofline, a speed-of-light estimate
Lin and others (2026)
, a launcher-only A/B, or a kernel for a neighboring shape, each of which the bank asks for by name. A reference buys a cheaper and sharper bound, and no more: the equipped agent had far more of it in its prompt and gained nothing. The no-reference campaign, where the seed is the fastest kernel known, is the setting the released agent targets and the one we have not yet run.
Threats to validity.
The head-to-head is a single run per arm, with different front-ends and a steered arm against an unsteered one; we control the task, evaluator, hardware, baseline, and correctness gate, and count the human turns in the steered arm’s budget. The case study is one operator family where a strong upstream existed. Wall-clock speedups include dispatch overhead, which inflates the
edge
sets (Appendix
B
). The kernels were built before the traces were analyzed, and one author labeled the messages, so the taxonomy may fit the sessions we had; every trace is released so the labeling can be checked.
Limitations and future work.
The Hopper kernel loses on fp8 dense prefill and softcapped prefill, and the Blackwell kernel does not yet build fp8 inputs or dense head dimensions of 192 and above; neither covers the backward pass. The next targets are other deep kernels (latent-attention decode, grouped GEMMs for mixture-of-experts layers, fused collectives, persistent megakernels
Jin et al. (2026)
) and end-to-end evaluation on the production path
Oliaro et al. (2026)
.

8
Conclusion
For deep kernel optimization the scarce resource is not information but direction. An autonomous agent equipped with a parsed profiler, retrieval, reference material, and a knowledge store plateaued at half the speed of FlashAttention-3 after 133 steps on a 26-configuration task; the same class of model, given a minimal harness and forty messages of which seventeen were questions, passed it in the same week on the same machine. The questions that mattered contained no answers: they moved the agent to a layer it had not examined or made it defend a limit it had declared.
KernelSeek
packages this: an operator-agnostic agent that makes a CUDA seed faster with no faster implementation to imitate, a verifiable goal enforced by the harness, a question bank the agent applies to itself, and an evolutionary wrapper whose directives are questions. Its case-study output, dependency-free FlashAttention kernels for Hopper and Blackwell, correct on every scenario and faster than upstream, is released with the harness, the bank, and every trace.

Appendix A
The Full Question Bank
The bank has three layers. The first is the released bank of 101 core questions plus an operator plug-in (§
2.3
), organized by where a performance gap might be and meant to be worked through in order, so that the scope, the bound, the harness, the host, and the routing are settled before the kernel body is touched. The second is the list of questions the skill asks the agent to put to itself at every iteration. The third is the set of question categories observed in the human messages of the case study, with verbatim examples.
Every core entry satisfies five admission rules. It carries no answer: it names a class of hypothesis or a quantity to measure, never a technique to apply. It is answerable by a measurement with stock tools (the profiler, the disassembler, the assembler’s resource report, clock counters, the harness). It is phrased against a bound, a roofline or a per-tile cycle budget, never against a reference implementation; the five questions that only make sense when another implementation is available to measure form their own conditional group. It names an architecture only as a parameter, and must make sense on Ampere, Hopper, and Blackwell alike. And it names no operator: the attention structure the case study needed (paging, softmax, split-KV, grouped heads) lives in a plug-in that the template at the end of this section describes.

O. Operator plug-in template.
A plug-in supplies, for one operator, five things: its regimes; its irregular-work structure; its non-MMA units; its reduction splits; and its indirect loads. Each has a generic ancestor in the core (entries 2, 71, 40, 73, and 34 respectively), so the core already asks about it in the abstract and the plug-in only names the instance. The attention plug-in used in the case study lists, as regimes, prefill versus decode, dense versus paged key–value cache, short versus long context, and grouped or multi-query heads; as irregular work, causal and sliding-window masks and variable lengths; as non-MMA units, the softmax exponential and the online rescale; as reduction split, split-KV decode and its combine; and as indirect loads, page-table gathers. Its eleven questions (whether grouped and multi-query losers show the same relative gap as multi-head at equal tokens; whether the softmax and correction of two stacked tile halves overlap or serialize; whether a paged loser is still feed-latency-bound after the gather and preload fixes; whether contiguous loads on the same math would meet the bound; whether the hardware gather or a coalesced asynchronous copy is the right loader; whether short decodes pay gather and descriptor setup that does not amortize; whether the gap tracks page fragmentation; whether the decoupled key and value rings are balanced on the unequal-head-dimension path; whether softmax, rescale, and masking between MMAs burn issue slots that specialized warps would hide; whether the split-KV heuristic, the combine, or the partial buffers are the cost; and whether paged-to-contiguous on the same problem size closes the gap) are the attention instances of core entries 2, 45, 34, 34, 34, 22, 34, 61, 40, 73, and 34.

Appendix F
Benchmark Methodology
Timing.
Each scenario is timed on one GPU with every implementation run back to back from byte-identical inputs. After 20 warm-up calls, the harness takes three trials of 40 CUDA-event samples each, flushing a 32 MB buffer through L2 and synchronizing before every sample, and reports the median of the 120 samples; the per-scenario speedup is the upstream median over the implementation’s median. Clocks are not locked, which is why only ratios are reported. cuDNN and FlashInfer are given the same packed and paged buffers as our kernel and the upstream, and a scenario is marked unsupported for an implementation when it rejects the configuration or fails correctness.
Correctness.
Every candidate’s outputs are compared with the upstream kernel’s and with an fp32 reference on every scenario, with a per-dtype tolerance; a candidate that fails any scenario scores zero. In the head-to-head, the equipped agent’s gate additionally required token-for-token equality with FA3 on a greedy decode inside vLLM.
Repeats and noise.
The Hopper suite was run as three full repeats in each of four independent batches on the 8
×
