Rethinking Criteria Exposure in Embodied Self-Improvement Wenbo Huang 1 Jianxin Bi 1,2 Shunyu Yang 1 Ruiyang Si 1 Rui Ni 1 Qiang Wang 1 Shulong Jiang 1 Yintianrun Zhang 1 Feixiang Ruan 1 Kewei Zhang 1 Ye Huang 1 Shukai Gong 1 Daquan Zhou 1,∗ Affiliation: [ Affiliation: [ Abstract Self-improving embodied agents are increasingly being developed and compared on robot simulation benchmarks. Several
recent works expose the benchmark’s success criteria to the learning agent, a practice we call Criteria Exposure (CE).
These criteria, originally written to verify policies trained by imitation learning, often demand more than the
corresponding language instructions, so a score from such a verifier may not faithfully reflect whether the instructed
task was completed, especially for robot-control programs written by an agent.
As embodied coding agents report ever higher benchmark scores, a natural question arises: when a coding agent is
exposed to the benchmark’s criteria, does it generate code that better performs the instructed task, or code that
simply overfits to the verification criteria?
We conduct controlled experiments with a self-improving agent with and without CE, and find that with CE the agent
quickly produces code with a higher success rate under the given criteria, but this advantage disappears when the
criteria are changed slightly without departing from the original instructions. More importantly, the skills the agent
develops under CE transfer poorly across tasks.
We further propose instruction-faithful criteria and show that they better reflect the performance of agent-generated
programs. Keywords: embodied agent, self-improvement, robot policy evaluation Figure 1 : Learning the task or the verifier? zero-shot [ 19 , 15 , 12 , 29 ] , but preserve nothing from one
episode to the next, which makes self-improvement across episodes a natural next
step [ 28 , 31 , 36 ] . Such agents are developed and compared on embodied
simulation benchmarks [ 9 , 20 , 22 ] originally built to evaluate
end-to-end policies trained by imitating demonstrations, such as vision-language-action (VLA)
models [ 6 , 17 , 4 , 25 ] and world action models
(WAMs) [ 26 ] . The actual success criteria of these benchmarks often ask for more than the
instruction; in the press_by_number task of RoboDojo, for instance, the criteria require the blue button to be
pressed exactly twice, between two groups of red presses, which the instruction never states ( figure 1 a). For
policies trained by imitation, this gap is harmless, since their demonstrations already contain every detail the
criteria verify: order, counts, end states, and the return of both arms. An agent that receives only the instruction,
however, lacks this information. Existing self-improving methods therefore expose the criteria to the agent to different degrees, from rule
descriptions [ 10 ] and published scoring tables [ 18 , 13 ] to numeric
thresholds [ 14 ] , formal goal predicates [ 32 ] , and the source code of the
verifier [ 3 ] . A robot in the real world, by contrast, receives only its instruction and, at most, a success judged by a
human or a VLM. Our research question is: when a self-improving embodied agent is exposed to the
success criteria, does the code it iteratively produces better perform the instructed task, or simply overfit to the
verification criteria? Studies of benchmark validity and of specification gaming show that criteria can demand more than the instruction states and
that agents exploit a verifier they can
inspect [ 16 , 40 , 37 ] , but we are
not aware of work that measures how much of a reported gain in embodied self-improvement comes from reading the
criteria, or whether it survives when the criteria change. We therefore compare two settings of one fixed self-improvement loop, in which a frozen coding
agent acts and a learner agent turns its episodes into a library of skills and memory: with Criteria Exposure
(w/ CE), the learner reads the criteria; without it (w/o CE), the learner sees only whether each episode succeeded. We
ask three questions: on which tasks exposure raises the score; whether its lead survives a change of criteria, both of
the criteria that judge the episodes and of the criteria the learner reads; and whether what it adds carries over to
new tasks and to two further benchmarks, LIBERO-PRO and RoboCasa365. To change the criteria, we rewrite them for the RoboDojo tasks whose criteria ask for more
than the instruction, so that they verify only what the instruction asks. On these tasks every episode is judged by
both versions, and in a separate run the learner reads the rewritten criteria instead of the original ones. Our experiments reveal three findings. First, under the original criteria, exposure helps only on tasks whose criteria
ask for more than the instruction; when criteria and instruction agree, learning from success alone does just as well. Second, the score of exposure follows the criteria
it read: when the same episodes are judged by instruction-faithful criteria, its advantage disappears, as the exposed
libraries lose successes while the success-only libraries gain them ( figure 1 b); conversely, a library learned from
the rewritten criteria scores far lower when judged by the original ones. Third, the lead of exposure does not reappear on new RoboDojo tasks, and across benchmarks neither the exposed nor the
success-only library gives a clear gain over the empty library; only experience gathered within the target benchmark
does. Taken together, these results suggest a practical principle for evaluating embodied self-improvement: the learner
should receive only the feedback a robot would receive in the real world, and the benchmark’s criteria should verify what the
instruction says. A gain that holds when the criteria are changed but still agree with the instruction reflects what
the agent has learned about the task. Seen
this way, whether the learner reads the criteria is not an implementation detail of a self-improving agent but a
choice about measurement, and it determines what a reported gain means. 2 Related Work Code-writing embodied agents and their benchmarks. Language models plan and act for robots by selecting skills [ 1 ] , writing policy
code [ 19 ] , or composing value maps [ 15 ] ; recent agents close the loop with code
replanning and thin tool layers [ 12 , 29 , 13 ] , and frontier models now control
robots zero-shot. These agents are evaluated on benchmarks built for vision-language-action
policies trained on demonstrations [ 6 , 17 , 4 , 25 ] :
RoboDojo [ 9 ] ,
LIBERO and LIBERO-PRO [ 20 , 38 ] , RoboCasa365 [ 22 ] , and
RoboTwin 2.0 [ 8 ] . These works score agents with criteria written for demonstration-trained
policies; whether the criteria verify only what the instruction asks is rarely examined on its own, and we measure how this gap
enters the score of a self-improving agent. Self-improving agents and the feedback their learners see. In digital domains, programs are evolved against automatic
evaluators [ 23 , 34 ] , agents reflect on textual
feedback [ 28 , 36 ] , skills are verified in a game
world [ 31 ] , and code is revised against tests [ 33 ] . Embodied systems differ in
how much of the success criteria their learners or agents see. Some give only success or keep
the grader isolated [ 27 , 21 ] ; others add rule descriptions with coarse
feedback [ 10 ] , published scoring tables and partial numeric
thresholds [ 18 , 13 , 14 ] , formal goal predicates [ 32 ] ,
or the source code of the verifier [ 3 ] . In RATs, the verifier reads goal
predicates and ground truth while the policy writer’s side is filtered [ 35 ] . In these systems the
amount of criteria information is mostly fixed by design rather than varied as an experimental factor. The closest comparison is an
ablation of EmbodiedEvo between task descriptions alone and descriptions with the wiki, but both arms are judged by the
original criteria [ 18 ] . We treat exposure as the variable and test what it gains under a second set
of criteria. Specification gaming and benchmark validity. Optimizing a proxy diverges from the intended objective [ 2 , 30 ] , with measurable
effects of reward misspecification [ 24 ] . Language agents exploit tests and evaluators they can
inspect [ 37 , 5 ] , and agentic benchmarks need checks of their own
validity [ 16 , 40 ] . Where rule-based criteria are missing, success is judged by
VLMs [ 11 ] or by autonomous real-world evaluation [ 39 ] . Building
on these studies, we further show that on embodied benchmarks the act of reading the criteria turns the gap between
criteria and instruction into score, and we measure this share with counterfactual criteria that score the same
instructions. 3 Preliminaries and Study Design This section describes the controlled study we use to analyze what a self-improving embodied agent learns from the
success criteria. The study compares two settings of one loop, w/ CE and w/o CE, and answers three questions: where
exposure raises the score under the original criteria, whether the advantage holds when the criteria change, and
whether the library carries over to new tasks and benchmarks. We describe in turn the loop and its two settings,
criteria-limited tasks, instruction-faithful criteria, and the evaluation protocol. 3.1 Self-Improvement Loop and Criteria Exposure Figure 2 : Overview of the self-improvement loop. Left, learning: the actor (GPT-6 Astra, high reasoning

trajectory S τ S_{\tau} accepts or by accepting one S τ S_{\tau} rejects (criteria looser than the instruction); all other
tasks are faithful . Such tasks appear in every benchmark we examined, RoboDojo, LIBERO [ 20 ] (whose instructions and goals LIBERO-PRO keeps), RoboCasa365 and RoboTwin 2.0 [ 8 ] , and their
disagreements fall into a few kinds. The criteria may ask for more than the instruction states, such as an order, a
count or an end state: in RoboDojo, press_by_number requires a button protocol its instruction never gives.
They may be stricter than the instruction needs, such as an unstated tolerance for an object resting on another in
LIBERO. They may contradict the instruction, as in a LIBERO task that asks to stack a bowl on the front bowl while its
criteria require the back one. Or they may verify less than the instruction asks: in RoboTwin 2.0,
shake_bottle_horizontally succeeds once the bottle is lifted. We rewrite criteria and re-judge episodes on RoboDojo only. Its criteria report a score and stages
besides success, so an exposed learner reads the most complete information there, and we compared the criteria of all
of its tasks with their instructions; rewriting the criteria of other benchmarks and repeating these experiments on
them is left to future work. We distinguish seven types: hidden
rules, hard thresholds, timing and irreversible events, criteria defects, extra end states, loose criteria,
and contradictions. In RoboDojo, after a manual review of every task, the 20 criteria-limited tasks have
26 variants, and hidden rules and hard thresholds are the most common types. Criteria-limited is defined with respect to the
instruction the agent receives at reset; the RoboDojo website publishes part of these rules in its task descriptions and
scoring tables. 3.3 Instruction-faithful Criteria For each criteria-limited task we rewrite only the criteria code, never the instruction, and leave the original
repository untouched: hidden rules are removed or relaxed, defects fixed, hard thresholds relaxed to what still clearly
satisfies the instruction, loose criteria tightened where the simulator state can detect the difference, and the
return of both arms kept. The two criteria judged the same episodes independently, and every episode
whose verdict flipped was checked by hand without finding a misjudgment by the new criteria.
Each evaluation episode is then judged by both criteria, giving SR orig \text{SR}^{\text{orig}} and SR faith \text{SR}^{\text{faith}} . Since both criteria score the same
instruction on the same episode, any difference between the two success rates comes from what the criteria verify, not
from behavior. C faith C^{\text{faith}} is ours and serves as a proxy for S τ S_{\tau} ; we discuss this in section 5 . 3.4 Experiment Settings and Evaluation Protocol To isolate the effect of exposure, the two settings of each comparison use the same actor, primitives, round budget
and development layouts. The 25 learned tasks comprise 16 criteria-limited and 9
faithful tasks, grouped in advance by whether their criteria were rewritten; Appendix A.4 lists the tasks. Development uses layouts 0–4 of seed 1 and
testing layouts 0–19 of seed 0. We report the success rate (SR) and compare
settings layout by layout. All experiments use the same actor and learner.
To reuse libraries, we distill the libraries of ten source tasks into one shared library and apply it to
disjoint target tasks, in RoboDojo and on two further benchmarks with aligned primitives. We use three simulated benchmarks with different roles. RoboDojo is the main benchmark: its rule-based criteria
report a score and stages, and all three questions are answered on it. LIBERO-PRO is a single-arm tabletop
benchmark where zero-shot success is already near the ceiling; it tests whether transfer still has room at high scores. RoboCasa365 has household kitchen scenes and the lowest zero-shot scores; it tests cross-benchmark experience
and also hosts in-benchmark learning. 4 Experiments and Analysis We test three hypotheses: • H1. Exposure raises the score only on tasks whose criteria ask for more than the instruction
( section 4.1 ). • H2. If exposure teaches the verifier rather than the task, its lead shrinks or reverses when the same
episodes are judged by instruction-faithful criteria ( section 4.2.1 ), and each exposed library scores best
under whichever criteria its learner read ( section 4.2.2 ). • H3. What exposure adds does not carry over to new tasks or benchmarks ( section 4.3 ). 4.1 Exposure under the Original Criteria We first judge the benchmark’s way, by the original criteria, which are also the criteria the exposed learner reads. 4.1.1 Success-only versus Exposed Self-Improvement Setup. assumed to make self-improvement better. Our results show a condition: when the criteria verify what the instruction
says, success alone tells the learner what the task requires, and what remains, a reliable procedure, is learned
equally well in both settings. Exposure helps only with rules that the instruction omits and that failures do not
reveal. • The advantage of exposure measures the benchmark as much as the agent. Because it comes from what the
instruction leaves out, the gain of exposure grows with the gap between a benchmark’s instructions and its criteria.
Some of what the criteria reveal is legitimate task knowledge, but it belongs in the instruction: benchmarks that
state in the instruction what their criteria verify (counts, order, end states, the reset of the arms) and release
versioned criteria with a comparison against the instructions would let self-improvement be compared on
the task alone. 4.2 Changing the Criteria: Task or Verifier? Section 4.1 judged with the criteria the exposed learner read. Changing them separates two explanations. If

score from reading the criteria is commonly taken as faster learning of the task. A learner that reads the
criteria, however, revises the library until it passes the verifier it reads, so the library learns the task only as
far as the criteria agree with the instruction. Under instruction-faithful criteria the exposed library loses
successes that the success-only library gains, and each exposed library scores best only under the criteria it read:
on press_by_number it solves all 20 layouts under C orig C^{\text{orig}} and none under C faith C^{\text{faith}} , and the library that read C faith C^{\text{faith}} solves 65.0% of its test episodes under C faith C^{\text{faith}} but 13.5% under C orig C^{\text{orig}} .
Self-improvement should therefore be measured without exposure by default, with the success rate under
instruction-faithful criteria reported alongside: a robot in the real world has no criteria code, so the part of a
gain that survives a change of criteria is the part to expect there. • Exposure changes what the learner writes down. A learner that reads the criteria can explain a
failure by the condition it violated, so its experience is phrased in the terms of one verifier; a learner that sees
only success has to find a physical cause, and its rules are closer to regularities of the robot. About a third of
the exposed memory entries cite the criteria code, and none of the success-only ones do. 4.3 Reusing the Library: What Carries Over Finally, we carry the libraries to new tasks in RoboDojo and to two further benchmarks. 4.3.1 Across Tasks within RoboDojo Setup. RoboCasa365 do better, 33 (w/o CE) and 29 (w/ CE) of 100. 4.3.3 Synthesis and Implications • What exposure adds is specific to the task it was learned on. One might expect the setting that scores
higher on its own tasks to transfer better. It does not: the lead of exposure disappears on target tasks. Exposed experience carries the conditions and precision needed
to pass one task’s criteria, which become noise or overhead elsewhere, whereas rules about the gripper hold across
tasks. • Experience transfers where the bottleneck is shared. Within a benchmark the robot, the scenes and
perception stay the same, and learned rules carry over. Across benchmarks the general part of the experience is
already covered by the target’s prompt, while what the target lacks has no counterpart in the source, so the
transferred skills go unused; experience gathered on the target benchmark, even on other tasks, addresses its own
bottleneck and helps. 5 Limitations and Future Work The instruction-faithful criteria are ours, and the feedback is simulated. C faith C^{\text{faith}} serves as our proxy for
the success the instruction asks for, S τ S_{\tau} . Neither setting’s libraries saw the rewritten criteria when they were
written; rules and types were fixed in advance; a second seed was judged independently and every flipped episode
checked. Still, only RoboDojo’s criteria were rewritten,
and success-only feedback in simulation is cleaner than the success a human or a VLM reports for a robot in the real
world, which has no criteria code. Next steps are an independent review, released criteria code, rewritten criteria for a second
benchmark, and VLM success detection in place of the simulator’s success [ 11 ] . Exposure is studied at two levels, as current systems implement it. Most existing systems sit at intermediate
levels (rule descriptions, published scoring tables, partial thresholds), which we did not study separately. A next step
is to expose these levels one at a time and measure how the gain under C orig C^{\text{orig}} , and its loss under C faith C^{\text{faith}} , grow
with what the learner reads. Samples are small and the mechanisms are inferred. Each task has 20 test layouts, so quantitative claims use
pooled results and single tasks are described only qualitatively; and distillation variance was not estimated. The accounts of what exposure writes into the library, of what carries over and of how the actor uses transferred libraries rest on entry text, skill calls and
action statistics, not on ablations. Next steps are repeated distillations and ablations that remove or swap individual
entries, such as the re-measuring entries of the distilled w/ CE library or the memory entries of a
translated library. 6 Conclusion In this paper, we studied what a self-improving embodied agent learns when it reads the success criteria, by comparing
one self-improvement loop with and without Criteria Exposure, under the benchmark’s own and instruction-faithful
criteria, and on new tasks and benchmarks. Our results show that self-improvement from success alone is real and
survives a change of criteria, whereas the score that Criteria Exposure adds comes mainly from satisfying the criteria
that were read: it appears only where the criteria and the instruction disagree, disappears under instruction-faithful
criteria, and across benchmarks neither the exposed nor the success-only library gives a clear gain, while only
in-benchmark experience helps. These findings
provide a more careful reading of reported self-improvement gains on embodied benchmarks and practical guidance for
measuring them: use the feedback a robot receives in the real world, and judge by criteria that verify what the instruction says.
