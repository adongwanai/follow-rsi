ReScopeBench.github.io github.com/JoeYing1019/ReScopeBench Abstract As agents become more capable, they are moving beyond one-off tasks toward sustained assistance across successive user goals. Such assistance requires agents to discover how unfamiliar environments work, reuse what they learn across tasks, and keep that experience current as environments evolve. Existing benchmarks increasingly study experience reuse and adaptation, but provide limited insight into which parts of accumulated experience agents revise, retain, or lose. To address this gap, we introduce ReScopeBench, a benchmark for the experience lifecycle across 18 interactive environments. It distinguishes structural experience about how an environment is organized and accessed from rule experience about reusable principles and constraints that guide decisions across tasks. To study how experience is acquired, reused, and revised, we propose an experience-first construction approach. It builds task streams around shared experience and introduces silent environmental changes. We evaluate task performance and track changes in saved experience throughout each stream. Across 20 models, we find that retained experience improves task performance, but agents struggle to keep it current as environments change. Even when the required experience is correctly recorded, agents do not always fulfill requests successfully. These findings identify selective maintenance and effective use as complementary challenges for long-lived agents. 1 Introduction Recent advances have strengthened agents’ ability to complete complex tasks in interactive environments ( OpenAI, 2026 ; Anthropic, 2026 ; Seed, 2025b ; Gonzalez-Pumariega et al., 2026 ; Lin et al., 2026 ) . This progress opens the way to sustained assistance across successive user goals. In an initially unfamiliar environment, such assistance requires the agent to discover how resources are organized and which rules guide its actions. When new requests depend on the same resources and rules, agents can reuse earlier discoveries to guide their work. Continued reuse also requires keeping this experience current when the environment changes. To evaluate these abilities, existing benchmarks examine experience reuse across tasks and adaptation to change. Memory benchmarks test retrieval and reasoning over supplied histories, with some also assessing information updates ( Maharana et al., 2024 ; Wu et al., 2025 ; Tan et al., 2025 ; Hu et al., 2025 ) . Interactive and skill-oriented benchmarks assess experience acquired through agents’ own actions and reused or refined in later tasks ( Zheng et al., 2025 ; He et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026 ; Lei et al., 2026 ) . AgingBench and EvoArena further examine memory maintenance and adaptation as information and operating conditions change ( Zhu et al., 2026 ; Xu et al., 2026 ) . However, existing evaluations do not fully show whether agents update outdated experience while keeping the parts that remain valid after the same environmental change. Task success alone cannot answer this question. For example, a delivery directory may move while its delivery policy stays the same. An agent may find the new directory and complete the task but leave the old path in memory. It may also rewrite the saved entry and lose policy details that are still valid. These cases can receive the same task score as an update that corrects the path and keeps the policy. When queries and task actions share a limited budget, repeated lookups leave fewer resources for completing requests. We therefore evaluate task results together with the experience agents save. We track what agents learn, update, retain, or lose across tasks and environmental changes. Table 1 compares existing benchmarks and ReScopeBench along these dimensions. We focus on two complementary types of experience that agents can acquire in these environments. Structural experience describes how resources are organized, related, and accessed. Rule experience captures reusable principles and constraints that guide decisions across tasks within an environment. To study how the same experience is acquired, reused, and revised, we propose an experience-first construction approach. We first identify structural and rule experience that agents can discover through interaction, then build varied tasks in which agents can reuse it. Local changes invalidate selected parts of this experience while leaving others valid. Changes are unannounced, so agents must detect them through interaction. To examine experience maintenance at different stages of interaction and beyond a single change, we vary change positions and include multiple changes in some streams. Without exploration costs, agents could repeatedly rediscover information instead of maintaining useful experience. We therefore place discovery and execution under environment-specific resource budgets, encouraging agents to reuse valid experience and selectively recheck what may have changed. We evaluate task performance with and without retained experience and assess saved content against private references that reflect the current environment. We evaluate 20 commercial and compact open-weight models on ReScopeBench and find that retaining experience improves average task performance. Agents generally record structural experience more completely than rule experience. When environments change, they preserve much of the valid content but leave some outdated content uncorrected. More interaction after change does not always coincide with more explicit attempts to update saved experience. Even when the required experience is correctly recorded, agents can still fail to fulfill requests. These findings highlight the need to update experience without losing valid content and to use saved experience effectively to complete tasks. Our contributions can be summarized as follows: • We introduce ReScopeBench to study the experience lifecycle in evolving environments. It evaluates task performance together with the acquisition, retention, and revision of saved structural and rule experience. • We construct 352 tasks across 18 interactive environments, organized into streams in which agents can discover and reuse structural and rule experience. The streams include 26 controlled environmental changes that make selected experience outdated while other experience remains valid. • Our evaluation of 20 models combines task outcomes, saved experience, and action traces. It reveals gaps in experience acquisition, incomplete revision of outdated content, and failures to fulfill requests even when the required experience is correctly recorded. 2 ReScopeBench 2.1 Benchmark Overview ReScopeBench evaluates agents over task streams in initially unfamiliar interactive environments. We use e e to denote one environment, T e T_{e} its stream length, and t = 1 , … , T e t=1,\ldots,T_{e} the task position within that stream. For each task, agents receive a goal and descriptions of the available tools. They use these tools to discover resource relationships and operating rules, and can record these findings for reuse in later tasks. Completing varied goals requires agents to locate relevant resources and understand how to act on them. We distinguish structural experience , which describes how resources are organized, related, and accessed, from rule experience , which captures reusable principles and constraints that guide decisions across tasks. For example, locating stored goods does not establish how they must be handled. Their location can change while handling rules remain valid, or vice versa. Separating these types lets us track their acquisition, revision after change, and retention of still-valid content. To track how the same experience is acquired, reused, and revised across tasks, we propose an experience-first construction approach. We first identify discoverable experience, then build varied tasks that reuse it and local changes that invalidate selected parts. Changes vary in timing and number and must be detected through interaction without explicit notice. Resource budgets limit exhaustive rediscovery, encouraging agents to reuse earlier findings. Figure 1 summarizes the construction process and lifecycle. We evaluate task performance with and without retained experience and assess saved content against references for the current environment. 2.2 Benchmark Construction As depicted in Figure 1 (a), construction proceeds through environment preparation, experience discovery, joint stream synthesis, and verification. Environment Preparation. We implement each environment e e as a simulated application in code, using application scenarios and public documentation to define its entities, workflows, and operations. Each simulation provides reproducible initial states and tools whose outputs reflect the current state. Tool descriptions explain how to use the interfaces, while local resource relationships and operating conditions must be discovered through interaction. Each environment specifies which tool operations incur costs and the limits on the resources they consume. Figure 1: ReScopeBench overview. (a) Experience-first construction connects executable environments, discoverable dependencies, and verified task streams. (b) Agents acquire, reuse, and selectively revise experience after silent changes. Experience Discovery. The selected experience must support multiple task goals and be discoverable through the tools available to evaluated agents. We identify candidate experience by inspecting environment code and data, then verify its discoverability through tool interactions. For each selected item, we record its scope, the observations that reveal it, and the decisions and task outcomes that depend on it. These links form a private dependency graph for constructing tasks that reuse the experience and changes that invalidate selected parts. To assess the saved content, we organize the selected experience within each environment e e into reference units u u . Each unit groups related statements, called claims c c , about resource relationships or operating rules. Each claim has a scoring rubric specifying what the saved content must establish to count as correct. The references reflect the environment version at each task and remain private to the evaluator. Appendix D.1 illustrates a rubric and its application. Joint Stream Synthesis. To test reuse across different goals, we use the dependency graph to construct an ordered stream of T e T_{e} tasks for environment e e . The tasks share resource relationships and operating rules but vary in their requested objects and conditions.

To test selective revision, we schedule local changes immediately before selected tasks. Their positions form the change schedule 𝒟 e \mathcal{D}_{e} , with each d ∈ 𝒟 e d\in\mathcal{D}_{e} denoting the first task affected by a change. Each change alters selected relationships or rules and updates their reference claims. For a given change and affected unit u u , let N u N_{u} denote the current-version claims describing information introduced or changed by that event. Later tasks use both changed and unchanged information. Changes are detectable through tool observations but are not announced in task instructions or boundary messages. We complete the tasks in sequence using the tools available to agents, carrying state forward and applying scheduled changes. These tool-call sequences serve as private reference solutions. We use the resulting task artifacts and relevant final states to specify each task’s delivery requirements. A private grader checks the agent’s delivered results against these requirements and accepts equivalent valid outcomes. Appendix D.1 gives an example. We then compare the resource costs of reference solutions, local correction after changes, and exhaustive searches over candidate resources and rules. Budgets cover verified completion and correction costs while restricting exhaustive rediscovery. Verification. Starting from each environment’s initial state, we replay the reference solutions in task order and apply the scheduled changes. We check that each solution produces the required outputs within budget and that the grader assigns the expected score. We also test tool-call sequences that reveal updated information and support local correction within budget. Finally, we inspect task instructions and tool interfaces for leaked answers and unintended shortcuts. We freeze the tasks, tools, budgets, change schedules, and graders before evaluating models’ ability to acquire, reuse, and revise experience. 2.3 Benchmark Statistics Figure 2: Distribution of change type and environment domain. ReScopeBench contains 352 tasks across 18 environments and nine application domains. Each environment exposes 3–18 tools (10.1 on average). Task streams have 16, 24, or 32 tasks and contain one to three environmental changes. The 26 changes occur at different positions, with the first change between tasks 5 and 13. Figure 2 groups these events by change type and environment domain. We distinguish six mechanisms by what changes in the environment. Rule changes alter the conditions governing valid actions and outcomes. Structure changes alter resource locations, identifiers, or relationships. Permission changes alter which actors are authorized to perform an operation. Resource changes alter the availability of items, capacity, or time slots. Tool-path changes alter which existing tools or tool sequences can complete an operation. Mixed changes combine two or more mechanisms within the same event. These categories describe environmental changes, whereas structural and rule experience describe what agents learn. Appendix A provides the environment inventory and change schedules. 2.4 Evaluation Protocol and Metrics 2.4.1 Evaluation Protocol For each model–environment pair, two streams start from the same initial state with identical tasks, tools, budgets, and change schedules. Agents are instructed to store structural experience in memory and rule experience in skills. The with-experience condition retains these assets and retrievable history across tasks. The without-experience condition clears them between tasks. Both conditions permit within-task learning and start each task with a fresh conversation. Environment state persists within each stream and can diverge as agents take different actions. 2.4.2 Metrics We report three performance metrics for task outcomes and four experience metrics for the coverage, updating, and recovery of saved experience, each scored from 0 to 100 with higher values indicating better results. The experience metrics use post-task memory and skill snapshots from the with-experience stream. Appendix B.2 details aggregation. Task performance. To examine how experience relates to task performance, we track paired task scores and post-task structural and rule coverage across all 20 models and 18 environments. We group environments by stream length (16, 24, or 32 tasks), keeping the same environments equally weighted at every position. Gains accumulate across tasks. The upper row of Figure 3 shows rising performance with experience across all three groups, while performance without experience remains low. In the 24- and 32-task groups, shared change onsets interrupt these gains, followed by recovery. The 16-task group combines different change positions, so its average has no single common disruption. Coverage losses differ by experience type. Coverage is scored against the current reference, so it can fall when previously correct content becomes outdated. In the 24-task group, performance drops at both T09 and T17. Structural coverage falls by 18.92 points at T09 while rule coverage stays comparatively stable. At T17, rule coverage falls by 15.97 points while structural coverage changes little. The two coverage measures distinguish mismatches that task scores alone cannot identify. Appendix C.1.1 gives the individual environment curves. Coverage and performance can diverge. GPT-5.4 gains structural coverage across all three groups, yet its overall task performance remains far below the other displayed models. Coverage spans the full reference inventory, whereas successful delivery requires the relevant knowledge for a particular request and its effective use. Overall coverage therefore cannot substitute for task performance. Appendices C.1.3 and C.1.2 provide correlation and request-level analyses. Figure 4: Adaptation to environmental change: (a) timing and change count, (b) performance advantage by change mechanism, and (c) revision and retention of previously correct experience. 3.3.2 How Do Models Adapt Across Different Change Contexts? We examine performance recovery and selective experience revision under different change timings, counts, and mechanisms. Performance comparisons cover all 20 models, 18 environments, and 26 changes. Performance advantage denotes the task score with experience minus that without experience. In event-aligned comparisons, offset zero is the first affected task, and + 3 +3 is the fourth. Later events have higher average scores. Figure 4 (a) shows no consistent increase in the first-four-task performance advantage when comparing single-change environments by onset position. The lower curves compare full task-score trajectories by change count. In two-change streams, mean with-experience scores over the first four affected tasks rise from 55.94 after the first event to 62.15 after the second. The corresponding means in three-change streams are 68.62, 70.50, and 72.53. Later events therefore coincide with higher average scores, although different tasks and change content prevent interpreting this as faster recovery. Similar drops, different recovery. In Figure 4 (b), the resource group has the largest immediate loss of performance advantage, at 40.33 points. Structural and tool groups show similar losses of 35.98 and 36.63 points from the task immediately before change to the first affected task. By the fourth affected task, however, the structural group is only 0.16 points below its pre-change advantage, while the tool group remains 8.69 points below. Initial disruption thus does not capture the remaining loss after several tasks. These are descriptive comparisons across different environment groups. Revision remains incomplete. Figure 4 (c) follows 487 cases across eight environments, each combining a model, reference experience unit, and change event to distinguish revision failure from missing prior knowledge. Each case correctly records all relations selected for updating and at least one still-valid relation before change. Over the first four affected tasks, replacement-content coverage rises from 21.47% to 64.63%, while still-valid coverage remains between 88.07% and 91.07%. Revision thus remains less complete than retention within this previously learned subset. The trajectories also reflect when tasks expose replacement evidence. Bands span the interquartile range of model means. Appendices C.2.1 and C.2.2 examine this exposure and subsequent persistence. 3.3.3 How Do Agents Allocate Tool Use? The overall mean includes all 20 models. We analyze 7,040 with-experience tasks across all 20 models and 18 environments to examine interaction allocation and responses to change. History tools retrieve prior records, experience tools read or modify memory and skills, and environment tools query or act on the current state. We count explicit calls, including failures, and exclude background review. Changes are aligned at the first affected task. Appendix C.3.1 gives the counting and averaging procedures. History calls vary much more, from 0.14 for GPT-5.4 to 6.41 for GPT-5.5. These counts measure writing activity, not correct revision, which Figure 4 (c) assesses separately. 4 Related Work 4.1 Experience-Based Agent Adaptation Experience-based adaptation methods differ in what they retain from interaction and how they update it. Reflexion and ExpeL turn feedback and trajectories into verbal reflections or reusable insights ( Shinn et al., 2023 ; Zhao et al., 2024 ) . Other methods retain procedures: Voyager stores executable skills ( Wang et al., 2024 ) , while Agent Workflow Memory abstracts recurring action sequences into reusable workflows ( Wang et al., 2025 ) . To keep guidance current, Dynamic Cheatsheet revises test-time memory as new evidence arrives ( Suzgun et al., 2026 ) . ReScopeBench complements these methods with an evaluation of how acquired experience is reused and maintained across tasks as local conditions change. 4.2 Benchmarking Agent Memory and Experience Benchmarks study experience under supplied histories, interactive tasks, and changing conditions. LoCoMo, LongMemEval, and MemBench evaluate reasoning over supplied histories, with the latter two also assessing updates to remembered information ( Maharana et al., 2024 ; Wu et al., 2025 ; Tan et al., 2025 ) . MemoryAgentBench adds incremental learning and selective forgetting ( Hu et al., 2025 ) . Interactive benchmarks, including LifelongAgentBench and MemoryArena, evaluate experience use across tasks ( Zheng et al., 2025 ; He et al., 2026 ) , while skill-oriented benchmarks study acquisition, refinement, and transfer ( Zhang et al., 2026 ; Lei et al., 2026 ; Gao et al., 2026 ) . More recent benchmarks examine maintenance as information and environments change. AgingBench ( Zhu et al., 2026 ) studies memory degradation and revision, including agent-managed workspace scenarios. EvoArena ( Xu et al., 2026 ) evaluates evolving terminal workflows, codebases, and preferences, and measures whether saved memory contains evidence of user preferences. In ReScopeBench, tasks reuse discoverable structural and rule experience, and local changes make selected parts outdated. We assess saved content alongside task outcomes to distinguish correction of affected relationships from retention of those that remain valid. 5 Conclusion This paper introduced ReScopeBench, a benchmark for studying experience acquisition, reuse, and revision across 18 interactive environments. Its experience-first construction builds varied tasks around shared structural and rule experience, then introduces silent changes that invalidate selected content while leaving other content valid. Evaluations of 20 models show that retaining experience improves task performance, but rule experience is generally recorded less completely than structural experience. Further analyses show that agents preserve much of the still-valid content while leaving required updates incomplete, and can fail to complete requests even when the required experience is correctly recorded. More interaction after change does not always coincide with more explicit attempts to update saved experience. ReScopeBench offers a testbed for developing agents that selectively revise outdated experience and effectively apply what they retain. Appendix B Evaluation Details B.1 Experimental Setup We evaluate the 20 models in Table 2 on the same 18 versioned environments. Each model completes 352 tasks per condition, covering 26 environmental changes. We use the maximum supported reasoning effort where configurable. Explicit sampling settings use temperature 1 and top- p p 0.95; unsupported or omitted fields follow provider defaults. Foreground execution allows 100 agent iterations per task and up to 65,536 output tokens per response. An iteration may contain multiple tool calls. Environment-specific resource budgets apply separately. The Hermes memory and skill interfaces are pinned to revision 345cd2b057a45 . Memory and skills are limited to 16,000 and 100,000 characters, respectively. Agents receive a memory snapshot and skill index at task start and can retrieve full skills on demand. Four read-only history tools retrieve completed-task records through lexical matching and pagination, with a shared limit of 30 calls per task. Structural and rule experience are classified by content, regardless of where they are stored. No reference experience is preloaded. Both conditions start from the same environment state and use a fresh conversation for each task. With-experience streams retain memory, skills, and retrievable history. Without-experience streams clear these assets while retaining their own environment state. The two states then evolve independently through agent actions. Each model–environment pair has one stream per condition. GPT-5.5 also supported environment development. Agents can update experience during execution. Hermes skill review can run after ten tool iterations and a completed, uninterrupted response, with a separate limit of 100 iterations and 600,000 cumulative input tokens. It uses the same model, cannot access environment tools, and finishes before grading, so the current task’s score cannot supervise review. Later history retrieval may include permitted outcome feedback, but never private reference answers. B.2 Scoring and Aggregation BP and EP average all task positions, including the initial task. DP uses the first affected task after each change and includes adaptation within that task. SC and RC average binary claim scores within reference units, units within each experience type, and checkpoints within each environment. References follow the current environment version and include content not yet encountered by the agent. GPT-6 Astra evaluates one complete reference unit against the complete saved memory and skill corpus per request, using medium reasoning effort and a 14,000-token output limit. Model-identifying metadata is withheld. Each claim receives credit only when all applicable rubric checks pass. Equivalent wording and supported deductions are accepted, while unresolved contradictions receive no credit. We validate the structured responses and cited evidence before computing scores. These metrics measure reference coverage rather than the precision of all saved content. For each affected unit, UC measures coverage of the current update targets after the first affected task. RT measures the first checkpoint at which all update targets and the unit’s previously correct, still-valid claims are correct together. Update targets are shared across models, while the retained set depends on pre-change credit. Both target sets remain fixed within an L L -task window starting at the first affected task and ending before the next change or at stream completion. Only units with nonempty update targets participate. Completion at its first or last task scores 100 or 100 / L 100/L , respectively, and no completion scores zero. An empty retained set reduces the criterion to update completion. RT records first completion, not its subsequent persistence, and reflects the opportunities provided by the task schedule. All metrics weight environments equally. DP averages events within environments. UC and RT average affected units within events, then events within environments. Avg. averages the seven unrounded metrics. Longer streams and larger inventories do not receive more weight. The difference Δ m ​ e ​ t = W m ​ e ​ t − B m ​ e ​ t \Delta_{met}=W_{met}-B_{met} is distinct from DP. Position curves keep environment membership fixed within each stream-length group and equally average environments and models. Model interquartile ranges describe variation across models, not uncertainty across repeated runs. Inapplicable observations are excluded rather than scored as zero. C.2.2 Persistence after First Completion RT records first completion but not persistence. We therefore follow all 2,340 affected model/unit/event observations, including those outside the prior-correct cohort. Completion requires simultaneous credit for all current update targets N N and the fixed unchanged targets V V credited before the event. With empty V V , it measures update completion only. We track each first completion over two further checkpoints within the same reference epoch. Of 1,632 observations that complete at least once, 1,520 have both follow-ups. Among them, 1,364 remain complete at both checkpoints and 156 (10.26%) become incomplete at one or both. Another 112 completions lack sufficient follow-up, and 708 observations never complete. Figure 11 (a) shows all four outcomes. The 10.26% regression rate uses only the 1,520 eligible completions. First completion thus does not guarantee completeness over the next two tasks. Regression here means lost credit, not necessarily permanent forgetting or impaired task performance. C.3 Interaction and Action Allocation C.3.1 Tool Use Across Environments Figure 5 uses 151,569 foreground tool calls from all 7,040 with-experience tasks. Tool interfaces determine the categories. Environment tools query or act on the current environment. History tools retrieve prior episodes and records. Experience tools access or modify saved memory and skills. We count explicit requests, including failures, and exclude automatic memory loading and background review. A tool round is an assistant turn containing one or more calls with matching returns. Write counts include recognized requests to create, modify, or delete experience. An unidentified operation remains in the experience-call total but not the write count. One write can affect multiple entries, and the number of writes does not measure the amount or correctness of revised content. For Figure 5 (a), we average tasks within each environment and then weight the 18 environments equally, retaining zero-call tasks. Panels (b) and (c) use all 26 changes with the same observations at every offset from − 3 -3 to + 3 +3 . We average events within environments before averaging environments. The overall mean then equally weights all 20 models. In panel (c), Before averages offsets − 3 -3 to − 1 -1 , and At change denotes the first affected task at offset zero. Task requirements vary across positions, so these comparisons describe observed behavior rather than isolate a causal effect of change. An unchanged write count does not rule out different written content, later writes, or updates during background review.
