The Trinity: Capability-Driven Recursive Self-Improvement for Multimodal Agents The Trinity Research Team Project Page GitHub Abstract Multimodal intelligence can be viewed as human-guided co-evolution between worlds and agents through interaction.
However, scaling this process faces two bottlenecks: constructing heterogeneous, verifiable environments is costly, and manual Harness iteration cannot keep pace with growing task diversity and complexity.
A key insight is that tasks with different modalities and surface forms can share the same underlying capability requirements.
We therefore introduce The Trinity , a recursive self-improvement framework coupling world generation with Harness evolution through three roles: Creator, Explorer, and Evolver.
Drawing on verified execution trajectories, Evolver shifts the basis of task organization from surface categories to shared requirements, reorganizing the original task tree into a hierarchical capability tree.
We introduce Capability-Driven Real2Sim2Real : for heterogeneous tasks at the same node, Creator abstracts their common requirements into simplified, verifiable simulated worlds for capability learning, while evaluation on original tasks measures transfer.
Along this capability tree, Explorer progressively evolves reusable capability modules through selective inheritance, forming a Harness tree whose validated updates accumulate across iterations within a fixed topology.
The resulting Harnesses specialize in different capabilities, motivating a Capability-Aware Harness Router that uses public task information to select compatible module configurations from the tree.
Our evolved Harnesses improve mean performance by 34 points across 28 training benchmarks and 26 points across 18 unseen benchmarks, including two real-world embodied tasks. We view multimodal intelligence as an evolving loop: worlds provide tasks, humans guide learning, and agents interact with the world to produce verifiable outcomes [ 16 , 17 ] .
As tasks and environments grow increasingly complex, this loop faces two bottlenecks:
(1) Constructing large-scale, heterogeneous, and verifiable environments manually is costly, motivating automated world generation; and
(2) Agent capability is increasingly constrained by the Harness [ 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 ] , whose manual iteration cannot keep pace with growing task complexity and diversity. Inspired by Harness recursive self-improvement [ 24 , 25 , 26 ] and agent–environment co-evolution [ 27 , 28 , 29 ] , we ask whether this manual loop can improve continuously.
Generated worlds provide adaptive curricula, while verified interaction feedback guides Harness updates. However, extending this process across heterogeneous text-only and multimodal tasks [ 30 , 31 ] presents a challenge. Current Harness RSI works focus on text-modality tasks (e.g., coding and math [ 24 , 23 , 32 , 33 ] ) and adopt task-specific evolution [ 34 , 22 , 35 , 25 ] .
This approach is suitable when tasks share similar capability requirements, but can lead to Harness overfitting and poor generalization across heterogeneous tasks.
Conversely, a global Harness struggles to accommodate conflicting task demands, including performance–efficiency trade-offs in embodied tasks, limiting its efficiency and adaptability. A key insight is that tasks with different modalities and surface forms can share the same underlying capability requirements.
For example, although ARC-AGI-3 [ 36 ] and Micromouse [ 37 ] differ in task structure, both rely on accurate perception and state tracking: the former requires recognizing grid states and their changes, while the latter requires continuously updating environmental information during maze exploration.
Grouping tasks by these shared capability requirements makes the underlying mechanisms a common learning objective, enabling generalization across tasks. Position. Current RSI training relies heavily on task-specific optimization, limiting capability generalization across tasks. We advocate a shift from task-driven to capability-driven self-improvement, where shared capability requirements across tasks guide the learning and optimization of multiple specialized Harnesses. This shift highlights the role of shared capabilities in cross-task generalization and naturally motivates a capability-aware Router that matches tasks to suitable Harnesses based on their capability requirements. To operationalize this position, we introduce The Trinity , a recursive self-improvement framework coupling environment generation with agent evolution through Creator, Explorer, and Evolver.
Using verified trajectories, Evolver identifies task capability requirements and groups tasks sharing them, reorganizing the original task tree into a hierarchical capability tree.
Heterogeneous tasks at the same node share a learning objective, motivating Capability-Driven Real2Sim2Real : Creator abstracts their common requirements into simplified, verifiable environments, while evaluation on the original tasks measures capability transfer.
Along the capability tree, Explorer evolves a Harness tree through selective inheritance and local refinement, while Creator evolves at corresponding nodes and consolidates validated updates into a new Base Harness at the end of each iteration. The Harnesses specialize in different capabilities, requiring task-dependent selection.
For Explorer, our Capability-Aware Harness Router uses verified execution experience and public task information to select a matching branch of Explorer’s Harness tree.
For Creator, overlap within its Harness tree motivates merging complementary modules and removing near-duplicates to form a new Base Harness.
Explorer and Creator play distinct roles in RSI processes along the same capability tree. Contributions. ❶ Capability-driven multimodal RSI. We make shared capability requirements the organizing principle of Harness evolution. Capability-Driven Real2Sim2Real turns these requirements into compact training worlds, yielding a reusable module-based Harness tree whose specialization motivates capability-aware routing.
❷ Capability–module priors for Harness design. We connect task-level capability requirements to reusable module configurations, using empirical associations and controlled interventions to examine which module support matters for a task.
❸ Efficient evolution and transfer. We show that targeting shared decision logic supports effective Harness improvement at low evolution cost, with transfer across heterogeneous tasks, unseen benchmarks, and real-world embodied settings. 2 Preliminaries 2.1 Multimodal Agentic Tasks Long-horizon multimodal agentic tasks span interactive games [ 38 ] , GUI manipulation [ 39 ] , and embodied interaction [ 13 , 14 ] . They combine perception, understanding, reasoning, and generation with memory, planning, and adaptation across observation–action sequences. Agents must retain evidence, revise hypotheses, and adjust plans as observations arrive. Because later decisions depend on earlier actions and feedback, misread observations or forgotten constraints can propagate across steps. Agent performance thus increasingly depends on effective Harness organization and coordination. Explorer collects trajectories; Evolver organizes requirements from specifications and verified trajectories into a capability tree (Sections 3.2 – 3.3 ).
Through Capability-Driven Real2Sim2Real , Creator builds simplified, verifiable worlds; original-task evaluation measures transfer (Section 3.3 ). Explorer selectively inherits and refines reusable modules, accumulating validated updates in a fixed-topology Harness tree (Sections 3.4 and 3.5 ). The Capability-Aware Harness Router selects a compatible branch using public task information and verified experience (Section 3.6 ). 3.2 Stage 1: Task Tree Construction Benchmark Organization. Evolver normalizes public multimodal benchmarks into specifications of goals, observations, actions, feedback, task-completion criteria, and budgets, independently checked against their sources. A common observation–action interface preserves information access and scoring rules. Benchmarks are grouped into five requirement domains: rule discovery, constrained problem solving, perception and control, multi-agent interaction, and long-horizon environments. The training/unseen split is fixed before capability induction. World Exploration. Explorer collects trajectories from sampled training instances using ℋ 0 \mathcal{H}_{0} under fixed settings. Only training records support capability-tree construction, world generation, module learning, and Router training (Section 3.6 ). Unseen capability requirements are assessed only after the tree is fixed, for evaluation. Instance selectors and initial-state hashes are fixed in advance. Diagnostic reruns reset memory; completed runs are retained, while infrastructure failures are marked invalid and retried. Hash-chain checks verify execution records. 3.3 Stage 2: Capability Tree Construction Evolver derives requirements from task goals, permitted observations, action rules, and task-completion criteria, linking them to rules and trajectory steps satisfying or violating these criteria through rule-based checks. Host-private information supports only retrospective verification. Requirement Organization. At each node, Explorer executes admitted worlds with inherited and local modules. Evolver compares successful and failed trajectories using public observations, actions, and host-assigned outcomes to identify node-specific improvements. It develops reusable modules through host-registered bindings in the six-protocol library (Section 2.2 ), refining interfaces, bounded configurations, or implementations. Updates record node, predecessor-version, episode, and executable-example references.
Before local updates, the host validates evidence, ownership, interfaces, lineage, and typed input–output assertions; ancestor modules remain preserved. Bindings use public context and permitted memory under a fixed runtime policy. Multiple bindings per protocol may coexist, with model calls determining execution order. Admission verifies grounding and executability; evaluation measures performance.
Creator evolves only upon verifier-detected failures. At each iteration’s end, validated proposals are consolidated with existing Base Harness modules, merging complementary modules and removing near-duplicates. Self-improvement spans Gödel machines [ 87 ] , LLM systems that rewrite their own agent code [ 88 , 89 , 24 ] , automated agent design [ 90 , 91 , 92 ] , prompt optimization [ 93 , 94 , 95 ] , and learning from self-feedback and reflection [ 96 , 97 , 98 ] . With weights fixed, improvement shifts to the harness, which is synthesized from environment feedback [ 33 ] , repaired from failed trajectories [ 32 , 35 ] , self-revised [ 19 ] , optimized as code [ 18 , 20 ] , adapted at test time [ 99 ] or per task [ 22 ] , improved at the meta level [ 100 , 101 ] , and shown to lift frozen MLLMs [ 21 ] ; benchmarks and surveys map this space [ 102 , 23 , 103 ] . Closest to ours are JIT-Agent’s harness generator [ 25 ] , HarnessX’s typed primitives [ 104 ] , HSI’s per-task-family harnesses [ 34 ] , ModularRSI’s modules evolved on benchmark-disjoint tasks [ 26 ] , and the flat capability map of Harness Continual Learning [ 105 ] and regime-branched harness tree of Adaptive Auto-Harness [ 106 ] , both evolved on the task stream. Trinity instead induces a capability tree from training trajectories, shares prefixes across 44 heterogeneous benchmarks with selective module inheritance, evolves Harnesses in Creator-generated worlds that exclude target-task layouts and solutions, and evaluates on held-out benchmarks and real robots. Unlike LLM routers [ 40 ] , ours routes among capability-specific Harnesses over one frozen backbone. Co-evolution of Agents and Environments. Open-ended co-evolution [ 107 , 108 , 109 ] , unsupervised environment design [ 110 , 29 ] , curricula [ 111 , 112 ] , and embodied co-design [ 28 ] adapt training to the learner; Voyager grows a skill library under an automatic curriculum [ 113 ] . Loops co-evolve language agents with environments [ 114 ] , optimizers [ 115 ] , or harnesses [ 116 , 117 ] ; environment generation spans embodied [ 118 , 119 ] , tool-use [ 120 ] , and terminal settings [ 121 , 122 ] , language world models [ 123 ] , and game verification [ 124 ] . SPADE is closest [ 27 ] : one LLM designs environments with verification code and learns roles by reinforcement learning. Trinity keeps the backbone frozen and couples world generation with Explorer and Creator Harness evolution along a capability hierarchy, execution outcomes refining Explorer and verifier-detected failures refining Creator. 6 Conclusion We advocate Capability-Driven Recursive Self-Improvement and introduce The Trinity , a framework that couples world generation with Harness evolution through Creator, Explorer, and Evolver.
By organizing heterogeneous tasks around shared capability requirements, the framework constructs simplified, verifiable worlds whose execution feedback guides the evolution of reusable modules.
These modules form specialized Harness configurations, with capability–module correlations and controlled interventions providing empirical support for capability requirements as priors for module design and composition.
To apply these evolved capabilities across tasks, capability-aware routing selects suitable Harness configurations using public task information and verified execution experience.
Experiments across diverse training and unseen benchmarks demonstrate improved aggregate performance and cross-task generalization, while deployment in two real-world embodied settings further illustrates the transfer of these capabilities to physical environments. 7 Discussion Limitations. Our framework uses web search for benchmark discovery, but retrieval coverage and result quality may vary across queries.
In addition, our current study focuses primarily on self-improvement at the Harness level. Outlook. Capability-driven learning offers a new paradigm for generalizable agent improvement, where shared capability requirements across tasks guide learning and optimization. Our framework and results at the Harness level demonstrate the promise of this perspective. Looking ahead, we envision dynamically expanding the capability tree to capture emerging capability requirements and extending this paradigm to model parameter training, where capability-aware routing and RSI form a closed loop that jointly drives capability discovery, acquisition, and refinement. 8 Authors Core Contributors: Weidong Tang 1,2 Ruiheng Wang 3 Jingxuan Zhang 4 Hexin Wang 1 Contributors: Haoyuan Chen 1 Zhengfeiyang Zhang 1,4 Yixing Ma 1,5 Zichen Jiang 4 Ziyu Guo 6 Haodong Li 6 Zhengbo Jiao 7 Zhiqing Cui 7 Xinxiang Yin 8 Shuo Liang 1,4 Zhenglin Wan 4 Hongzhan Lin 4 Wangbo Zhao 7 Kaipeng Zhang 9 Corresponding Authors: Pengfei Zhou 1,4 Odin Zhang 6 Yang You 4 Affiliations: 1 InfRec 2 Xidian University 3 Tongji University 4 National University of Singapore 5 University of California, Berkeley 6 The Chinese University of Hong Kong 7 The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology 8 The University of Hong Kong 9 Shanghai Innovation Institute References [1] J. Appendix A Task Tree The benchmark inventory comprises 44 benchmarks organized into five domains and 15 subclasses (Figure 9 ): 26 training benchmarks, 16 unseen benchmarks, and two embodied benchmarks, SOON [ 67 ] and CaP-X [ 68 ] , each evaluated both in simulation and on a real robot (Appendix E ). The five domains are Latent Rule & Mechanism Discovery; Constraint Solving, Puzzles & Design; Perception, Navigation & Control; Multi-Agent Strategy & Social Interaction; and Long-Horizon Open-Ended Environments. Counting the simulation version of each embodied benchmark as a training benchmark and its real-robot version as an unseen benchmark gives the 28 training and 18 unseen benchmarks of Section 4.1 . Every subclass contains at least one training and one unseen benchmark. Figure 9 : Task tree. The 44 benchmarks are grouped into five domains and 15 subclasses. Training benchmarks take the colour of their domain and unseen benchmarks a lighter shade of it; SOON and CaP-X show their simulation and real-robot versions; * marks environments rebuilt from published rules. Candidate benchmarks were collected through agent web search over five types of source: research benchmarks released with code or data, public AI competitions, human contests and testing standards, published games and puzzle communities, and professional practice. Because a multimodal agent must perceive its world through whatever interface that world exposes, the inventory spans every input modality in which agentic tasks are posed, and each benchmark retains the observation protocol of its source. Each modality probes a different part of an agent’s capability. Image input (photographs, rendered scenes, puzzle images, and robot camera views) tests perception from pixels, viewpoint selection, and the grounding of decisions in what is seen. Video input tests evidence acquisition over time: selecting frames within a budget, tracking events across them, and understanding what happened. Boards, grids, and maps rendered as text test spatial reasoning and state tracking over a symbolic visual state; with pixel-level perception removed, a failure can be attributed to reasoning rather than to seeing. Plain text tests the capabilities a multimodal agent relies on downstream of perception: rule discovery, experimental design, planning, negotiation, and strategic interaction. Keeping all four modalities in one inventory lets visual and text benchmarks share nodes in the capability tree (Appendix B ). Nine of the training sources publish their rules but provide no agent-facing environment: Chess Tactics [ 50 ] , Eleusis [ 41 ] , Euro NCAP AEB [ 63 ] , Master Keying [ 55 ] , Micromouse [ 37 ] , Pallet Rack Inspection [ 60 ] , Saboteur [ 73 ] , The Long Dark [ 81 ] , and WCA Cube Solving [ 53 ] (marked * in Figure 9 ). We rebuilt each of them through agent web search from the published rules and examples, preserving the mechanics, information access, and scoring of the source; the rebuilt environments present their state to the agent as images or symbolic text. Every unseen benchmark other than the two real-robot versions runs on its official environment or evaluation code, e.g., the BALROG harness [ 38 ] , the PHYRE simulator [ 70 ] , the DiG-bench server [ 49 ] , the ALE-Bench judge [ 58 ] , the Video-MME-v2 scorer [ 71 ] , and the Besiege game of BuildArena [ 59 ] . Appendix C Harness Tree Explorer and Creator both start from H 0 and evolve along the same capability tree, but they retain their learned modules differently. Explorer keeps a Harness at every node except e: each node selectively inherits its parent’s modules and refines its own local modules, and the resulting Harness tree is retained for routing. Creator keeps a single shared library: within an iteration, its node Harnesses inherit updated parent modules, and at the end of the iteration the modules proposed at all nodes are consolidated with the current library into the Base Harness of the next iteration. Explorer’s trajectories drive Evolver’s local module revisions, whereas worlds rejected by the verifiers drive Creator’s module proposals. Every module is a versioned function behind a prompt-facing interface and is installed only after the host checks its evidence, interface, and version lineage. C.1 Explorer Harness Tree Explorer plays every benchmark with the frozen backbone (Appendix D ), and each instance uses the best-scoring compatible node Harness under the Oracle setting. Every node Harness starts from H 0 , which has no learned module, and combines the inherited modules at their retained versions with the node’s local modules; a benchmark at c → \to d → \to a therefore uses the modules of c, c → \to d, and c → \to d → \to a, subject to selective inheritance (Figure 12 ). At each node, Explorer plays the admitted training levels with these modules, and Evolver compares successful and failed trajectories to create or revise local modules; the host validates evidence, ownership, interfaces, and version lineage before every update, and ancestor modules are preserved. An updated parent is inherited only if it achieves strictly more host-verified task completions than the previously inherited modules under the same validation tasks, trial count, and budget; otherwise the previous modules are kept. A local module overrides an inherited module bound to the same identifier. Module calls take public inputs and return structured advice without advancing the environment, with at most two calls per step and none forced. Within each iteration, Creator visits the nodes top-down: the children of the root start from the Base Harness, and every other node inherits its parent’s updated modules. Each node keeps its own guidance, one construction note, and one public action budget per iteration. Verifier-detected failures trigger local evolution, and at the end of the iteration the validated proposals of all nodes are consolidated with the current library in learning order (Equation 7 ): validated revisions replace their registered predecessors; among new modules, distinct capabilities are retained, partial overlaps are merged to preserve complementary functionality, and near-duplicates keep the earlier-learned module and discard the later one. The consolidated library becomes the Base Harness of the next iteration; it is shared by all nodes and exposed as a single tool that runs one module per call. The host, not the model, supplies the inputs of each call: the build digest, the submission allowance, and, for a rule module, a measurement of the build. Fields that the host does not supply remain unknown, and missing evidence never passes. Before submission, the host reruns the applicable audits and blocks any missing, stale, or conflicting result; Creator returns errors in the frozen task design or verifier contract to their owner instead of rewriting them. In the module names, a witness is a recorded route run on the current build; the commitment cost is the number of extra actions that a wrong commitment costs over the direct route; the screening band is the performance range into which a world must fall; and a neutralized control is the previous version with the changed difficulty axis reset. The final Creator module library holds 25 modules (Figure 13 ). Eleven are pre-RSI modules, audits that form the initial module library of the Creator Harness and apply the host’s formulas to recorded routes, claims, edits, or submissions: Commitment Cost Arithmetic Audit, Witness and Action Ceiling Audit, Difficulty Axis Range and Feasibility Audit, Screening Band Direction Diagnosis, Layout Novelty Ledger, Effective Difficulty Change Audit, Single Change Attribution Audit, Repair Scope Gate, Claim and Execution Evidence Census, Bounded Search Declaration Audit, and Submission Budget and Repeated Rejection Guard. The other 14 are rule modules, predicate programs over one kind of host measurement learned from rejected worlds, each listed with the nodes at which it was formed: Production and Private Witness Isolation (d), Accepted Entry and Terminal Consistency (d → \to a), Reasoning Change and Direction Audit (a, c, d → \to a, e), Public Visual State and Probe Coverage Audit (a, b, c), Required Composition and Negative Route Audit (b, d → \to b), Target Attributes, Classes, and Acceptance Audit (a, b, c, d → \to b), Controlled Identity and Frame-State Alignment Audit (b, c, d → \to a), Action Polarity and Coordinate Basis Audit (a, b), Interaction Coverage and Decision-Time Visibility Audit (b, d), Neutralized Control Initialization and Completion Audit (b, d → \to a), Meaningful Exploratory Witness Pricing (e), Submission Artifact Completeness (a), Task Design Schema and Axis Type Handoff (c, d → \to a), and Authoritative Verifier Contract Handoff (all 11 nodes). For SOON we rebuilt the office from its floor plan and from photographs and video of the site: the side room where the robot starts, the doorway into the office, the rows of desks with their chairs and cabinets, and the white pedestal fan beside the target desk. The office is a 3D scene in which the walls and furniture are collision obstacles: each action moves the robot by the nominal distance or angle of the real robot’s motion profile, a move that hits an obstacle counts as a collision and ends the episode as a failure, and after every action the agent receives the head-camera image rendered from the robot’s new pose with the height, downward pitch, and field of view of the real camera. On the real robot a person judges every episode on site, and an episode in which the robot collides with an obstacle is a failure. The G1 starts in the side room, walks through the doorway into the office, and must stop at the desk next to the white fan and touch it within 30 movement actions. For CaP-X we built the table scene in the MuJoCo physics simulator [ 125 ] with the official AlohaMini2 model, and with objects of the same size and colour as the real ones: three paper cups, 9 cm tall, 7 cm across the mouth, and 5 cm across the closed bottom, stand upside down on a white table, and in most layouts a 2 cm cube, red, green, or blue, lies under one of them. The AlohaMini2 stands at the near edge of the table with its two six-joint arms; its mobile base and lift stay still. After every action the agent sees the head camera, the wrist cameras on both grippers, and a fixed side camera; it moves a gripper in small steps while the host solves the arm’s inverse kinematics, and within 30 actions it must report whether a cube is present and, if it is, its colour and the cup it is under.
