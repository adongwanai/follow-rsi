AbstractMultimodal intelligence can be viewed as human-guided co-evolution between worlds
and agents through interaction. However, scaling this process faces two bottlenecks: constructing
heterogeneous, verifiable environments is costly, and manual Harness iteration cannot keep pace with
growing task diversity and complexity. A key insight is that tasks with different modalities and surface
forms can share the same underlying capability requirements. We therefore introduceThe Trinity,
a recursive self-improvement framework coupling world generation with Harness evolution through
three roles: Creator, Explorer, and Evolver. Drawing on verified execution trajectories, Evolver shifts
the basis of task organization from surface categories to shared requirements, reorganizing the original
task tree into a hierarchical capability tree. We introduceCapability-Driven Real2Sim2Real: for
heterogeneous tasks at the same node, Creator abstracts their common requirements into simplified,
verifiable simulated worlds for capability learning, while evaluation on original tasks measures transfer.
Along this capability tree, Explorer progressively evolves reusable capability modules through selective
inheritance, forming a Harness tree whose validated updates accumulate across iterations within a fixed
topology. The resulting Harnesses specialize in different capabilities, motivating aCapability-Aware
Harness Routerthat uses public task information to select compatible module configurations from the
tree. Our evolved Harnesses improve mean performance by 34 points across 28 training benchmarks
and 26 points across 18 unseen benchmarks, including two real-world embodied tasks.

1. Introduction
Multimodal foundation models have rapidly advanced in understanding, reasoning, and generation
across text, images, video, and audio [1–12], enabling agents to perceive, decide, and act in open
environments [13–15]. We view multimodal intelligence as an evolving loop: worlds provide tasks,
humans guide learning, and agents interact with the world to produce verifiable outcomes [16,17]. As
tasks and environments grow increasingly complex, this loop faces two bottlenecks: (1) Constructing
large-scale, heterogeneous, and verifiable environments manually is costly, motivating automated
world generation; and (2) Agent capability is increasingly constrained by the Harness [18–23], whose
manual iteration cannot keep pace with growing task complexity and diversity.
Inspired by Harness recursive self-improvement [24–26] and agent–environment co-evolution [27–
29], we ask whether this manual loop can improve continuously. Generated worlds provide adaptive
curricula, while verified interaction feedback guides Harness updates.
However,extendingthisprocessacrossheterogeneoustext-onlyandmultimodaltasks[ 30,31]presents
a challenge. Current Harness RSI works focus on text-modality tasks (e.g., coding and math [23, 24,
32, 33]) and adopt task-specific evolution [22, 25, 34, 35]. This approach is suitable when tasks share
similar capability requirements, but can lead to Harness overfitting and poor generalization across
heterogeneous tasks. Conversely, a global Harness struggles to accommodate conflicting task demands,
including performance–efficiency trade-offs in embodied tasks, limiting its efficiency and adaptability.
A key insight is that tasks with different modalities and surface forms can share the same underlying
capability requirements. For example, although ARC-AGI-3 [36] and Micromouse [37] differ in task
structure, both rely on accurate perception and state tracking: the former requires recognizing grid
states and their changes, while the latter requires continuously updating environmental information
during maze exploration. Grouping tasks by these shared capability requirements makes the
underlying mechanisms a common learning objective, enabling generalization across tasks.
Position.Current RSI training relies heavily on task-specific optimization, limiting capability
generalization across tasks. We advocate a shift from task-driven to capability-driven self-
improvement, where shared capability requirements across tasks guide the learning and opti-
mization of multiple specialized Harnesses. This shift highlights the role of shared capabilities in
cross-task generalization and naturally motivates a capability-aware Router that matches tasks
to suitable Harnesses based on their capability requirements.
To operationalize this position, we introduceThe Trinity, a recursive self-improvement framework
coupling environment generation with agent evolution through Creator, Explorer, and Evolver.
Using verified trajectories, Evolver identifies task capability requirements and groups tasks sharing
them, reorganizing the original task tree into a hierarchical capability tree. Heterogeneous tasks
at the same node share a learning objective, motivatingCapability-Driven Real2Sim2Real: Creator
abstracts their common requirements into simplified, verifiable environments, while evaluation on
the original tasks measures capability transfer. Along the capability tree, Explorer evolves a Harness
tree through selective inheritance and local refinement, while Creator evolves at corresponding nodes
and consolidates validated updates into a new Base Harness at the end of each iteration.
The Harnesses specialize in different capabilities, requiring task-dependent selection. For Explorer,
ourCapability-Aware Harness Routeruses verified execution experience and public task information
to select a matching branch of Explorer’s Harness tree. For Creator, overlap within its Harness tree
motivates merging complementary modules and removing near-duplicates to form a new Base Harness.
Explorer and Creator play distinct roles in RSI processes along the same capability tree.

2. Preliminaries
2.1. Multimodal Agentic Tasks
Long-horizon multimodal agentic tasks span interactive games [38], GUI manipulation [39], and
embodied interaction [13, 14]. They combine perception, understanding, reasoning, and generation
with memory, planning, and adaptation across observation–action sequences. Agents must retain
evidence, revise hypotheses, and adjust plans as observations arrive. Because later decisions
depend on earlier actions and feedback, misread observations or forgotten constraints can propagate
across steps. Agent performance thus increasingly depends on effective Harness organization
and coordination. Dynamically coordinating capabilities over long interactions and adapting to
increasingly large, heterogeneous task sets remain key challenges.

3.1. Framework Overview
The Trinity couples environment generation and Harness evolution with a frozen backbone (Figure 2).
Explorer collects trajectories; Evolver organizes requirements from specifications and verified trajec-
tories into a capability tree (Sections 3.2–3.3). ThroughCapability-Driven Real2Sim2Real, Creator
builds simplified, verifiable worlds; original-task evaluation measures transfer (Section 3.3). Explorer
selectively inherits and refines reusable modules, accumulating validated updates in a fixed-topology
Harness tree (Sections 3.4 and 3.5). TheCapability-Aware Harness Routerselects a compatible
branch using public task information and verified experience (Section 3.6).

3.4. Stage 3: Harness Tree Construction
For Explorer, capability-driven RSI builds a Harness tree aligned with the capability tree. At each node,
Explorer executes admitted worlds with inherited and local modules. Evolver compares successful
and failed trajectories using public observations, actions, and host-assigned outcomes to identify
node-specific improvements. It developsreusable modulesthrough host-registered bindings in the
six-protocol library (Section 2.2), refining interfaces, bounded configurations, or implementations.
Updates record node, predecessor-version, episode, and executable-example references. Before
local updates, the host validates evidence, ownership, interfaces, lineage, and typed input–output
assertions; ancestor modules remain preserved. Bindings use public context and permitted memory
under a fixed runtime policy. Multiple bindings per protocol may coexist, with model calls determining
execution order. Admission verifies grounding and executability; evaluation measures performance.
Creator evolves only upon verifier-detected failures. At each iteration’s end, validated proposals are
consolidated with existing Base Harness modules, merging complementary modules and removing
near-duplicates. Section 3.5 details persistence, selective inheritance, and consolidation.

5. Related Work
Recursive Harness Self-Improvement.Self-improvement spans Gödel machines [87], LLM sys-
tems that rewrite their own agent code [24, 88, 89], automated agent design [90–92], prompt
optimization [93–95], and learning from self-feedback and reflection [96–98]. With weights fixed, im-
provement shifts to the harness, which is synthesized from environment feedback [33], repaired from
failed trajectories [32, 35], self-revised [19], optimized as code [18, 20], adapted at test time [99]
or per task [22], improved at the meta level [100, 101], and shown to lift frozen MLLMs [21];
benchmarks and surveys map this space [23, 102, 103]. Closest to ours are JIT-Agent’s harness gen-
erator [25], HarnessX’s typed primitives [104], HSI’s per-task-family harnesses [34], ModularRSI’s
modules evolved on benchmark-disjoint tasks [26], and the flat capability map of Harness Continual
Learning [105] and regime-branched harness tree of Adaptive Auto-Harness [106], both evolved
on the task stream. Trinity instead induces a capability tree from training trajectories, shares pre-
fixes across 44 heterogeneous benchmarks with selective module inheritance, evolves Harnesses in
Creator-generated worlds that exclude target-task layouts and solutions, and evaluates on held-out
benchmarks and real robots. Unlike LLM routers [40], ours routes among capability-specific Harnesses
over one frozen backbone.
Co-evolution of Agents and Environments.Open-ended co-evolution [107–109], unsupervised
environment design [29, 110], curricula [111, 112], and embodied co-design [28] adapt training
to the learner; Voyager grows a skill library under an automatic curriculum [113]. Loops co-evolve
language agents with environments [114], optimizers [115], or harnesses [116, 117]; environment
generation spans embodied [118, 119], tool-use [120], and terminal settings [121, 122], language
world models [123], and game verification [124]. SPADE is closest [27]: one LLM designs environ-
ments with verification code and learns roles by reinforcement learning. Trinity keeps the backbone
frozen and couples world generation with Explorer and Creator Harness evolution along a capability

6. Conclusion
We advocate Capability-Driven Recursive Self-Improvement and introduceThe Trinity, a framework
that couples world generation with Harness evolution through Creator, Explorer, and Evolver. By
organizing heterogeneous tasks around shared capability requirements, the framework constructs
simplified, verifiableworldswhoseexecutionfeedbackguidesthe evolution ofreusablemodules. These
modules form specialized Harness configurations, with capability–module correlations and controlled
interventions providing empirical support for capability requirements as priors for module design and
composition. To apply these evolved capabilities across tasks, capability-aware routing selects suitable
Harness configurations using public task information and verified execution experience. Experiments
across diverse training and unseen benchmarks demonstrate improved aggregate performance and
cross-task generalization, while deployment in two real-world embodied settings further illustrates
the transfer of these capabilities to physical environments.
7. Discussion
Limitations.Our framework uses web search for benchmark discovery, but retrieval coverage and
result quality may vary across queries. In addition, our current study focuses primarily on self-
improvement at the Harness level.
Outlook.Capability-driven learning offers a new paradigm for generalizable agent improvement,
where shared capability requirements across tasks guide learning and optimization. Our framework
and results at the Harness level demonstrate the promise of this perspective. Looking ahead, we
envision dynamically expanding the capability tree to capture emerging capability requirements and
extending this paradigm to model parameter training, where capability-aware routing and RSI form
a closed loop that jointly drives capability discovery, acquisition, and refinement.

A. Task Tree
The benchmark inventory comprises 44 benchmarks organized into five domains and 15 subclasses
(Figure 9): 26 training benchmarks, 16 unseen benchmarks, and two embodied benchmarks,
SOON [67] and CaP-X [68], each evaluated both in simulation and on a real robot (Appendix E).
The five domains are Latent Rule & Mechanism Discovery; Constraint Solving, Puzzles & Design;
Perception, Navigation & Control; Multi-Agent Strategy & Social Interaction; and Long-Horizon
Open-Ended Environments. Counting the simulation version of each embodied benchmark as a
training benchmark and its real-robot version as an unseen benchmark gives the 28 training and
18 unseen benchmarks of Section 4.1. Every subclass contains at least one training and one unseen
benchmark.
Figure 9| Task tree.The 44 benchmarks are grouped into five domains and 15 subclasses. Training
benchmarks take the colour of their domain and unseen benchmarks a lighter shade of it; SOON and
CaP-X show their simulation and real-robot versions; * marks environments rebuilt from published
rules.
Candidate benchmarks were collected through agent web search over five types of source: research
benchmarks released with code or data, public AI competitions, human contests and testing standards,
published games and puzzle communities, and professional practice. Because a multimodal agent
must perceive its world through whatever interface that world exposes, the inventory spans every
input modality in which agentic tasks are posed, and each benchmark retains the observation protocol
of its source. Each modality probes a different part of an agent’s capability. Image input (photographs,
rendered scenes, puzzle images, and robot camera views) tests perception from pixels, viewpoint
selection, and the grounding of decisions in what is seen. Video input tests evidence acquisition
over time: selecting frames within a budget, tracking events across them, and understanding what

B. Capability Tree
Independent agents propose capability categories from the task specifications and verified trajectories
of the training benchmarks, a judge consolidates them, and reviewers independently check definitions,
evidence, assignments, and coverage. The result is six capabilities and 30 defect types (Table 7):
a Perception & State Tracking (PST), b Hypothesis & Counterpart Inference (HCI), c Constraint
Checking & Feedback Repair (CFR), d Goal Commitment & Value Trade-off (GVT), e Video Evidence
Acquisition (VEA), and f Video Comprehension (VC). Each defect type is an observable failure
pattern that evidences a gap in its capability. Every training record is used for capability induction,
tree construction, level generation, module evolution, and the Router’s training memory. Unseen
benchmarks are recorded only after all Harness training has finished, from their runs with H0; these
records go through the same categorization procedure and are anchored to the fixed taxonomy
without changing it.

The six capabilities are parallel and non-overlapping, with no causal relation among them; ordered
by the breadth of the domains each capability spans, the fixed order c, d, a, b, e, f defines the paths
of the capability tree, and most benchmarks require more than one capability. The flat capability tree
(Figure 10) shows this directly: each capability lists every benchmark that requires it, so a benchmark
with several requirements appears under each of them.
Figure 10| Flat capability tree.Bricks keep the colours of Figure 9; unseen benchmarks are in
italics; * marks rebuilt environments.
The capability tree (Figure 11) organizes the same assignment by combination: the requirement set of
each training benchmark, sorted in this order, forms a path from the root, and these paths are merged
along their common prefixes. Each node stands for one combination of capabilities, read along the
branch from the root: for example, node c holds the benchmarks that require constraint checking
alone, its child c→d those that also require goal commitment, and c→d→a those that further require
perception and state tracking. A benchmark sits at the node of its own combination, regardless
of its domain. AdaPlanBench, Master Keying, Memory Maze, Pooltool Billiards, and TextQuests,
for instance, come from three domains and differ widely in content, from adaptive planning and
lock-system design to maze navigation, billiard shots, and text adventures; yet they share the same
capability requirements and therefore all sit at c→a. An edge adds a capability; it does not imply
any dependency or causal relation between the two capabilities. The 11 non-root nodes define the
shared structure of the Explorer and Creator Harness trees, and both evolve along the paths and
structure of the capability tree (Appendix C). Unseen benchmarks are then placed at the nodes of

C. Harness Tree
ExplorerandCreatorbothstartfromH 0 andevolvealongthesamecapabilitytree, buttheyretaintheir
learned modules differently. Explorer keeps a Harness at every node except e: each node selectively
inherits its parent’s modules and refines its own local modules, and the resulting Harness tree is
retained for routing. Creator keeps a single shared library: within an iteration, its node Harnesses
inherit updated parent modules, and at the end of the iteration the modules proposed at all nodes are
consolidated with the current library into the Base Harness of the next iteration. Explorer’s trajectories
drive Evolver’s local module revisions, whereas worlds rejected by the verifiers drive Creator’s module
proposals. Every module is a versioned function behind a prompt-facing interface and is installed
only after the host checks its evidence, interface, and version lineage.
C.1. Explorer Harness Tree
Explorer plays every benchmark with the frozen backbone (Appendix D), and each instance uses
the best-scoring compatible node Harness under the Oracle setting. Every node Harness starts from
H0, which has no learned module, and combines the inherited modules at their retained versions
with the node’s local modules; a benchmark at c→d→a therefore uses the modules of c, c→d, and
c→d→a, subject to selective inheritance (Figure 12). At each node, Explorer plays the admitted
training levels with these modules, and Evolver compares successful and failed trajectories to create
or revise local modules; the host validates evidence, ownership, interfaces, and version lineage before
every update, and ancestor modules are preserved. An updated parent is inherited only if it achieves
strictly more host-verified task completions than the previously inherited modules under the same
validation tasks, trial count, and budget; otherwise the previous modules are kept. A local module
overrides an inherited module bound to the same identifier. Module calls take public inputs and
return structured advice without advancing the environment, with at most two calls per step and
none forced.

C.2. Creator Shared Harness
Creator builds the simplified training worlds of each node with a coding harness, created per node
and iteration, with a workspace for the world and an evidence directory outside it. Its Base Harness at
the first iteration is H0 extended with the pre-RSI modules listed below. Within each iteration, Creator
visits the nodes top-down: the children of the root start from the Base Harness, and every other node
inherits its parent’s updated modules. Each node keeps its own guidance, one construction note, and
one public action budget per iteration. Verifier-detected failures trigger local evolution, and at the
end of the iteration the validated proposals of all nodes are consolidated with the current library in
learning order (Equation 7): validated revisions replace their registered predecessors; among new
modules, distinct capabilities are retained, partial overlaps are merged to preserve complementary
functionality, and near-duplicates keep the earlier-learned module and discard the later one. The
consolidated library becomes the Base Harness of the next iteration; it is shared by all nodes and
exposed as a single tool that runs one module per call. The host, not the model, supplies the inputs
of each call: the build digest, the submission allowance, and, for a rule module, a measurement of
the build. Fields that the host does not supply remain unknown, and missing evidence never passes.
Before submission, the host reruns the applicable audits and blocks any missing, stale, or conflicting
result; Creator returns errors in the frozen task design or verifier contract to their owner instead of
rewriting them.

the number of extra actions that a wrong commitment costs over the direct route; the screening band
is the performance range into which a world must fall; and a neutralized control is the previous version
with the changed difficulty axis reset. The final Creator module library holds 25 modules (Figure 13).

E.3. Embodied Benchmarks
The simulation of each embodied benchmark runs in an environment built from its real scene, which
closely reproduces the layout and furnishings of the site, with a model of the same robot as the real
runs: a Unitree G1 for SOON and an AlohaMini2 for CaP-X. For SOON we rebuilt the office from its
floor plan and from photographs and video of the site: the side room where the robot starts, the
doorway into the office, the rows of desks with their chairs and cabinets, and the white pedestal
fan beside the target desk. The office is a 3D scene in which the walls and furniture are collision
obstacles: each action moves the robot by the nominal distance or angle of the real robot’s motion
profile, a move that hits an obstacle counts as a collision and ends the episode as a failure, and after
every action the agent receives the head-camera image rendered from the robot’s new pose with the
height, downward pitch, and field of view of the real camera. On the real robot a person judges every
episode on site, and an episode in which the robot collides with an obstacle is a failure. The G1 starts
in the side room, walks through the doorway into the office, and must stop at the desk next to the
white fan and touch it within 30 movement actions. For CaP-X we built the table scene in the MuJoCo
physics simulator [125] with the official AlohaMini2 model, and with objects of the same size and
colour as the real ones: three paper cups, 9cm tall, 7cm across the mouth, and 5cm across the closed
bottom, stand upside down on a white table, and in most layouts a 2cm cube, red, green, or blue, lies
under one of them. The AlohaMini2 stands at the near edge of the table with its two six-joint arms;
its mobile base and lift stay still. After every action the agent sees the head camera, the wrist cameras
on both grippers, and a fixed side camera; it moves a gripper in small steps while the host solves the
arm’s inverse kinematics, and within 30 actions it must report whether a cube is present and, if it is,
its colour and the cup it is under. Figure 69 compares the real and simulated scenes directly.
