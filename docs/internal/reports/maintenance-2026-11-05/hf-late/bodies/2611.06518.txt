Abstract
Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) has advanced generative model post-training in verifiable domains such as code and mathematics, but evaluating video quality, especially emotional expression, often depends on subjective aesthetics and has no uniquely correct target.
Existing approaches typically rely on fixed rewards for visual quality, motion quality, and text alignment that poorly capture emotional intent and can invite reward hacking, while reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is difficult to scale and subject to individual aesthetic differences that complicate the calibration of facial, body motion, and chromatic cues across narrative contexts.
We introduce
EmoReward
, a new self-evolving training mechanism that distills evidence reliability from reward traces collected during an actual training run to adaptively guide multi-reward weight allocation in subsequent training by determining which emotional evidence to trust and how much to weight it.
EmoReward first plans specific grounding cues from the emotional prompt to guide evaluation, then uses heterogeneous signal primitives to score the generated video during the act stage.
Reflective Reward Gating
calibrates their contributions through instance-level gates for phase, face confidence, conflict, and subtlety, then renormalizes weights over the active set to produce the sample’s fused reward.
RewardMemory
then estimates each primitive’s reliability from rollout scores and outcomes within each emotional regime in the finished training run, then fixes bounded multipliers that calibrate fusion weights in the next training run without updating reward models.
Experiments on HunyuanVideo 1.5 show improvements across multiple RL algorithms and compare EmoReward with other agentic reward methods under SAGE-GRPO.
Ablations and transfer experiments on SD 3.5 and FLUX further support adaptive reward calibration for visual generation.

5  Conclusion
In this paper, we proposed EmoReward, a self-evolving reward calibration framework
for emotional video generation reinforcement learning.
EmoReward combines instance-level Reflective Reward Gating with RewardMemory, which transfers provider-reliability estimates from a completed training run to subsequent optimization without updating reward-model parameters. Experiments across video optimizers, complemented by ablations,
image-transfer analyses, and human ratings, support the utility of adaptive calibration
while identifying objective-dependent trade-offs. These findings suggest that
completed-run experience can inform the allocation of trust among heterogeneous
reward providers, providing a foundation for further research on reward-system evolution across training.

Appendix A
Limitations and Future Work
Beyond emotional video generation.
EmoReward studies a training-adaptive reward system that transfers evidence about
provider reliability between generator-training runs without updating reward-model
parameters. The present evaluation focuses on emotion, where different scenes call
for different combinations of facial, gestural, semantic, and motion evidence.
A broader application would require a task-appropriate provider pool and a meaningful
outcome for calibration. Action fidelity and audio–visual correspondence are natural
visual extensions; language models and tool-use agents could similarly combine
semantic judges, execution checks, and trajectory-level assessments. Whether the same
calibration rule transfers effectively remains an empirical question, especially when
providers share errors or outcomes favor only part of the task.
Iterative evolution across training runs.
A completed training run can be treated as a unit of experience for its successor.
Repeating this procedure could produce a sequence of generators and reward-allocation
tables, with each generation contributing new traces. Our experiments evaluate a
reference-to-calibrated transition, not sustained improvement across many generations.
Such an extension must address distribution shift, stale evidence, and feedback loops
that reinforce a proxy’s errors. Retaining diverse historical traces and evaluating
held-out outcomes would be important tests of whether repeated calibration remains useful.
Model-level human feedback.
The trace representation also suggests a way to carry human assessments of a completed
model into subsequent training. For example, a reviewer who finds movement too weak
could request greater emphasis on suitable motion providers in the affected contexts;
excessive motion could motivate the opposite adjustment. This would complement
sample-level preference feedback with structured assessments of a model’s final
behavior, translated into bounded changes to the next run’s reward allocation.
Mapping subjective feedback to providers and regimes, reconciling different raters,
and preventing compensating failures remain open design questions. The ordinal study
reported here evaluates outputs; it does not implement this proposed feedback mechanism.
Appendix B
Human Evaluation: Ordinal Ratings
Study and comparison groups.
The study includes 18 participants and 30 prompts, with one video per prompt from
each of three methods: the pretrained generator without RL (GT), SAGE-GRPO, and
SAGE-GRPO with EmoReward. GT is a generator baseline, not a ground-truth human video.
Participants assessed each output separately on three criteria using Bad, Normal,
or Good. The supplied aggregate results contain 540 judgments for each method–criterion
combination, consistent with the full participant–prompt product. Across all three
methods and criteria, there are 4,860 categorical judgments. These ordinal counts
are separate from the earlier pairwise preference protocol; pairwise choices are
not converted into categorical ratings.

affect, including whether expression and gesture fit its emotional tone.
Temporal coherence
concerns continuity of expression, identity, and movement
through the clip.
Visual quality
concerns perceptual detail, anatomy, and visible
artifacts. The categories distinguish poor, intermediate, and good quality on each
axis. They are rated separately rather than collapsed into a single preference or
an assumption that stronger emotional intensity is always better.

C.7  Score normalization and validity
The evaluated fusion consumes the providers’ bounded scores directly. An optional
running-statistic normalization is not an additional transformation applied to those
scores. Availability and confidence are retained separately from score magnitude:
unavailable evidence receives a neutral fallback but zero effective contribution.
Motion confidence is incorporated once through its channel gate, while an activity
mask handles unavailable motion observations. This convention avoids counting the same
confidence twice. The micro, body, and temporal channels are constant in the analyzed
reference traces; their neutral readings do not establish the absence of visible faces
or bodies in the generated videos.
