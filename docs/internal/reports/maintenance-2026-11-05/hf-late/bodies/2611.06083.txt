Abstract
Omni models answer questions about videos by jointly processing sampled frames and audio in one turn, with progress driven largely by scaling model size and omni training data.
We ask whether a model needs to hear the audio directly at all, and present SleuthVLM, an agent harness built on a VLM that never hears the audio directly: speech will be transcribed into text, and the VLM will sleuth the video on its own without any predefined workflow.
On four omni benchmarks with videos up to 90 minutes, SleuthVLM achieves the best average accuracy:
75.0%
, 2.6 points above Gemini 3.8 Flash, and it also tops the Video-MME-v2 leaderboard with
77.8%
; with an open-source 27B VLM, it beats all open-source omni models.
We draw three findings: SleuthVLM beats a model that glances; hearing is not the bottleneck, agentic ability is; omni understanding needs better agentic ability, not just more omni data for modality alignment. So we provide a simple but effective starting point for doing omni video understanding.

Introduction
Many questions about videos require understanding both visual and audio content. Omni models address this task by combining a vision-language model (VLM) with an audio encoder and training on paired omni data, enabling them to answer questions from sampled frames and the audio track in one turn.
Closed-source models such as Gemini 3.8 Flash, Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash
(
Team, 2026a
)
and many open-source models follow this recipe, and the field improves them mainly by making model size bigger and using more data
(
Tang et al., 2025
;
Zhang et al., 2024
;
Wang et al., 2025
;
Wu et al., 2025
;
Cui et al., 2026
)
.
We think this recipe gets two things wrong.
First, it assumes that the model must hear the audio directly
(
Xu et al., 2025b
;
Cui et al., 2026
;
Tang et al., 2025
)
.
In practice, most of the audio that questions depend on is speech (75.2% of the questions of LVOmniBench
(
Tao et al., 2026
)
), and speech is easily turned into text with time stamps.
Teaching a model to hear makes every new skill depend on costly joint training
(
Chowdhury et al., 2024
;
Jiang et al., 2025
)
, and it weakens the reasoning and tool use that strong VLMs already have.
Second, it assumes that one turn is enough.
A fixed sampling rate covers the whole video thinly, while the answer often sits in a few seconds
(
Bai et al., 2023
;
Liu et al., 2023
;
Wu et al., 2026
)
; in a long video those seconds are easily missed, but this kind of model cannot go back
(
Wu et al., 2024
;
Chen et al., 2025
;
Fu et al., 2025
;
Yang et al., 2025
)
.
Many works in video understanding have recognized this problem
(
Zhang et al., 2026
;
Fan et al., 2024
;
Ren et al., 2026
;
Xing et al., 2026
;
Tao et al., 2025
)
.
The Qwen team runs Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash and Gemini 3.8 Flash as agents in the Qwen Code harness and gains up to 10.3 points on long videos, and Video-Index
(
Song et al., 2026
)
finds that agent tools add about 20 points over a fixed input.
We agree: a model should sleuth a video for the evidence it needs rather than take in everything at once.
The community should therefore rethink how omni models are built: omni models such as Qwen3-Omni, MiniCPM-o and video-SALMONN have the most to gain by moving from one turn to agentic understanding.
Following this question, we propose SleuthVLM, which answers audio-visual questions with a VLM that never hears the audio directly (
Figure
2
).
The audio is transcribed once with word-level timestamps, and the VLM uses a small set of tools to search the video and transcript.
We specify only its accessible resources, a brief agentic guide, and a budget; the VLM decides where to look, how closely to inspect, and when to stop, using the transcript as an index to locate relevant moments in long videos.
SleuthVLM establishes a new state of the art (
Figure
1
), achieving an average accuracy of 75.0% across four omni benchmarks, 2.6 points above the strongest closed-source omni model, Gemini 3.8 Flash. It also leads every metric on the Video-MME-v2 leaderboard, reaching 77.8% accuracy, 6.8 points above Gemini 3.8 Flash. With Qwen3.8-27B, SleuthVLM outperforms every open-source omni model by 13.8 points.
Our experiments reveal three findings:
•
Searching outperforms passive observation.
Agentic inference improves accuracy by up to 10.3 points, and our simple harness outperforms agents equipped with specialized tools.
•
Agentic ability matters more than direct audio access.
Even with access to audio, omni models in our harness perform below SleuthVLM with Opus 5.5, which relies on transcripts; omni models also need strong agentic capabilities.
•
Small agentic models are highly competitive.
An open 27B VLM running on a single GPU matches the performance of closed-source omni models.
Taken together,
SleuthVLM provides a simple yet effective starting point for agentic omni video understanding.

Related Work
Omnimodal Large Language Models.
Omnimodal large language models aim to understand video, audio, and text within a single model
(
Xu et al., 2025b
;
Cui et al., 2026
;
Tang et al., 2025
;
Xie and Wu, 2024
)
.
They typically extend a vision-language model (VLM) with an audio encoder and train on large amounts of paired audio-visual data, enabling them to answer from sampled frames and audio in a single pass.
Open-source models following this approach include Qwen2.5-Omni
(
Xu et al., 2025a
)
, Qwen3-Omni
(
Xu et al., 2025b
)
, MiniCPM-o
(
Cui et al., 2026
)
, the video-SALMONN series
(
Tang et al., 2025
)
, LongCat-Flash-Omni
(
Team et al., 2025
)
, and Nemotron-Nano-Omni
(
Deshmukh et al., 2026
)
.
Closed-source models such as Gemini, Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash
(
Team, 2026a
)
, and Qwen3.5-Omni-Plus
(
Team, 2026b
)
lead audio-visual benchmarks.
Progress in this direction relies heavily on scaling data and models, while acquiring new capabilities requires costly multimodal training.
Such training also costs part of the reasoning and tool-use abilities of strong VLMs, and a single pass over fixed frame samples cannot revisit moments relevant to a question.
SleuthVLM instead uses a VLM that accesses audio information through timestamped text and tools.
Video Understanding Agent.
Agents offer a promising approach to understanding long and complex videos
(
Ren et al., 2026
;
Zhang et al., 2026
;
Fan et al., 2024
;
Xing et al., 2026
;
Tao et al., 2025
)
.
Early methods reason over clip captions or decompose questions into steps handled by specialized tools, while recent agents actively decide where to look.
Audio-visual agents follow a similar progression: OmniAgent
(
Tao et al., 2025
)
equips o3 with tools backed by separate models; OmniReasoner
(
Chen et al., 2026b
)
and OmniAgent-7B
(
Xing et al., 2026
)
train omni models to use tools; and the Qwen3.8-Omni report evaluates agents in the Qwen Code harness.
TFO
(
Deria et al., 2026
)
provides a frozen VLM with frames and a speech transcript but answers in a single pass.
In contrast, SleuthVLM equips a single VLM with general-purpose tools over video frames and a timestamped transcript, allowing it to decide where to look, how closely to inspect, and when to stop.
Concurrent methods also trail SleuthVLM: Omni-Decision
(
Ma et al., 2026
)
combines a GPT-5.2 planner with Gemini-3.1-Pro perception, while sandboxed coding agents
(
Chen et al., 2026a
)
process videos using scripts and transcripts; both underperform Gemini-3.1-Pro in a single pass.
SleuthVLM exceeds Omni-Decision by 11.8 points on WorldSense and the best coding agent by 15.5 points on LVOmniBench (
Section
7
).

Setup
Benchmarks.
We evaluate on four omni video question-answering benchmarks, spanning videos from 30 seconds to 90 minutes: Daily-Omni (1,197 questions on 684 clips of 30 or 60 s), WorldSense (3,172 questions on 1,662 videos, averaging 2.4 min), OmniVideoBench (1,000 questions on 628 videos, averaging 6.4 min), and LVOmniBench (1,014 questions on 275 videos of 10–90 min, averaging 34.6 min).
We also evaluate on Video-MME-v2 (3,200 questions on 800 videos, averaging 10.4 min, with questions grouped in sets of four), which reports average accuracy and a grouped nonlinear score across three difficulty levels.
All questions are multiple choice.
Compared methods.
Table

compares SleuthVLM with closed-source and open-source omni models, as well as omni and VL + ASR agents.
The agent baselines include OmniAgent, OmniReasoner, Qwen Code agents, and TFO.
We use scores reported in the corresponding papers or technical reports; all closed-source results are from the Qwen3.8-Omni technical report.
Implementation details.
The reasoning model is Qwen3.8-27B, served with vLLM on H200/A100 GPUs; SleuthVLM (Opus 5.5) uses Claude Opus 5.5 instead and changes nothing else.
All reasoning models run with high reasoning effort; the ablations in

Main results
Table
1
compares SleuthVLM with frontier omni models and agents on the four benchmarks; SleuthVLM sets a new state of the art both with a closed-source and with an open reasoning model.
Comparison with omni models.
Without direct access to audio, SleuthVLM with Opus 5.5 achieves the highest average accuracy of 75.0%, outperforming all closed-source omni models.
Its lead grows on longer-video benchmarks, reaching 4.7 points on OmniVideoBench and 4.8 points on LVOmniBench (
Figure
3
a), suggesting that agents benefit from revisiting evidence missed by fixed frame sampling.
With an open 27B model, SleuthVLM outperforms the strongest open-source omni model by 13.8 points on average and 18.8 points on LVOmniBench; it also exceeds the similarly sized Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B by 16.8 points.
Comparison with agents.
SleuthVLM (Opus 5.5) outperforms OmniAgent, which coordinates o3 with three audio and video models, by up to 10.8 points, and SleuthVLM (Qwen3.8-27B) surpasses the trained 7B agents by 15.6 to 26.3 points without any training.
The Qwen team has also moved towards agentic omni understanding with its Qwen Code harness; without hearing the audio directly, SleuthVLM (Opus 5.5) is 2.1 points above its Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash agent and on par with its Gemini 3.8 Flash agent on OmniVideoBench, and ahead of both on LVOmniBench.
TFO, which also gives a VLM a transcript but answers in a single pass, trails SleuthVLM (Qwen3.8-27B) by about 12 points: the transcript makes the audio accessible, and the agent turns it into accuracy.

Finding 1: Searching outperforms passive observation.
Fixed sampling limits performance: when used as agents, the same omni models gain up to 10.3 points (
Tables
1
and
3
).
SleuthVLM also adapts its search to each video: with medium effort, it uses an average of 5.8 turns per question on Daily-Omni and 17.0 on LVOmniBench (
Figure
3
b).
The design of the agent matters. Earlier agents delegate perception to task-specific tools backed by other models; OmniAgent, for example, uses seven such tools.
In contrast, the SleuthVLM harness specifies the resources and budget available to the VLM while leaving perception decisions to the model itself (
Section
3.3
).
With this harness, a single VLM without direct audio access outperforms both OmniAgent and the Qwen Code agent based on Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash.

Finding 2: Agentic ability matters more than direct audio access.
The advantage of SleuthVLM comes from the agentic ability of its reasoning model.
Even when omni models use the same harness and can listen to audio, SleuthVLM with Opus 5.5 remains the strongest, with its largest lead on logical reasoning (
Table
3
).
Trained on large amounts of audio-visual data to hear, omni models lose part of the reasoning and tool-use ability that a VLM keeps (
Section
5.3
).
Much of the relevant audio information is accessible through the transcript: 75.2% of LVOmniBench questions involve speech (
Table
4
), which is captured as timestamped text, while an external audio tool handles other audio information (
Section
4.3
).
A VLM equipped with audio tools therefore performs strongly on omni understanding, exceeding Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B by 16.8 points.
Omni models also benefit from agentic inference: run as agents in our harness, they gain up to 7.3 points.
Finding 3: Small agentic models are highly competitive.
SleuthVLM with Qwen3.8-27B runs on a single GPU, yet outperforms the closed-source Qwen3.5-Omni-Plus on average and performs within the range of closed-source omni models (
Figure
1
a).
The same harness also scales with the reasoning model: Opus 5.5 adds another 8.2 points.
A small model with strong agentic capabilities already matches closed-source omni models, highlighting the value of developing agentic ability alongside scaling.

5.1
Answering from the timed transcript
In
Figure

The transcript gives the decisive clue, a moment in (a) and the sound itself in (b), and the frames confirm it.

Hearing the music through a tool
In
Figure

One call to
audio_caption
on the first 30 s returns a fast opening that turns slow and gentle near 0:08, and the model checks it against the strumming in the frames.
5.3
Agentic ability: a VLM against an omni model
In
Figure
4
d, Claude Opus 5.5, a VLM, and Gemini 3.8 Flash, an omni model that may also see, answer the same question in the same harness: what was the host saying when a viewer first commented, at 05:19 of an 83-minute stream?
The key step is to bind the comment to the sentence spoken at that moment, “same category of business”.
Opus 5.5 reads the frames and the transcript of the same window and binds them in five turns.
Gemini 3.8 Flash finds the comment at 05:19 and the word “business” at 05:18.8, then rescans the whole video, listens to a window that ends before the comment, and answers Money after 27 turns and 21 times the tokens.
This is the agentic gap of Finding 2: trained on large amounts of audio-visual data to hear, the omni model loses the planning and evidence binding that a VLM keeps.
6
Conclusion
We investigated whether omni video understanding requires direct audio access and introduced SleuthVLM, an agent harness that enables a VLM to read a word-timestamped transcript and autonomously search the video.
SleuthVLM establishes a new state of the art across four omni benchmarks and on Video-MME-v2.
With Opus 5.5, it achieves an average accuracy of 75.0% across the four benchmarks, outperforming all closed-source omni model, and leads the Video-MME-v2 leaderboard with 77.8%.
With an open 27B VLM, it outperforms all open-source omni models by 13.8 points.
Our results highlight three findings:1.
Searching outperforms passive observation
, 2.
Agentic ability matters more than direct audio access
, and 3.
Small agentic models are highly competitive
.
SleuthVLM provides a simple but effective starting point for doing omni video understanding.

Implementation details
This section complements
Section
4.1
.
Reasoning model and serving.
Qwen3.8-27B runs in thinking mode with high reasoning effort (medium for the ablations and the results marked medium), sampled at temperature 1.0, top-
p
p
0.95 and top-
k
k
20, with at most 8192 generated tokens per turn (reasoning included) and up to four tool calls per turn.
It is served with vLLM on one H200 GPU per shard, with sixteen questions in flight per GPU; a finished question is replaced by the next one at once.
Earlier reasoning stays in the history, so prompts only grow and the prefix cache is reused; when a prompt exceeds 110k tokens, the oldest images are dropped from it.
Claude Opus 5.5, Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash and Gemini 3.8 Flash are called through APIs with the same harness, prompts and budget; time spent waiting on rate limits does not count towards
T
max
T_{\max}
.
Commands.
view
shows up to four images per call, and a contact sheet counts as one image;
watch
shows a segment of at most 30 s at 1 to 8 fps and at most 32 frames;
vsheet
tiles up to 64 time-stamped frames,
vframes
extracts up to 32 frames, and
vcrop
takes its box on a 0–1000 scale of a native-resolution frame.
listen_audio
returns a mono 16 kHz clip of the original audio.
Each command may run for 120 s, and at most 8000 characters of its output are kept in the conversation.
Each question gets its own sandbox in memory, with a muted copy of the video, and every command is logged.
Transcription.
Long recordings are cut at quiet points into pieces of at most 180 s, transcribed and aligned piecewise, and merged back on the video’s time axis.
When alignment fails, for example for a language the aligner does not cover, the video is transcribed in 30 s chunks and the transcript carries chunk-level times, with no word file.
Transcribing the 3249 videos of the four benchmarks, about 302 hours of audio, takes about 113 minutes on a single H200.

A VLM against an omni model (
Figure
10
).
The question comes from an 83-minute live stream and asks what the host was talking about when a viewer named Elminster commented for the first time.
Both models work in the same harness, with the same transcript and the same tools, and Gemini 3.8 Flash may in addition listen to the original audio.
The question is decided by a single check in time.
The comment first appears at 319 s (05:19).
The host says “when you put money in, you like to know how that money’s doing” over 308.1–314.0 s and then “similar to No Before, same category of business” over 314.1–324.6 s, with the word “business” at 318.8–319.3 s.
Since 319 s lies in the second sentence, the answer is Business.
The VLM binds the evidence in five turns.
At T1, SleuthVLM (Opus 5.5) reads the question and the transcript and sees that the name is not in the speech: Elminster is a viewer whose comment is shown on screen.
At T2, it searches the transcript for the words of the options and obtains the candidate sentences with their times.
At T3, one contact sheet of the first ten minutes shows where the chat appears.
At T4, it zooms in on 05:10–05:35 and, in the same turn, reads the transcript of 05:00–05:45, so that the picture and the speech of one window are in front of it together.
The comment appears at 05:19–05:20, and the model binds it to the sentence spoken at that moment: “Between 5:14 and 5:24 the host is saying ‘same category of business’ ”.
It answers Business at T5, after two images and 27k tokens (
Table
11
).
Every call serves the one open question, where the comment falls in the speech, and the search stops as soon as that question is settled.
The omni model finds the evidence and loses it.
SleuthVLM (Gemini 3.8 Flash) collects every piece of this evidence.
At T3, its search of the transcript returns both the money sentence and the business sentence.
At T8–T10, crops of single frames show the comment absent at 05:18 and present at 05:19.
At T15–T20, it crops a reference avatar and scans the frames for its colour, which returns 319 s as the first appearance.
At T21, the word times put “business” at 05:18.8–05:19.3, the very moment of the comment.
At this point the moment, the sentence and the word are all in its history.
It does not use them.
At T22–T23, it listens to 05:05–05:25 and rereads the transcript of the same lines; at T24–T25, it scans the whole video for later appearances of the commenter, which adds appearances from 05:20 to 07:16 and beyond but nothing about 05:19; and at T26, it listens to 04:55–05:15, a window that covers the money sentence and ends four seconds before the comment.
At T27, it answers Money with the reason “when Elminster’s chat message appeared on screen for the first time around 05:19, the host was talking about investing money”.
Its own reason places the comment at 05:19 and quotes a sentence that ended at 05:14.
The omni model spends 27 turns, 21 times the tokens and 7.8 times the time of the VLM, listens to the audio twice, and still answers wrongly (
Table
11
).
Where the agentic ability is lost.
The trajectory of the omni model breaks at the three points an agent depends on.
First, it does not bind evidence across modalities: it holds the visual moment and the spoken word for that moment, but never joins them into one fact.
Second, its later calls do not converge on the open question: after the first appearance is settled, it rescans the whole video and listens to a window that does not contain the moment it has already found.
Third, it does not check its answer against its own evidence: a reason that names 05:19 and quotes a sentence that ended at 05:14 contradicts itself.
The VLM does all three within a single turn.
Hearing does not close this gap; the omni model listens twice and still misses the moment it found itself.
This is the cost of training an omni model on large amounts of audio-visual data: the training that teaches the model to hear takes away the planning, evidence binding and self-checking that a VLM keeps, and these abilities, not hearing, decide the question.

Sandbox rules and trajectory audit
Rules.
The sandbox of each question holds the muted video,
question.md
with the question and its options, and the two transcript files; none of them carries the answer.
Commands run with the sandbox as working and home directory and a minimal search path.
Before a command runs, a guard checks it against fixed rules and refuses absolute paths to system directories, climbing out with
..
, network, package, process and system commands such as
curl
,
wget
,
ssh
,
git
,
pip
and
sudo
, the Python modules for network and process control, and extracting audio with
ffmpeg
.
A refused command is not run, and the model is told the reason.
Every access is logged.
The model acts only through four tools:
view
opens only image files inside the sandbox,
watch
plays only the sandbox video and
answer
ends the question, so any other file can be reached only through
bash
.
Every
bash
command and its output are written to the result file, and every refused command is kept with its reason.
Audit.
We audit every trajectory and log of SleuthVLM (Qwen3.8-27B, medium) on the four benchmarks, 6,383 questions and 71,132 tool calls, including every command that builds a path at run time.
No command opens an annotation or result file, no output contains an answer, and every attempt to reach the network was refused: there is no answer leakage.
