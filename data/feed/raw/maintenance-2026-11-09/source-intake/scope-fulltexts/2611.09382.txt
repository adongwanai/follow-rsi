3]Fudan University Exploring Weight-Space Programming: Capabilities, Generalization, and Limitations Junru Hu Cheng Tang Wei Li Hongze Zhu Chun-Mei Feng Qi Zhang Yirong Chen Ming Hu Affiliation: [ Affiliation: [ Affiliation: [ Abstract Weight-Space Programming (WSP) enables reasoning agents to directly inspect and modify neural network parameters, offering an alternative to gradient-based model adaptation. However, its capabilities, cross-task generalization, and practical limitations remain insufficiently understood. We systematically investigate WSP through 16 editing trajectories across seven benchmarks and two model scales (Qwen3.5-0.8B and Qwen3.5-9B). Our findings reveal three key insights. First, WSP can achieve substantial performance gains through highly localized interventions. On full held-out evaluations, accuracy improves by up to 29.45, 4.42, 36.72, and 17.11 percentage points on MATH-500, GPQA Diamond, IFEval, and SciBench, respectively. Notably, modifying only 1,023 parameters yields a 29.45-point improvement. Second, WSP exhibits promising but uneven cross-task generalization. A Math-edited checkpoint improves GPQA Diamond, IFEval, and SciBench by 3.03, 12.18, and 20.10 points, respectively, without further editing, while leaving SciCode performance unchanged. Third, effective editing remains challenging and inconsistent. Outcomes vary substantially across trajectories, and six of the 16 trajectories retain the original weights when candidate improvements cannot be reliably validated. Together, these results highlight WSP’s potential for highly localized model adaptation while revealing important challenges in intervention discovery, reliability, and cross-task transfer. task-specific weight-space interventions. 1 Introduction Adapting pretrained neural networks typically relies on optimizing model parameters using task-specific data, as exemplified by parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods such as LoRA [ 3 ] . Prior work has also demonstrated that model behavior can be altered through direct weight interventions, including knowledge editing [ 6 , 7 ] and task-vector arithmetic [ 4 ] . Recently, Weight-Space Programming (WSP) has introduced a different approach to model adaptation, enabling reasoning agents to inspect, interpret, and directly modify neural network parameters [ 10 ] . Rather than relying on gradient-based optimization or predefined editing procedures, WSP allows agents to diagnose model behavior, identify candidate computational mechanisms, and construct targeted weight modifications. This offers a promising direction for adapting pretrained models without conventional fine-tuning, potentially reducing the dependence on large task-specific training datasets while enabling more deliberate interventions in the weight space. Early results demonstrate the potential of this approach. In the original WSP study, GPT-6 Astra improves the MATH-500 accuracy of Qwen3.5-0.8B from 7.9% to 48.1% by modifying only approximately 0.016% of its parameters, primarily addressing reasoning loops that prevent the model from completing its responses [ 10 ] . The resulting improvements also extend to instruction following, suggesting that localized interventions can affect behavior beyond their original target. However, the same study reports limited success when attempting to improve a model without such identifiable failure modes. These observations raise a fundamental question:To what extent can reasoning-driven weight interventions serve as a general approach to model adaptation, rather than a means of repairing specific behavioral failures? Despite these promising initial results, the effectiveness of WSP across diverse tasks, model scales, and independently executed editing processes remains insufficiently characterized. To address this question, we investigate WSP along three complementary dimensions. Capabilities : How effectively can WSP improve different model abilities, and how do the structure and scale of weight interventions relate to their outcomes? Generalization : Do improvements obtained through task-specific editing transfer to other capabilities without additional modifications, or do their effects remain confined to the target task? Limitations : How reliably can agents discover, validate, and select effective interventions, and how do editing outcomes vary across trajectories and model scales? These questions are particularly important because successful local modifications do not necessarily imply broader behavioral improvements, as also observed in prior studies of model editing and generalization [ 14 ] . In this work, we conduct a systematic empirical investigation of WSP through 16 editing trajectories on Qwen3.5-0.8B and Qwen3.5-9B, covering seven benchmark families: MATH-500, MMLU-Pro, IFEval, GPQA Diamond, MedXpertQA, SciBench, and SciCode [ 2 , 13 , 15 , 9 , 16 , 12 , 11 ] . Our results reveal both the potential and the practical challenges of WSP. On full held-out evaluations, successful interventions achieve substantial improvements across multiple tasks, including a 29.45-percentage-point gain on MATH-500 from modifying only 1,023 parameter values. We further find that a Math-edited checkpoint transfers to GPQA Diamond, IFEval, and SciBench, improving accuracy by 3.03, 12.18, and 20.10 percentage points, respectively, without further editing. However, intervention structures and outcomes vary considerably across trajectories, and six of the 16 trajectories ultimately retain the original weights when candidate improvements cannot be reliably validated. Together, these findings demonstrate that WSP can enable powerful and highly localized model adaptation, while highlighting the challenges of reliably discovering effective interventions and predicting their effects across tasks.

and limitations of Weight-Space Programming.
Across diverse tasks and model scales, WSP achieves
substantial held-out improvements and exhibits
asymmetric cross-task transfer, with both beneficial
and adverse effects. Independent editing trajectories
further show that effectiveness depends not only on
the number of modified parameters, but also on
intervention structure, computational targets, and
exploration paths. However, development gains do not
always translate into reliable held-out improvements,
and repeated evaluations incur substantial computational
costs. The mechanisms governing successful editing and
generalization remain unclear, including the effects
of jointly modifying multiple parameter locations
and the influence of development data size and diversity.
Moreover, WSP has yet to be systematically evaluated
on longer-horizon reasoning and complex agentic tasks.
These findings highlight the need for more reliable
intervention selection, efficient evaluation, and
deeper mechanistic understanding. References [1] K. Cobbe, V. Kosaraju, M. Bavarian, M. Chen, H. Jun, L. Kaiser, M. Plappert, J. Tworek, J. Hilton, R. Nakano, C. Hesse, and J. Cited by: §1 , §3.1 , Table 4 . \beginappendix 5 Supplementary Experimental Protocol Each search pairs an editor model, GPT-6 Astra at maximum reasoning effort, with a target model, Qwen3.5-0.8B or Qwen3.5-9B. The editor observes the target only through forward requests, with a budget of 50 successful requests per search. It may also run up to 300 Python analyses per search to inspect weights, analyze returned observations, and compute edits. These analyses do not run inference on the target model. There are two request types, and each successful request of either type counts once against the budget. A generation request (G) samples answers from the target and returns them with task scores when the items come from a graded set. A teacher-forced request (TF) scores continuations supplied by the editor, returning conditional log-probabilities and any requested activations. TF requests yield no task accuracy. The editor chooses the questions, batch size, number of samples, token cap, and weight states for each request, and it may adapt these choices to earlier observations. One request can therefore contain many prompts and samples, as well as both the original and the edited weights. Editing is gradient-free and acts directly on parameters. The editor uses no backward passes, no optimizers, and no iterative loss fitting. Edits are computed in float32 and written to the BF16 checkpoint. Because of this, the number of values an editor targets can be larger than the number of stored values that actually change. Table 3 lists the settings shared by all searches. The Math Primary and Math Rerun 2 searches used a broad ability-improvement objective from the start. Math Rerun 1 began with a loop-focused objective that was broadened after 46 successful requests. The GPQA and MedXpertQA objectives were broadened after 27 and 26 requests, respectively. These independent trajectories vary in prompt history and in the editor’s adaptive experimental choices.
