Yingjie Li 2 2 footnotemark: 2 Qiao Cheng 2 2 footnotemark: 2 Yitao Duan 2 2 footnotemark: 2 Abstract Streaming speech recognition is increasingly consumed by machines, such as
translators and voice agents, that act on each token as it appears. A token
that is later revised costs wasted work or a wrong action. In this work, we present
Confucius4-R2T2 (short for Real Real-Time Transcription), a stable-prefix streaming ASR model
that never modifies its earlier output while maintaining high accuracy and low latency. R2T2 is obtained by post-training Qwen3-ASR-1.7B on 1,500 hours of Chinese
and English audio while freezing its pre-trained audio encoder and projector. The adaptation is
based on the concept of Longest Stable Prefix (LSP), a new notion that we
introduce in this work. We prove that LSP is monotone under the prefix order, consistent with the full-audio
output, and maximal among labels that equal a whole earlier output.
Learning from LSP labels converts a full-context model into a stable-prefix
streaming one without any architectural change. Extensive evaluations show
that R2T2 brings streaming recognition close to the base model’s
full-context quality while delivering stable text with low emission lag. Its
gains extend beyond the post-training languages, demonstrating a practical
path from full-context ASR to stable streaming. We open-source the model weights and code
at https://huggingface.co/netease-youdao/Confucius4-R2T2 and https://github.com/netease-youdao/Confucius4-R2T2 . 1 Introduction Streaming automatic speech recognition (ASR) must produce useful
transcriptions while audio is still arriving. The rapid advance of large
language models (LLMs) with strong reasoning and tool-use capabilities
increases the demand for partial transcripts to be consumed immediately by
something else: a live caption on a screen, a downstream machine translation
system, or a voice AI agent that must decide when to respond and which tool
to call. In all of these settings, an emitted token is an action , in
the sense that downstream components start working on it. A well-documented
failure mode is that tokens emitted early are later revised as more audio
arrives, a phenomenon variously called flicker ( Shangguan et al., 2020 ) , revision ( Niehues et al., 2018 ) , or hypothesis instability ( Bruguier et al., 2023 ) . When the
recognizer revises a token, the consumer must discard or redo the work built
on it, and a human reader sees the text change. A revision is therefore not
a cosmetic defect. It is wasted computation and, in an agent, a possibly
wrong side effect. This irreversibility is why output stability ,
meaning that earlier output is never modified by later output, matters as
much as accuracy and latency. Stability has been recognized as a practical concern for some time, and prior
work addresses it from different perspectives and under different terms.
Some methods rely on inference-time commitment rules. Liu et al. (2020) introduce local agreement , which commits only the
longest common prefix of two consecutive partial hypotheses. Polák et al. (2022) extend this to the beam level, call the committed
output a stable prefix , and compare several commitment strategies
(Hold- n n , LA- n n , SP- n n ). Arivazhagan et al. (2020) propose target-suffix deletion (mask- k k ), which discards the last k k tokens
of each partial hypothesis. A complementary line trains for stability
indirectly. Prefix training ( Niehues et al., 2016 ; Niehues et al., 2018 ) exposes the model to randomly truncated training pairs, and self-training ( Sen et al., 2023 ) further improves the flicker–latency trade-off.
Both lower the revision rate, but neither guarantees that earlier tokens are
never revised. In simultaneous machine translation, Hoang et al. (2026) train on stable-prefix targets and obtain stability by construction through
prefix decoding. In this work, we present Confucius4-R2T2, a streaming ASR model that treats output
stability as a first-class requirement. Before proceeding, we state
precisely what we require of such a model, because the word “stable” has
been used for different objects such as a statistic of a system’s output over time ( McGraw and Gruenstein, 2012 ) , an inference-time commit rule applied to an
unchanged model ( Liu et al., 2020 ) , or a training target ( Hoang et al., 2026 ) . Definition 1 (Stable-prefix streaming ASR model) . Let x ≤ t x_{\leq t} be the audio received up to time t t . A streaming ASR model ℳ \mathcal{M} is a recognizer together with its decoding strategy.
Let y t = ℳ ⁡ ( x ≤ t ) y_{t}=\mathcal{M}(x_{\leq t}) be its released text at time t t . ℳ \mathcal{M} is a stable-prefix streaming ASR model if, for every
audio signal x x and all t ≤ t ′ t\leq t^{\prime} , y t ⪯ y t ′ y_{t}\preceq y_{t^{\prime}} , where ⪯ \preceq denotes the prefix order. We highlight two key properties. First, it is purely behavioral . It constrains only the ordering of
outputs, not mechanisms such as causal encoders or chunked attention, nor
training recipes. Such mechanisms are neither sufficient (a causal encoder
can feed a decoder that still revises) nor necessary (a bidirectional encoder
behind a non-retracting commit rule is stable). Second, stability is orthogonal to accuracy and latency. A system that emits nothing until
the end of the utterance is stable but slow, and one that emits a fixed token
sequence is stable but inaccurate. Accuracy and latency already have
well-calibrated metrics, so we treat stability as a constraint to be
satisfied while optimizing them. These considerations motivate our definition of the longest stable prefix in Section 4.3 ,
leading to some subtle but important differences from related concepts, and enabling an
effective non-streaming-to-streaming adaptation method. Confucius4-R2T2, short for Real Real-Time Transcription, is a stable-prefix
streaming ASR model built by post-training Qwen3-ASR-1.7B on 1,500 1{,}500 hours of
additional Chinese and English audio. It retains the pre-trained audio encoder
and its bidirectional processing within the available audio window, so the
encoder is neither causalized nor masked. Confucius4-R2T2 supports configurable
chunk sizes from 80 80 ms to 2 2 s. Our main contributions are as follows: 1. We introduce a new concept of Longest Stable Prefix (LSP) and prove
a number of its properties. We show that our definition provides the label that can
be extracted from a non-stable model to train a stable one. We
prove that our label is monotone under the prefix order, consistent with the full-audio output, and
maximal among labels that equal a whole earlier output. 2. We present LSP learning, a two-stage training recipe that converts a full-context
model into a stable-prefix streaming model with LSP labels, without causalizing the
encoder or using attention masks. 3. We open-source Confucius4-R2T2 and conduct extensive evaluations on a range of benchmarks.
Our experiments show that Confucius4-R2T2 achieves state-of-the-art performance in terms of recognition
accuracy (approaching full-context recognizers) and latency, even compared against proprietary models,
while maintaining a stable-prefix guarantee. 2 Related Work Streaming ASR trades off accuracy, emission latency, and output
stability. We organize prior work by which of these it controls.
Stability, meaning that emitted text is never revised, is a property of
the whole model, including its decoding and commit rule, not of the
encoder alone. Architectures. Streaming architectures bound the acoustic context seen by the encoder,
through monotonic attention ( Chiu and Raffel, 2018 ) , or through chunked,
cascaded, or dual-mode models that serve both streaming and
full-context operation ( Yu et al., 2021 ; Zhang et al., 2021 ; Wu et al., 2021 ; Narayanan et al., 2021 ) .
Released systems include the streaming Zipformer transducer
X-ASR ( Guo, 2026 ) and the cache-aware FastConformer–RNNT model Nemotron 3.5 ( NVIDIA, 2026 ) .
Pre-trained full-context models have also been converted for streaming,
by adding a CTC branch with streaming attention masks ( Zhou et al., 2025 ) or by causalizing the encoder ( Krichli et al., 2026 ) .
These designs decide how context is processed. They do not by
themselves guarantee that emitted text is final. Output stability. Work on stability falls into three groups. Measurement: McGraw and Gruenstein (2012) call a prefix stable if all
later incremental results share it, and estimate, per word, the
probability of this event so that a client can display the longest
prefix above a threshold; Shangguan et al. (2020) analyze instability of partial hypotheses. Inference-time repair: reranking favors consistency with
earlier output ( Bruguier et al., 2023 ) , while Local Agreement
and hold- n n commit a common prefix of successive hypotheses or
withhold their last n n tokens ( Liu et al., 2020 ) . The
same idea is used with frozen Whisper models ( Macháček et al., 2023 ; Wang et al., 2024 ) , and the
Qwen3-ASR-1.7B configuration in our comparisons re-decodes accumulated
audio and revises an unconfirmed suffix ( Shi et al., 2026 ) .
These are per-step rules. Paired with a buffer that never retracts, they
yield non-retracting output, but the underlying recognizer keeps
revising and agreement is not learned. Training: in simultaneous translation, Hoang et al. (2026) train on targets anchored to the translation of the complete source,
keeping the committed length nondecreasing, and obtain flicker-free
output by decoding with the committed prefix forced.
Our target follows the same final-anchored principle. 3 3 3 We became
aware of Hoang et al. (2026) , which was released very recently, after our method was developed; the
two lines of work were pursued independently. It differs in crediting a raw output only when the whole output is a
prefix of the full-audio output, and it is used in speech recognition. Emission timing in speech language models. Speech ReaLLM defers generation with a BLANK token and
alignment-based training ( Seide et al., 2024 ) , SpeechLLM-XL

These methods time emission against the acoustics. We instead time it
against what the full-context model will output. 3 Preliminaries 3.1 Notation Let x = ( x 1 , … , x T ) x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{T}) be an utterance of T T audio frames of δ = 80 \delta=80 ms,

context is appropriate (sufficient but not excessive) to decode the right content?
This needs a target for what the model should emit
at time t t , and a practical way to label that target at scale. Targets. A forced aligner such as Qwen3-ForcedAligner ( Shi et al., 2026 ) gives word timestamps, which could serve as context
boundaries. However, a token’s own acoustic features are often insufficient to determine it, and
later audio disambiguates; requiring emission at the timestamp would force the
model to guess. Residual interference. More context is not always better. Revision of
earlier words as audio arrives is a known effect ( McGraw and Gruenstein, 2012 ; Shangguan et al., 2020 ) . In our setting, when we
feed audio progressively to a full-context model, the number of correct tokens
does not grow monotonically, because the newly appended tail disturbs earlier
tokens, a phenomenon we call residual interference . A streaming
model must be trained to be resilient against such interference. Prior work bounds context by construction, through chunk-wise incremental
decoding ( Shi et al., 2026 ) or limited look-ahead ( Wang et al., 2020 ; Moritz et al., 2020 ) , rather than
learning when the available context suffices. 4 Approach 4.1 Design Considerations Accuracy and latency conflict, and the stable-prefix property is trivial to
obtain if either is ignored. The open problem is stability together with full-context accuracy and low latency. We therefore adapt a full-context model with a frozen encoder : no
causalization or attention masks. A pre-trained model brings strong
representations, data efficiency and a within-model reference for measuring
streaming loss ( Kumar et al., 2025 ) . Its main liability is the
train–test mismatch: the encoder was trained on complete utterances, so its
representations of truncated audio differ from those of the full utterance.
XLSR-Transducer ( Kumar et al., 2025 ) , Two-Pass Whisper ( Zhou et al., 2025 ) and Chunked AED ( Zeineldeen et al., 2024 ) address the mismatch by training the encoder
with chunking or masking. Since our encoder is frozen, we address it in the
decoder, through longest-stable-prefix learning: the decoder learns to emit Wait when the audio received so far does not determine the next token.
The mismatch is thus handled by deciding when to commit, not by changing the
representations. Freezing the encoder keeps the bidirectional representations that give the
pre-trained model its accuracy, and leaves full-context decoding to depend only
on the decoder. Causalization is costly: Krichli et al. (2026) report that causalizing Whisper’s encoder by LoRA
fine-tuning is worse than leaving it intact at chunk sizes above roughly
300 ms. A primary motivation for causal encoders is computational efficiency, since a
bidirectional encoder must be re-run on the growing audio at each query. We
show that this cost can be reduced with inference optimizations such as cache reuse and Wait -terminated decoding (Section 7 ). The Wait token separates the timing decision (when to emit) from the
content decision (what to emit) in the supervision, rather than building timing
into the encoder. DSM and F-DSM ( Takagi et al., 2026 ) also separate the two
decisions in LLM-based streaming ASR. Unlike F-DSM’s factorized head, our Wait is a token in
the same vocabulary as the text tokens without any architectural change. This
remains a recognition task, and streaming behavior emerges when the model learns to emit the right content
against residual interference, making near-lossless streaming conversion a natural
consequence of the design rather than an empirical coincidence. 4.2 Architecture R2T2 is post-trained from Qwen3-ASR-1.7B, which consists of the Qwen3-1.7B LM, a projector, and an AuT encoder with 300M parameters and a hidden size of 1,024. AuT
is an attention-encoder-decoder (AED) based ASR model that downsamples Fbank features by a factor of eight, yielding a 12.5 Hz token rate. It also uses a dynamic flash attention window size ranging from 1 s to 8 s, which we utilize to implement encoder caching (Section 7 ). Other than that, there is no architectural change. In particular, no additional layers or heads are added. 4.3 Longest Stable Prefix Learning We address the challenges mentioned in Section 3.3 with Longest Stable Prefix (LSP) learning. We believe both issues are interesting directions for future exploration. 5.2 LSP Annotation Given a model ℳ \mathcal{M} , the audio sequence x = ( x 1 , … , x T ) x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{T}) is fed into ℳ \mathcal{M} progressively frame-by-frame at an interval δ = 80 ​ ms \delta=80\,\mathrm{ms} to produce LSP ⁡ ( ℳ , x , t ) \mathrm{LSP}(\mathcal{M},\,x,\,t) . During the same process, we also record the intervals where the model’s output agrees with its LSP.
These are the “stable” regions of the model. We merge consecutive stable regions into intervals and label them by their starting and ending timestamps I i = [ s i , e i ] I_{i}=[s_{i},e_{i}] . Each interval is a maximal run of consecutive audio-prefix endpoints at which the teacher hypothesis equals the LSP and that prefix remains unchanged. The start s i s_{i} is the first endpoint in the run, and the end e i e_{i} is the last; an uncertain continuation or a change of prefix ends the run. We store each interval with its corresponding stable prefix. Outside the stable intervals, the model’s outputs are unstable. four techniques: cache reuse (Section 7.1 ), Wait -terminated decoding (Section 7.2 ),
bounded context (Section 7.3 ), and pre-fill verification of the revisable suffix (Section 7.4 ).
Through LSP training (Section 6 ), the model
emits only the stable prefix supported by the received audio and defers the
uncertain tail with Wait . Its output therefore never needs to be rolled
back, and an update that predicts Wait ends right after the pre-fill.
Recomputation of old audio is removed by caching, which the 8-s attention
windows of the Qwen3-ASR-1.7B encoder make possible, and the context is bounded so
that the cost per update does not grow over long streams. None of these optimizations changes the trained model. At inference, a provisional-token budget U U can be used to delay the
commitment of a suffix of predicted text so that subsequent
updates can reconsider it. This withholding principle is
related to the hold- n n policy of Liu et al. (2020) . For U > 0 U>0 , the decoder may use the provisional suffix,

non-overlapping windows, whose size we fix to 8 s at inference. Once a window
is complete, its outputs no longer depend on later audio and can be cached.
Only the active window is re-encoded at each update; it grows from 2 to 100
positions over its 50 updates. Decoder-Side Caching. The prompt head and the completed windows form
a prefix of the decoder input that is identical across updates, so their KV
cache is reused. Everything after this prefix is pre-filled again at each
update: the active window, the prompt tail, and the retained text. This
includes the committed text: its tokens are fixed, but the audio preceding it
has changed, and so have its KV entries. 7.2 Wait -Terminated Decoding The prediction at the last pre-fill position is the first action of each
update. If it is Wait , the update ends without any serial decoding
step; otherwise, the model decodes greedily until it predicts Wait or
EOS. The number of serial steps therefore follows the amount of newly generated
text, and updates that fall in silence or where the continuation is not yet
stable cost a single pre-fill. Qwen3-ASR-1.7B , in contrast, regenerates five tokens at
every update whether or not new speech has arrived, which at 160 ms amounts
to 31.25 serial steps per second. 7.3 Bounded Context Caching removes completed audio from the recomputation but not from the
context: the cached windows and the re-pre-filled transcript still grow with
the stream. We therefore retain only the W W most recent completed windows in
addition to the active one, and evict each older window together with the text
committed before that window ends. Because text is always committed after the
audio it transcribes, eviction never removes the transcript of retained audio,
which would otherwise be transcribed a second time. The context is thus
limited to 8 ​ ( W + 1 ) 8(W{+}1) s of audio and its transcript. We use W = 1 W=1 , in line
with prior findings that LLM-based streaming ASR needs only limited left
context ( Jia et al., 2025 ) . Since eviction changes the left
context of the retained window, its KV cache is rebuilt once per eviction,
i.e., once every 8 s. 7.4 Pre-fill Verification With U > 0 U>0 , the last U U generated tokens remain revisable. Instead of
regenerating them as Qwen3-ASR-1.7B does, we verify them in the pre-fill. Since the
previous suffix r k − 1 r_{k-1} is part of the decoder input, the pre-fill already
yields the model’s greedy prediction at each of its positions under the new
audio. We keep the longest prefix of r k − 1 r_{k-1} that agrees with these
predictions, truncate the KV cache at the first disagreement, take the
replacement token from the pre-fill logits, and resume decoding. This is the
verification step of speculative
decoding ( Leviathan et al., 2023 ; Chen et al., 2023 ) , with the
previous suffix as the draft. Under greedy decoding, it produces the same
output as regeneration up to numerical precision, and a suffix that is still
correct costs no decoding step. Qwen3-ASR-causal ( Fuxa, 2026 ) applies a similar check to the whole hypothesis; we restrict it to the U U -token suffix so that committed text is never revised. The accuracy and

trade-off: among the short-chunk baselines, stable-prefix models tend to
offer lower emission lag at higher recognition error. Non-stable prefix
models, including the evaluated proprietary APIs, can use later context
to correct earlier hypotheses, often achieving better final WER/CER but
stabilizing later, as seen in the Chinese results of Commercial A and
Commercial B. Changes to displayed text cause transcript flicker and may
require downstream reprocessing ( McGraw and Gruenstein, 2012 ; Bruguier et al., 2023 ) . The two
Qwen3-ASR-1.7B variants at 2 s and U = 5 U=5 illustrate the cost of requiring
stable output: both achieve similarly strong final recognition, but
withholding provisional text produces substantially higher mean and
initial lag than displaying revisable hypotheses. R2T2 narrows this gap by combining strong recognition quality with emission
lag comparable to the short-chunk stable-prefix baseline. Its explicit
commitment boundary supports immutable incremental output, whereas
applications receiving revisable text need an additional stability or
finalization policy ( Macháček et al., 2023 ) . Retrospective lag
reflects revisions that change earlier text through later prefix fixation,
while unchanged provisional text receives credit from its first appearance.
Permitting revisions therefore does not invariably increase this metric,
and flicker frequency itself is not measured here. R2T2 uses Wait to end decoding for the current audio update; detecting the end of speech

Appendix A The Longest Stable Prefix as a Learning Target To the best of our knowledge, there are already two definitions in the literature under the name “stable prefix”.
The two, together with ours, are not the same object; there is no total order
among them (the final-anchored formulation contains the other two, while the
other two straddle each other), and they coincide only on trajectories that are
already append-only. The differences between them are exactly the differences
that matter when the
stable prefix is used as supervision rather than as a diagnostic. We state all
three, establish their exact relations
(Section A.2 ), and then identify what our formulation is
maximal for (Section A.4 ). We use the notation introduced in Section 3.1 . We first highlight the following: 1. Two different “futures”. The material available for judging a
prefix at time t t is either the trajectory { y t ′ } t ′ ≥ t \{y_{t^{\prime}}\}_{t^{\prime}\geq t} that
the same decoding run goes on to produce, or the model’s own full-context output y ¯ \bar{y} . The first is a fact about the path this run
took, the second a fact about what the same model would produce with the whole
utterance in view.
Definition 3 anchors to the first, Definitions 4 and 2 to the second. 2. All three are hindsight quantities. Each needs material that does
not exist at t t , so none of them is observable online. This is not a defect of
one formulation: it is what makes all three usable as offline labels ,
and it is why the applied literature estimates rather than measures stability
online [ McGraw and Gruenstein, 2012 ] . The difference between the three is
therefore what a label certifies, not when it can be computed. 3. Accuracy is not part of the definitions. All three are anchored, directly or
indirectly, to the model’s own full-context output rather than a ground truth transcription.
They constrain when the model may commit, not what it should say. This is deliberate. Accuracy already has calibrated instruments
— WER, CER, and their domain-specific variants — but streaming stability does not.
Keeping the target self-referential makes it a statement about
commit timing alone, so that a streaming model can be compared with its own
non-streaming self on a single scale: the definition says nothing about the gap
between that self and the truth, and WER measures exactly that gap. A.1 Three Formulations We present them as three different maximizations over the same material,
not as a ladder of increasing strength; Section A.2 shows that no
such ladder exists. Definition 3 (All-future criterion; McGraw and Gruenstein, 2012 ) . Let y 1 , … , y T y_{1},\dots,y_{T} be the incremental results actually produced as the
utterance is decoded, and let ℱ t = { y t ′ : t ′ ≥ t } \mathcal{F}_{t}=\{y_{t^{\prime}}:t^{\prime}\geq t\} be the
later results of that same run, y t y_{t} included. A prefix of y t y_{t} is stable at t t if every member of ℱ t \mathcal{F}_{t} shares it. failure mode attached to it. Anchor (the run’s trajectory vs. its endpoint). Anchoring to the
trajectory, as Definition 3 does, certifies that the tokens
survived every output the run produced. That is the strictest requirement on
settledness among the three, and it costs label length whenever an intermediate
output departs from a prefix and a later one returns to it. Anchoring to y ¯ \bar{y} certifies only that the tokens survive the last output, which is the
endpoint of that same trajectory. Since y T = y ¯ y_{T}=\bar{y} , the two tests are not
independent: they agree at T T and differ only in what they require of the
intermediate steps. Anchoring to y ¯ \bar{y} makes every label token one that
reappears in the model’s own full-context commitment. Whether that commitment is
right is a separate question, which this definition deliberately
leaves to WER rather than folding into the target. Model admissibility (the label equals an entire earlier output vs. it is
a computation over outputs). The final-anchored label is a maximum of partial
agreements ℓ j \ell_{j} . Since ℓ j ≤ n j \ell_{j}\leq n_{j} , it never exceeds the length of a
single output, but it need not equal any output: it credits the agreeing head of an
output whose tail the model itself revised, and how much it credits is decided by
comparing that output with the complete y ¯ \bar{y} , hence by evidence
carried by x > t x_{>t} . Supervising x ≤ t ↦ L t FA x_{\leq t}\mapsto L_{t}^{\mathrm{FA}} therefore
asks the model to commit content whose evidence has not arrived, and to treat a
state in which it had not settled as if it had — the failure mode we call label
leakage, the supervision-side counterpart of the right-context lag discussed in
Section 3.3 . Definition 2 is the intersection: anchored to the model’s own
full-context output because it is a prefix of y ¯ \bar{y} , and realizable because it
is one of the model’s own streaming outputs.
Theorem 1 shows that the intersection is not merely inhabited but
has a longest element, so the construction costs nothing in label length among
targets with both properties. Self-distillation without a teacher. Because the label L t L_{t} is the
output generated by the same backbone from the same audio, conversion requires no external
aligner, no stronger teacher model and no distributional gap between the label
and what the model can reach. The labeling procedure is a filter over the
model’s own outputs, which is also why it transfers to any checkpoint without
retraining the labeller. Relation to a model’s stable-prefix property. Definition 1 and the all-future criterion
(Definition 3 ) describe the same property from two sides: by
Proposition 5 , a system is a stable-prefix model exactly when
its all-future stable prefix covers its whole emission at every step. The longest
stable prefix is therefore uninformative on a stable-prefix model’s own output,
where it is simply the output, and informative only on a system that revises.
The full-context model ℳ \mathcal{M} run on truncated audio is such a system: y t = ℳ ⁡ ( x ≤ t ) y_{t}=\mathcal{M}(x_{\leq t}) ends in a tail guessed from incomplete audio,
which later outputs may change. The longest stable prefix is thus the part of a
non-stable model’s output that can be kept, and it is the label we extract from ℳ \mathcal{M} in order to train a stable-prefix model. A.5 LSP learning as the paradigm for stable-prefix streaming recognition We now connect the target to the behaviour it is meant to induce.
Recall y t = ℳ ⁡ ( x ≤ t ) y_{t}=\mathcal{M}(x_{\leq t}) , the model’s output on the clip heard so far. The ideal streaming recognizer is a prefix predictor, not a
transcriber. Theorem 1 says that at time t t the longest target
that is safe to commit and that the model has itself realized is L t L_{t} , the
longest earlier output of the model that is wholly a prefix of y ¯ \bar{y} . The
objective is therefore x ≤ t ↦ L t x_{\leq t}\mapsto L_{t} , a prediction of what the
final transcript will begin with , not x ≤ t ↦ y t x_{\leq t}\mapsto y_{t} , a transcription
of the clip. The two differ in both directions. y t y_{t} normally carries a tail
beyond L t L_{t} , a completion guessed from a clip that ends mid-phrase, which later
audio is free to revise and which an append-only system must therefore not emit;
and when the current clip output is shorter than an earlier fully agreeing one, y t y_{t} is shorter than L t L_{t} instead (Proposition 3 , second
witness). The relation between the two is a comparison, not a decomposition.
Training on y t y_{t} , or on the words of y ¯ \bar{y} whose audio has already ended
(typically longer than L t L_{t} by the right-context lag, though not guaranteed to
be, since L t L_{t} may contain a word whose audio has not yet ended), teaches the
model to emit content that the commit rule would make permanent, whatever later
audio shows. Appendix B Training Configuration The tables below summarize the training corpus, augmentation,
