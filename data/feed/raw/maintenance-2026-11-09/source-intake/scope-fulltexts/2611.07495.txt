Affiliation: John Hopcroft Center for Computer Science Affiliation: Shanghai Jiao Tong University Affiliation: Shanghai, China Email: gjxiong@sjtu.edu.cn Abstract Learned orchestration has emerged as a promising paradigm for large language
model (LLM)-based multi-agent systems, in which a central orchestrator
selects agents and controls the information they receive throughout a task.
Despite rapid empirical progress, existing methods train the orchestrator by
crediting each decision with its own advantage, implicitly assuming that the
other decisions in the episode stay fixed. This assumption fails in orchestration, where the contribution of each decision
to the final outcome depends on the decisions preceding it, so the gain of a
joint update is not the sum of its per-decision effects. To address this challenge, we introduce IAPO , an interaction-aware
policy optimization framework that explicitly accounts for interactions among
decisions. Specifically, IAPO exactly decomposes the performance difference between two
policies into the effects of individual decisions and their interactions , which we prove can account for half of the attainable gain , and then optimizes
both from sampled trajectories while certifying that every update is an
improvement . On long-input versions of GSM8K, MATH, DROP, MBPP and HumanEval, against fifteen published multi-agent and workflow-search systems on the same 7B executor, IAPO’s deployed plans reach the strongest system’s accuracy with fewer tokens , and removing the interaction term costs 7 to 17 points of accuracy on the two code benchmarks . Keywords: multi-agent orchestration, policy improvement, interaction effects, certified updates, LLM agents November 5, 2026 1  Introduction Large language model (LLM)-based agents have achieved strong results in
multi-step reasoning ( Yao et al., 2022 ) , code generation ( Shinn et al., 2023 ) , and tool use ( Schick et al., 2023 ) .
Organizing several such agents into a multi-agent system extends these
capabilities further, as agents that debate ( Du et al., 2023 ) , role-play ( Li et al., 2023 ) , or converse ( Wu et al., 2023 ) often outperform each of
their members on complex tasks. This benefit, however, depends on
how the collaboration is organized: which agent acts next, which artifacts it
may read, and when the process terminates ( Zhang, 2026 ) . What an agent reads also determines how much it influences the others, and
hence what a population of interacting agents comes to believe collectively ( Liu et al., 2026a ) . Figure 1: Overview of IAPO. (a) Orchestration decisions depend on one
another: a new earlier worker helps only if a later call may read its output,
so neither change helps alone. (b) IAPO credits each orchestrator step and
every pair of steps in one pass over each training trajectory, and fits one
bounded update per step size. (c) With the candidates fixed, IAPO computes two
lower bounds on the gain from fresh trajectories, keeps the larger one, and
updates the policy only if it is positive. Early multi-agent systems specify this organization manually through fixed role
assignments and communication pipelines ( Hong et al., 2024 ; Wu et al., 2023 ) ,
which limits their transfer across tasks and domains. Subsequent work automates
parts of the design: GPTSwarm ( Zhuge et al., 2024 ) and G-Designer ( Zhang et al., 2024 ) optimize communication topologies, MasRouter ( Yue et al., 2025 ) learns to route queries among agents, and ADAS ( Hu et al., 2025 ) , AFlow ( Zhang et al., 2025b ) , and MaAS ( Zhang et al., 2025a ) search over entire workflows. Nevertheless, these methods
largely fix the organization before execution and cannot adapt it to the
intermediate results produced during a task. More recently, orchestration has
been cast as a sequential decision problem, in which a central orchestrator
observes the evolving state of a task and is trained by reinforcement learning
(RL) to decide how to proceed at every step ( Dang et al., 2026 ; Nielsen et al., 2026 ) . The same principle has been extended
to training the coordinated agents jointly with the orchestrator ( Liu et al., 2026b ; Zhao et al., 2026 ) , producing systems that surpass the
strongest individual agents at reduced inference cost ( Dang et al., 2026 ; Nielsen et al., 2026 ) . Although this paradigm has shown clear empirical gains, its training
objectives are inherited directly from single-agent policy optimization ( Schulman et al., 2017 ; Shao et al., 2024 ) , which credits each decision
with its own advantage while treating all other decisions in the episode as
fixed. This approximation is justified by trust-region arguments that bound its error
through the per-step policy divergence ( Kakade and Langford, 2002 ; Schulman et al., 2015 ) , and multi-agent extensions
control the error in the same per-step manner ( Kuba et al., 2021 ; Zhong et al., 2024 ) . Orchestration violates the
premise of these arguments in two ways. First, the decisions within an episode
depend on one another, since the effect of each decision on the outcome
changes with the decisions made before it. A joint change to several
decisions can therefore improve performance even when every single change
degrades it, and an objective that adds up per-decision credits cannot detect
such joint improvements. Second, an orchestrator makes many decisions in one
episode. Even if each decision changes only slightly, the errors add up across
decisions, so a per-step trust region no longer keeps the
approximation accurate over the length of a whole episode. To address these challenges, we develop an interaction theory of policy
improvement for orchestration and propose IAPO (Interaction-Aware
Policy Optimization). We show that the performance difference between two
policies is a finite sum of interaction terms among decisions. Each term can be
estimated from trajectories without a critic, and the existing objective is the
first-order term. This objective has the correct gradient but misses all
interactions between organizational decisions, such as which worker to call
and which outputs it may read, so any update rule that treats these decisions
separately can lose half of the possible gain. We further bound each
interaction term by how often the decisions involved are made, which implies
that a trust region should limit the policy change weighted by visit counts. Based on
this theory, IAPO optimizes the first- and second-order terms in one pass over
each trajectory and accepts an update only if a high-confidence lower bound on
its gain is positive. We prove that every accepted update improves performance
with high probability. On controlled orchestration tasks, IAPO reaches the best
possible gain on all three tasks, while the existing objective fails on two,
and IAPO never accepts a harmful update. On long-input versions of five benchmarks, against fifteen published multi-agent and workflow-search systems on the same 7B executor, IAPO’s deployed plans reach the strongest system’s accuracy with fewer tokens in every environment and, at that system’s token budget, exceed it significantly on GSM8K, MATH and DROP under at least one cost weight; removing the interaction term costs 7 to 17 points of accuracy on code. • Exact Interaction Theory: We derive an exact, finite expansion
of the performance difference between two orchestration policies, which
identifies existing objectives as its single-decision terms and characterizes
the omitted interactions through their curvature, visit-count bounds, and
sample complexity. • Practical Algorithm: We propose IAPO, which jointly optimizes
the single-decision and pairwise terms in one pass per trajectory and accepts
an update only when its gain is certified with high confidence. Its
analysis extends to jointly trained agents. • Theoretical and Empirical Validation: We prove that ignoring
interactions can forfeit half of the attainable gain and that IAPO accepts
only improving updates with high probability, and we confirm on controlled
tasks that IAPO recovers gains missed by existing objectives without accepting
harmful updates, and on five LLM orchestration benchmarks that it matches or exceeds the strongest of fifteen baselines at lower cost. 2  Problem Formulation In this section, we formulate multi-agent orchestration as an episodic MDP and
