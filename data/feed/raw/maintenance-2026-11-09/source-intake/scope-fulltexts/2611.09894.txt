Unlike direct video generation, Video-Coder writes and executes code to build a 3D scene and renders a draft video, which guides the downstream video generator with explicit structure and motion signals. 1 Introduction Video generation has advanced rapidly with diffusion-based and autoregressive models Brooks et al. (2024) ; Google DeepMind (2025) ; Kuaishou (2024) ; Gao and others (2025) ; Team Wan and others (2025) , achieving remarkable visual fidelity and short-term temporal consistency. However, these models learn to fit the video distribution in pixel or latent space without explicitly modeling the underlying 3D world. When prompts involve precise spatial relationships, multi-object interactions, or physically governed motion, the generated videos often exhibit incorrect object counts and positions, inconsistent occlusions, implausible trajectories, and objects that deform or vanish Meng and others (2025) ; Zheng and others (2025) ; Motamed et al. (2025) ; Bansal and others (2025) ; Kang and others (2025) . Moreover, end-to-end generated videos are hard to edit or reproduce, leaving users to rewrite prompts by trial and error. Meanwhile, large language models (LLMs) have shown strong capabilities in code generation and agentic tasks Chen and others (2021) ; Yang and others (2024) ; Wang and others (2025a) . Code is a precise, executable, and composable representation that naturally describes scene geometry, object motion, and camera trajectories: a short program can unambiguously specify that “three spheres fall simultaneously from different heights”, a constraint that pixel-level models struggle with. Prior work has used LLMs to generate code for 3D scene construction or visual content creation Sun and others (2023) ; Hu and others (2024) ; Huang and others (2024) ; Lin and others (2024) ; Lian and others (2024) , but mostly in a single-turn manner or with fixed prompting pipelines, without refining code from execution feedback or optimizing for final video quality. Motivated by these observations, we propose Video-Coder, a code-driven video generation agent that formulates video generation as an agentic programming task.
As shown in Figure 1 ,
given a text prompt, the agent writes and executes code to construct a dynamic 3D scene and renders it into an draft video, which explicitly specifies structure and motion and guides a downstream video generator to produce the final video. Since the scene is determined by an executable program, generation becomes controllable, interpretable, and verifiable. Through multi-turn interaction, the agent further observes execution errors and rendered results to refine its code. To achieve this goal, we build a dedicated data construction pipeline. We collect diverse prompts from multiple video generation datasets and use Gemini 3.8 Flash Google DeepMind (2026) with the Video-Coder Harness to generate agentic trajectories, in which the model iteratively writes, executes, and revises code until the rendered Draft Video matches the prompt. The prompt and Draft Video are then fed into MiniMax-H3 MiniMax (2024) to synthesize a ground-truth reference video. After quality filtering, we obtain Video-Coder-SFT-6k and Video-Coder-RL-2k.
In addition, we introduce the Video-Coder Harness, a unified interactive environment integrating code execution, 3D scene rendering, and execution feedback for agent training and inference. Based on these resources, we train Video-Coder in two stages: SFT equips the model with fundamental scene-programming and tool-calling capabilities;
while harness-native agentic RL
further optimizes its programming and tool-calling trajectories.
To ensure the executability of the generated program and the quality of the final video, we design two reward signals: execution-based reward and video-based Reward. The execution-based reward measures the executability of the generated program, while the video-based reward evaluates the quality of the generated video. Additionally, we introduce an annealing schedule that gradually reduces the impact of the execution-based reward throughout the training process, allowing the model to rely more on the video-based reward in later stages. In this way, execution-based reward stabilizes early-stage training, while the video-based reward gradually dominates in later stages and directly optimizes the final video quality. This design provides stable and informative learning signals for RL training, thereby improving both the reliability of code execution and the visual and motion quality of the generated videos. Extensive experiments show that Video-Coder consistently improves video generation performance across different backbones, bringing around 7 points gains for MiniMax-H3 on PhyGenBench, demonstrating that explicitly constructing 3D scenes with executable code effectively enhances the physical plausibility and controllability of generated videos. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • To the best of our knowledge, Video-Coder is the first attempt to explore training a multimodal coding agent for video generation. Representative systems include Sora Brooks et al. (2024) , CogVideoX Yang and others (2025) , HunyuanVideo Kong and others (2024) , Wan Team Wan and others (2025) , Kling Kuaishou (2024) , and Seedance Gao and others (2025) , while autoregressive models such as VideoPoet Kondratyuk and others (2024) offer an alternative paradigm. Despite their visual fidelity, recent benchmarks Bansal and others (2025) ; Meng and others (2025) ; Motamed et al. (2025) ; Zheng and others (2025) show that these models frequently violate object counts, spatial relations, and physical laws, and Kang et al . Kang and others (2025) find that scaling alone is insufficient to learn such laws. To improve controllability, prior work conditions generation on explicit signals such as depth Esser and others (2023) , trajectories and camera poses Wang and others (2024) ; He and others (2025) , 3D tracking videos Gu and others (2025) , or physical simulation Liu and others (2024) ; Lv and others (2024) . However, these signals are typically provided by users or produced by hand-crafted pipelines. Video-Coder acts as a coding agent to automatically construct these signals, representing them as an executable 3D scene and rendering them into a draft video, thereby providing structural and motion signal guidance for the downstream generation model. 2.2 Code-Driven Visual Generation LLMs have shown strong capabilities in code generation and agentic tasks Chen and others (2021) ; Yang and others (2024) ; Wang and others (2025a) ; Chen et al. (2026) , motivating their use as planners or programmers for visual content creation. Early works use LLMs to produce 2D layouts that guide diffusion models Feng and others (2023) ; Lian and others (2024) ; Lin and others (2024) , which, however, cannot faithfully describe 3D geometry, occlusion, or camera motion. Others adopt executable programs as the 3D representation, generating or editing scenes via Blender Blender Online Community () code Sun and others (2023) ; Hu and others (2024) ; Huang and others (2024) . Closer to our setting, GPT4Motion Lv and others (2024) scripts physical motions in Blender to guide video diffusion, and Video-CoCo Li et al. (2026) uses code as an intermediate chain-of-thought for physically consistent video generation. Nevertheless, most of these approaches rely on prompting frozen, often proprietary, models within fixed pipelines, without optimizing the model for the quality of the final video. In contrast, Video-Coder trains a multimodal coding agent to write, execute, and revise programs over multiple turns in an executable and verifiable harness. 2.3 Agentic Reinforcement Learning Agentic reinforcement learning (RL) has recently emerged as an effective paradigm for training large language model (LLM) agents to perform multi-step reasoning and tool interaction Jin and others (2025) ; Feng and others (2025) ; Wang and others (2025b) . Building on RL with verifiable rewards Shao and others (2024) ; Guo and others (2025) ; Yu and others (2025) ; Wang et al. (2025) , recent studies explore training agents that interact with external tools and environments through long-horizon trajectories. For example, Search-R1 Jin and others (2025) trains LLMs to interleave reasoning with real-time search engine calls, masking retrieved tokens to stabilize optimization. ReTool Feng and others (2025) integrates real-time code execution into the reasoning process, enabling the model to learn when and how to invoke a code interpreter from outcome rewards. SWE-RL Wei and others (2025) scales RL to real-world software engineering with lightweight rule-based rewards that measure the similarity between generated and ground-truth patches. ARPO Dong and others (2025) introduces an entropy-based adaptive rollout mechanism tailored to multi-turn tool-use agents. AdaTooler-V Wang et al. (2026) proposes an adaptive tool-usage framework for image and video tool use, which dynamically adjusts reward scales to encourage invoking visual tools only when they provide measurable benefits. Gen-Searcher Feng et al. (2026) further extends agentic RL to image generation, training an multimodal deep search agent for image generation.
However, training coding agents for physically consistent and temporally coherent video generation using agentic reinforcement learning remains largely unexplored. 3 Method 3.1 Dataset Construction High-quality training data is essential for training a coding agent for video generation. However, such data does not naturally exist: each sample must simultaneously contain (i) a text prompt describing a dynamic scene; (ii) a complete multi-turn agentic coding trajectory; and (iii) a grounded video. To address this challenge, we design a four-stage data construction pipeline (see Figure 2 ), consisting of text prompt construction, agentic trajectory generation, grounded video synthesis, and data cleaning and filtering. Text Prompt Construction. We first collect candidate prompts from the captions of multiple public video generation training datasets Wang et al. (2023) ; Nan et al. (2024) ; Ju et al. (2024) , covering a wide range of domains, including everyday indoor and outdoor scenes, object interactions, physical phenomena ( e.g . , falling, collision, rolling, and bouncing), and camera motion. Since these captions are written for pixel-level generation, they typically emphasize appearance attributes such as texture, lighting, and style, and often contain content that cannot be constructed from geometric primitives and keyframed motion alone. We therefore use an LLM to rewrite and filter them. On the one hand, each prompt is rewritten into a scene-centric description that explicitly specifies the objects involved and their counts, spatial layout, motion process, and camera behavior. On the other hand, we discard prompts that primarily rely on fine-grained appearance, human facial expressions, or other content that cannot be effectively modeled through 3D scene programming. Agentic Trajectory Generation. Given the constructed text prompts, we generate agentic coding trajectories, in which the agent iteratively writes, executes, revises Blender code and render a Draft Video in the Video-Coder Harness. The resulting Draft Video provides precise structure and motion guidance for the subsequent ground-truth video synthesis. Meanwhile, these trajectories also serve as valuable supervision data for the subsequent supervised fine-tuning (SFT).
Specifically, we connect Gemini 3.8 Flash Google DeepMind (2026) to the Video-Coder Harness ( Section 3.2 ). For each prompt, the model plans the scene and writes Blender Python code to create objects, animate their motion, and configure the camera, then executes it in the Harness, which returns error messages on failure or rendered frames on success. Based on this feedback, the model revises its code and re-renders the scene until the Draft Video matches the prompt or the turn limit T max T_{\max} is reached. We record each episode, including reasoning, code, tool calls, execution feedback, and rendered observations, as a multi-turn trajectory. Figure 2: Overview of our data construction pipeline. It consists of four stages: text prompt construction, agentic trajectory generation, ground-truth video synthesis, and data cleaning and filtering, yielding two datasets: Video-Coder-SFT-6k and Video-Coder-RL-2k. Ground-Truth Video Synthesis. The draft video determines the geometric structure, spatial layout, and motion of the scene, but contains no textures or realistic appearance. We feed the text prompt together with the draft video of each agentic trajectory into MiniMax-H3 MiniMax (2024) , which synthesizes a video whose structure and motion follow the white-model video and whose appearance follows the prompt. The generated videos
serve as synthesis ground truth for training the coding agent. Data Cleaning and Filtering. The raw outputs of the above stages inevitably contain failed or low-quality samples. We therefore apply filtering at three levels:
(1)Execution validity: we discard trajectories that never render successfully, exceed the turn or token budget, or contain invalid tool calls;
(2)White-model video quality: we remove videos that are nearly static, empty, have the main subject out of frame, or exhibit degenerate camera viewpoints;
(3)Prompt–video consistency: we use Seed2.0 Seed (2026) to judge whether the white-model video correctly reflects the objects, counts, spatial relationships, and motion described in the prompt, and remove samples scoring below a threshold. After filtering, we split the data into two non-overlapping subsets. Video-Coder-SFT-6k contains 6k samples, each consisting of a prompt, a complete agentic trajectory, and a ground-truth reference video, and is used for supervised fine-tuning. Video-Coder-RL-2k contains 2k prompt–reference video pairs and is used for agentic reinforcement learning. 3.2 Video-Coder Harness Generating high-quality Blender programs typically requires iterative refinement, but reliance on human feedback makes this process difficult to scale.
To this end, we build the Video-Coder Harness , an environment in which model can run its code, observe the outcome, and revise accordingly.

The agent edits its python script with ordinary shell commands and verifies it with two tools: check_script , a fast static check, and run_blender , which executes the script and renders the draft video.
After each tool call, the Harness returns three types of feedback:
(1)Execution feedback. Syntax errors, missing cameras or render calls, and runtime tracebacks. If execution fails, this is the only feedback.
(2)Content checks. For a successful render, the Harness verifies the video format and inspects what the camera actually sees, reporting empty frames, static videos, or objects that leave the view, together with a brief scene summary.
(3)Visual feedback. A few evenly spaced frames of the rendered video are returned as images, allowing model to inspect its own output and adjust layout, motion timing, or camera framing. Verification and acceptance. An episode ends when the agent submits or when a limit is reached.
A submission is not trusted directly: the Harness re-runs the final script from scratch in a clean directory and accepts the episode only if this independent re-execution again passes all checks.
This guarantees that every accepted result is reproducible from the code alone, and the re-rendered video v ( T ) v^{(T)} is exported as the final white-model video. 3.3 Training Recipe In this section, we train Video-Coder to act as a multimodal coding agent that iteratively writes, executes, and revises Blender programs for video generation.
Our training recipe follows a two-stage pipeline,
consisting of SFT and harness-native agentic RL. Two-Stage Training. We initialize Video-Coder from Qwen3.5-9B Qwen Team (2026) . In the first stage, we perform supervised fine-tuning on Video-Coder-SFT-6k . This stage teaches the model to act as a multi-turn coding agent: planning a dynamic 3D scene, writing Blender Python code, invoking the tools ( check_script and run_blender ), interpreting the returned execution and visual feedback, and revising the code accordingly. In the second stage, we further optimize the model on Video-Coder-RL-2k with harness-native agentic reinforcement learning, which enables the model to learn more effective scene-programming and revision strategies.
It is worth noting that during training, the video generator remains fixed; we only optimize Qwen3.5-9B to produce executable Blender programs whose rendered white-model videos serve as structural and motion guidance for the final video generation. To ensure both the executability of generated programs and the quality of final video generated by video generator, we design two reward signals: execution-based reward and video-based reward. The execution reward R e ​ x ​ e R_{exe} measures the executability of generated programs. A reward of 1 is assigned if all tool call formats are correct and the final Blender program executes successfully and passes all environment checks; otherwise, the reward is 0. The video reward is normalized to the range [0, 1].

PhyGenBench Meng and others (2025) evaluates physical commonsense across four categories: mechanics, optics, thermal, and material dynamics. We follow its official evaluation protocol, which uses GPT-4o Hurst and others (2024) as an MLLM judge to assess how faithfully a generated video adheres to the physical principles implied by its prompt, providing a comprehensive measure of the physical knowledge instantiated by a generative model. VBench-2.0 Zheng and others (2025) aims to evaluate intrinsic faithfulness beyond surface quality; we focus on its physical dimensions (mechanics, thermotics, and material),
with each dimension probed by a dedicated prompt suite and scored to reflect adherence to real-world physical laws.
PhyGenBench reports a per-category consistency score in [0, 1] and their average. VBench-2.0 reports per-dimension plausibility as a percentage. Higher is better on every metric. As shown in Table 1 , Video-Coder-9B + Mimax-H3 achieves the best average score of 0.67 among all compared methods, outperforming the strong open-source model Wan2.2-TI2V-5B (0.54), the HunyuanVideo (0.33), and commercial systems such as Gen-3 (0.51) and Kling (0.49). It ranks first on the all four categories, namely Mechanics (0.68), Optics (0.79), Thermal (0.60) and Material (0.61).
Notably, although Video-Coder was trained with Minimax-H3 as the rollout generator during reinforcement learning (RL), it generalizes well to other generators at test time. Specifically, it improves OmniWeaving from 0.48 to 0.51 (an increase of 16.28 points). These results not only demonstrate the effectiveness of our coding agent, but also its strong transferability and robustness across video generators with vastly different native capabilities. Overall, these results indicate that our proposed Video-Coder is a versatile and powerful model capable of enhancing the physical consistency of video generation. Results on VBench-2.0. (2) + Video-Coder-SFT , which trains Video-Coder with SFT only;
(3) + Video-Coder-wo-annealing , which combines the two rewards with fixed weights, i.e . , R = α ⋅ R video + R exe R=\alpha\cdot R_{\text{video}}+R_{\text{exe}} ;
(4) + Video-Coder-wo-video reward , which uses only the execution reward during RL;
(5) + Video-Coder-wo-execution reward , which uses only the video reward during RL; and

Agentic RL further improves the score to 0.67, bringing an overall improvement of 6 points over the vanilla generator.
This shows that while SFT provides a strong initialization for the model’s scene modeling capabilities, RL remains essential for further optimizing multi-turn scene programming behaviors and generating draft videos that more faithfully adhere to the input prompts, ultimately leading to higher-quality final videos.
Moreover, removing either the video reward or the execution reward leads to clear performance degradation, with scores dropping to 0.64 and 0.63, respectively. This confirms that the two reward signals play complementary roles. Specifically, the execution reward supervises the model to achieve stable early-stage training, while the video reward provides fine-grained supervision for structure, motion, and camera behavior.
Finally, Video-Coder-wo-annealing yields a score of only 0.65. The performance drop may be due to the over-exploitation of the execution reward: it masks the fine-grained differences in the video reward, encouraging the agent to generate executable yet conservative programs. The annealing strategy encourages the model to rely more on video quality-based rewards in the later training stages , alleviating this issue.
