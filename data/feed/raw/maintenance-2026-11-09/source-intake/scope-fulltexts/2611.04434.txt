We show that exactness at all input lengths becomes a finite, checkable property of a network when each of its steps reads only a bounded view of an external working memory. In Sketchpad, Qwen3.5-0.8B with a low-rank adapter executes arithmetic procedures on a rendered sheet. Each forward pass reads a three-cell view and emits one step: a digit, a carry or borrow, and the next operation. A fixed runtime moves the view and writes no digit of a result. We prove in Lean that the model is then exact on all inputs of any length if and only if it is exact on the finite set of views the runtime presents, that no finite test suffices when steps also read earlier steps, and that the guarantee tolerates bounded numerical perturbation and extends to compositions of procedures. All 2,446 views pass on the deployment device, a laptop GPU running the model in a web browser. The certified model performs signed addition and subtraction, long multiplication and division, square roots and greatest common divisors at arbitrary length. It solved 200 of 200 pre-registered problems in the browser, and 220 of 220 sums of up to 1,000 digits and products of up to 20 digits, of which GPT-6.1 Sol, the strongest of ten other models, solved 217. A symbolic runtime built on these procedures solves, expands and factors polynomials with every number computed by the model, and a multiplicative gate on the adapter recovers the base model exactly when the sheet is not in use. 1 Introduction Language models now answer most long-arithmetic problems correctly, but this accuracy is measured on samples. Chain-of-thought prompting 1 , 2 and changes to positional encoding and data format 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 extend the lengths at which transformers add and multiply correctly. In each case, correctness on an unseen input remains an estimate, and the errors that remain are silent. In our comparison, GPT-6.1 Sol answered 217 of 220 problems correctly; its three errors, on sums of 300 and 1,000 digits, were completed answers well within the token budget, in the same form as the correct ones. Tool use 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 is exact, but places the computation outside the network. A guarantee for all inputs requires that behaviour on unboundedly many inputs be fixed by finitely many facts. This holds when each step of a computation reads only a bounded view of an external memory and no other state passes between steps. The steps then range over a finite set of views, and a network that is correct on each view is correct on every run of any length. The observation is classical: it underlies Moore’s analysis of experiments on finite automata 15 , the verification of learned recursive programs 16 and a recent certificate for universal computation by language-model decoding 17 . It also has a converse. If a step may read the steps before it, as in chain of thought, no finite test suffices. Sketchpad makes this hypothesis hold by construction for a pretrained vision-language model. A runtime lays out a problem on a rendered sheet and shows the model one view at a time: three cells of the sheet. In one forward pass, the model reads the view through its vision encoder and outputs the step’s results and the next operation. The runtime writes the outputs and moves the view, and nothing else passes between steps (Fig. 1 a). The procedures are the classical pencil-and-paper ones, and a low-rank adapter 18 trained on their 2,446 views teaches them to Qwen3.5-0.8B. We prove in Lean that exactness on the views is equivalent to exactness at all lengths, and that the restriction to views is necessary (Section 3 ). We then certify the condition on the hardware that runs the model, a laptop GPU executing it in a web browser in 16-bit precision. All 2,446 views pass with a smallest margin of 3.02 logits, so the certificate tolerates any change of the logits smaller than 1.51. With these procedures the model adds, subtracts, multiplies and divides integers of any length and computes square roots and greatest common divisors. It answered all 220 problems of a pre-registered comparison in which the strongest of ten other models answered 217 (Section 4 ). Because certified procedures compose, a symbolic runtime built on them solves, expands and factors polynomials with every number computed by the model (Section 5 ). Finally, a scalar gate on the adapter restores the base model exactly, and a dialogue adapter learns to call the sheet when a reply needs exact computation; it stays within 2 points of the base model on instruction following and MMLU but loses 5.8 points on fresh grade-school word problems (Section 6 ). The guarantee covers the model’s steps and is relative to the runtime, whose layouts, outer loops and reference procedures are conventional code; Section 3 states what they must satisfy. Per-length results: Extended Data Fig. 1 . 2 The sheet machine Sketchpad has three parts: a sheet that holds all state, a model that maps a view of the sheet to the outputs of one step, and a runtime that moves the view and writes the outputs. The design serves one requirement: the input of each step must come from a finite set that can be listed in advance. Model. Qwen3.5-0.8B 19 combines a vision encoder with a 24-layer language model in which six layers use full attention and eighteen use Gated DeltaNet 20 . The only trained component is a rank-16 LoRA adapter on 150 projection matrices, with 10.2 million parameters (1.2% of the model). We call it the arithmetic adapter. Section 6 derives from it a dialogue adapter, and preliminary adapters appear only as contrasts. Device measurements were made on the GPU of an Apple M4 laptop, which we call the M4. Views. The sheet is a grid of 32-pixel cells, each blank or a digit, drawn from a fixed glyph set. A view is three cells, rendered as a strip of 32 × \times 96 pixels that the vision encoder reads as three image tokens. Except in quotient estimation, the three cells form one column of the sheet. A step’s prompt is an operation token, the view, = and two placeholder tokens, and the model’s outputs are read at these three positions in one forward pass. The prompt contains nothing else. We call this the pinned-sheet policy; under it, the model’s output is a function f f of the view alone. Tables. For each operation, a table lists every view the operation can meet, with its correct outputs: column addition ( SUM , 242 views), the five operations of long multiplication (1,553), subtraction ( SUB , 242), estimation of a quotient digit ( EST , 400) and decrement of an estimate ( DEC , 9). With 𝒪 \mathcal{O} the nine operations and G G the eleven glyphs, the rows form the set V = ⨆ o ∈ 𝒪 V o , V o ⊆ { o } × G 3 , | V | = 242 + 1,553 + 651 = 2,446 , V=\bigsqcup_{o\in\mathcal{O}}V_{o},\qquad V_{o}\subseteq\{o\}\times G^{3},\qquad|V|=242+1{,}553+651=2{,}446, (1) whose terms count addition, multiplication and the calculator tables SUB , EST and DEC (Extended Data Table 1 ). Runtime. For each procedure, a runtime of a few hundred lines lays out the operands, extracts views, calls the model and writes its outputs. It copies digits, as when a digit is brought down in division, but writes no digit of a result. In multiplication, the model’s next-operation output determines the control flow. In division and square roots, the runtime fixes the outer loop and the model’s outputs decide its branches. Independent procedures run in lockstep and share forward passes (Methods). Training. We train the adapter directly on the tables, with cross-entropy, a hinge loss on the logit margin and a distillation penalty toward the base model on plain text 21 (Methods). Rows whose margin falls below 2 are replayed, and training stops when all rows have margins of at least 3. The tables are learned, not supplied by the base model. Without the adapter, Qwen3.5-0.8B passes 12 of the 2,446 views, all of them SUM views that copy a single digit; with eight solved views of the same table added to the prompt, it passes 158 of 2,380 (Methods). The other two, transcribing a problem into calls and combining their results, belong to the symbolic runtime, which is conventional code, and we guard them by checking each answer before it is returned. The runtime follows the textbook procedures. It expands products and collects terms, solves linear equations by rearrangement and quadratics by the quadratic formula, expands powers by the binomial theorem with coefficients from Pascal’s rule, factors by the rational root theorem and synthetic division, and solves linear systems by elimination (Fig. 4 a). Irrational roots are returned as radicals with square factors removed, for example 3 ± 5 3\pm\sqrt{5} . Rational roots are checked by substitution and irrational pairs by Vieta’s relations in integer arithmetic, both exactly. Expansions are evaluated at x = 1 x=1 , which detects any single wrong coefficient but not errors that cancel there (Methods). Under fault injection, the checks rejected 200 of 200 incorrect roots and 185 of 200 runs with one corrupted arithmetic result; in the other 15 runs the corruption did not change the answer (Fig. 4 b). Computer algebra reduces algebra to arithmetic in another way, by evaluation at points followed by interpolation 26 or Kronecker substitution 27 . Under the cost model, the textbook route is faster on ten of eleven benchmark problems and equal on the eleventh, a plain multiplication: 2.8 s against 4.5 s for a linear equation, 12.1 s against 33.1 s for ( x + 2 ) 5 (x+2)^{5} and 16.9 s against 31.5 s for factoring x 3 − 6 ​ x 2 + 11 ​ x − 6 x^{3}-6x^{2}+11x-6 . A preliminary arithmetic adapter changed perplexity on plain text by only 0.65%, yet it lowered strict instruction-following accuracy on IFEval 28 by 16.4 points, an instance of catastrophic forgetting 29 , 30 . We therefore multiply every adapter branch by a scalar graph input g g : y = x ​ W + g ​ s ​ ( x ​ A ⊤ ) ​ B ⊤ , y | g = 0 = x ​ W , y=xW+g\,s\,(xA^{\top})B^{\top},\qquad y\big|_{g=0}=xW, (6) where W W is the frozen weight, A A and B B are the adapter matrices and s s is the LoRA scale (Fig. 5 a). At g = 0 g=0 each branch contributes an exact floating-point zero, provided it is finite, so the gated model is the base model; we confirmed this bit for bit for logits, hidden states and cached states (Methods). At g = 1 g=1 , the certificate on the M4 reproduces all 2,446 margins. The gate is set by the runtime, not by the model. Switching it within a reply, so that the base model writes the prose and the adapter only the sheet’s program, leaves the decision to compute with the base model, whose recall is 0.809 (Methods). We therefore keep g = 1 g=1 throughout a conversation and trained the dialogue adapter, starting from the arithmetic adapter, to decide by itself: it emits a short program for the sheet when a reply requires exact computation; generation then pauses, and the runtime executes the program and returns its results into the reply (Methods). Training combines supervised data with self-distillation 21 and on-policy distillation 31 , 32 toward the gated-off model, which limits forgetting 33 . Reinforcement learning with GRPO 34 follows, against an execution-based reward and with a KL penalty toward the supervised checkpoint, and every stage keeps the table objective. Against criteria fixed before training (Methods), the dialogue adapter invokes the sheet on held-out phrasings with precision 0.994 and recall 0.989, against 0.971 and 0.809 for the base model prompted with four examples. It writes the correct computation for 92.8% of word problems, 96.7% of equations and 98.3% of expressions embedded in prose. On the benchmark items used during development, it stayed within 1.6 points of the base model on IFEval ( − - 1.5 ± \pm 2.0), GSM8K 35 ( − - 1.6 ± \pm 3.4) and MMLU 36 ( − - 1.4 ± \pm 1.6), each less than one standard error from the criterion of − - 2. Because it was the fifth candidate scored on those items, we rescored it on items not used before, against predictions fixed in advance. MMLU held, at − - 2.0 ± \pm 1.1 on 1,000 questions, but GSM8K did not: − - 5.8 ± \pm 1.7 on the 1,069 remaining test problems, so the retention criterion is not confirmed for multi-step word problems. On the longest sums of our comparison, where the strongest frontier models made silent errors, its answers carry this guarantee, and the same certified procedures support symbolic algebra and sit behind an exact gate in a dialogue model. The certificate covers the model’s steps. The runtime that sequences them, the transcription of a problem into calls and the translation of a word problem into a computation are conventional code or uncertified model behaviour; the dialogue adapter mistranslates about 7% of held-out word problems, and on fresh grade-school problems it reasons less well than the base model, 5.8 points below it on GSM8K. In algebra, the answer checks guard these parts without being complete: they verify the roots found, not that all roots were found, and evaluation at a point is a spot check. The guarantee holds for the execution path on which the margins were measured, and on a new GPU it requires the full check, of which the client by default repeats only the lowest-margin tenth. Inputs must be rendered in the model’s glyph set, the algebra is limited to polynomials of low degree in one variable and linear systems of up to three equations, and a step takes about 0.1 s on a laptop GPU. Theorem 2 bears on methods that estimate accuracy on samples. When a step reads the steps before it, as in chain of thought and in most formats studied for length generalisation, no finite test certifies the model, however long the tested inputs. Context disciplines such as the inductive scratchpad 41 and PENCIL 42 bound what a step reads in order to generalise. The pinned-sheet policy, like the decoding of Lewandowski et al. 17 , is the limit in which a step reads only a bounded view, and Theorem 1 shows that in this limit a finite check suffices. The price is a runtime that sequences the steps, and the gain is a guarantee that does not depend on which inputs were tested. Methods Model and adapter The language model of Qwen3.5-0.8B 19 has 24 layers with hidden size 1,024; layers 4, 8, …, 24 (numbered from 1) use full attention (8 query and 2 key-value heads), the others Gated DeltaNet 20 . The LoRA adapter (rank 16, scale 2) is applied to the query, key, value and output projections of the 6 attention layers (24 matrices), the two input projections and the output projection of the 18 Gated DeltaNet layers (54) and the three projections of each of the 24 feed-forward blocks (72): 150 matrices and 10,223,616 parameters, against 873,438,784 in the base model. For deployment the model is exported to ONNX, with a 16-bit vision encoder and language model and 4-bit token embeddings (16-bit, looked up inside the decoder graph, for the dialogue adapter); the adapter is folded into the decoder graph. Two additional graph inputs implement the gate and the steering intervention, and one additional output returns hidden states. Subtraction ends on a row that reports a final borrow, in which case the operands are swapped and the sign recorded. In division and square roots, a multiply-subtract pass of the divisor (or trial divisor) by a guess runs MUL one column ahead of SUB , two views per forward pass, with separate rows for the carry and the borrow. The runtime checks each next-operation output against the pass ( A within it, Z at its end for MUL , S for SUB ), and the borrow SUB writes out of the top column is the branch: no borrow keeps the guess, a borrow lowers it with DEC . In division the divisor is then added back with SUM , and the carry out of the top column, which cancels the borrow, decides whether to lower again; in square roots the pass is repeated from the saved remainder. A guess of 0 from EST comes with the next-operation N and skips the pass. In square roots, the first root digit is at most 4, and when it is 2 the second is at least 5; the runtime starts these at 4 and 9, the largest values the scale allows, so that the first is lowered at most twice and the second at most four times, and every other digit at the estimate of EST . Each root digit is thus fixed by the model’s multiply-subtract and DEC steps; the runtime copies a lowered digit into the trial divisor, and a carry out of the trial divisor’s last digit is added to the next column with ADD , whose running-total cell is then blank. Algebra The algebra runtime parses a problem, constructs the derivation and obtains every number through the calculator interface; independent calls are batched. Each line of the derivation records the model calls that produced its numbers. The default check substitutes rational roots into the collected equation and evaluates expansions at x = 1 x=1 ; the strict check substitutes into the original expressions and evaluates expansions at x = − 1 , 0 , 1 , 2 x=-1,0,1,2 . Gating The gate multiplies each adapter branch before it is added to the frozen projection. At g = 0 g=0 the branches are still evaluated, which adds the adapter’s 1.2% of parameters to each forward pass. The identity test compared the base, ungated and gated graphs bit for bit under ONNX Runtime 1.31.1 (the Python package) on CPU, on 100 chat prompts (first user turns of the No Robots test split 43 ; one prefill and 24 greedy steps each, 2,500 forward passes) and on 2,688 table views: the 2,446 views and the 242 addition views again in the padded prompt form. Every logit, hidden state and cached state of the gated graph equals that of the base model at g = 0 g=0 and that of the ungated adapter at g = 1 g=1 , and greedy completions of the 100 chat prompts are identical. Dialogue fine-tuning The training data comprise about 3,150 synthetic dialogues, about 30% of which should not invoke the sheet, and 547 problems from the GSM8K training split, solved by the base model with each calculation replaced by a call to the sheet. A call is a Markdown code block labelled sheet , with one calculator expression or equation per line; generation stops when the block closes, the runtime executes the block and appends one result line per expression, and generation resumes, with at most two blocks per reply. Every training step, in the supervised, distillation and reinforcement-learning stages alike, includes a batch of table rows under the objective ( 9 ), with low-margin rows replayed, and every stage was required to end with all 2,446 rows passing. The held-out set contains 780 items from unseen templates. Invocations are scored by execution. Criteria were fixed before training: precision and recall of invocation of at least 0.95; correct invocations for at least 90% of word problems and 95% of equations; IFEval, GSM8K (250 test problems) and MMLU (500 test questions; both subsets drawn with seed 0) within 2 points of the base model. GSM8K is evaluated at g = 1 g=1 with the sheet in the loop; in these evaluations, and in the reward, the blocks are executed by a reference implementation of the procedures in exact integer arithmetic rather than by the model, which by Theorem 1 returns the same results. The dialogue adapter was the fifth candidate scored on the same benchmark items. We therefore rescored it and the base model on the 1,069 GSM8K test problems and 1,000 MMLU test questions not used before, with predictions and a stopping rule fixed in advance (GSM8K − - 1.5 within [ − - 4.5, +1.5]; MMLU − - 1.0 within [ − - 3.0, +1.0]; failure below − - 2 by more than one standard error). GSM8K fell outside its band: 0.491 against 0.549 (139 problems gained, 201 lost). The adapter called the sheet on 619 problems and solved 327, where the base model solved 368 of the same; on the other 450 it solved 198 against 219. MMLU stayed inside its band, at 0.463 against 0.483. As an alternative to training the decision, we switched the gate within a reply: the base model, prompted with four examples, writes the prose at g = 0 g=0 , and an adapter writes only the block at g = 1 g=1 ; two preliminary dialogue adapters served as block writers (Fig. 5 b,c). Mechanism The analyses use the arithmetic adapter. Depth ℓ \ell counts layers from the input: h 0 h_{0} is the embedding and h ℓ h_{\ell} the output of the ℓ \ell -th of the 24 layers, so the full-attention layers end at depths 4, 8, …, 24. The logit lens ( 7 ) is computed at every depth at the first output position, for the 1,795 addition and multiplication views. The decision depth of a view is the first depth after which the final output remains the top token. Fig. 6 b projects the final-layer states, after the final normalisation and with the table’s mean subtracted, onto an orthonormal basis of the three leading Fisher discriminant directions of the output classes, with the within-class covariance shrunk 90% toward a multiple of the identity; the nearest-centre shares are measured in this projection. The class-mean intervention ( 8 ) acts at the first output position on 80 views per table drawn without replacement with a fixed seed, excluding the halting view of SUM and the MUL views with a blank multiplicand digit, and the target is drawn uniformly from the table’s other outputs; the class means are taken over the same eligible views (241 SUM views and the 1,000 MUL views with a multiplicand digit). Sketchpad ran all 220 problems in batched lockstep in PyTorch, 64,700 views in 1,001 forward passes. Related work Neural arithmetic. The Neural GPU learned binary addition and multiplication that generalise beyond the training length 44 , 45 , and specialised arithmetic units followed 46 , 47 . For transformers, positional encodings and data formats determine length generalisation 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , and fine-tuned models reach high accuracy on bounded arithmetic 48 , 49 , with division and gcd remaining difficult 50 , 51 , 52 . Neural cellular automata and differentiable transducers trained from scratch add and multiply far beyond their training lengths 53 , 54 , 55 . Mechanistic analyses find addition circuits correct on almost all inputs 56 . Exactness has been proved under idealised training 57 , for small recurrent networks that add inputs of any length after training with a description-length objective 58 , and for a purpose-built transformer parameterisation that evaluates Boolean circuits of any depth 59 . Sketchpad differs in using a pretrained vision-language model, in covering division, square roots and gcd, and in certifying the trained weights by enumeration, where these works estimate accuracy on samples or prove exactness for idealised, small or purpose-built networks. Procedures in language models. Neural programmer-interpreters learned grade-school addition as a sequence of steps on a scratch pad read through pointers 60 , the architecture whose generalisation Cai et al. later verified 16 , and a controller moving over a two-dimensional grid learned multi-digit addition that extends to thousands of digits 61 . CAEF fine-tunes Llama-3.1-8B to execute arithmetic operators step by step on a text tape, with one adapter per operator 62 . Other work embeds non-learned arithmetic modules 63 , 64 , compiles programs into transformer weights 65 , 66 , 67 , 68 , trains transformers to predict program execution traces 69 , or lets a language model emulate an interpreter 70 . Tool use. Toolformer 10 , PAL, Program of Thoughts, ToRA, ReTool and ToolRL 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 71 and recursive language models 72 delegate computation to external interpreters. Verification. Neural network verification typically bounds outputs over continuous input regions 73 ; here the input set is finite, as in computer-assisted proofs of a small transformer’s accuracy on a task of fixed input size 74 , except that a finite set of views covers inputs of every length. Our theorems specialise results on the identification of finite machines 15 , 75 , the verification of learned recursive programs 16 and certified universal computation by language models 76 , 17 . Lewandowski et al. likewise restrict each decoding step to a fixed prompt and a local pair of symbols, and check a pretrained model’s output on all 1,857 rules of a universal system by exact match at temperature zero; Sketchpad trains a small model to meet the condition and certifies margins on the deployment path, which Theorem 3 makes robust to that path’s numerical differences. Discrete neural algorithmic reasoning forces a processor through finite predefined states, so that its learned algorithms can be proved correct on any input 77 ; it trains processors from scratch, where Sketchpad certifies a pretrained model. The context discipline resembles the inductive scratchpad 41 and PENCIL 42 . Certification from examples can be hard in general and length-generalisation bounds can be uncomputable 78 , 79 ; the pinned-sheet policy avoids these obstacles by making the input set finite. Visual input and scratchpads. Pixel-based language models 80 , 81 , 82 and visual context compression 83 , 84 read rendered text. A small network learned addition and subtraction of seven-digit numbers, but not multiplication, from pictures of numbers 85 , and vision-language models still fail at multiplication from images 86 . Visual scratchpads support multimodal reasoning 87 , 88 , 89 , 90 , 91 . Visual Sketchpad 87 shares the name of our system, which refers to Sutherland’s Sketchpad 92 . Algebra. Transformers have been trained for symbolic integration 93 , polynomial simplification 94 and Gröbner bases 95 , and large models solve school algebra with chain of thought 96 , 97 . None of these is exact by construction. Continual learning. Learning without Forgetting 21 , progressive networks 98 , elastic weight consolidation 30 and adapter routing 99 address forgetting; on-policy distillation and the analysis of Shenfeld et al. relate forgetting to divergence from the base model on its own outputs 31 , 32 , 33 . Our gate is a hard switch in the spirit of progressive networks. Activated LoRA likewise applies an adapter only to tokens after its invocation, reusing the base model’s cache for earlier tokens 100 , and modality-gated adapters leave inputs of other modalities bit-for-bit unchanged, as their authors verify by exact-equality tests 101 . In Sketchpad the gate is a graph input set by the runtime. Appendix B Certificate format A certificate is a JSON file distributed with the model graph. It specifies the prompt forms under which it was measured, the numerical precision of each graph, the library version, hashes of the glyph set and of every model file, the device and the time, and, for each table, the number of rows, the number passed and every row’s margin in a fixed order, to 10 − 3 10^{-3} logits. A client accepts a certificate only if every hash matches the loaded files, and recomputes the lowest-margin tenth of each table before use.
