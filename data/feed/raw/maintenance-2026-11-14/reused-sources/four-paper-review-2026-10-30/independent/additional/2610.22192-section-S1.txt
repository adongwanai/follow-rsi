1  Introduction Terminal agents are evaluated by persistent changes to an executable environment rather than the plausibility of their responses. Benchmarks such as SWE-bench and Terminal-Bench require repository inspection, dependency repair, and recovery to culminate in a state that passes a protected verifier [ 17 , 28 ] . Long-Horizon-Terminal-Bench extends this challenge to long dependency chains, where substantial local progress need not produce a single complete solution [ 22 ] . Learning must therefore connect successful exploration to reproducible execution and connect delayed verification to the decisions that made it possible. Neither fluent plans nor an increasing count of locally passed checks alone closes this loop. A first obstacle is temporal dilation : the clock of token generation differs from the clock of environmental change. Hundreds of tokens can express one shell action, while thousands of observation tokens introduce no additional policy decisions. When GAE traverses an interleaved token chain, repeated discount and trace factors attenuate distant credit according to textual length rather than consequential interaction [ 34 , 13 ] . Masking observations from the policy loss does not remove their recursion nodes. Two executions with the same decisions and terminal outcome can thus receive different credit simply because one produces more verbose logs. Long contexts also motivate chunking and summarization, but these operations are different: splitting an unchanged chain permits exact boundary transport, whereas summarization changes the conditioning history and may remove details needed for future decisions. A second obstacle is value drift across recursive supervision . Repository-grounded trajectories, environment inversion, and task synthesis broaden executable supervision [ 45 , 25 , 31 ] . Recursive Self-Rewrite (RSR) transfers complementary-harness discoveries through runbook planning, leakage review, and fresh public execution [ 23 ] . Rewriting changes which actions the actor prefers and therefore which histories its critic encounters. Subsequent reinforcement changes these distributions again; reward recalibration additionally changes the return being predicted. Resetting the critic discards learned outcome structure, whereas transferring it without calibration can retain systematic prediction errors. These errors enter token advantages and value bootstraps, so a stronger actor does not automatically supply reliable values for its new histories, rewards, or execution horizons after a supervised policy transition. Reward alignment introduces a related difficulty. Terminal task families expose different numbers of semantic checks, and a failed trajectory can accumulate many passing checks without satisfying the complete execution contract. A single global scale can overemphasize families with large verifiers; a purely proportional progress reward can continue to favor incomplete behavior after the policy becomes capable of strict success. Our fixed-trajectory analysis makes this distinction concrete: under the earlier reward, failures receive 50.09% of total reward mass, and some failures pass every executed scenario check but miss the terminal gate. The objective is not to eliminate partial credit, which supports exploration, but to keep such credit subordinate to verified completion as training progresses. We introduce Terminal-RSI , alternating actor-only RSR with critic-inheriting Recursive Policy-Refine (RPR) . Starting from the RSR-1 actor and a Warm-up Critic Model , RPR coordinates phase-local value calibration with reward evolution and a fivefold actor-rate reduction in the second cycle. We build on T1 terminal infrastructure, PPO, GAE, and value-preparation precedents rather than claiming these primitives independently [ 46 , 35 , 49 , 50 ] . Their recursive coupling is the focus: supervised transitions preserve critic weights, and each new policy-refinement phase adapts inherited values before joint optimization. Asynchronous execution further requires distinguishing pipeline scheduling from the likelihood mismatch induced by policy updates and training–inference implementation differences [ 47 ] . Contributions. (i) A recursive actor–critic framework. We specify Base, RSR-1, RPR-1, RSR-2, and RPR-2 through verified rewriting, critic handoff, and phase-adaptive optimization. The initial critic-only epoch and four-update phase-local calibrations serve distinct roles, preserving useful return structure without assuming that values remain calibrated after each actor transition. (ii) Credit assignment and reward evolution. Turn-Boundary Skip-Observation GAE (TB-GAE) retains token-specific baselines while applying decay across turns. We characterize fixed-input continuation, truncation error, and conditional variance. Family Dense Reward (FDR) calibrates family progress; Success-Prioritized Reward (SPR) separates strict success from bounded partial credit. Independent rescoring reduces failed reward share from 50.09% to 28.72%, preserving outcome labels across reward variants and isolating their allocation of credit. (iii) Extensive evaluation cross multiple terminal benchmarks. Terminal-RSI-27B reaches 50.58% four-suite mean success, up 29.90 and 16.33 percentage points over Base and RSR-1. LHTB reaches 0.37 process reward and 5/46 strict completions; SWR100 doubles to 6.00% . Complete stage trajectories and checker-level cases expose nonmonotonic branches and verification gaps, separating retained performance from unsupported claims of component-level causality. The evaluation distinguishes measured task completion, shaped reward, and checker coverage instead of treating these signals as interchangeable measures of capability across changing policies, tasks, and verification contracts.
