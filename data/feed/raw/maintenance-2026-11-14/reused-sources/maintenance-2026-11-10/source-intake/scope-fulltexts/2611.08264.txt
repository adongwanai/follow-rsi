and novel-view rendering quality. 1 Introduction Feed-forward 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) directly predicts a 3D
Gaussian representation ( Kerbl et al., 2023 ) from input images, avoiding
the costly per-scene optimization of conventional 3DGS.
This efficiency makes it attractive for large-scale 3D reconstruction and
digital content creation.
The ultimate goal is a single model that generalizes reliably across
arbitrary scene scales, camera configurations, and target viewpoints, yet
such broad generalization remains challenging. A prevailing paradigm is to first predict per-pixel depth using a
geometry foundation model ( Wang et al., 2025 ; Lin et al., 2025 ; Team et al., 2026 ; Jiang et al., 2026 ; Yu et al., 2026 ) ,
and then predict per-pixel Gaussian attributes based on the resulting
geometry in its radial-distance-normalized coordinate space. Radial Distance Normalization (RDNorm) uses the mean radial
distance of reconstructed 3D points to the reference-camera origin as a
scene-level scale, and globally rescales the scene to canonicalize this
statistic. However, RDNorm does not directly canonicalize the spacing between
neighboring Gaussian centers, which we refer to as Gaussian
spacing . Since adequate surface coverage requires the Gaussian scale to match the
local Gaussian spacing, variations in this quantity directly induce shifts
in the corresponding learning targets. As illustrated in Fig. 2 , visually similar regions can exhibit
substantially different Gaussian spacings after RDNorm under different
scene scales and camera configurations. Consequently, similar local
observations may correspond to different Gaussian-scale targets across
scenes, introducing ambiguity in scale supervision.
A predictor biased toward smaller Gaussian scales may
under-cover regions with large Gaussian spacing and produce holes,
whereas one biased toward larger scales may cause excessive overlap in
densely sampled regions and lead to over-smoothing, as shown in
Fig. 7 .
This ambiguity in Gaussian-scale supervision poses a major bottleneck to
robust feed-forward 3DGS generalization across varying scene scales and
camera configurations. Figure 2: Normalization space. (a) Radial Distance Normalization canonicalizes a global
radial statistic but leaves Gaussian spacing varying across scenes
and camera configurations, resulting in inconsistent
supervision targets.
(b) Gaussian Spacing Normalization canonicalizes a representative statistic of Gaussian spacing, providing a more consistent spatial reference for Gaussian prediction. To address this issue, we introduce SplatAnything , a
feed-forward 3DGS framework equipped with Gaussian Spacing
Normalization (GSNorm) . The key idea of GSNorm is to derive the
scene-level normalization factor from a representative statistic of
Gaussian spacing, rather than from a global radial statistic. Under the
per-pixel Gaussian prediction paradigm, where each valid image pixel
corresponds to a Gaussian center, perspective projection relates Gaussian
spacing to scene depth and camera intrinsics. Based on this relation,
GSNorm computes Gaussian spacing over all valid observations and uses its
median as a robust scene-level normalization factor. The reconstructed
geometry is then rescaled accordingly, canonicalizing the representative
Gaussian spacing across scenes. This provides a more consistent spatial reference for Gaussian-scale prediction, alleviating ambiguity in supervision caused by variations in
scene scale and camera configuration and thereby improving generalization
across diverse scenes and viewpoints. To rigorously assess SplatAnything and prior methods, we further
introduce a cross-dataset benchmark for in-the-wild evaluation.
Unlike conventional protocols ( Wang et al., 2026a ; Xu et al., 2025b ; Xu et al., 2025a ; Ye et al., 2026 ; Ren et al., 2026 ) , which often focus on
training-related domains and interpolated target views, our benchmark
covers diverse datasets, scene scales, image resolutions, and camera
configurations, while explicitly varying input-view sparsity and
emphasizing challenging extrapolated viewpoints. Extensive experiments show that SplatAnything
achieves state-of-the-art performance, with particularly strong gains under large scene and viewpoint shifts. In summary, our contributions are: • We identify ambiguity in Gaussian-scale supervision across varying

challenging extrapolated viewpoints. This benchmark enables a
controlled evaluation of cross-scene and cross-view generalization. 2 Related Work Geometry foundation models. Recent feed-forward geometry foundation models ( Wang et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2025 ; Lin et al., 2025 ; Liu et al., 2025 ; Jiang et al., 2026 ) directly recover depth, point maps, and camera poses from RGB observations, providing strong geometric priors across diverse scenes.
These models typically employ radial distance normalization to keep reconstructed geometry within a numerically stable range. While effective for geometry prediction, such normalization does not ensure consistent Gaussian spacing across scenes, cameras, and image resolutions. This becomes critical when the normalized geometry is reused for per-pixel Gaussian prediction, since Gaussian scales are defined in the same 3D coordinate system. Feed-forward 3D Gaussian Splatting. Feed-forward 3DGS methods directly infer Gaussian representations from image observations, often by first estimating per-pixel depth and then predicting Gaussian attributes. pixelSplat ( Charatan et al., 2024 ) reconstructs 3D Gaussians from image pairs by learning a 3D probability distribution over Gaussian centers. DepthSplat ( Xu et al., 2025b ) combines monocular depth priors with multi-view matching, while ReSplat ( Xu et al., 2025a ) further refines predicted Gaussians through rendering-based optimization. However, limited geometric accuracy can become a bottleneck under large viewpoint extrapolation.
More recent methods ( Jiang et al., 2025 ; Lin et al., 2025 ; Team et al., 2026 ; Anonymous, 2027 ) leverage strong geometry foundation models
to initialize Gaussian centers and predict the remaining attributes.
Although this substantially improves reconstruction quality, these
methods still inherit the radial-distance-normalized space of their
geometry backbones, in which Gaussian spacing can vary substantially
across scene scales and camera configurations, leading to ambiguity in
Gaussian-scale supervision. Such supervision ambiguity makes it difficult
to learn a single Gaussian predictor that generalizes reliably across
diverse input settings.
Other approaches ( An et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2026 ) do not rely on
explicit geometric priors. While they can achieve strong rendering
quality at interpolated viewpoints, the learned Gaussian geometry can
become less reliable under large viewpoint extrapolation. 3 SplatAnything 3.1 Formulation Given N N input images ℐ = { I i } i = 1 N \mathcal{I}=\{I_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N} ,

To provide a more comprehensive evaluation, we design a new benchmark with the following properties: Cross-domain and cross-scale coverage. These datasets span a wide range of physical scene scales, providing a substantially broader test of cross-domain and cross-scale generalization. Controlled extrapolation and observation sparsity. We sample context views from a compact trajectory span and select target
views using an occlusion-aware symmetric co-visibility criterion.
The input–target overlap is controlled within 0.2–0.6 , with
lower overlap corresponding to more challenging extrapolation.
We further evaluate 1/4/8/16/32/64 input views under a fixed
context span to isolate the effect of observation density.

We design multiple strategies to construct the training pairs for SplatAnything . Source-View Sampling Strategy. We construct source views to cover diverse camera-motion patterns while maintaining sufficient geometric overlap for reliable reconstruction by the geometry foundation model. We consider four representative trajectory
patterns: forward-traversal , where the camera moves primarily along the viewing direction; lateral-sweep , where the camera translates mainly sideways while maintaining a similar viewing direction; outward-looking , where the camera remains approximately stationary and rotates to observe different directions; and inward-orbit , where the camera moves around a local region while maintaining an inward-facing view toward that region. For trajectories with substantial translation, we further vary the sampling stride to produce either densely overlapping or wide-baseline source views, covering different levels of parallax and input sparsity. During training, the trajectory pattern and sampling stride are randomly selected from a dataset-specific mixture, with distance and stride budgets adapted to each dataset. Validation uses a fixed deterministic sampling configuration for reproducibility. Examples of the different source-view sampling patterns are shown in Fig. 8 . Sampling strategy for target views. Our target-view sampling aims to provide supervision across diverse viewpoint changes, ranging from interpolation to challenging extrapolation, in order to improve the viewpoint generalization ability of our model. For interpolation views , we select a held-out view between the input views; for extrapolation views , we randomly sample diverse extrapolation patterns, including advancing along the camera trajectory to inspect local scene details at a closer range, as well as moving away from the observed trajectory to view the scene from novel positions and orientations. the main paper. Our benchmark spans seven datasets and evaluates different
input-view counts of 4 4 , 8 8 , 16 16 , 32 32 , and 64 64 . The protocol is
designed to independently control input-view sparsity and target-view
difficulty, while ensuring identical evaluation conditions across methods. Source-view construction. Instead of distributing the source views uniformly over the entire
capture, we sample them from a compact contiguous arc of the camera
trajectory. This design leaves a meaningful portion of the capture
outside the observed region, making it possible to evaluate genuinely
extrapolated novel views rather than views that are always bracketed by
the inputs.
To make different input-view counts directly comparable, we adopt a fixed-span strategy. For each scene, we first determine the
trajectory span at the sparsest setting of 4 4 source views. We select
the widest arc whose adjacent source-view pairs maintain sufficient
geometric overlap, measured by symmetric co-visibility; specifically,
the 20 20 -th percentile of adjacent-pair co-visibility is required to be
at least 0.35 0.35 . The resulting span and offset are then reused for 8 8 , 16 16 , 32 32 , and 64 64 views, with additional views inserted within
the same trajectory range. Therefore, all view-count settings share the
same spatial coverage, and only the observation density changes. This
allows the effect of input sparsity to be evaluated without conflating
it with changes in scene coverage. Target-view selection. Target views are sampled from frames outside the source-view arc and are
selected according to an occlusion-aware symmetric co-visibility measure.
For each candidate target view, valid pixels are back-projected using
ground-truth depth and projected into the source views. Pixels that are
geometrically consistent with at least one source view are treated as
observed, while occluded and out-of-frustum pixels reduce the overlap
score; pixels for which visibility cannot be determined due to invalid
depth are excluded from the statistic. We compute co-visibility in both projection directions and define C sym = min ⁡ ( C fwd , C bwd ) . C_{\mathrm{sym}}=\min\left(C_{\mathrm{fwd}},C_{\mathrm{bwd}}\right). (15) The symmetric formulation is important for asymmetric camera motions,
such as moving toward the scene, where a one-sided overlap measure can
remain high even under a substantial viewpoint change. We evaluate up
to 40 40 candidate target views per scene and retain those whose symmetric
co-visibility lies in 0.20 ≤ C sym < 0.60 . 0.20\leq C_{\mathrm{sym}}<0.60. (16) Up to four target views are selected from this interval, prioritizing
lower co-visibility and, when necessary, larger parallax. In this way,
target-view difficulty is explicitly controlled by geometric overlap
rather than by temporal or camera-index distance alone, providing a
consistent extrapolation regime across datasets and methods. Evaluation without input poses. For pose-free methods, we estimate a scene-level similarity transformation between the predicted source-camera poses and their ground-truth counterparts, and apply this transformation to the predicted Gaussian representation. The aligned Gaussians are then rendered from the ground-truth target-camera poses, and the resulting images are evaluated against the corresponding target views. Ground-truth source poses are used only for this post-hoc
alignment and are not provided to the model during inference. This
allows pose-free and pose-conditioned methods to be evaluated on
identical target viewpoints in the same ground-truth coordinate system. A.4 Implementation Details GS Head Architecture. The architecture of the GS Head follows WorldMirror 2.0 ( Team et al., 2026 ) .
For Gaussian attribute prediction, we parameterize the Gaussian scale in
log space and apply an exponential activation, followed by clamping to a
maximum value of 0.3 0.3 : 𝐬 = min ⁡ ( exp ⁡ ( 𝐬 ^ ) , 0.3 ) . \mathbf{s}=\min\left(\exp(\hat{\mathbf{s}}),\,0.3\right). (17) Gaussian rotations are represented as quaternions and normalized to unit
length, 𝐪 = 𝐪 ^ ‖ 𝐪 ^ ‖ 2 + ϵ . \mathbf{q}=\frac{\hat{\mathbf{q}}}{\|\hat{\mathbf{q}}\|_{2}+\epsilon}. (18) Opacity is activated using the sigmoid function, constraining them to [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] . The spherical harmonic coefficients are used directly without an additional nonlinear activation. Coordinate alignment. During training, the predicted Gaussians are rendered using the
ground-truth target camera poses for supervision. However, the camera
trajectory and depth predicted by the geometry model generally have an
arbitrary global scale relative to the ground-truth trajectory. Directly
combining the predicted depth with the ground-truth poses would therefore
lead to inconsistent geometry and camera scales, resulting in misaligned
rendering supervision.
To resolve this scale ambiguity, we estimate a scene-level alignment factor
from the predicted and ground-truth source-camera trajectories. Specifically,
we compute the ratio between corresponding pairwise camera baselines and use
their median as a robust global scale factor. The predicted depth maps are
then rescaled by this factor and back-projected using the ground-truth
source-camera poses to construct Gaussian centers in the ground-truth
coordinate system. The resulting Gaussians can therefore be rendered
directly using the ground-truth target-camera poses for supervision. This
alignment is applied to both the pose-free and pose-conditioned variants,
since the predicted camera trajectory can exhibit a global scale discrepancy
with respect to the ground truth in either setting. Voxel Merge Strategy. GSNorm canonicalizes the representative single-view point spacing across scenes and camera configurations, but it does not by itself eliminate the variation in primitive density caused by multi-view accumulation. Since each valid pixel predicts a Gaussian primitive, increasing the number of input views over the same spatial region can introduce multiple Gaussians at nearby 3D locations, making the resulting primitive density dependent on the number of overlapping observations. To reduce such redundancy, we apply a spatial voxel merging strategy following AnySplat ( Jiang et al., 2025 ) . Specifically, we predict a per-pixel weight map w p = { w i p } i = 1 N w^{p}=\{w_{i}^{p}\}_{i=1}^{N} and merge redundant per-view Gaussians within local voxels. This produces the final unified 3D Gaussian scene representation 𝒢 \mathcal{G} .
GSNorm and voxel merging therefore address two complementary sources of scale variation: GSNorm provides a consistent characteristic sampling scale for Gaussian prediction across scenes, whereas voxel merging regularizes the accumulation of primitives caused by overlapping multi-view observations. A.8 Additional Ablation Studies Normalization Space for Gaussian Prediction. The effect of Gaussian Spacing Normalization is evaluated in the main
paper. Here, we provide a more comprehensive comparison of different
normalization spaces, including Radial Distance Normalization
(RDNorm) , Camera Distance Normalization (CDNorm) , Depth Normalization (DNorm) , and our proposed Gaussian Spacing Normalization (GSNorm) .
RDNorm is described in the main paper.
CDNorm uses the median distance from all camera centers to a reference
camera as the scene-level normalization factor, whereas DNorm uses the
median depth over all frames.
Quantitative results are reported in
Table 13 . CDNorm is highly sensitive to camera trajectory: a small camera baseline
yields a small normalization factor and excessively large Gaussian
spacing in the normalized space, whereas larger camera displacement
induces stronger rescaling.
DNorm is less sensitive to camera motion, but normalizes only by scene
depth and therefore ignores the pixel-space focal length and image
sampling that directly affect Gaussian spacing.
Consequently, changes in field of view or image resolution can still
induce substantial variation in Gaussian-scale targets. To further illustrate the failure modes of CDNorm and DNorm, we examine
a challenging scene with a small camera baseline and a wide field of
view ( ∼ 100 ∘ \sim 100^{\circ} ).
As shown in Fig. 13 , both
methods produce excessively large Gaussian spacing, but for different
reasons: CDNorm is dominated by the small camera displacement, whereas
DNorm fails to account for the spacing increase induced by the wide
field of view.
This shifts the Gaussian-spacing distribution away from that seen during
training, causing the predicted Gaussian scales to be too small for
adequate surface coverage and resulting in severe holes.
In contrast, GSNorm canonicalizes a robust scene-level statistic of
Gaussian spacing, providing a more consistent spatial reference across
varying camera trajectories and intrinsics. Figure 11: Qualitative comparisons across diverse scenes and viewpoints with multi-view input. We further ablate four design choices: fine-tuning the pretrained geometry backbone, fixing the Gaussian centers,
scaling the training data, and adopting diverse view-sampling strategies.
Results are reported in Table 13 .
All variants follow the same training settings. The upstream
WorldMirror 2.0 geometry estimator remains frozen in all experiments. For w/o Fine-tuning Gaussian Backbone , the Gaussian Backbone is
also frozen at its WorldMirror 2.0 initialization, and only GSHead is
optimized. For w/o Fixed Gaussian Centers , GSHead additionally
predicts a per-pixel 3D center offset, instead of
fixing the centers to the unprojected geometry.
For w/o Data Scaling , we train on Hypersim, while keeping the number of training iterations unchanged.
For w/o View Sampling Strategy , we randomly select n n views and then partition them into source views and target views, while retaining the same numbers
of source and target views.
We find that fixing Gaussian centers produces the largest and most consistent gain across different input-view counts. When the centers are optimized only through rendering losses, the model can reduce image-space errors by
moving primitives away from the geometry prior, resulting in geometric
drift and poorer generalization to extrapolated viewpoints. Fine-tuning
the Gaussian Backbone adapts the pretrained geometric features to
appearance and Gaussian-attribute prediction, whereas scaling the
training data improves cross-domain robustness. The mixed view-sampling
strategy exposes the model to diverse camera motions and viewpoint
changes, leading to more reliable reconstruction under sparse inputs and
