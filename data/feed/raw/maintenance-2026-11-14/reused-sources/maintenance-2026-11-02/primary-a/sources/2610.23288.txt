CoEvolve: Self-Evolving Code Policies from Skills to Strategies for Humanoid Robots
Recent advances in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and World Action Models (WAMs) have expanded the manipulation and whole body capabilities of individual robots. Yet a single robot remains limited by its embodiment, and many real world tasks require capabilities that can only emerge through cooperation. Existing multi robot frameworks largely emphasize task decomposition, allocation, and replanning, while providing limited support for adapting executable coordination when physical execution deviates from the intended behavior. We present CoEvolve , a framework that represents team coordination as executable code over fixed robot motor skills. CoEvolve introduces Verifier Guided Coordination Evolution, where a multimodal foundation model instantiates coordination strategies through motion primitives, semantic objectives, temporary robot roles, and bounded execution parameters. Physical verification evaluates each executed transition, rejected behaviors trigger structured coordination revision from an accepted checkpoint, and verified experience is retained to guide subsequent decisions. We evaluate CoEvolve with two simulated Unitree G1 humanoids transporting a freely supported rigid payload through constrained corridors. Across 15 base task runs with three multimodal backbone families, CoEvolve completes 8 tasks, with every backbone achieving end to end success. Additional evaluations yield verified placement on two additional corridor layouts and after both same-backbone and cross-backbone experience initialization, while exposing persistent limitations in motion precision and contact stability. These results support executable coordination as a practical interface for adapting long horizon multi robot collaboration under shared physical constraints. Our code will be available at https://github.com/Innovationlzc/CoEvolve .
1 Introduction
Recent advances in robot foundation models have equipped individual robots with increasingly general visuomotor capabilities, enabling them to follow language instructions and perform diverse manipulation tasks ( Brohan et al., 2022 ; Brohan et al., 2023 ; Kim et al., 2024 ; Black et al., 2024 ) . Yet some physical tasks require capabilities that cannot be provided by one robot alone. Transporting an extended object through a constrained environment, for example, requires coordinated support, relative motion, corner negotiation, and placement ( De Vincenti and Coros, 2023 ; Pandit et al., 2025 ) . Collaboration is therefore not simply the aggregation of individual skills. Robots that can independently walk, turn, and move laterally must still determine how to combine these capabilities while carrying a shared object. We view this formation of team behavior as a form of co-becoming : effective cooperation is realized through the changing relationship between individual capabilities and the shared physical state.
This motivates our central question: given fixed individual motor skills, how can an agent instantiate and revise executable team coordination as a shared physical task unfolds? Multi-robot planning methods address task decomposition, allocation, and collaboration through structured and language-model planning ( Cai et al., 2025 ; Mandi et al., 2024 ; Kannan et al., 2024 ; Liu et al., 2025a ; Yuan et al., 2026 ) , while learned coordination methods optimize joint or decentralized policies through training ( Zhang et al., 2026a ; Doshi et al., 2026 ) . In parallel, agents improve textual skills, memory systems, and executable implementations through feedback and experience sharing ( Yang et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2025 ; Weng et al., 2026 ) . Code as Policies and subsequent robot programming systems also generate and revise executable behaviors through interaction ( Liang et al., 2023 ; Fu et al., 2026 ; Lu et al., 2026 ; Xiao et al., 2026 ) . We study a complementary challenge: revising coordination when robots are physically coupled through a shared payload. In this setting, an individual action can alter both a teammate’s support conditions and the feasibility of subsequent joint motion. As a result, a valid task plan does not guarantee that the current coordination pattern remains executable. The key challenge is therefore to instantiate joint behavior from fixed motor skills and revise the relevant coordination relation when physical execution deviates, for example when unequal displacement disrupts formation or a constrained turn requires temporary support and mover roles.
We introduce CoEvolve , a framework for adapting executable team coordination during shared physical tasks. Figure 1 summarizes the motivation and overall interaction loop. CoEvolve separates fixed individual motor capabilities from the coordination strategy that composes them for the current task phase. Rather than selecting from a library of complete strategies, the agent instantiates coordination through motion primitives, semantic objectives, temporary roles, coordination modes, and bounded execution parameters. The evaluated setting provides simulator measurements and route references, while the model determines how these capabilities should be combined within the resulting physical constraints.
Our Verifier Guided Coordination Evolution mechanism uses physical execution evidence to determine which aspects of coordination should change. A rejected transition triggers a revision to the maneuver, the relationship between robot roles, or the execution parameters. Revised policies are evaluated after restoration of the latest accepted simulator checkpoint. Verified outcomes also contribute complementary forms of experience: abstract coordination records guide subsequent decisions, while accepted executable examples inform code generation. Foundation model and motor policy weights remain fixed. The adaptation concerns the task plan, instantiated coordination strategies, and stored experience rather than the acquisition of new motor skills.
We evaluate CoEvolve with two simulated Unitree G1 humanoids carrying a freely supported rigid payload through constrained corridors. The base evaluation contains eight complete tasks across fifteen runs and three foundation model families. We further compare against four adapted external baselines and two component variants, evaluate additional corridor layouts, and study reuse of verified cooperative experience across foundation model backbones. These experiments distinguish demonstrated execution capability from causal component effects: some comparison, scene, and transfer records differ in runtime configuration, interaction budget, or continuation conditions. 2 Related Work
Self-Evolving Agents.
Self-evolving agents adapt multiple components of an agent system. Building on feedback accumulation and skill reuse in Reflexion and Voyager ( Shinn et al., 2023 ; Wang et al., 2023 ) , SkillOpt optimizes textual skills for frozen agents, while MemEvolve evolves the memory architecture itself ( Yang et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2025 ) . Continual Harness adapts prompts, sub-agents, skills, and memory within an ongoing episode, and Hyperagents additionally makes the improvement procedure editable ( Karten et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026c ) . Related systems automate the design or modification of the agent itself, including Automated Design of Agentic Systems and the Darwin Gödel Machine ( Hu et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2026b ) . Group-Evolving Agents (GEA) enables experience sharing among evolving coding agents, whereas CORAL supports autonomous multi-agent search through shared persistent memory ( Weng et al., 2026 ; Qu et al., 2026 ) . These approaches concern agent implementations, knowledge, or search processes. CoEvolve instead studies online revision of executable inter-robot coordination during a shared physical task, with motor skills and runtime contracts fixed.
Robot Policy Refinement from Execution Feedback.
REMAC revises multi-robot task plans through reflection, while HyCodePolicy and Act-Observe-Rewrite revise executable robot programs from execution feedback ( Yuan et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2025b ; Kumar, 2026 ) . ASPIRE combines multimodal failure diagnosis, program repair, and reusable skill discovery, including bimanual manipulation; ENPIRE automates policy improvement through real-robot experimentation and reset ( Lu et al., 2026 ; Xiao et al., 2026 ) . These works already connect program revision with physical experience. CoEvolve focuses on physically coupled humanoid transport, where failures can require changes to the selected maneuver or temporary support roles. It combines structured coordination revision with physical transition checks, checkpoint recovery, and sequential retrieval of strategy records and accepted code exemplars.
3 Method
Figure 2 illustrates the overall architecture of CoEvolve , from semantic coordination decisions to executable policies, physical verification, and experience-driven revision.
3.1 Overview and Coordination Representation
Task formulation.
CoEvolve separates fixed individual motor skills from mutable team coordination. For N N robots manipulating a shared payload, a persistent semantic plan P t = [ ( q k , γ k , σ k , t ) ] k = 1 K P_{t}=[(q_{k},\gamma_{k},\sigma_{k,t})]_{k=1}^{K} records task phases, completion criteria, and status. The host provides route-segment geometry and progress constraints, while the model determines how the robots coordinate within those constraints. State, observation, and route interfaces are detailed in Appendix A.1 .
Executable coordination.
We use executable code as the coordination interface, following the Code as Policies paradigm ( Liang et al., 2023 ) , but generate paired robot policies under a shared coordination decision. Above the fixed motor interface, CoEvolve exposes coordinated linear transport, pivot, and alignment. The Task Orchestrator commits z t = ( κ t , d t , m t , ρ t 1 : N ) z_{t}=(\kappa_{t},d_{t},m_{t},\rho_{t}^{1:N}) , where κ t \kappa_{t} is the coordination primitive, d t d_{t} the semantic objective, m t ∈ { sync , asym } m_{t}\in\{\texttt{sync},\texttt{asym}\} the coordination mode, and ρ t 1 : N \rho_{t}^{1:N} the temporary robot roles. These variables denote the coordination fields of the committed subtask; its structured representation also carries formation intent, completion evidence, and execution constraints.
The Policy Coder supplies bounded execution parameters θ t 1 : N \theta_{t}^{1:N} , yielding χ t = ( z t , θ t 1 : N ) \chi_{t}=(z_{t},\theta_{t}^{1:N}) and executable team policy Π t = ( π t 1 , … , π t N ) \Pi_{t}=(\pi_{t}^{1},\ldots,\pi_{t}^{N}) . Each robot receives a separate policy containing one allowed action call followed by an explicit stop. The host validates the paired policies as one logical team transaction and dispatches them to separate motor deployments. Logical grouping does not imply identical physical response. The motor interface and execution constraints are given in Appendix A.2 .
3.2 Verifier Guided Coordination Evolution
Executing Π t \Pi_{t} from the latest accepted state s t s_{t} produces candidate state s t + s_{t}^{+} . The verifier returns E t = Verify ⁡ ( s t , Π t , s t + ) E_{t}=\operatorname{Verify}(s_{t},\Pi_{t},s_{t}^{+}) and determines acceptance from measured task progress, payload support and stability, robot configuration, and environmental contacts. A multimodal evaluator assists scene interpretation and failure diagnosis, while measured physical quantities determine transition acceptance. An accepted transition becomes the next trusted checkpoint; the semantic plan advances only when the active phase satisfies its completion criterion.
A rejected transition triggers typed revision rather than unrestricted policy regeneration. Given failed strategy χ t \chi_{t} and verifier evidence E t E_{t} , the revision scope ℓ t ∈ { Strategy , Coordination , Parameter } \ell_{t}\in\{\textsc{Strategy},\textsc{Coordination},\textsc{Parameter}\} changes ( κ t , d t ) (\kappa_{t},d_{t}) , ( m t , ρ t 1 : N ) (m_{t},\rho_{t}^{1:N}) , or θ t 1 : N \theta_{t}^{1:N} , respectively. Fields outside the selected scope remain fixed. The approved child strategy constrains the next policy-generation step rather than being replaced by an unrelated coordination decision, and the resulting policy is evaluated again from the latest accepted checkpoint. Verification, revision validation, and recovery rules are detailed in Appendix A.3 .
3.3 Verified Cooperative Experience
CoEvolve maintains two dynamic stores, ℳ t = ( ℳ t D , ℳ t A ) \mathcal{M}_{t}=(\mathcal{M}_{t}^{D},\mathcal{M}_{t}^{A}) . Strategy memory ℳ t D \mathcal{M}_{t}^{D} stores abstract coordination knowledge, while action exemplar memory ℳ t A \mathcal{M}_{t}^{A} stores accepted executable implementations. Strategy records capture applicability conditions, observed failure patterns, and repair directions, whereas action exemplars retain executable code and effective parameters.
Before orchestration, strategy records are retrieved using the active phase q t q_{t} and recent failure context ξ t \xi_{t} as r t D = RetrieveStrategy ⁡ ( ℳ t D , q t , ξ t ) r_{t}^{D}=\operatorname{RetrieveStrategy}(\mathcal{M}_{t}^{D};q_{t},\xi_{t}) . After the Orchestrator commits ( κ t , d t , m t ) (\kappa_{t},d_{t},m_{t}) , compatible action exemplars are retrieved as r t A = RetrieveExemplar ⁡ ( ℳ t A , κ t , d t , m t ) r_{t}^{A}=\operatorname{RetrieveExemplar}(\mathcal{M}_{t}^{A};\kappa_{t},d_{t},m_{t}) . Thus, strategy retrieval informs what coordination to attempt , while exemplar retrieval informs how to implement the selected coordination .
Experience is updated only after physical execution:
Accepted transitions may provide reusable strategy records and executable exemplars, while rejected transitions may provide verified counterexamples. Unexecuted repair proposals are not promoted as successful experience. Record schemas, retrieval, and promotion rules are given in Appendix A.4 .
4 Experiments
4.1 Experimental Setting
Task and observations.
We evaluate CoEvolve on cooperative payload transport in MuJoCo ( Todorov et al., 2012 ) . Two Unitree G1 humanoids jointly carry a freely supported 0.5 ​ kg 0.5\,\mathrm{kg} rigid payload through constrained corridors. The task requires coordinated transport, corner negotiation, target placement, and release. The base task additionally requires both robots to retreat while remaining upright.
The model receives simulator measurements, global and payload-centered observations, the active task phase, and retrieved experience when enabled. The host provides route-segment geometry and progress constraints. The model selects the coordination primitive, temporary roles, coordination mode, and bounded execution parameters within these constraints.
Execution and models.
Each robot uses an independent SONIC deployment with frozen policy weights ( Luo et al., 2026 ) , together with task-specific skill wrappers and support posture control. The host validates paired robot policies as one team transaction and dispatches their planner commands. The controllers retain independent observation histories and execution threads, so joint dispatch does not imply identical physical responses.
We evaluate GPT-5.4-mini, Gemini-3.5-Flash, and Claude-Sonnet-4.6. One backbone performs all semantic roles within a run. Except for experience reuse experiments, dynamic memory starts empty and updates remain local to that run.
4.2 Evaluation Protocol and Metrics
A physical transaction is one joint robot command dispatched for physical execution. Accepted and rejected executions consume the physical budget, while outputs rejected before dispatch consume model calls but no physical transactions. The base task uses an 80-transaction budget and requires placement, release, and robot retreat. Layout and experience-reuse evaluations use verified placement and release as their endpoint.

4.3 Cross-Backbone Evaluation
We first test whether the same coordination interface supports different foundation model backbones. Each backbone is evaluated on five base-task runs under the same task definition and endpoint.

Table 1 shows successful full-task execution under all three backbones. Claude achieves the highest EA (86.5%) and PAUC@80 (64.5%) but fewer completions than GPT and Gemini, separating local execution validity from terminal success. The same distinction appears within one GPT run: after reaching the fourth milestone at transaction 47, 31 of the remaining 33 transactions are accepted, including 27 setup actions, yet no further milestone is certified. Claude’s failed runs instead terminate during generation or repair, which EA does not measure because no physical transaction is dispatched. Long-horizon performance therefore depends on revisions that continue phase advancement, not only on verifier-accepted execution.
4.4 Method Comparisons and Component Analysis
We next test whether physical feedback alone is sufficient for long-horizon repair, and whether structured revision and verified experience address different failure modes. We compare CoEvolve with REMAC ( Yuan et al., 2026 ) , COHERENT ( Liu et al., 2025a ) , CaP-Agent0 ( Fu et al., 2026 ) , and ASPIRE ( Lu et al., 2026 ) , adapted to the same cooperative transport task and robot interface. We also evaluate Flat Reflective Repair and CoEvolve without Dynamic Experience. Implementation details are given in Appendix B.2 .
Structured revision and verified experience target different repair failures.
Table 2 shows full-task completion only for CoEvolve , although all alternatives reach partial progress. A CaP-Agent0 trace illustrates why feedback alone can be insufficient: after receiving a requirement for different yaw corrections, the repair changes both values from 2.5 to 2.0 and preserves the rejected relation. Typed revision instead separates changes to the maneuver, role relation, and execution parameters. The component variants expose a second distinction. Removing Dynamic Experience still reaches 60.0% VP but yields no completion, with EA at 63.8% and PAUC@80 at 46.4%. Flat Reflective Repair terminates at the payload-drop limit in four runs, whereas the no-memory variant ends in four generation/repair stalls and one budget exhaustion. These failure profiles are consistent with structured revision localizing what should change and verified experience supplying evidence for what to try next, without isolating either effect causally.
Figure 3 provides a concrete execution-level view of the contrast observed in Table 2 . Both methods reach a nontrivial turning stage, but their subsequent executions diverge. CoEvolve continues through the corner and eventually completes the task, whereas REMAC first produces a rejected pivot, then executes a smaller accepted pivot, but later terminates at the payload-drop limit. The comparison shows that local executability and verifier acceptance are not sufficient for sustained task progress: an accepted correction may still leave the team in a configuration that does not support the remaining transport sequence. This trace is consistent with the role of structured revision in changing the coordination relation after failure, rather than only producing another locally executable motion.

We then test whether successful coordination on the base task remains executable under different corridor geometries. Three additional layouts are evaluated with GPT-5.4-mini using three runs per layout. The endpoint is verified placement and release without the final robot retreat. Scene geometry is shown in Appendix B.1 .
Local progress does not guarantee future physical feasibility.
Table 3 records verified placement in Layouts 2 and 4, while Layout 3 reaches no placement despite the highest VP (58.3%) and PAUC@160 (53.6%). The execution traces in Figure 4 illustrate this discrepancy: Layout 3 advances through several certified milestones before payload support degrades. Because the payload is supported through finite contact rather than rigid attachment, an accepted transition can reduce the support margin required by later maneuvers. Checkpoint acceptance therefore certifies the executed transition, not the feasibility of the remaining task. The result identifies future support and clearance as constraints that local transition verification does not explicitly optimize.

4.6 Cross-Model Experience Reuse
Finally, we test whether verified experience can be reused outside the backbone that produced it. GPT-5.4-mini is initialized with experience banks collected from successful GPT, Gemini, or Claude executions. No simulator state, checkpoint, controller history, or trajectory is transferred.
Bank size alone does not explain cross-model reuse.
Table 4 records two placements with Gemini-derived experience, one with GPT-derived experience, and none with the Claude bank. The target selects, instantiates, and verifies policies, so the experiment transfers coordination evidence rather than a solved trajectory. GPT and Gemini provide 17 and 16 strategy records, compared with only four from Claude, which may reduce the decision coverage available to the target. Record count is not sufficient, however: GPT has more action exemplars than Gemini but no pivot exemplar, while both Gemini and Claude include pivot examples. The observed differences are therefore more consistent with coverage and state-specific applicability than with bank size alone. Unequal source banks and the absence of a matched empty-memory condition prevent a causal transfer claim.
5 Conclusion
We propose CoEvolve , a framework for adapting executable multi-robot coordination over fixed motor skills through physical verification, structured revision, and verified experience. Across three foundation model families, CoEvolve achieves end-to-end cooperative transport, while additional evaluations show successful execution under new layouts and same-model and cross-model experience reuse. The experiments also separate local execution validity from sustained task advancement: accepted actions can fail to resolve the current coordination bottleneck, and verified progress can precede later loss of physical support. These findings identify structured coordination revision as a useful interface for long-horizon cooperation while leaving motion precision, contact stability, and physical recovery as open limitations.
6 Limitations
CoEvolve has limitations. First, end-to-end execution incurs substantial wall-clock cost because model inference, physical execution, verification, and recovery recur throughout a run (Appendix C.4 ). Second, rejected executions are recovered by restoring an accepted simulator state rather than a physically executable recovery policy, which limits direct transfer to real robots. Third, the frozen motor interface provides bounded motion intentions rather than precise endpoints: unequal robot responses, residual motion after stopping, and accumulated formation drift can reduce clearance and destabilize the shared payload. Payload stability also depends on finite contact regions, friction, and simulator contact dynamics, which we do not systematically randomize in the study. Finally, all experiments use simulation, privileged physical state information, and supplied route references. Real-world deployment will require perception from onboard sensing, more accurate motion and contact models, safe physical recovery, and evaluation under broader environments and hardware conditions. The relatively small and heterogeneous evaluation cohorts also limit strong causal claims about individual components, scene generalization, and experience transfer.

Ethics Statement
This work studies multi-robot coordination entirely in simulation and does not involve human participants, personal data, or deployment on physical robots. The proposed framework is intended for research on cooperative robot reasoning and execution. Although physical verification and recovery are used within the simulator, they should not be interpreted as safety guarantees for real-world deployment. Applying the system to physical robots would require additional safeguards, state and contact estimation, calibrated motion control, and hardware-specific safety validation.

Appendix A Additional Method Details
A.1 State, Plan, and Role Interfaces
The state s t = ( x t 1 : N , x t o , c t , 𝒢 ) s_{t}=(x_{t}^{1:N},x_{t}^{o},c_{t},\mathcal{G}) contains robot states, payload state, contact and support measurements, and scene geometry. Observations combine serialized measurements, global and payload-centered views, and geometric references for the active route segment. These references may include world directions, local progress targets, corridor boundaries, and completion gates. They are distinct from the semantic plan P t = [ ( q k , γ k , σ k , t ) ] k = 1 K P_{t}=[(q_{k},\gamma_{k},\sigma_{k,t})]_{k=1}^{K} , which records task phases, completion evidence, and status rather than an executable waypoint sequence.
The tuple z t = ( κ t , d t , m t , ρ t 1 : N ) z_{t}=(\kappa_{t},d_{t},m_{t},\rho_{t}^{1:N}) denotes the coordination fields of the committed subtask. Its structured representation additionally contains formation intent, completion evidence, and execution constraints. The Coder supplies bounded numerical parameters θ t 1 : N \theta_{t}^{1:N} , giving χ t = ( z t , θ t 1 : N ) \chi_{t}=(z_{t},\theta_{t}^{1:N}) and the executable team policy Π t = ( π t 1 , … , π t N ) \Pi_{t}=(\pi_{t}^{1},\ldots,\pi_{t}^{N}) . Thus, the Orchestrator determines the coordination decision, while the Coder determines its numerical and programmatic implementation.
The roles in Table 5 use the selected backbone through different prompts and output schemas, not independently trained models. Their structured proposals remain subject to host validation.
A.2 Motor Capabilities and Team Execution
The motor interface wraps separate SONIC deployments with frozen policy weights ( Luo et al., 2026 ) . Available skills are summarized in Table 6 . The transport primitives are coordinated_linear_transport , coordinated_pivot , and coordinated_alignment .
Linear transport uses a common translation objective; pivoting assigns temporary inner-support and outer-mover roles; alignment uses a reference robot while the other makes a bounded correction. Initialization, placement, release, and retreat are lifecycle operations rather than additional transport primitives.
Synchronous coordination uses compatible commands for a shared objective; asymmetric coordination permits different temporary roles or parameters. Neither guarantees identical robot displacement. Each robot policy contains one allowed action call followed by an explicit stop. The host checks output structure, allowed methods, numerical bounds, syntax, and consistency of the paired policies before dispatch. Generated policies cannot change the verifier, invoke simulator restoration, or access low-level controller handles. Joint dispatch is a logical transaction, not a hard real-time synchronization barrier.
A.3 Verification, Revision, and Recovery
Acceptance depends on measured payload support and stability, robot state, environmental contacts, phase validity, and progress relative to the committed objective. Visual interpretation assists diagnosis but cannot override contradictory physical measurements. An accepted setup or corrective action can establish a checkpoint without completing a task phase; phase advancement requires separate completion evidence.
Revision acts on the failed strategy χ t = ( κ t , d t , m t , ρ t 1 : N , θ t 1 : N ) \chi_{t}=(\kappa_{t},d_{t},m_{t},\rho_{t}^{1:N},\theta_{t}^{1:N}) . Strategy revision concerns the maneuver and objective, coordination revision concerns mode and temporary roles, and parameter revision concerns bounded numerical arguments. The host checks the child against its failed parent and the selected mutable fields. Once approved, that revision constrains the next orchestration and code-generation step rather than being discarded by an unrelated decision.
After rejection, the host restores the latest accepted simulator state, clears transient execution state, resynchronizes the controllers, and verifies the restoration. A fingerprint of the checkpoint, normalized policy, and effective arguments blocks identical rejected transactions at that checkpoint. Recovery neither physically reverses the motion nor automatically searches earlier checkpoints. Separately selected diagnostic restart points are evaluation conditions, not autonomous recovery actions.
Algorithm 1 summarizes this loop. Its model procedures include bounded validation and repair; an inability to produce a valid next transaction can terminate execution before the physical budget is exhausted. Only dispatched physical transactions consume that budget. Execution attempt identifiers can therefore differ from cumulative transaction counts.
A.4 Experience Contents and Retrieval
The two dynamic stores are written as ℳ t = ( ℳ t D , ℳ t A ) \mathcal{M}_{t}=(\mathcal{M}_{t}^{D},\mathcal{M}_{t}^{A}) . The static skill catalog belongs to the capability interface and does not evolve. Strategy records store applicability conditions, coordination principles, observed failure patterns, and repair directions. The active phase filters applicable records, while recent failure type and level contribute to ranking. The next primitive, objective, and mode are not query conditions because they have not yet been selected.
After the coordination decision is committed, action exemplars are matched by primitive, semantic objective, and coordination mode. Phase only ranks otherwise compatible exemplars. These entries retain accepted code and effective parameters but remain subject to the current subtask and execution constraints. Strategy retrieval therefore informs the coordination decision; exemplar retrieval informs its implementation.
Promotion requires agreement between a record’s claim, the executed behavior, and the verifier outcome. Eligible accepted executions can yield positive strategy records and executable exemplars; rejected executions can yield counterexamples. Unexecuted repairs are not successful experience. Strategy records omit scene coordinates, checkpoint poses, and numerical action magnitudes, whereas exemplars retain concrete implementations. Standard fresh runs start with empty dynamic memory; transfer runs preload a fixed source bank and update only their own subsequent experience.

C.4 Run-Time Accounting
Run time is measured as elapsed wall-clock time within recorded execution segments, summed once across all segments belonging to each logical execution. It includes model and API waiting, physical execution, verification, recovery, and finalization. Manual pauses, code modification, and queue waiting between segments are excluded, and concurrent subprocess durations within a segment are not summed separately.
The selected archive contains 69 deduplicated logical executions. Timing is complete for 65 executions; the remaining four retain confirmed lower bounds from available continuous event spans. The resulting cumulative run time is at least 112.8 112.8 hours, with a mean of at least 98.1 98.1 minutes per execution. The Full condition in Table 2 reuses the five GPT executions in Table 1 and is not counted again. The incomplete timing records are COHERENT run 203, Layout 3 runs 200 and 201, and Claude-to-GPT run 203. Missing intervals are not imputed as zero.
Run time describes end-to-end execution-process duration rather than robot motion time, GPU-hours, or the calendar duration of the evaluation campaign. It covers only the selected evaluation archive, not development experiments, manual code changes, or diagnostic work outside these records. Shorter run time does not necessarily indicate greater efficiency because unsuccessful runs may terminate early, while repeated verification and recovery can extend a run substantially.
Mean run time over all executions should also be distinguished from run time conditioned on success. The five GPT base-task executions average 254.7 254.7 minutes, whereas the three successful GPT executions average 142.4 142.4 minutes. One budget-exhausted GPT run lasts approximately 11.65 11.65 hours and substantially increases the all-run mean. We therefore report all-run mean time as an end-to-end execution statistic rather than as a better-is-lower efficiency metric.
