Yiran Cheng Affiliation: Huawei openJiuwen Team Shaowu Pan Affiliation: Huawei openJiuwen Team Xinyu Zhang Affiliation: Huawei openJiuwen Team Qijun Li Affiliation: Huawei openJiuwen Team Abstract Open-ended tool agents can complete the same objective through different tool paths, and their quality depends jointly on the interaction trace, environment state, and final artifacts, so such tasks rarely admit an executable ground-truth reward. The central challenge is therefore to construct a reward from existing experience that exposes the current policy’s failures while remaining stationary during optimization. We introduce Reward Engine, which, like a self-motivated student, first works out what success requires and then optimizes toward it. From the task and initial trajectories, it builds a RewardPack of testcases for required correctness and rubrics for graded quality, each executed as a check that returns a judgment with evidence. The frozen RewardPack then guides the agent to revise and select its outputs, and can also serve as a reward for reinforcement learning. On WorkBuddy Bench Office and Claw-Eval, the constructed reward largely agrees with the benchmark graders, not reversing their ordering on 82.3% and 80.5% of candidate pairs. Tool agents are increasingly asked to do open-ended work, such as reconciling spreadsheets, drafting reports from mixed-format files, or triaging tickets [ Yao et al., 2025 ; Tencent Youtu Lab et al., 2026 ; Ye et al., 2026 ] . Unlike verifiable tasks such as math with final answers or code with unit tests [ Cobbe et al., 2021 ; Jimenez et al., 2024 ] , whose rewards directly drive reinforcement learning [ Shao et al., 2024 ] , these tasks come with no reward: many solutions are acceptable, and quality depends on the trace, environment state, and delivered artifacts. Constructing the reward thus becomes the key, and harder, problem; without it, an agent can neither critique and select among its attempts nor train on them. Common proxies fall short: a weekly report whose completion rate reads 92% although the source rows give 78% passes a file-existence check and can pass a holistic model judge, whose verdicts depend on presentation and ordering [ Zheng et al., 2023 ; Wang et al., 2024 ] . Like a self-motivated student facing an untaught task, the agent must first work out which constraints must hold and where there is room to excel ( Figure 1 ), and the resulting reward must meet three requirements: • Evidence-grounded: each criterion, whether a hard constraint (testcase) or graded quality (rubric), must be judged by recomputing it from the artifact, trace, and environment and returned with a receipt, such as “expected 78%, observed 92%: fail”. The receipt tells a critic what to fix and a selector why one candidate ranks higher, and it keeps a violated requirement apart from an unreadable file or missing evidence. • Tested before use: a drafted check can itself be wrong, for instance by reading the wrong column or accepting a near miss, so the reward must be tested against labeled counterexamples before the agent relies on it, and a repair must not break cases it already judged correctly. • Stationary: the reward must stay fixed while the agent is revised or selected against it and change only between rounds; otherwise an apparent gain may come from a moved target rather than a better output. Execute by reward (orange): the frozen pack supports revision and selection. 3. Update reward (green): products, receipts, and use-time defects inform the next construction round. Benchmark scores are reserved for evaluation. Existing evaluators and reward-construction methods meet these requirements only in part ( Table 5 in Appendix A ). Model judges, rubric judges, and trained verifiers [ Zheng et al., 2023 ; Kim et al., 2024 ; Saad-Falcon et al., 2025 ; Gunjal et al., 2026 ; Cobbe et al., 2021 ] score outputs, but their judgments are not bound to re-runnable procedures, and at most partially record the evidence behind them [ Zhuge et al., 2025 ; Rao and Callison-Burch, 2026 ] . Reward-construction methods write executable reward code or tests [ Ma et al., 2024 ; Xie et al., 2024 ; Chen et al., 2023 ] but keep no evidence receipts, so a violated requirement cannot be told apart from a broken check. Most importantly, none tests the reward for a task on labeled counterexamples before optimizing against it, or accepts a repair only if earlier agreement is preserved; the closest, EvoRubrics [ Ding et al., 2026 ] , adapts its rubrics adversarially but updates them at every training step, so the target moves while it is used. What is missing is a reward that is evidence-grounded, tested against counterexamples before use, and held fixed while the agent is optimized against it. We introduce Reward Engine ( Figure 2 ) to close this gap: it builds such a reward for each task and uses it to improve the agent’s deliverable. Each round has three stages: Build reward. From the task and a few initial trajectories, never hidden answers or benchmark scores, a Jury drafts the task’s obligations as testcases or graded rubrics, and a Builder binds each to an executable check that returns a judgment with an evidence receipt ( Sections 2 and 3.1 ). A quality-control loop tests the reward on labeled control, error, and boundary cases and accepts only repairs that lose no previously matched case; the result is frozen as a RewardPack ( Section 3.2 ). Execute by reward. A Critic turns failed criteria into revision feedback, and a Jury ranks the revision with the round’s other candidates; a candidate replaces the fresh task-only one only if it ranks strictly higher and fails no test the task-only candidate passes ( Section 3.3 ). Update reward. Products, receipts, and defects found during use inform only the next RewardPack, so the reward never shifts while it is used; a meta loop across task families, which we do not evaluate, can further refine how rewards are built. On WorkBuddy Bench Office and Claw-Eval, the RewardPack does not reverse the benchmark grader’s ordering on 82.3% and 80.5% of candidate pairs and raises Pass 3 \mathrm{Pass}^{3} over Baseline from 45.0% to 54.0% and from 78.4% to 83.7% ( Section 4.2 ). Our main contributions are (1) An evidence-grounded RewardPack of testcases for required correctness and rubrics for graded quality, built from the task and initial trajectories, each criterion and rubric bound to an executable evidence check ( Sections 2 and 3.1 ). (2) Reward-centric self-improvement , in which the frozen RewardPack guides Critic-driven revision and Jury selection of the deliverable and is updated only between rounds ( Sections 3.3 and 2 ). (3) Two-benchmark evidence on WorkBuddy Bench Office and Claw-Eval that the RewardPack largely agrees with the benchmark graders and raises the delivered output’s pass rate over Baseline ( Section 4 ). 2 Problem setup Tasks and objective. A task supplies public requirements x x , including its input files, and a tool environment. An actor π \pi , a language model in a fixed tool harness, takes actions a t ∼ π ( ⋅ ∣ h t ) a_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid h_{t}) given its context h t h_{t} and delivers a product y y : the artifact, its interaction trace, and the resulting environment state. The task’s quality q ⁡ ( y ) q(y) , here the benchmark grader’s score, is hidden. The system sees only the public information ℐ \mathcal{I} : x x , updates from the task’s own (possibly simulated) user, public background knowledge the task does not forbid, the execution history, and a few initial runs ℬ \mathcal{B} of π \pi on x x alone. From ℐ \mathcal{I} it must (i) construct a reward R R whose judgments track q q and (ii) use R R to deliver a product y ⋆ y^{\star} of higher q q than π \pi attains alone; Section 4.2 measures (i) by order agreement with the grader and (ii) by the scores and pass rates of y ⋆ y^{\star} . The weights of π \pi are never updated: what evolves is the reward and the process that builds it. Hidden answers and grading outputs are excluded by design. Reward. (1) A criterion c i c_{i} is a testcase (required correctness, z i ∈ { 𝗉𝖺𝗌𝗌 , 𝖿𝖺𝗂𝗅 } z_{i}\in\{\mathsf{pass},\mathsf{fail}\} ) or a rubric (graded quality, z i ∈ { 𝖿𝖺𝗂𝗅 , 𝗉𝖺𝗋𝗍𝗂𝖺𝗅 , 𝖿𝗎𝗅𝗅 } z_{i}\in\{\mathsf{fail},\mathsf{partial},\mathsf{full}\} against written anchors); z i = ⊥ z_{i}=\bot means no judgment, and several bound tests give a tuple ( Section B.2 ). A check κ i \kappa_{i} is a stored, re-runnable procedure, either code, such as recomputing a rate from source rows, or a model judgment over evidence it first locates, so it need not be deterministic. The receipt e i e_{i} references the inspected evidence and records the execution status, keeping apart an observed violation (a failing judgment), insufficient evidence ( ⊥ \bot , normal status), and a check that did not run ( ⊥ \bot , error status). A RewardPack stores one version R v R_{v} with the records needed to audit and re-run it ( Table 7 ). R ⁡ ( y ) R(y) is a vector, not a scalar, so each consumer states its use: the Critic turns failing judgments and receipts into feedback ( Eq. 5 ); selection ranks candidates by a Jury that reads artifacts and receipts, and rejects any candidate that fails a test the task-only candidate passes ( Eq. 6 ); and the grader comparison orders candidates by the share of resolved criteria that pass. Reinforcement learning would need a similar reduction and is not evaluated. Requirements. The requirements of Section 1 become conditions on R R . Evidence-grounded : every judgment other than ⊥ \bot carries a receipt naming the parts of y y and x x it used. Tested before use : R R is first run on labeled cases ( y j , i j , z j ⋆ ) (y_{j},i_{j},z^{\star}_{j}) , each a product, a targeted criterion, and a reference label assigned blind to R R ; R R agrees on a case if z i j = z j ⋆ z_{i_{j}}=z^{\star}_{j} , is unresolved if z i j = ⊥ z_{i_{j}}=\bot , and disagrees otherwise, and a repair must keep every case already agreed on ( Section 3.2 ). Stationary : of the three layers, the actor π \pi , the reward R R , and the construction state ϕ \phi that builds rewards ( Section 3.1 ), only π \pi is fixed throughout. We index actor steps by t t , rounds by n n , reward versions by v v , and updates of ϕ \phi by k k . Quality control may produce several versions within a round; the last is frozen for the round’s execution stage, where feedback changes only the context h t h_{t} , and revisions of R R or ϕ \phi take effect only at round boundaries. A gain within a round therefore comes from a better product, not a moved target. 3 Reward Engine Algorithm 1 gives the per-task procedure over the three stages of Figure 2 ; Appendix B gives the notation, RewardPack schema, and implementation details. 3.1 Constructing criteria and checks Jury. Three Jury members draft obligations from the public task before seeing any artifact, then revisit them against the initial task-only runs ℬ \mathcal{B} , which show where the current policy falls short and where acceptable solutions differ. A reducer merges the drafts into criteria, each marked as a testcase or a rubric, keeping each criterion’s source, applicability, and fail/partial/full anchors; unresolved obligations stay visible. For the weekly report, Jury fixes the reporting period and the completion-rate definition. An obligation that no member proposes remains an omission that this review cannot detect ( Section B.3 ). Builder. Builder turns each criterion into executable tests, refining its fail/partial/full anchors into the rubric a model-judge check applies (Reward Builder), and binds them to inputs, tools, and judgment procedures (Harness Builder); for the weekly report, the completion-rate test recomputes the rate from the source rows. Construction is guided by the construction state ϕ = ( ω , ℳ ) \phi=(\omega,\mathcal{M}) , which pairs Strategy ω \omega with Memory ℳ \mathcal{M} , under a scope, delivery contract, and budget: R 0 ∼ Build ϕ ⁡ ( ℐ ) , R_{0}\sim\Build_{\phi}(\mathcal{I}), (2) where Build \Build may instead return an explicit construction failure. Each receipt of Eq. 1 is bound to the inspected artifact, environment snapshot, inputs, scope, and check version, so a judgment can be audited, and a receipt is reused only when its artifact, environment snapshot, scope, and check version all still match ( Section B.2 ). Strategy and Memory. Strategy ω \omega holds module guidance and settings for quality control (QC, Section 3.2 ), and Memory ℳ \mathcal{M} stores construction lessons with their sources and validation status. Stage 3 of every round ( Figure 2 ) adds lessons to Memory, so Memory grows within the inner loop, whereas Strategy changes only in the meta loop of Algorithm 1 . That loop proposes updates of ϕ \phi on training task families and selects them on disjoint validation families; it changes how evaluators are built ( ϕ \phi ), not how tasks are solved ( π \pi ). We do not evaluate the meta loop: Section 4 runs the inner loop only. 3.2 Self-attack and regression-gated repair A drafted check may be implemented incorrectly, or an obligation may be missing or mis-stated. WorkBuddy Bench Office has 50 mixed-format office tasks scored 0–100 by deterministic rules and a model judge [ Tencent Youtu Lab et al., 2026 ] . Claw-Eval provides executable environments and human-checked rubrics [ Ye et al., 2026 ; Claw-Eval Contributors, 2026 ] ; we use 104 frozen task versions from 68 scenarios (36 bilingual pairs), scored 0–100 by its native grader with a custom judge ( Appendix D ). Compared outputs. Each task is executed in sequential rounds. We compare Selected by reward with Baseline, Revision, uniform choice among the generated candidates, and an Oracle that selects the candidate with the highest benchmark score. The packs may have seen evaluated candidates; this analysis does not measure generalization to unseen candidates. 5 Related work Judges, rubrics, and verifiers. Model judges scale open-ended evaluation but are sensitive to position and presentation [ Zheng et al., 2023 ; Wang et al., 2024 ] . Fine-grained rubric judges [ Kim et al., 2024 ] , rubrics and checklists as rewards [ Gunjal et al., 2026 ; Viswanathan et al., 2025 ] , reusable rubric evaluation [ Rao and Callison-Burch, 2026 ] , and natural-language unit tests [ Saad-Falcon et al., 2025 ] make criteria explicit, and Agent-as-a-Judge adds agentic inspection [ Zhuge et al., 2025 ] . Trained verifiers and process reward models score solutions or steps [ Cobbe et al., 2021 ; Lightman et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2025 ] , and AgentRewardBench documents disagreement between automatic and expert judgments of web-agent trajectories [ Lù et al., 2025 ] . Our unit of evaluation is instead a criterion bound to an executed procedure whose receipt names the artifact, scope, and version, with explicit status for errors and unresolved evidence. Constructing and repairing rewards. Language models can design rewards from descriptions [ Kwon et al., 2023 ] and write reward code refined with training feedback [ Ma et al., 2024 ; Xie et al., 2024 ] ; CodeT selects programs with generated tests [ Chen et al., 2023 ] ; and EvoRubrics co-evolves rubrics adversarially with the policy [ Ding et al., 2026 ] . Reward Engine instead tests the reward against counterexamples before use, gates repairs by regression on accumulated cases as in software regression testing, and freezes the result while it is used ( Table 5 ). Freezing fixes the standard but does not remove proxy misspecification, since a fixed proxy can still be overoptimized or hacked [ Skalse et al., 2022 ; Gao et al., 2023 ; Pan et al., 2024 ] . Feedback, refinement, and persistent experience. Self-Refine, Reflexion, CRITIC, and TextGrad revise outputs or system components with language feedback [ Madaan et al., 2023 ; Shinn et al., 2023 ; Gou et al., 2024 ; Yuksekgonul et al., 2025 ] , although re-prompting alone can account for gains attributed to critique [ Stechly et al., 2025 ] . ExpeL, ADAS, and SkillRL retain lessons, agent designs, or skills [ Zhao et al., 2024 ; Hu et al., 2025 ; Xia et al., 2026 ] , and Constitutional AI, PPO, and GRPO update parameters from feedback or rewards [ Bai et al., 2022 ; Schulman et al., 2017 ; Shao et al., 2024 ] . Reward Engine exposes the measurement behind its feedback, and its persistent target is evaluator construction rather than task solving. 6 Conclusion Reward Engine makes reward construction part of solving open-ended tool tasks. From public task requirements and execution experience, it builds evidence-grounded checks for required correctness and graded quality, tests them against counterexamples, and holds the resulting reward fixed while it guides revision and selection. Updates between rounds incorporate new evidence without changing the standard during use. On WorkBuddy Bench Office and Claw-Eval, this approach raises three-run consistency over Baseline and improves selection over uniform choice among the same candidates by 1.83 and 2.36 points, respectively, with 95% confidence intervals excluding zero. Mean delivery gains over Baseline are clearest on Claw-Eval. The constructed reward also does not reverse the benchmark graders’ ordering on over 80% of evaluated candidate pairs. These results support constructing and validating rewards as a practical route to improving tool-agent outputs at inference time. This is admissibility for reuse, not semantic truth, and it cannot supply missing observations, so a modified workbook needs fresh receipts and a checker repair keeps only receipts whose bindings still match. Information boundary. Construction and feedback use only ℐ \mathcal{I} ( Section 2 ). Genuine user updates enter ℐ \mathcal{I} , and internal suggestions never add requirements. Hidden answers and grader outputs are excluded by design. Public benchmark materials that an actor retrieves count as public knowledge ( Table 12 ). B.3 Construction and regression-gated repair Jury. Three Jury members draft obligations from the public requirements and permitted knowledge, then reconsider them against the initial task-only artifacts. Each phase needs at least two usable drafts; missing outputs do not count as agreement, and too few drafts or a failed reduction yield no publishable specification. A reducer merges both phases, and validation maps, defers, or excludes with a reason every identified obligation. Obligations that were never proposed remain a semantic omission risk that validation cannot detect. Knowledge, strategies, and QC hypotheses may suggest checks but do not establish requirements. Builder and delivery contracts. Builder produces the bindings in Table 7 under the caller’s scope, construction budget, and delivery contract, returning an admissible RewardPack or an explicit construction failure that remains in the delivery denominator. The Outcome contract requires schema, binding, and smoke checks and permits synthetic fixtures. Publication additionally requires a receipt for every bound test on a real artifact, not all of them errors, a completed review, and a selection rationale. The artifact must come from a sandboxed actor run charged to construction or a verified archive captured before grading. Self-attack and repair. Adversarial review executes the control, error, and boundary cases of Section 3.2 . Reference models label anonymized material against public task evidence, blind to the RewardPack, the attack rationale, and the predicted labels, and replay preserves case identities and material. Beyond Eq. 4 , the implementation accepts a candidate only if known reference labels are unchanged and fresh boundary cases around the repair are supported; otherwise the incumbent is kept. Structural review checks bindings but not semantics, so a rebuild without adversarial review leaves its semantic correction unverified. QC routes confirmed checker defects to Builder, missing or mis-stated obligations to Jury, and independently established artifact defects to actor feedback, and leaves uncertain attributions unresolved; infrastructure failures permit bounded remeasurement. A checker repair preserves the criteria and unrelated checks, and a criterion repair remeasures only the affected bindings. Defects found while a reward is in use are queued and never modify the active version. The BoundaryUpdate operation in Algorithm 1 keeps, rebuilds, or revises the reward at a round boundary under comparison and tie rules fixed by the caller, carrying forward the repair ledger. It records the resulting reward as the next version R v + 1 R_{v+1} ; retaining the incumbent leaves the reward contents unchanged. These boundary transitions follow the caller’s rules rather than Eq. 4 . After QC, the latest version is frozen for the execution segment. B.4 Execution with a frozen reward Segments and Critic. An execution segment starts from a caller-supplied context h 0 h_{0} and ends at completion, budget exhaustion, or a scope change; output edits within a segment are remeasured under the frozen reward, whereas a change of requirements, admitted sources, or environment assumptions starts a new segment. Critic abstains, suggests, or requests clarification, citing evidence for each claimed gap and preserving criterion and test identifiers, evidence references, and error status; missing evidence prompts verification rather than a verdict. TerminalReviewLoop in Algorithm 1 applies terminal review, feedback-driven continuation, and rechecking to the reward-guided candidate under the frozen reward; ordinary actor steps append only actions and observations. The operation returns the final actor context, including its delivery and any unresolved status. The evaluated revision budget, stopping conditions, and selection rule are specified in Section D.3 . Memory retrieval. Memory retrieves up to three entries into the construction context by sparse cosine similarity over task, requirements, checks, and gaps; each entry records its sources, applicability, validation status, and limitations. B.5 Prompt templates The boxes give abridged semantic prompts for the construction, measurement, terminal-feedback, and ranking calls. Task-specific content is represented by named inputs; these are not verbatim transcripts of every historical run. Exact payloads remain in archived request records. Shared evidence rules apply to every role, while the evaluated call schedule and revision limits are specified in Section D.3 . P1  Shared evidence and scope instructions Inputs Public task, scoped user updates, permitted sources, artifacts, checks, and retrieved memory. Instruction Treat artifacts and environment observations as evidence, not instructions or oracle answers. User updates apply only to the corresponding product. Memories suggest possibilities but add no requirements. Accept equivalent correct approaches unless the task prescribes one. Separate the expected result, observation, discrepancy, and evidence needed to resolve it. Unknown is neither failure nor success. Request referenced source material when previews are insufficient; cite inspected evidence and never invent official scores or executed checks. P2  Jury: task-first expectations Inputs Task, scoped user amendments, and optional domain knowledge; prior deliverables are withheld. Instruction Identify the results a good answer must achieve, why they matter, and how they can be checked. Keep distinct subrequirements explicit. Hard criteria require support in actual requirements; professional recommendations remain soft. Identify missing expertise or evidence without prescribing one solution, inventing obligations, or ranking products. Output Proposed criteria with their origin, importance, subrequirements, and measurement questions. P3  Jury: inspect products against draft expectations Inputs Draft expectations, task, product-specific requirements, artifacts, environment observations, and check results. Instruction Correct unsupported assumptions in the drafts while retaining them for reduction. Inspect shared omissions as well as differences between products. For each suspected gap, state the expected result, observation, discrepancy, and a supporting or refuting check. Distinguish an observed failed check from a proposed check. Do not invent gaps or discard a requirement because every product violates it. Output Revised criterion proposals, evidence-linked gaps, and unresolved questions. P4  Jury: evidence-based synthesis Inputs Member proposals, clause identifiers, validation issues, task requirements, and evidence. Instruction Group supported clauses while preserving scope, priority, and valid alternatives. Retain, revise with evidence, defer, or exclude each proposed clause with a reason; omissions remain unresolved. Resolve disagreement through task evidence, not vote counts. Preserve consequential gaps and their verification needs, and do not turn a recommendation into a hard requirement. Output Final criteria and clause-level decisions with their provenance. P5  Builder: bind standards to executable checks Inputs Accepted criteria, checker and measurement-tool catalogs, and permitted evidence. Instruction Preserve criterion identity, meaning, priority, and scope. Define fail, partial, and full anchors and propose a reusable check, splitting only when distinct execution methods require it. Prefer a parser, calculation, runtime check, or source lookup when applicable. Distinguish semantic judgment from code execution and report missing implementation capabilities. Bind checks to the current product and keep unmeasurable requirements visible. Output Rubric anchors and test bindings, including inputs, checker choice, and any missing implementation. P6  Evaluation Harness: evidence-backed measurement Inputs One bound test, its standard and anchors, current product, sources, and executed tool observations. Instruction Evaluate only the supplied standard against observed evidence. Reading a file does not establish correctness. Request missing evidence or a supported tool; never simulate its execution. A failure needs a localized defect, a pass needs support for the material requirements, and unresolved essential evidence stays unknown. Separate advisory improvements from the verdict. Output A pass, fail, or unknown judgment with reasons, localized gaps, and evidence references. The Harness attaches execution receipts and error status. P7  Terminal Critic: review and target verification Inputs Deliverable, frozen criteria, available test identifiers, open questions, and earlier findings. Instruction Review correctness and completeness using claims as written. Cite evidence for defects, preserve correct work, and recheck earlier findings for regressions. Prior observations are not current passes. Select targeted checks for consequential doubts, distinguishing defects from measurement errors and missing evidence. Keep improvement suggestions separate from established failures. Output Evidence-linked findings, proposed checks, remaining gaps, and review status. The controller executes checks and applies the limits in Section D.3 . P8  Jury: rank completed candidates Inputs Candidates, their actual user requirements, accessible evidence and receipts; the reducer also receives member comparisons. Instruction Compare outcomes rather than effort or compliance with advice. Identify supported quality differences before ranking. Do not transfer undisclosed requirements between products. Use ties when differences lack support and unknown when evidence is insufficient. Shared defects remain defects in the winner; ranking does not establish absolute completion. Resolve disagreements through evidence rather than vote counts. Output Ordered candidate groups, ties and unjudgeable candidates, with evidence-linked reasons; account for each candidate exactly once. Schema validation, checker dispatch, evidence binding, revision limits, and the task-only fallback are controller operations outside these prompts. Appendix C Theoretical Analysis C.1 Ratchet property Proposition 1 (Ratchet) . If R v + 1 R_{v+1} is obtained from R v R_{v} by the acceptance rule Eq. 4 , then G v ⊆ G v + 1 G_{v}\subseteq G_{v+1} , and | G v + 1 | ≥ | G v | + 1 |G_{v+1}|\geq|G_{v}|+1 when the candidate is accepted. Hence, along such transitions, a case on which the reward once agreed with its reference label is never lost, and at most | Q | − | G 0 | |Q|-|G_{0}| repairs are accepted, where Q Q is the final ledger. Proof of Proposition 1 . Judgments are recorded once per version and replayed, so G v G_{v} is fixed given v v , and on Q v Q_{v} the recorded judgments of R v + 1 R_{v+1} are those of R ~ \widetilde{R} if it is accepted and those of R v R_{v} otherwise. Since Q v ⊆ Q v + 1 Q_{v}\subseteq Q_{v+1} , this gives G v + 1 ⊇ G ~ G_{v+1}\supseteq\widetilde{G} after acceptance and G v + 1 ⊇ G v G_{v+1}\supseteq G_{v} after rejection. After acceptance, the no-regression clause gives G ~ ⊇ G v \widetilde{G}\supseteq G_{v} , so G v ⊆ G v + 1 G_{v}\subseteq G_{v+1} in both cases. The fixes-a-failure clause further supplies some j ∗ ∈ F v ∩ G ~ j^{\ast}\in F_{v}\cap\widetilde{G} ; as R v R_{v} disagrees on j ∗ j^{\ast} , we have j ∗ ∈ G v + 1 ∖ G v j^{\ast}\in G_{v+1}\setminus G_{v} , so | G v + 1 | ≥ | G v | + 1 |G_{v+1}|\geq|G_{v}|+1 . Each accepted repair thus adds at least one case to a set contained in Q Q , so at most | Q | − | G 0 | |Q|-|G_{0}| repairs are accepted.
∎ The statement concerns the set G v G_{v} ; the agreement rate on Q v Q_{v} can still fall when an attack round adds failing cases. It is a property of the acceptance rule, not of the attacker: it says nothing about cases that were never generated, and a systematically wrong reference label is ratcheted in as easily as a correct one. Round-boundary updates follow the caller’s rules ( Section B.3 ) and are covered only when they pass the same gate. Held-out evaluation of the frozen reward against independent labels addresses these gaps. Appendix D Experimental details D.1 Benchmarks D.1.1 WorkBuddy Bench Office WorkBuddy Bench Office is the Office subset of a role-based benchmark whose other subsets cover Code, Web, and Security [ Tencent Youtu Lab et al., 2026 ] . We use all 50 tasks of the version-1.0 Office subset with equal weight. They involve spreadsheets, CSV, PDF, and word-processing documents (24 data/file operations, 17 document operations, and nine workspace-automation tasks), and their difficulty lies in data relationships, document structure, state, and supporting evidence rather than file size. Agents run in a sandboxed Docker workspace through Harbor. The CompositeVerifier combines deterministic checks on the submitted artifacts with a model judge over extracted evidence through a task-specific weighted sum; across our 150 final receipts, the deterministic weight ranges from 0.70 to 0.95. A run passes at 80 (inclusive). These external graders are distinct from the internal RewardPack measurements. Among 450 round-candidate records, the only invalid score is the initial-round task-only score on analyst-forecast-extract-L3-018 , archived as 72.8 with an invalid-scoring flag; we count it as zero and retain the raw value and flag. Selection returned the revision in that round, so it is unaffected. D.1.2 Claw-Eval Claw-Eval evaluates autonomous agents on tasks with executable environments and human-checked rubrics [ Ye et al., 2026 , Claw-Eval Contributors, 2026 ] . It contains 300 tasks and 2,159 rubrics across nine categories (161 general, 101 multimodal, and 38 multi-turn tasks), provides sandboxed services, records trajectories, and distinguishes completion, safety, and robustness; its leaderboard uses Pass 3 \mathrm{Pass}^{3} over three independent trials. We use 104 frozen task versions from 68 scenarios, including 36 bilingual pairs, scored on the continuous 0–100 native scale with a custom model judge; a run passes at 75 (inclusive). Each task version is evaluated over three sequential rounds of task-only, revision, and hypothesis-guided candidates, yielding 936 graded round candidates. Baseline repetition and aggregation are defined in Section D.2 . Our empirical three-run success metrics follow the aggregation in Sections D.2 and D.4 ; the sequential rounds are dependent, unlike the leaderboard’s independent-trial protocol.
