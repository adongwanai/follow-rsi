However, practical mechanisms for skill ownership verification and leakage tracing remain underdeveloped. Watermark embedding before distribution offers a potential solution, but structured, compact skill instructions impose stringent requirements for factual and procedural accuracy. To address these challenges, we introduce SkillMarker, a KGW-based watermarking method that jointly targets functionality preservation and strong watermark embedding. SkillMarker unifies quality-constrained rewriting with signal-oriented candidate search. The former separates essential knowledge from flexible expression to preserve instruction fidelity; the latter explores alternative expressions to obtain a stronger watermark signal under preservation constraints. We release an open-source toolkit that integrates watermark embedding, quality auditing, and detection to support the protection of agent skills. SkillMarker targets ownership verification and leakage tracing in distributed skill ecosystems, where content may be copied in whole or in part, or incorporated into other skills. 1 Introduction Agent skills package domain expertise into reusable instructions and resources that agents load on demand to specialize their behavior without modifying model weights ( Zhang et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2026b ) . This encoded knowledge makes skills valuable assets for organizations and developers, while their reusable format allows that expertise to be deployed across tasks. However, distributing skill instructions in plaintext makes them easy to copy, redistribute, or incorporate into other skills. These risks motivate watermarking skill text before distribution to support ownership verification and leakage tracing, including when only fragments of the original skill remain. We therefore ask: Can we watermark the skill for ownership verification and leakage tracing without compromising its functionality? However, embedding a watermark into structured, compact skill instructions with stringent accuracy requirements poses two coupled challenges. C1: Preserving skill functionality during watermarking. Skill instructions encode precise operational requirements, so even minor changes to resource references, decision conditions, or required actions can change agent behavior. Their structured format and stringent accuracy requirements leave limited room for watermark embedding through rewriting. C2: Embedding a strong watermark under limited rewriting freedom. Compact skill instructions offer few opportunities to strengthen the watermark signal. Accuracy constraints further restrict alternative expressions: a weak logit bias may produce little watermark signal, while a stronger bias can change essential instructions. We introduce SkillMarker , a KGW-based watermarking method for agent skill ownership verification ( Kirchenbauer et al., 2023 ) . To address C1, we propose quality-constrained rewriting , which preserves protected content and constrains editable spans to preserve essential operational requirements. To address C2, we propose signal-oriented candidate search , which explores alternative expressions and selects candidates with stronger watermark signals among those that pass preservation checks. SkillMarker enables developers to verify skill ownership by embedding a keyed watermark before publishing a skill and later testing suspect text for a watermark signal. It also enables them to trace skill leakage by assigning distinct keys to distributed versions and testing suspect text for a watermark signal that can help identify its source version, including when instructions are copied in whole or incorporated into other skills. To support these applications, we also develop a toolkit that integrates watermark embedding, quality auditing, and detection. In summary, our contributions are threefold: • We propose SkillMarker, a KGW-based method that combines functionality preservation and strong watermark embedding for skill ownership verification and leakage tracing. • We study skill watermark performance under copy-paste and skill-merging scenarios, examining how these operations affect the watermark signal in reused content. • We develop an integrated toolkit for watermark embedding, quality auditing, and detection, supporting the assessment of both watermark signal strength and instruction fidelity. 2 Method Figure 2: Overview of SkillMarker. ① Quality-constrained rewriting preserves protected content, such as code and API identifiers, while rewriting editable spans. ② Signal-oriented candidate search ranks candidate rewrites by the green-token rate and uses lower perplexity to break ties. ③ Reconstruction preserves protected content, followed by independent quality auditing and detection in suspect text. 2.1 Problem Setup and Overview Let x x denote the Markdown body of an original skill. The toolkit provides independent quality auditing and supports both full-text and window-based watermark detection. 2.2 Quality-Constrained Rewriting Protecting literal content. To embed a watermark, we apply a logit bias that favors green-list tokens during rewriting. Without preservation constraints, rewriting can change executable code and invalidate a skill. For example, changing >= to > in the following code block changes when the retry limit is enforced: if attempts >= 3: raise RuntimeError Code and numerical thresholds allow little rewriting freedom, whereas surrounding prose often allows alternative expressions. We therefore parse the original skill and designate code, structural delimiters, and recognized literals as protected content that must remain unchanged. Regions with unresolved structure are also protected. We split the remaining prose into editable spans and protect tokens that cross the boundary of protected content. Editable spans below a threshold on the original token count remain unchanged. We rewrite one editable span at a time and then reconstruct the skill with protected content in its original order. Preserving instruction fidelity. Preserving protected content alone does not preserve a decision rule: changing all conditions to any condition changes behavior even if every threshold remains unchanged. We use in-context learning to make preservation constraints within editable spans concrete. The rewriting prompt combines explicit preservation instructions with paired input and rewrite examples. These examples show how to change wording while preserving the original language, facts, conditions, negations, numerical values, and technical identifiers. Preservation checks reject incomplete or empty candidates, unauthorized layout or syntax changes, and changes to protected content. Independent quality auditing then assesses language, semantics, completeness, and usability without exposing watermark scores to the reviewers. Downstream task performance is evaluated separately. Grouping token variants. Watermark embedding can favor token variants that disrupt formatting. For example, a hard watermark that blocks name but permits _name can produce an unwanted underscore and change Markdown formatting. A logit bias can likewise favor a token variant without excluding the original token. We therefore assign consistent green-list membership to token variants. Our implementation groups tokens that differ only in leading spaces or periods and assigns them a shared representative’s green-list membership; literal-underscore variants remain subject to preservation checks. Specifically, we sample a keyed subset of ⌊ γ ​ | 𝒱 | ⌋ \lfloor\gamma|\mathcal{V}|\rfloor vocabulary IDs and assign each representative’s membership to all tokens in its group. Applying a logit bias to Markdown symbols can favor formatting changes that disrupt skill structure. We therefore exclude designated formatting tokens from the logit bias. We therefore support both full-text and window-based detection using only the suspect text and watermark configuration. Window-based detection uses sliding windows to detect watermark signals within suspect text. The detector tokenizes the full suspect text once and reconstructs G t G_{t} from k k and each observed preceding token, using the same token-variant grouping as watermark embedding. It excludes E E and the first token. Without the positions of protected content, all remaining positions are eligible, including content that may have been protected during watermark embedding. For window-based detection, it scores overlapping windows of the same tokenization, preserving each token’s preceding token in the full suspect text, and applies a Bonferroni correction across the scanned windows. Repeated contexts and shared green-list membership can violate the binomial null assumptions; these probabilities therefore require independent calibration before being interpreted as empirical false-positive guarantees. For everyday/general scenarios, agent-skills ( Osmani, 2026 ) covers routine software engineering workflows, including planning, code review, testing, and debugging, while awesome-skills ( sickn33, 2026 ) provides a broader collection for writing, document processing, information retrieval, and data analysis. For professional scenarios, marketingskills ( Haines, 2026 ) focuses on marketing workflows such as content strategy, search engine optimization, advertising, and conversion optimization; skill-use-bench ( Han et al., 2026 ) provides executable tasks requiring specialized procedures, including software development, document processing, and scientific computation. We compare three settings: no skill, the original skill, and the watermarked skill. The updated runs use DeepSeek-V4-Flash through Open Code, with one DeepSeek-V4-Flash judgment per case. To reduce evaluation leakage and cross-setting contamination, we remove expected outputs from agent prompts and check each prompt for their absence; expected outputs remain available only to the judge. Grading files are kept off disk during agent execution and restored temporarily for subsequent scoring. We identify copies of the target skills using content fingerprints, remove them and their supporting resources from the evaluation environment, and restore only the skill version required by the current setting. Original and watermarked versions are never exposed simultaneously, and automatic skill discovery is disabled for the no-skill baseline. Each case runs in a separate temporary working directory, which is cleaned afterward to prevent artifacts from carrying over between cases. Execution traces are audited for unintended target-skill access, and contaminated baseline runs are rerun after cleanup. 3.2 Downstream Performance and Resource Usage Figure 3 reports pooled check pass rates, mean total token usage, and median runtime from the updated three-arm evaluation. We select three skills, from each source, we copy 256, 512, 1024, 1536, or 2048 tokens into an unwatermarked text containing 1024 or 4096 tokens before insertion. We vary the number of inserted fragments over { 1 , 2 , 4 , 8 } \{1,2,4,8\} . Within each condition, fragmentation splits the same copied content while preserving its total length and order. Three fixed seeds per source control the selection of copied fragments and insertion positions, yielding 360 watermarked documents and 360 paired unwatermarked controls. Detection and localization. Longer copied fragments generally increase detection rates, while window-based detection helps detect watermark signals diluted by the unwatermarked text. For example, when 2048 tokens are inserted contiguously into a 4096-token unwatermarked text, window-based detection reaches 100%, compared with 77.8% for full-text detection. Splitting the same content into eight fragments reduces window-based detection to 77.8%. Figure 5 shows that longer copied fragments also generally improve localization recall, whereas fragmentation reduces the localized fraction of the inserted content. For the same 2048-token copy into a 4096-token unwatermarked text, contiguous insertion yields 70.6% precision and 100% recall; eight fragments yield 36.8% precision and 56.2% recall. These results demonstrate detection and localization of copied content for the selected skills with strong watermark signals. Dispersed fragments can remain detectable even when they are only partially localized. This decline is not strictly monotonic for multi-scale detection, but both detectors retain lower detection rates with background skills than without them. Multi-scale detection helps recover some of the remaining evidence under this dilution. Both methods have the same observed false-positive rates on unwatermarked controls: 0.0%, 11.1%, and 16.7% for one, two, and four input skills. KGW and Unigram-Watermark bias token selection using context-dependent and fixed vocabulary partitions, respectively ( Kirchenbauer et al., 2023 ; Zhao et al., 2024 ) . Other approaches use tournament sampling (SynthID-Text), sentence-level rejection sampling (SemStamp), or optimal transport (textGrain) ( Dathathri et al., 2024 ; Hou et al., 2024 ; Li et al., 2026a ) . SWEET and STONE protect low-entropy positions and syntactic code tokens, respectively ( Lee et al., 2024 ; Kim et al., 2026 ) , while AgentMark watermarks planning decisions ( Huang et al., 2026 ) . Generation-time watermarking is typically deployed on the model-provider side to attribute generated outputs. SkillMarker instead enables skill developers to embed watermarks into reusable instructions before distribution, independently of the models that later execute them. Post-hoc watermarking. PostMark inserts semantically selected words, REMARK-LLM encodes binary signatures, and SrcMarker applies semantics-preserving code transformations ( Chang et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2024 ; Yang et al., 2024 ) . RedAct watermarks released agent traces through selective rewriting and behavioral signals ( Xu et al., 2026 ) ; SkillTrace audits skill reuse through expression, implementation, and operational traces ( Chen et al., 2026 ) . These methods establish watermarking and provenance auditing for existing artifacts, but semantic preservation in general text or released traces does not by itself establish instruction fidelity in skills. Skill protection must preserve operational requirements while embedding watermarks that provide sufficient evidence for attribution after reuse. SkillMarker addresses this joint requirement in distributed skill text; reliability studies of copy-paste attacks ( Kirchenbauer et al., 2024 ) motivate its evaluation under copy-paste and skill merging. 4.2 Text Steganography Neural Linguistic Steganography conceals recoverable messages through arithmetic coding ( Ziegler et al., 2019 ) ; zero-width characters offer invisible encoding but must survive redistribution. ICW induces output patterns through instructions, including covert triggers in modified documents ( Liu et al., 2026 ) . Its signal is carried by subsequent model outputs, whereas SkillMarker embeds watermarks directly into the distributed skill. Linguistic steganography focuses on concealed message recovery. Neither objective by itself establishes attribution of copied or merged skill instructions under strict functionality constraints. SkillMarker instead supports ownership verification and leakage tracing through statistical watermark signals under preservation constraints. 5 Conclusion We present SkillMarker, a KGW-based method for protecting distributed agent skills through quality-constrained rewriting and signal-oriented candidate search. Across four datasets, watermarked skills largely preserve downstream performance, with relative changes of − 2.61 -2.61 % to + 0.93 +0.93 % and dataset-dependent token overhead. Window-based detection improves detection of copied content diluted by unwatermarked text, while fragmentation weakens detection and localization. Watermarks remain detectable after merging with a limited number of background skills, although added background content weakens detection overall and elevated control false positives highlight the need for calibration. These findings support watermarking as a practical direction for skill ownership verification and leakage tracing.
