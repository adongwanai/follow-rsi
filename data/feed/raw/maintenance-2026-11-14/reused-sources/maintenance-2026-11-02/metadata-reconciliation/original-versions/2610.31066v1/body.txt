Abstract World Models (WMs) can support Multimodal Agents (MAs) through simulated experience and action-conditioned predictions, while MA interaction trajectories can provide feedback for refining WMs. Assessing this potential relationship requires evidence about both components. A pilot study of one MA–WM pairing on 150 cases across ten scenarios illustrates why coupled outcomes alone are insufficient for diagnosing failure. We introduce Dual Exam, a framework that evaluates MAs under a fixed environment response mechanism and WMs under fixed action interventions. The Agent evaluation compares 10 configurations on 1,088 items in 15 reporting categories across physical, digital, social, and scientific domains. The World Model evaluation comprises 400 cases across eight scenarios, with 50 cases per scenario; 15 of 16 configurations cover all eight scenarios, and one covers six. GPT 6 Astra attains the highest Agent Index of 31.3 under the selected case-count weights, while GPT-Image-2 leads the full-coverage WM Overall scores at 21.7. These track-specific scores use different tasks and metrics and are not directly comparable. Agent strengths remain category-specific, and the aggregate ordering depends on the domain mixture. The evaluations characterize task-specific execution and state-transition performance. They provide a basis for studying reliable MA–WM interaction, while whether these capabilities predict coupled performance or sustained reciprocal improvement remains to be tested. The task labels in this overview are illustrative; the evaluated Agent categories and counts are specified in Table 5 . 1 Introduction World Models (WMs) and Multimodal Agents (MAs) can improve each other in complementary ways. WMs support MAs through simulated experience and action-conditioned predictions ( Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018 ; Hafner et al., 2025 ; Gu et al., 2025 ) , while MA interaction trajectories provide feedback for refining WMs ( Zhang et al., 2026b ; Yu et al., 2026 ) . Recent work has coupled the two in specific settings ( Fang et al., 2025 ; Guo et al., 2025 ; Liu et al., 2026 ) . For example, a web agent can use a world model to anticipate candidate clicks, while its trajectories provide data for model refinement ( Fang et al., 2025 ) . Broader multimodal interaction requires both effective action selection and faithful responses to those actions. We assess these complementary requirements across physical, digital, social, and scientific domains ( Chu et al., 2026 ) . Our focus is task-specific component performance; the relationship between these measurements and gains from joint training requires separate validation. However, reciprocal improvement requires both MAs and WMs to remain reliable over repeated interaction. MAs still struggle with visual grounding, action sequencing, and sustained task completion ( Xie et al., 2024 ; Chen et al., 2025 ; Sun et al., 2026 ) . WMs also face challenges in memory consistency and action control ( Ye et al., 2026b ) . Visual fidelity or state similarity alone does not guarantee improved planning or successful task completion ( Shang et al., 2026a ; Huang et al., 2026 ) . These weaknesses become more apparent when MAs and WMs are coupled. The output of one repeatedly becomes the input to the other. WM-based simulation can improve planning ( Gu et al., 2025 ) , but the planning benefits depend on behavioral coverage, environment complexity, and whether the MA can use the predicted outcomes ( Li et al., 2025b ; Qian et al., 2026a ) . Model bias may also accumulate over repeated rollouts ( Janner et al., 2019 ) . End-to-end performance can show that the coupled system fails, but not why. Failures may come from MA decisions, WM responses, or their interaction. A component-level evaluation is therefore needed to characterize the capabilities that end-to-end outcomes alone cannot reveal. Our preliminary study tests one MA–WM pairing on 150 cases across ten scenarios, with 0% task success under the stated protocol (Section 2.1 ). The trajectories reveal state discrepancies and limited responses, but do not identify which component caused each failure. This motivates Dual Exam : controlled, separate assessments of MAs and WMs rather than causal attribution from coupled outcomes. The full inventory appears in Table 5 . 2.2 Dual Examination Framework Reliable MA–WM interaction requires both effective action selection and faithful action-conditioned state evolution. An Agent must choose actions that advance the task, while a WM must realize their consequences and preserve task-relevant state. These requirements are interdependent in coupled rollouts: WM outputs inform subsequent MA decisions, and MA actions determine the transitions the WM must generate. End-to-end outcomes therefore reflect both components and their interaction. Dual Exam examines these complementary requirements through two controlled evaluation tracks. The Agent track fixes the environment response mechanism to assess action selection and task completion, while the World Model track fixes action interventions to assess the resulting state evolution. Together, the tracks characterize capability gaps on both sides of the interaction loop. Specifically, the Agent track evaluates a candidate A θ A_{\theta} against a fixed reference environment response process: a t ∼ A θ ​ ( h t ) , o t + 1 ∗ = 𝒯 ∗ ​ ( s t , a t ) , a_{t}\sim A_{\theta}(h_{t}),\qquad o_{t+1}^{*}=\mathcal{T}^{*}(s_{t},a_{t}), where s t s_{t} is the reference environment state, h t h_{t} is the observation–action history available to the Agent, and o t + 1 ∗ o_{t+1}^{*} is the returned reference observation. 𝒯 ∗ \mathcal{T}^{*} denotes the fixed response mechanism, including observation generation; it need not expose the underlying state to the Agent. The Agent is responsible for action selection, verification, recovery, and task completion. The World Model track instead fixes a specification-defined intervention sequence a 1 : T ∗ a_{1:T}^{*} and evaluates the resulting observations: o ^ t + 1 = M ϕ ​ ( s ^ t , a t ∗ ) . \hat{o}_{t+1}=M_{\phi}(\hat{s}_{t},a_{t}^{*}). Here, s ^ t \hat{s}_{t} denotes the generated context carried forward by the WM interface, rather than an observed ground-truth state, and o ^ t + 1 \hat{o}_{t+1} is its next generated observation. The superscript ∗ * identifies reference inputs or responses. Holding a mechanism fixed controls its variation across candidates; it does not certify its correctness. Appendix C distinguishes recorded validation evidence from unresolved reliability checks. Both tracks assess task-relevant behavior against specified goals and constraints, while retaining track-specific cases, interaction protocols, and verification procedures. Table 2 compares this scope with representative Agent and World Model benchmarks. Shared specification, distinct measurements. Both tracks specify an initial condition, permitted intervention, required change, protected state, and evidence rule. For example, a specification can require moving an object into a particular container while preserving that container’s identity. In an Agent test, the reference environment executes the Agent’s chosen actions and the verifier checks the goal. In a WM test, a prescribed packing action is supplied and generated observations are checked for the requested change and preserved identity. This example explains how the same invariant can be expressed in both tracks; it does not report a matched pair of evaluated episodes. Shared specification principles do not imply shared tasks, numerical scores, or training data. Diagnostic scope. The two tracks identify component-level capability gaps under controlled conditions. They do not by themselves establish the cause of a particular coupled failure or whether joint training will yield sustained reciprocal improvement. These questions require matched interaction experiments and evaluation across successive training rounds. 2.3 Agent Examination: Control the World, Test the Agent The Agent Examination tests whether a candidate MA can complete tasks in a controlled, specification-consistent environment through observation interpretation, state reasoning, planning, action selection, progress verification, and error recovery. The fixed-intervention track tests whether valid action inputs produce valid state evolution. Its illustrative failures show that responding to the current instruction is only part of this requirement. Qwen-Image-3.0 restores the apparatus view following an instruction in the pendulum case (Appendix Figure 21 ), but introduces additional cups in the cup-stacking case, violating the initial quantity constraint (Appendix Figure 23 ). MiniMax H3 preserves the cup count yet introduces an unrequested red tray, whereas GPT-Image-2 preserves the original tray colors (Appendix Figure 24 ). These cases illustrate distinct failures in realizing requested changes and preserving unaffected state; they do not establish the causes of aggregate ranking differences. In coupled interaction, such state errors can become misleading observations for the Agent. Digital scenarios remain challenging across configurations. All evaluated configurations score at most 16.5 on Web and 13.3 on Game (Table 4 ). Even models with stronger results elsewhere remain weak in these scenarios: MiniMax H3 scores 53.8 on Embodiment but only 3.7 on Web and 8.5 on Game, while Qwen-Image-3.0 scores 46.1 on Daily Life but 10.7 on Web and 11.5 on Game. These results identify digital scenarios as a shared weakness under the evaluated protocol, although differences in task difficulty and scoring prevent attributing the gap solely to the domain. Scenario averages do not establish suitability for a partner Agent. GPT-Image-2 leads WM Overall with 21.7 versus 17.3 for MiniMax H3, but their ordering reverses on Embodiment, at 20.9 versus 53.8. WM Overall weights the eight scenarios equally, whereas a particular Agent may encounter a different distribution of states and actions. A higher overall score therefore need not imply more reliable responses along that Agent’s trajectories. Scenario profiles narrow this uncertainty, but selecting an effective Agent–World Model pairing requires evaluation on the interactions the pair actually produces. Joint perspective. Reliable task progress requires both effective Agent decisions and World Model responses that correctly realize action effects while preserving task-relevant state, since these responses also inform the Agent’s judgment of progress. The two tracks assess these complementary capabilities, but do not establish how their scores jointly predict coupled success. 4 Discussion 4.1 Related Work Existing benchmarks typically assess one component. Agent benchmarks measure tool use, collaboration, workplace execution, transferable skills, and long-horizon computer operation ( Liu et al., 2024 ; Yao et al., 2024 ; Barres et al., 2025 ; Xu et al., 2024 ; Li et al., 2026 ; Sun et al., 2026 ) , whereas World Model benchmarks evaluate prediction, embodied functionality, latent dynamics, memory, action control, and behavioral consistency ( Shang et al., 2026b ; Chen et al., 2025 ; Vafa et al., 2024 ; Ye et al., 2026b ; Huang et al., 2026 ) . These benchmarks assess competence, not reciprocal improvement. Prior work supports both directions. Dyna and learned World Models use simulated trajectories to improve planning and policies ( Sutton, 1990 ; Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018 ; Hafner et al., 2025 ; Gu et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2026 ) . Conversely, Agent-directed exploration and policy rollouts improve held-out World Model learning or adapt simulators ( Kim et al., 2020 ; Chebotar et al., 2019 ) , while recent methods refine World Models from interaction feedback ( Zhang et al., 2026b ; Yu et al., 2026 ) . DreamZero jointly predicts video and actions for robot control and reports transfer to new tasks and embodiments ( Ye et al., 2026a ) . Such results support studying action-conditioned visual models in embodied systems; they do not validate our cross-domain rankings or establish a reciprocal training loop. These gains are demonstrated within bounded task distributions. Narrow-domain systems begin to close the loop. WebEvolver ( Fang et al., 2025 ) couples Agent experience with an evolving World Model, GenEnv co-evolves LLM Agents and environment simulators, and COMAP alternates World Model and policy updates ( Fang et al., 2025 ; Guo et al., 2025 ; Liu et al., 2026 ) . 4.2 Toward Agent–World Model Co-evolution World Models can improve Agents through simulated experience, as DreamerV3 demonstrates across diverse control tasks ( Hafner et al., 2025 ) . Such benefits depend on controlling model bias: MBPO uses short simulated rollouts to support policy learning ( Janner et al., 2019 ) . The relevant test is whether this experience improves Agent behavior beyond the model used for training. Agents can, in turn, improve World Models by collecting informative interaction data. Directed exploration improves predictive model learning ( Kim et al., 2020 ) , while SimOpt uses real policy rollouts to refine simulator parameter distributions and improve policy transfer ( Chebotar et al., 2019 ) . These updates improve the model or simulator; the Agent’s task score alone does not measure the collected data’s training value. Connecting these directions offers a route to co-evolution, but separate gains do not establish a stable loop. WebEvolver links them by training a World Model on Agent trajectories and using it for policy training and look-ahead planning ( Fang et al., 2025 ) . We hypothesize that reciprocal improvement across broader multimodal tasks may become sustainable once both components reach sufficient task-specific competence, but neither this transition nor the required capability levels has been established. Dual Exam diagnoses component capabilities rather than co-evolution itself. Our observed execution and state-consistency failures identify possible obstacles to useful interaction, not evidence of limited learnability. In the packing case, commands continue after the container changes (Figure 2 ); this observation motivates diagnosis but does not test whether either component can learn from the interaction. Future work should compare joint updates with Agent-only, World-Model-only, and no-update controls, using fixed held-out tests across rounds and seeds to track both components and closed-loop task success. This would test whether both components improve over successive rounds and whether these gains translate into more reliable joint task performance. Implications for system design. The observed cases suggest concrete targets for subsequent experiments. Invalid routes and action orders motivate explicit checks of global constraints before committing to a sequence. Changes in container identity, object count, or unrelated scene attributes motivate verification that requested updates preserve protected state. Resource-allocation examples motivate checks of joint constraints rather than individual choices alone. These are hypotheses motivated by illustrative failures, not established explanations of aggregate performance. Their utility should be tested through controlled interventions on the same cases, measuring both strict task completion and any separately defined intermediate progress. 4.3 Evaluation Limitations Evaluation limitations. The reported scores depend on task selection, reference environments, verification rules, and configuration-specific execution settings. Fixed conditions reduce one source of variation but do not establish that the reference mechanism is error-free. The current presentation does not report an independent, stratified agreement estimate for the World Model output scores. The packing audit is additionally retrospective: its rubric was specified after inspecting the trajectory, and its labels have not been independently adjudicated. These limits should be addressed by documenting environment validation, auditing scored outputs independently, and reporting disagreements, ambiguous checks, and invalid-run counts. Such evidence is needed to quantify measurement reliability beyond the illustrative examples. Scientific aggregation and evidence coverage. The Scientific scores combine binary repair and checkpoint outcomes with graded optimization results. Our clipping of Borden and DABIC to [ 0,100 ] [0,100] is a Dual Exam aggregation convention, not an official EdgeBench normalization rule; a shared range does not equate task difficulty. We retain valid Edge submissions recorded within the selected run’s allowance of at most 43,200 seconds, including submissions before early termination. This does not imply that every run completed the full horizon. The supplied trace archive is truncated: the four added configurations’ Wireless endpoints were checked against their best-submission judge reports, but the remaining added-task endpoints and evaluator-version equivalence have not all been independently verified. Execution harnesses also differ across configurations. Appendix A.13 preserves raw objectives and details these evidence limits. Limits of the comparisons. The leaderboard reports descriptive point scores, without repeated-run uncertainty estimates or significance tests. Differences between configurations therefore do not establish statistically significant superiority. Original category means are available only to one decimal place, so derived aggregates retain this baseline precision limit. The separate tracks also do not establish how component scores predict coupled performance. Action-sensitive validation would benefit from controls such as unchanged-frame, no-op, and shuffled-intervention outputs, with scoring targets defined in advance for each control. Matched interaction experiments and repeated evaluations are needed to test robustness, partner suitability, and the effect of corrective feedback. These questions are distinct from the descriptive rankings reported here. Unperformed controls and next tests. We have not run copy-last-frame, no-op, or shuffled-action baselines for the present WM scores. A copy-last-frame control should be scored against the original requested changes and protected-state checks separately; a true no-op intervention should instead use an identity-transition target, and shuffled interventions require targets derived from the shuffled sequence. These controls test different failure modes and should not share an incompatible target. Likewise, budget curves and correction-versus-repetition tests require matched cases and equal resources. Existing multi-attempt illustrations are descriptive and do not substitute for such controls. A stratified independent output audit remains necessary before claiming calibrated judge reliability. 5 Conclusion Dual Exam evaluates Multimodal Agents under fixed environment response mechanisms and World Models under fixed action interventions, producing complementary performance profiles across four domains. The Agent evaluation compares 10 configurations on 1,088 items in 15 reporting categories; GPT 6 Astra achieves the highest case-weighted Index of 31.3. The World Model evaluation contains 400 cases across eight scenarios, with 50 cases per scenario. GPT-Image-2 leads the full-coverage WM Overall scores at 21.7. Configuration-specific coverage is reported separately, and the two tracks use different tasks and metrics, so their aggregate scores are not directly comparable. Scientific evaluation spans 109 items and combines strict repair and checkpoint outcomes with graded optimization scores; Astra leads its within-domain aggregate at 50.6. Category leaders differ, and equal-domain weighting changes the overall ordering below the leading configuration. The results characterize model–harness performance under the evaluated specifications and weighting choices, subject to the documented evidence and precision limits. Predicting coupled success and demonstrating sustained reciprocal improvement require matched interaction and learning experiments beyond these separate assessments. A.1 Domain Selection, Task Categories, and Interpretation Purpose of the classification. We organize the agent evaluation using three levels: domains, task categories, and individual evaluation items. The four domains provide complementary settings for examining goal-directed behavior: Physical concerns actions and constraints in physical scenes; Digital concerns interaction with software interfaces; Social concerns decisions involving other agents and shared resources; and Scientific concerns technical workflows with checkable scientific or computational deliverables. This is an operational organization of the selected benchmark tasks, rather than an exhaustive taxonomy of intelligence or evidence that the domains measure independent latent abilities. In particular, scientific tasks can use digital interfaces, and physical tasks can involve both planning and manipulation. Category granularity. Physical categories emphasize task demands, Digital categories emphasize interaction environments, Social categories distinguish strategic interaction and resource-governance settings, and Scientific categories organize subject matter. We therefore use task categories , rather than treating all category labels as equivalent, mutually exclusive cognitive capabilities. The mixed organizing criteria support interpretable reporting of this inventory but limit claims about universal capability coverage. Appendix D Preliminary Study Protocol and Packing-Case Diagnostics Study configuration. The preliminary study uses the same model pairing throughout: GPT-5.6 Sol selects actions, and Seedance 2.5 generates the resulting observations. The 150 cases are evenly distributed across Minecraft, Daily Life, Embodied Tasks, Web UI, Driving, Desktop Software, Industrial Systems, Natural Ecosystems, Scientific Experiments, and Social Interaction, with 15 cases per scenario. Excluded runs. Initialization failures and technical failures are excluded from the reported 150 cases and from the task-success denominator; they are not counted as task failures. The reported 0% success rate therefore corresponds to zero successes among the 150 included cases, rather than among all attempted runs. Interaction budget. Each case is run once, with a maximum of 20 interaction steps and no episode-level retries. Repeating or revising an action within the ongoing trajectory is distinct from restarting a case and consumes the same step budget. The navigation retries discussed below are such within-trajectory actions, not additional evaluation attempts. Response versus repair. Agent Corrective Response records a targeted action following a visible discrepancy, not whether that action restores the required state. Successful repair would require separate evidence from subsequent observations that the same discrepancy has been resolved; task success additionally requires the complete goal and all constraints. Thus the 25.0% response rate for Social Interaction is a case-averaged behavioral rate, not a repair-success rate or the fraction of successful tasks. It cannot be converted into event counts without the underlying case-level denominators. Differences in discrepancy types and response opportunities also prevent interpreting a higher response rate as stronger recovery ability. The current aggregate results do not establish which responses produce repairs, and response behavior alone is insufficient as an optimization objective: ineffective responses may still receive credit. Evaluating repair outcomes and checking credited responses against the original discrepancies are needed to assess this risk. Evidence and scope.
