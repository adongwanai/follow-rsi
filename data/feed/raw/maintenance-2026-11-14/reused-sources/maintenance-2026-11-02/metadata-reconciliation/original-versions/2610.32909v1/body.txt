\code https://github.com/HorizonRobotics/HoloMotion/tree/master/holoboxing \calmregular HoloBoxing: A Simple Recipe for Humanoid Boxing with 7 Minutes of Single-GPU Training \calmregular Xihan Ma Equal contribution. \calmregular Bo Zhang Equal contribution. \calmregular Boxiong Li \calmregular Yuhan Bao \calmregular Ziyue Liu \calmregular Yucheng Wang Abstract HoloBoxing presents a simple recipe for learning humanoid boxing by building on a pretrained whole-body controller. The recipe separates reusable motor skills from task decisions: a compact planner requests future body-point targets, while the frozen controller handles joint-level execution. A learned decoder maps 32-dimensional actions to structured target windows, and measured-state imitation initializes the planner before reinforcement learning. Task learning then refines interaction behavior while reusing the decoder and controller, avoiding the need to relearn whole-body control. This combination keeps task optimization focused on a compact upper policy and supplies an initial behavior before reward-driven exploration. Starting from that available controller, the recipe accounts for about seven minutes of additional training, including reused representation fitting and a single-GPU task run. This excludes controller pretraining, data preparation, evaluation, and search. Humanoid boxing adds an opponent, changing contact, and the need to remain upright after an exchange. Learning all of these behaviors from joint commands couples motor execution with task decisions. HoloBoxing instead asks how far a simple, short training recipe can go when whole-body motor capability is already available. Reusable whole-body controllers make task adaptation above existing motor capability a practical starting point. The recipe separates what must be learned for boxing from what can be reused. A pretrained HoloMotion v2 controller executes movement requests and remains frozen. A learned target decoder represents short windows of future body-point poses with a 32-dimensional action. A compact feedback planner selects these actions from measured robot state and opponent positions. Task learning therefore adapts movement requests while retaining the underlying execution capability. We initialize this planner through measured-state imitation before refining it with reinforcement learning. The fixed controller first tracks reference motions to collect actual robot observations and future-target labels. Supervised learning turns those pairs into an initial feedback policy. Standing warmup and interaction against a fixed standing opponent then adjust behavior through rewards. The decoder and lower controller are reused throughout, as illustrated in Figure 1 . These choices make the training problem smaller in three concrete ways. Controller reuse removes joint-level motor learning from task adaptation. The target representation reduces the planner’s action dimension from 225 coordinates to 32 latent variables. Imitation supplies an initial behavior before reward-driven exploration. Together, they define the simple recipe whose training cost and resulting behavior we evaluate. The primary recipe accounts for 7.0 minutes, combining a new single-RTX-4090 run with previously recorded target-representation fitting. A 21.4-minute long version provides a higher-budget comparison. Simulation experiments assess whether this short adaptation produces interactive boxing, while matched-budget controls remove imitation initialization or the target decoder. We also test observation choices and representation quality to explain the interfaces used by the recipe. Detailed timings and competitive outcomes appear in the experiments; assumptions and remaining validation needs are discussed separately. \calmregular 2 \calmregular Background and motivation Humanoid boxing combines motor execution with decisions about approaching, striking, and responding to an opponent. Our focus is the additional learning needed when a general-purpose controller already supplies whole-body movement. Three established ideas motivate a simple task-adaptation recipe. Reuse motor capability. Motion imitation learns physically simulated movement from reference trajectories ( Peng et al., 2018 ; Luo et al., 2023 ) . Recent humanoid systems extend this approach toward reusable whole-body control ( Fu et al., 2024 ; Luo et al., 2025 ; Chen et al., 2026a ) . A task policy can request movement while a fixed controller converts those requests and robot feedback into joint commands. HoloBoxing uses the released HoloMotion v2 controller in this role. The boxing planner therefore learns task decisions above existing motor capability, rather than relearning joint-level execution. This separation also defines the cost boundary: we measure new task training and exclude the inherited controller’s pretraining. The task-specific problem is consequently to choose useful movement requests in response to an opponent. This differs from acquiring a broad motor repertoire and motivates evaluating adaptation time separately from controller development. Represent movement requests compactly. Latent representations organize reusable movements and skills ( Peng et al., 2022 ; Tessler et al., 2023 ; Zhu et al., 2023 ) , including spherical representations for state-conditioned motor control ( Tan et al., 2026 ) . HoloBoxing learns a decoder from a normalized 32-dimensional action to 225 future body-point target coordinates. This gives the planner a compact action interface while retaining the same lower controller. The representation supplies target structure, while the controller supplies motor feedback. Since reconstruction quality alone does not establish physical executability, we evaluate both target error and tracking behavior. The decoder and controller play different roles: one structures future requests, while the other responds to the robot’s current physical state. Keeping these roles separate lets the task policy reuse both interfaces as learning progresses. Initialize before learning interaction. Imitation supplies an initial behavior, and reinforcement learning adjusts it through executed interaction. Sports and boxing systems combine motion knowledge with task objectives in several forms ( Ren et al., 2025 ; Chen et al., 2026b ; Zhang et al., 2026 ; Wang et al., 2026 ; Luo et al., 2024 ; Yin et al., 2026 ) . Adapting frozen motor models also provides a reusable basis for new behaviors ( Vainshtein et al., 2026 ; Park et al., 2026 ) . HoloBoxing initializes its planner from measured robot observations collected during reference tracking, then refines it through standing warmup and interaction rewards. The decoder and whole-body controller remain frozen throughout task learning. Measured observations matter because the executing robot can deviate from the reference trajectory. Training the planner on these states connects its initial behavior to the feedback available during interaction. Reinforcement learning can then focus on adapting that behavior to task rewards. Together, these choices confine task adaptation to a compact feedback planner with an initial behavior and a reusable execution interface. The experiments measure the training cost and boxing behavior of this combined recipe. Component controls examine its ingredients without assuming a separate causal speedup from each one. \calmregular 3 \calmregular HoloBoxing The recipe learns task decisions above a fixed motor controller. The planner, value estimator, and style discriminator train during task learning. The target decoder and HoloMotion controller stay frozen. The value estimator predicts future reward, and the discriminator compares simulated motion with reference examples. Reinforcement learning therefore refines task decisions while retaining the target representation and motor capability. \calmregular 4 \calmregular Evaluating short task adaptation The evaluation asks whether the reused hierarchy produces interactive boxing after the recorded short training procedure. It measures training time, competitive behavior, and the effects of removing task-policy components. These measurements address different parts of the recipe: time quantifies adaptation cost, while behavior tests what that budget achieves. All evaluated policies share the packaged robot, actuator settings, arena, simulator, and referee. They retain different observation definitions, so the league is not an observation-controlled comparison of methods. A valid hand contact scores a point, while a sustained fall can end the bout with a knockout. We retain contacts and falls alongside match outcomes because an opponent can lose without receiving a scored punch. Every bout lasts at most sixty seconds. A fixed ten-round league gives each policy the same opponent coverage, with randomized sides and recorded initial-state seeds. Elo summarizes outcomes within this frozen participant pool; component controls provide comparisons within the HoloBoxing recipe. Recorded states, actions, and referee events allow replay without rerunning simulation. HoloBoxing assigns motor execution, target structure, and task decisions to separate components. The pretrained controller supplies feedback-based movement execution, while the decoder turns compact actions into requests that controller can follow. Measured-state imitation connects robot observations to those requests before task rewards refine the planner. The same interfaces persist across stages, so task adaptation reuses the decoder and controller instead of rebuilding their capabilities. Why short adaptation is possible. The recipe confines task learning to the upper policy and its training objectives. A 32-dimensional action replaces direct prediction of 225 future-target coordinates, and supervised examples provide the initial feedback behavior. The recorded cost shows that representation fitting and initialization occupy a small part of the selected training path. Most recorded training time is spent on standing and interaction, after reusable execution and target structure are already available. This explains the allocation of work; it does not establish an isolated causal speedup for each design choice. What the experiments show. The seven-minute training path produces scored boxing interaction and ranks second in the evaluated pool. The 21.4-minute long version ranks first. With 128 final-stage updates, the 7-min version yields stronger league outcomes than either component removal. The representation studies also show why a low reconstruction loss alone is insufficient: the selected targets must remain physically executable. Together, the experiments assess how the recipe’s interfaces and initialization support the behavior obtained within its budget. Limitations and future work. The study uses a small motion corpus and one selected task-training seed. The reported time measures additional learning above an available general-purpose controller, excluding its pretraining, preparation, and search. The primary learner also reuses a task-specific standing opponent whose original training is excluded. This is a task-adaptation comparison; methods may inherit different motor capabilities and representations. Independent seeds and matched timing studies are needed to isolate component effects on learning speed. The direct-target control changes action dimension and exploration geometry along with removing the decoder. The league is not a fully controlled comparison: methods retain different state encodings, history, and auxiliary inputs. Our upper planner retains 78 observation features, but the frozen lower controller also consumes robot feedback. A smaller upper-policy input therefore does not establish a smaller information budget for the complete system. External policies also receive no destination fine-tuning. League outcomes consequently describe these checkpoint systems rather than establish method superiority. Our efficiency result is minute-scale additional training above reusable motor control; the available budgets do not establish a universally fastest method. Terminal wins also require contact and fall statistics because a knockout can follow an opponent’s unforced fall. Future work should broaden motion and reset coverage and test alternative lower controllers. The current experiments are simulation-only; real-robot boxing and transfer remain untested. \calmregular 7 \calmregular Conclusion HoloBoxing combines a pretrained whole-body controller, a compact target representation, measured-state imitation, and task reinforcement learning. Its simplicity comes from separating motor execution from task decisions and reusing the controller and decoder throughout task adaptation. The latent target interface reduces the planner’s action dimension, while imitation supplies an initial behavior before reward-driven interaction. The primary recipe accounts for about seven minutes of additional training above an available controller. A 21.4-minute long version improves league outcomes at greater training cost. Boxing experiments demonstrate scored interaction, and matched-budget controls examine the roles of initialization and target representation. The study supports this combined recipe under the measured conditions; it does not isolate each component’s contribution to training speed. Appendix D League rules and opponent interfaces All six current participants share the packaged robot, actuator parameters, arena, simulator, and contact referee. Only the current ten-round, 150-bout league contributes to the reported ranking. Scoring and ratings. Legal hand contacts against the opponent’s head or torso, including pelvis targets, accumulate normal impulse at each 5 ms physics step. A 5 N ⋅ \cdot s threshold within 20 ms scores one point, consumes that impulse, and starts a 200 ms cooldown for that hand. Ground contacts and foot strikes do not score. Pelvis or torso height below 0.30 m above the ring starts a fall timer. A continuous three-second fall confirms a knockout, awards the opponent 1000 points, and ends the bout. Earlier observed falls determine knockout precedence; simultaneous confirmed falls produce a draw even when hit scores differ. Otherwise, the 60-second timeout uses score, with equal scores producing a draw. Each round covers all fifteen unordered pairs once, with randomized sides and recorded initial-state perturbations and sampling seeds.
