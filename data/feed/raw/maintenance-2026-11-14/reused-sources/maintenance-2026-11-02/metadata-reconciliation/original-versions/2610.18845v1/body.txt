For each new task, experts design a training recipe and tune it against a benchmark. We show that this loop can be handed to an agent. Any verifiable task can be cast as a harness and a verifier , where the harness defines what the agent may use and the verifier defines what counts as success. Every EO task can be made verifiable, through labeled benchmarks, independent sensors, physical models or the repeated observation of the same place. Given only these two components, a general-purpose coding agent builds the EO model and improves it recursively on its own outputs. Settings long treated as separate research problems, from learning with a handful of labels to adapting across domains, become choices of harness and verifier. On four EO benchmarks spanning optical, SAR and multispectral time-series imagery, agent-built models surpass the state of the art and keep most of that accuracy from about 1% of the training labels. The resulting models surpass the state of the art on four benchmarks ( Table 2 ). 1 Introduction Earth observation (EO) models are still built by hand. Each new sensor, region or task starts another round of engineering, in which experts choose a backbone, design a training recipe and tune it against a benchmark. Foundation models have raised the starting point of this cycle without removing it Cong et al. (2022) ; Reed et al. (2023) ; Guo et al. (2024) ; Xiong et al. (2024) ; Jakubik et al. (2025) ; Siméoni et al. (2026) , and on PANGAEA they do not consistently outperform a supervised UNet Marsocci et al. (2026) ; Xiao et al. (2025) . Across the many sensors, regions and label taxonomies of EO, expert engineering time decides how many tasks receive a strong model. The cost is highest where EO matters most. When a disaster strikes, a new satellite comes online, or a wildfire, flood or forest loss must be mapped in a new region, a strong model is needed within days, yet expert engineering takes weeks to months and many agencies and countries have no machine learning team at all. Meanwhile, AI has advanced fastest wherever success can be checked automatically. Verifiers drive progress in mathematical reasoning Cobbe et al. (2021) ; Guo et al. (2025) and program discovery Romera-Paredes et al. (2024) ; Novikov et al. (2025) , and coding agents such as Claude Code and Codex now write, run and debug training code for hours, earning medals in machine learning competitions Jiang et al. (2025) ; Chan et al. (2025) and improving training scripts against a validation metric Karpathy (2026) . EO agents have so far orchestrated fixed, human-built models and tools Singh et al. (2024b) ; Liu et al. (2024) ; Feng et al. (2026) , and a recent agentic research loop refines fully supervised baselines by one to three points Kang et al. (2026) . The EO task itself has not yet been handed to an agent. Our premise is that any verifiable task can be cast as a harness and a verifier. The harness defines what the agent may use, such as data, pretrained checkpoints, libraries and compute, and the verifier defines what counts as success by scoring the agent’s model ( Fig. 1 ). Every EO task can be made verifiable, and EO offers unusually many ways to do so. A decade of community benchmarks scores models on labeled held-out data, independent sensors and in-situ measurements check retrieved quantities, physical models check consistency with the observations, and repeated observation of the same place checks any prediction about its future. Building an EO model then becomes optimization against a verifier, and settings long treated as separate research problems become choices of these two components. Learning from a handful of labels restricts the labeled data in the harness, label-budget search asks for the smallest training set that passes a target score, and domain adaptation places the verifier on a target domain. We call this formulation RSI4EO. Given a harness and a verifier, a general-purpose coding agent builds the model by itself. It writes training code, trains models, queries the verifier and decides what to try next, offline and without human input. The verifier is sealed and returns a score, and containerized execution keeps the held-out data and the network out of the agent’s reach Harbor Framework Team (2026) ; Merrill et al. (2026) ; Shi et al. (2026) . The agent improves the EO model recursively on its own outputs Yudkowsky (2013) ; Zelikman et al. (2024) and designs this recursion itself. With six labeled images, it runs eight generations of self-training, each taught by the best pipeline the verifier has seen, and ends by automating the loop with its own scripts. Because the two components specify the task completely, any agent can receive it. Agents equipped this way surpass the state of the art on all four benchmarks with published results, land-cover mapping Xia et al. (2023) , building damage assessment from optical and SAR imagery Chen et al. (2025) , SAR ship instance segmentation Wei et al. (2020) and crop-type mapping from Sentinel-2 time series Sainte Fare Garnot and Landrieu (2021) , each within 12 hours on four GB200 GPUs, and the same agent builds a surface-material classifier from hyperspectral pixels Thoreau et al. (2024) . With labels for 0.1 to 1.4% of the training set they keep most of that accuracy, and on OpenEarthMap six labeled tiles yield 60.0 test mIoU. On OpenEarthMap, 300 tiles selected without labels suffice to surpass every published method trained on all labels, and two agents that build their models in very different ways both surpass the published state of the art. These results point to a new paradigm for EO, in which people design harnesses and verifiers and agents build the models. A task’s harness and verifier stay fixed while agents improve, so EO models improve as agents do, without new human engineering. The time from a new need to a working model shrinks to the agent’s budget. After a disaster, responders can label a handful of image pairs from the affected area, pose them as a task and receive a damage model within a day. The few-label BRIGHT task, with one labeled image pair per disaster event, is a direct instance of this scenario. Wildfire, flood and deforestation mapping follow the same pattern, and a newly launched satellite can have a model within a day of its first labeled images. Above all, any agency or country that can define a task can obtain a model at the level of the published state of the art, whether or not it has a machine learning team. Research questions move upstream to which verifiers capture a task faithfully and what a harness must contain, and annotation moves from training sets to the held-out sets behind the verifier, which every benchmark already labels. Our contributions are threefold. • A Paradigm for Building EO Models. Any verifiable task becomes a harness and a verifier, which turns the many verification sources of EO into objectives that agents optimize directly and unifies learning settings as choices of these two components. • An Open EO Task Suite. We release the tasks of this paper in this form, covering optical, SAR, optical–SAR, multispectral time-series and hyperspectral imagery with pixel-level and instance-level outputs, runnable by any agent in offline Harbor containers. • Evidence across Tasks, Labels and Agents. Agent-built models surpass the state of the art on four benchmarks, keep 69 to 84% of that accuracy from 0.2 to 1.4% of the labels, and on OpenEarthMap beat every published full-label method from 11% of the labels. 2 Related Work Self-Improvement and Verifiers. The idea of machines that design better machines goes back to Good (1965) and was formalized in the Gödel machine Schmidhuber (2007) . Language models now realize parts of this program. STOP uses a language model to improve the scaffold that calls it Zelikman et al. (2024) , Gödel Agent and the Darwin Gödel Machine rewrite their own agent code Yin et al. (2025) ; Zhang et al. (2026) , and FunSearch and AlphaEvolve evolve programs against automatic evaluators Romera-Paredes et al. (2024) ; Novikov et al. (2025) . Self-training lets a model improve on its own outputs, from Noisy Student in vision Xie et al. (2020) to STaR and ReST EM in language Zelikman et al. (2022) ; Singh et al. (2024a) and AlphaGo Zero in games Silver et al. (2017) , and in RSI4EO the agent arrives at such loops for the EO model without being told to. Progress in this line follows the availability of verifiers Sutton (2001) ; Wei (2025) , from trained verifiers for mathematics Cobbe et al. (2021) ; Lightman et al. (2024) to reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards Lambert et al. (2025) ; Guo et al. (2025) , and recent surveys and roadmaps chart the path toward recursive self-improvement in general AI systems Gao et al. (2026a) ; Chen et al. (2026) ; Duan et al. (2026) . RSI4EO carries this verifier-driven view into a scientific domain, where verifiers range from labeled benchmarks to the repeated observation of the same place and the artifact that improves recursively is the EO model. Agents for Machine Learning Engineering. AutoML has automated parts of model development for over a decade, through hyperparameter optimization Feurer et al. (2015) ; Li et al. (2018) ; Falkner et al. (2018) ; Akiba et al. (2019) , neural architecture search Zoph and Le (2017) ; Liu et al. (2019) ; Real et al. (2020) and self-configuring pipelines such as nnU-Net Isensee et al. (2021) , each searching a space that people define. LLM agents widen this space to arbitrary code. AIDE, The AI Scientist and autoresearch write and revise training code against validation metrics Jiang et al. (2025) ; Lu et al. (2026) ; Karpathy (2026) , and MLAgentBench, MLE-bench and RE-Bench measure such agents on engineering and research tasks Huang et al. (2024) ; Chan et al. (2025) ; Wijk et al. (2025) . Domain systems apply these loops to medical image segmentation Gao et al. (2026b) ; Liu et al. (2026) and solar-panel mapping Lekavicius et al. (2026) . RSI4EO differs in how a task reaches the agent, as a containerized task in the format of recent agent evaluation suites Merrill et al. (2026) ; Shi et al. (2026) . The harness and a sealed verifier specify it completely, so label budgets and domain shift become part of the task definition and validation data stay outside the agent’s reach, a design that reward-hacking studies of frontier agents support Von Arx et al. (2025) . Earth Observation Models and Agents. EO foundation models learn transferable representations from optical, multispectral and SAR archives Cong et al. (2022) ; Reed et al. (2023) ; Fuller et al. (2023) ; Jakubik et al. (2023) ; Guo et al. (2024) ; Xiong et al. (2024) ; Tseng et al. (2025) ; Astruc et al. (2025) ; Jakubik et al. (2025) , and general-purpose backbones such as DINOv3 now ship satellite-pretrained variants Oquab et al. (2024) ; Siméoni et al. (2026) , with GEO-Bench and PANGAEA evaluating them under fixed protocols Lacoste et al. (2023) ; Marsocci et al. (2026) . Label-scarce and cross-domain settings have each produced dedicated methods, including semi-supervised segmentation Yang et al. (2023) ; Yang et al. (2025) ; Lu et al. (2023) ; Zhou et al. (2026) and unsupervised domain adaptation Hoyer et al. (2022) ; Hoyer et al. (2023) , also on LoveDA Wang et al. (2021) ; Zhao et al. (2024) . In RSI4EO these models and methods become ingredients that the agent selects, combines and trains. LLM agents for EO have so far orchestrated fixed tools and pretrained models Singh et al. (2024b) ; Liu et al. (2024) ; Feng et al. (2026) ; Xu et al. (2026) ; Talemi et al. (2026) , and OpenEarth-Agent extends them to creating their own tools Zhao et al. (2026) . RingMoClaw is a multi-agent loop that retrains remote sensing models and improves fully supervised baselines on four tasks Kang et al. (2026) . RSI4EO hands an agent the whole EO task, including its label budget and domain shift, and extends agent-built models to label-scarce regimes. 3 Method RSI4EO turns the construction of an EO model into a task that an agent solves. Section 3.1 defines a task by its harness and verifier, Sections 3.2 and 3.3 describe the two components as we instantiate them, Section 3.4 describes how the agent improves the model on its own outputs, and Section 3.5 shows how learning settings become choices of the two components. 3.1 Tasks as a Harness and a Verifier An EO task is a pair 𝒯 = ( ℋ , 𝒱 ) \mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{V}) . The harness ℋ \mathcal{H} fixes the resources the agent may use: labeled data 𝒟 L \mathcal{D}_{L} , unlabeled data 𝒟 U \mathcal{D}_{U} , pretrained checkpoints, software and a compute budget. 2 2 2 We use harness for this task-side environment. It is distinct from the agent harness, the scaffold that turns a language model into an agent. The verifier 𝒱 \mathcal{V} fixes what counts as success. The agent produces pipelines, where a pipeline π \pi is a self-contained directory of code and trained weights that maps input imagery to predictions. The verifier runs a pipeline on held-out data ( X V , Y V ) (X_{V},Y_{V}) that the agent never sees and returns its score s ⁡ ( π ) = m ⁡ ( π ⁡ ( X V ) , Y V ) , s(\pi)=m\big(\pi(X_{V}),\,Y_{V}\big), (1) where m m is the task metric. Over its budget the agent writes pipelines π 1 , … , π K \pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{K} , queries s ⁡ ( π k ) s(\pi_{k}) and decides what to try next. When the budget ends, its final pipeline π ⋆ \pi^{\star} is evaluated once on a hidden test set with the same metric, m ⁡ ( π ⋆ ​ ( X T ) , Y T ) m(\pi^{\star}(X_{T}),Y_{T}) . The formulation leaves every modeling decision to the agent. Architecture, losses, the use of unlabeled data, ensembling and inference all belong to the pipeline the agent writes, and the verifier is the only signal of success it receives. A task is fully specified by its two components, so any agent can receive it, and changing either component defines a new task. 3.2 Verifier The verifier is sealed. It runs in a separate container that holds the validation data, executes a submitted pipeline exactly as the final grader will, and returns the score through a tool call. The agent never receives validation images or labels. Because the verifier runs the whole pipeline, it scores what will be graded, including preprocessing, ensembling and test-time augmentation, and it exposes failures such as missing outputs before the final evaluation. The agent may query it as often as it likes. EO is rich in sources for such verifiers. Labeled benchmarks provide held-out sets, independent sensors and in-situ measurements check retrieved quantities, physical models check consistency with the observations, and repeated observation of the same place checks predictions against what is observed later. Each source yields a function that scores a pipeline without exposing its data to the agent. In this paper we build the verifier from labeled held-out sets, using the official validation split where it is not needed as the test set and otherwise a held-out portion of the training split selected by region or scene. 3.3 Harness The harness gives the agent what an EO researcher would start from. It contains the labeled training data and an unlabeled pool formed by the remaining training imagery, pretrained encoders without decoders or task heads (DINOv3 ViT-L/16 pretrained on satellite imagery, DINOv2 ViT-L/14, ResNet-18 to ResNet-101 and EfficientNet-B0 Siméoni et al. (2026) ; Oquab et al. (2024) ; He et al. (2016) ; Tan and Le (2019) ), the official source code of the DINO models, the Python deep-learning and geospatial stack together with QGIS, SAGA and GDAL, and a deliberately plain starter pipeline, an EfficientNet-B0 encoder with an FPN decoder Lin et al. (2017) . The agent has four GB200 GPUs and 12 hours, and no single training run may exceed 4 hours. The submitted pipeline must be a deep network that the agent trained, the given labels may not be edited, and the agent may annotate unlabeled data itself. Each task runs in an offline Harbor container Harbor Framework Team (2026) ; Merrill et al. (2026) , where the agent works without network access beyond its own model interface. When time ends, the submission directory is copied out and graded once on the hidden test set under an inference budget of 4 hours. Nothing else the agent created survives, so every model, weight and script the final pipeline needs must be inside the submission. 3.4 Recursive Improvement Within a task, the agent improves the EO model recursively on its own outputs. We call each model the agent trains a generation. A generation f t f_{t} leaves outputs O t O_{t} behind, including its predictions and class probabilities on the unlabeled pool, its checkpoints, and its predictions on held-out folds of the training data. The next generation is trained from these outputs, f t + 1 = Train ⁡ ( ℋ , O ≤ t ) , f_{t+1}=\mathrm{Train}\big(\mathcal{H},\,O_{\leq t}\big), (2) and the verifier decides which generation is kept and serves as the source of the next one. Two loops therefore run together. In the outer loop, the agent proposes pipelines and the verifier scores them. In the inner loop, each kept model becomes material for its successor, so the model improves along a lineage. The agent designs this recursion itself. The task instruction permits methods such as pseudo-labeling on the unlabeled pool but prescribes none, and the agent chooses the mechanisms, their order and the depth of the lineage from the verifier’s feedback. Across our runs the mechanisms fall into three families. At the data level, a teacher labels the unlabeled pool with hard pseudo-labels or soft class probabilities and a student trains on them, as in Noisy Student Xie et al. (2020) and knowledge distillation Hinton et al. (2015) . At the parameter level, new models start from earlier checkpoints, average several checkpoints, or grow from a smaller trained network. At the prediction level, the agent trains models on its own predictions, such as calibrators fitted to out-of-fold predictions and networks that refine its masks. Each generation thus learns from the previous one, the pattern behind STaR Zelikman et al. (2022) and AlphaGo Zero Silver et al. (2017) , here applied to the EO model the agent builds. 3.5 Learning Settings as Choices of Harness and Verifier Table 1: The five benchmarks. Full supervision gives the harness all training labels. Learning from few labels keeps one to five labeled samples per natural stratum of the data, such as a continent, a disaster event, a scene or a class, chosen so that together they cover every class, and moves the remaining training imagery to the unlabeled pool ( Table 1 ). A label-budget search fixes a label-free selection rule, ranks the training images with it, and asks for the smallest prefix whose labels let the agent reach a target score. Domain adaptation places the labeled data on a source domain and the verifier on the target domain. In every case the agent, the verifier interface and the final evaluation stay the same. This view also changes where annotation goes. Labels are needed for the verifier and for the final evaluation, which every benchmark already provides, while the training set can shrink to a handful of samples. Validation labels reach the agent only through the scores the verifier returns. 4 Experiments The experiments ask three questions that follow from the introduction. The first is whether one agent and one interface suffice across sensors, output types and label regimes. The second is how good the resulting models are, both against the plain pipeline that ships with the harness and against published methods. The third is how the amount of labeled data in the harness and the choice of agent change the outcome. We instantiate RSI4EO on five EO benchmarks that differ in sensor, output and metric ( Table 1 ). OpenEarthMap Xia et al. (2023) maps eight land-cover classes from sub-meter RGB imagery, BRIGHT Chen et al. (2025) grades building damage from pre-event optical and post-event SAR pairs, HRSID Wei et al. (2020) segments ship instances in SAR images, PASTIS Sainte Fare Garnot and Landrieu (2021) maps 18 crop types from Sentinel-2 time series, and the Toulouse data set Thoreau et al. (2024) classifies 32 surface materials from single hyperspectral pixels. Each benchmark yields a full-label task and a few-label task that keeps 0.1 to 1.4% of the training labels. On OpenEarthMap we add two points of the label-budget search, in which the harness holds the first 300 or 600 tiles ranked by k-center on frozen DINOv3 features Sener and Savarese (2018) , a rule that uses no labels. The hidden test set is the official test split of each benchmark, except on OpenEarthMap. Its test labels are withheld, so its official validation split serves as the test set and the verifier holds 300 tiles taken from the training split. Its 1,500 official test images join the unlabeled pool. For Toulouse we use the second of the eight official spatially disjoint splits. HRSID has no official validation split, and its verifier holds 20 complete scenes taken from the official training set. The agent therefore trains on 3,090 of the 3,642 images that published methods use. The Toulouse harness provides only spectra, without pixel positions and without a pretrained model. Agents and Protocol. The main agent is Codex with GPT-6-Astra, which runs every task. Claude Code with Claude Opus 5.5 runs the four OpenEarthMap tasks. Both agents use their highest reasoning effort and work for at most 12 hours on four GB200 GPUs in an offline container ( Section 3.3 ). The verifier returns the task metric, its per-class breakdown and the inference time, and it may be queried without limit. Each task runs once per agent. Reference Points. Comparison with Published Methods. The baselines include a multi-model ensemble on BRIGHT, fine-tuned EO foundation models on PASTIS and methods with test-time augmentation on OpenEarthMap. Each model is a system that the agent designed, trained and assembled from its own networks within 12 hours. On HRSID the agent reaches its score with 15% fewer training images than the published methods and without a pretrained detector. A run of this kind costs roughly 100 to 170 US dollars of model usage and at most 48 GPU hours ( Section A.2 ). Few Labels. With 0.2 to 1.4% of the training labels, the agent keeps 69 to 84% of its full-label score on OpenEarthMap, BRIGHT, HRSID and PASTIS. On OpenEarthMap, six labeled tiles yield 60.0 mIoU, close to the 60.5 of RS3Mamba trained on all 3,000 tiles. The formulation thus does not depend on one agent or one working style. 5 Analysis The trajectories record every command, message and verifier query of a run. We use them to examine three points. The first is that the agent improves the model recursively on its own outputs without being told to. The second is that the agent takes over the engineering that experts otherwise carry out. The third is how far a model selected against a sealed verifier generalizes to data the agent never accesses. Further observations on how the agents use the verifier and spend their budget, and on what follows for the design of harnesses and verifiers, are reported in Appendix A . The six-label OpenEarthMap run shows the recursion most clearly ( Fig. 2 ). The agent first uses the verifier to choose an encoder, then labels all 4,194 unlabeled tiles with its best model and trains a student on the six labeled tiles and this pseudo-labeled pool. From there it runs eight generations. In each, the teacher is the best pipeline the verifier has seen so far, and the student continues from the previous generation’s checkpoint. The verifier score rises in every generation, from 56.7 to 61.9 mIoU, which is 9.0 points above the best model the agent trained on the six tiles alone. The agent then automates this loop. It writes scripts that read the verifier log, promote the best pipeline to the next teacher and start a new generation when the teacher has improved. The last generation was launched, trained and verified by these scripts alone. The recursion thus extends from the model to the procedure that improves it. Figure 3: Progress within a run. A dot marks the time at which the agent ends the run, and the black line is the median over runs. The Same Pattern across Tasks. The few-label runs on OpenEarthMap, BRIGHT and HRSID all arrive at teacher–student loops over the unlabeled pool, with eight generations on OpenEarthMap, ten on BRIGHT and five on HRSID, and Claude Opus runs four on OpenEarthMap. No instruction asks for them. The loops also differ in what passes from one generation to the next. On BRIGHT the teacher is a three-stage pipeline of a building detector, a damage network and a building-level classifier, and on HRSID it is a detector followed by a network that predicts each mask from a crop around the detected box. The Verifier Steers the Recursion. On BRIGHT the first two generations add about 2 and 1 points. From the third generation on, the gains come from generations that change the loss or architecture of the student. The verifier shows the agent when another generation of the same kind stops paying, and the agent responds with such a change. 5.2 What the Agent Contributes Most of the Gain Arrives Early. Figure 3 shows how the verifier score of each run approaches its final value. Across the 10 runs of GPT-6-Astra with recorded trajectories, half of the final gain is reached after a median of 0.85 hours and 90% after 3.5 hours. The steps taken in this early phase are of a few recurring kinds. • Choosing Pretrained Encoders with the Verifier. Replacing the starter encoder by a frozen foundation encoder is the largest single step in several runs, with 11.1 points on six-label OpenEarthMap and 7.3 on few-label BRIGHT, each within the first 15 minutes. The verifier also corrects plausible priors. With six OpenEarthMap tiles, DINOv2 scores 52.2 mIoU and the satellite-pretrained DINOv3 47.2, and on PASTIS the agent finds that pixel-wise temporal models contribute more than DINO features. • Matching Model Capacity to the Labels. Across the few-label runs, early checkpoints often beat later ones, and the agent infers from the verifier that fitting a handful of labels more closely reduces accuracy. • Debugging. The largest step of the full-label HRSID run, 5.2 AP, comes largely from correcting a half-pixel offset in mask refinement, which the agent found by inspecting its outputs. Earlier in the same run it had traced misplaced masks to a loss of box-coordinate precision under mixed precision. • Task-Specific Structure. On BRIGHT with few labels, the agent separates the confidence that a pixel is a building from the confidence in its damage grade when it trains on pseudo-labels, which adds 2.2 points in a controlled comparison. It later assigns building footprints to a separate optical detector, which adds a further 1.1 points. These are the choices of backbone and training recipe that experts otherwise make by hand ( Section 1 ). Here the agent makes them from the verifier’s feedback, and it makes most of them within the first hours of a run. A model close to the final one therefore exists within the first hours of a run ( Section A.2 ). 5.3 Generalization beyond the Verifier The Verifier Score Carries Over. The agent selects among up to several hundred candidates with the verifier, and on OpenEarthMap, PASTIS, HRSID and BRIGHT the score it optimizes carries over to the hidden test. Table 5 in the appendix lists the verifier and test scores of every run. The hidden-test score lies within about 2 points of the verifier score in all seven OpenEarthMap runs listed there and within 0.2 points on PASTIS. On HRSID the test score is higher by about 3.5 AP, because the official test images come from the same scenes as the training images whereas the verifier holds separate scenes. The Gap Follows the Split. On OpenEarthMap the run with 341 queries has a gap of 1.9 points, and the run with 8 queries has a gap of 1.3. In both, and in every other OpenEarthMap run, the gap is concentrated in the same two classes, bareland and water. On BRIGHT, three quarters of the 3.5 to 4.0 point difference come from the destroyed class, which covers 1.38% of the validation pixels and 0.93% of the test pixels, and its test IoU of 61.5 is below the 65.4 that the first, untuned submission of the run obtained on the verifier. On these benchmarks the gaps mainly reflect differences between the validation and test splits. This agrees with studies of benchmark reuse, which find that repeated model selection on a held-out set causes little overfitting Recht et al. (2019) ; Roelofs et al. (2019) . 6 Conclusion We have shown that any verifiable EO task can be cast as a harness and a verifier, and that a general-purpose coding agent given these two components builds EO models that surpass the state of the art on four benchmarks and keep most of that accuracy from about 1% of the training labels. The formulation separates what people specify from what agents build. The harness and the verifier of a task stay fixed while the agents that solve it improve, so every advance in agent capability turns into better EO models without new human engineering. The time from a new disaster, satellite or region to a working model then becomes the agent’s budget, and any agency that can define a task can obtain a state-of-the-art model. As AI capability continues to rise, we see this as the paradigm in which EO models will be built, with human effort invested in the harnesses and verifiers that define the tasks. The observations of this appendix suggest two rules. The harness should contain what local validation needs, such as the grouping of samples into scenes, events or polygons. The verifier should be representative of the test distribution and can report the uncertainty of its scores.
