Codes: https://github.com/LINs-lab/ProAS . 1 Introduction Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as autonomous agents that make sequential decisions through interactions with external environments. Their planning and reasoning capabilities have enabled applications in software engineering ( ? ), personal assistance ( ? ), and graphical user interface navigation ( ? ). Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as the primary paradigm for elevating the agentic capacity of LLMs ( ?? ). During RL rollouts, agents generate trajectories composed of interleaved environment states and actions, which introduces a fundamental credit assignment challenge : How should trajectory-level rewards and penalties be attributed to individual actions? Actor-critic algorithms (e.g., PPO ( ? )) address this by learning a separate critic, which introduces substantial computational overhead and optimization instability in long-horizon settings ( ?? ). Conversely, critic-free algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) ( ? ) offer computational efficiency by broadcasting a trajectory-level advantage uniformly to every action ( ? ). However, this inherently treats all intra-trajectory actions identically, completely overlooking their critical differences in local validity, task contribution, and downstream inflence. As detailed in § 3.2 , we term this structural limitation the uniform credit assignment dilemma . To systematically understand the uniform credit assignment dilemma, we conduct a fine-grained action-intervention analysis (§ 4.2 ). Our findings reveal a dual suboptimality. First, in preferred trajectories with positive advantages, uniform credit inadvertently reinforces structurally invalid actions (i.e., unreasonable actions) that pollute the context history and impede the agent from reaching success. Second, in unpreferred trajectories with negative advantages, uniform penalization ignores the autoregressive nature of LLMs, where early deviations drive cascading failures; consequently, they unfairly suppress all actions equally without considering their actual downstream influence. To resolve this dilemma without introducing critic overhead, we propose Progress-Aware Advantage Shaping (ProAS) in § 4.3 . ProAS leverages an Agentic Reference Graph (ARG, mentioned in § 4.1 ), which abstracts task progression into dynamically evolving sub-goals to formally annotate whether an action is reasonable (structurally valid) or progress-advancing (driving task completion). Using these structural annotations, ProAS asymmetrically reshapes trajectory-level advantages in Eq. 7 : (1) Masking Unreasonable Incentives (MUI) zeros out the local advantages of unreasonable actions in preferred trajectories to prevent their inadvertent reinforcement. (2) Asymmetric Penalty Redistribution (APR) utilizes Future-Drift (Eq. 6 ), the inclusive count of downstream non-advancing actions, to assign proportionally heavier penalties to actions triggering severe downstream stagnation. Extensive experiments in § 6 on ALFWorld ( ? ) and WebShop ( ? ) using Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct ( ? ) validate ProAS as a versatile, plug-and-play enhancer. It universally elevates mainstream group-based RL baselines (GRPO ( ? ), DAPO ( ? ), GiGPO ( ? )), boosting success rates by up to 16.7 points (e.g., GRPO on 1.5B ALFWorld) and accelerating training convergence by 1.12 × 1.12\times to 2.44 × 2.44\times . On top of that, this orthogonal enhancement propels these standard algorithms to establish new state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance, consistently eclipsing the strong segment-level baseline, MiGPO ( ? ). Notably, GiGPO+ProAS outperforms MiGPO by a massive 13.1 points on WebShop with 1.5B model and secures the highest overall success rate of 94.3% on ALFWorld with 7B model. Furthermore, comprehensive ablations (§ 6.2 ) show that ProAS’s core mechanisms are both synergistic and superior to alternative advantage shaping variants, and confirm ProAS’s robust efficacy against incomplete or distilled ARGs, underscoring the necessity of ARG’s online evolution mechanism. In summary, our primary contributions are: • Problem Identification: We expose the uniform credit assignment dilemma in critic-free agentic RL, demonstrating how uniform advantages inadvertently reinforce contextual noise in preferred trajectories and ignore the downstream influence of actions in unpreferred ones. • Algorithmic Innovation: We propose Progress-Aware Advantage Shaping (ProAS), a plug-and-play framework. Guided by a dynamically evolving Agentic Reference Graph (ARG), ProAS achieves fine-grained, critic-free credit assignment via Masking Unreasonable Incentives (MUI) and Asymmetric Penalty Redistribution (APR). • Empirical Superiority: ProAS acts as a universal enhancer across multiple model scales and environments. It significantly accelerates training convergence, propels standard group-based algorithms to surpass strong SOTA baselines (e.g., MiGPO), and exhibits high robustness against imperfect and distilled reference guidance. 2 Related Works Multi-Turn Agentic Tasks. Critic-free RL like GRPO ( ? ) is widely adopted for LLM agents ( ??? ), but suffers from sparse-reward advantage vanishment ( ? ) and coarse trajectory-level credit assignment ( ? ). To quantify these step-level properties, § 4.1 constructs an Agentic Reference Graph (ARG). Guided by an intervention study probing these modulation effects (§ 4.2 ), § 4.3 introduces Progress-Aware Advantage Shaping (ProAS), a lightweight framework designed to resolve the uniform credit dilemma via asymmetric advantage shaping. Below, we formalize its construction and online evolution, followed by a two-tier annotation scheme to categorize step-level actions. Construction and Formalization. To ensure reliable action assessment, we enforce strict graph purity by pruning invalid transitions and self-loops (example in Appx. D.3 ). Furthermore, while sub-goals follow a strict sequence, their internal atomic requirements are often permutation-invariant (example in Appx. D.4 ). By modeling each sub-goal’s completion as a state equivalence class, we seamlessly unify these diverse execution paths. The vertex set 𝒱 = ⋃ m = 1 M g m \mathcal{V}=\bigcup_{m=1}^{M}\textbf{g}_{m} comprises M M sub-goals in sequential order. Each sub-goal g m = { s m , 1 , s m , 2 , … } \textbf{g}_{m}=\{s_{m,1},s_{m,2},\dots\} is an unordered set of permutation-invariant, valid states, strictly excluding error-induced states and redundant self-loops. Conversely, non-advancing actions ( PA t = 0 \text{PA}_{t}=0 ) encapsulate both explicit errors and structurally valid but stagnant steps. 4.2 Action-Intervention Analysis By leveraging the reasonableness and progress-advancement annotations to assess fine-grained action quality, our pilot study reveals how individual steps dictate outcomes, motivating our solution to the uniform credit assignment dilemma. Probing Unreasonable Actions. Unreasonable actions hinder task progress by polluting the context history and disrupting subsequent decisions ( ? ). Notably, they constitute a substantial proportion of successful GRPO trajectories (41.6% in ALFWorld, 39.6% in WebShop) of Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, where they are inadvertently reinforced by the uniform credit applied to preferred rollouts. To probe their impact, we select a clean ALFWorld trajectory (details in Appx. D.1 ) and sample 50 continuations, { c i } i = 1 50 \{c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{50} , from a fixed state under two context conditions. As illustrated in Fig. 1 (b), the clean control retains the original history, whereas the noise-injected condition prepends a four-step unreasonable segment. Despite sharing the identical underlying environment state, this visible context noise degrades the Expected Success Rate (ESR) from 82% to 58%. Insight 1: Unreasonable actions in the context actively impede the agent from reaching success. Suppressing Actions with Varying Future-Drift. Conversely, non-advancing (NA) actions are heavily concentrated in failed GRPO trajectories (93.1% in ALFWorld, 76.1% in WebShop), where they are suppressed uniformly. Given the autoregressive nature of LLMs, late-stage NA actions are often inevitable consequences of an already polluted context rather than making the mistake independently. Therefore, uniformly suppressing all actions within a failed trajectory is inherently unfair. To quantify the cascading effect, we define Future-Drift ( 𝒟 i , t \mathcal{D}_{i,t} , short as FD) as the inclusive count of downstream NA actions from step t t : 𝒟 i , t = ∑ t ′ = t | τ i | ( 1 − PA i , t ′ ) \mathcal{D}_{i,t}=\sum_{t^{\prime}=t}^{|\tau_{i}|}\big(1-\text{PA}_{i,t^{\prime}}\big) (6) To assess whether actions with higher Future-Drift warrant heavier penalization, we perform a targeted suppression intervention on a failed ALFWorld trajectory generated by Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct as shown in Fig. 1 (c). By preserving the trajectory prefix up to specific steps, forbidding the original action, and resampling 50 continuations, we measure the recovered ESR. Suppressing the actions with FD values of 47, 23, 11, and 4 yields ESRs of 24%, 16%, 10%, and 0%, respectively (case details in Appx. D.2 ). In this case, the higher the suppressed action’s Future-Drift, the more likely the agent is to recover and complete the task. Insight 2: Actions with higher FD should be prioritized for suppression. 4.3 Progress-Aware Advantage Shaping Building upon the empirical insights from § 4.2 , we propose the Progress-Aware Advantage Shaping (ProAS) framework, aiming to resolve the uniform credit dilemma by asymmetrically reshaping the trajectory-level advantage A i A_{i} into a step-wise, progress-aware signal A ~ i , t \tilde{A}_{i,t} : A ~ i , t = { Rea t ⋅ A i , if ​ A i > 0 , 𝒟 i , t 𝒟 ¯ i + ϵ ⋅ A i , if ​ A i < 0 ​ and ​ t ∈ 𝒯 , A i , otherwise, \tilde{A}_{i,t}=\begin{cases}\text{Rea}_{t}\cdot A_{i},&\text{if }A_{i}>0,\\ \frac{\mathcal{D}_{i,t}}{\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{i}+\epsilon}\cdot A_{i},&\text{if }A_{i}<0\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T},\\ A_{i},&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases} (7) where 𝒯 \mathcal{T} denotes the steps sharing the uniform advantage, 𝒟 ¯ i = 1 | 𝒯 | ​ ∑ t ′ ∈ 𝒯 𝒟 i , t ′ \bar{\mathcal{D}}_{i}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{D}_{i,t^{\prime}} is the average Future-Drift, and ϵ \epsilon ensures numerical stability. This formulation branches into two mechanisms: (1) Masking Unreasonable Incentives (MUI): Aligning with Insight 1 , it zeros out the local advantages of invalid actions ( Rea t = 0 \text{Rea}_{t}=0 ) in preferred trajectories ( A i > 0 A_{i}>0 , usually successful) to prevent inadvertent reinforcement. (2) Asymmetric Penalty Redistribution (APR): Reflecting Insight 2 , it leverages normalized Future-Drift to assign proportionally heavier penalties to early pivotal actions in unpreferred trajectories ( A i < 0 A_{i}<0 , usually failed), rectifying the unfairness of uniform suppression. 5 Experimental Setup To empirically validate the proposed ProAS framework, we conduct extensive experiments across complex, long-horizon agentic environments. To address this without critic overhead, we proposed Progress-Aware Advantage Shaping (ProAS), which leverages a dynamically evolving Agentic Reference Graph (ARG) to achieve fine-grained credit assignment via Masking Unreasonable Incentives (MUI) and Asymmetric Penalty Redistribution (APR). Extensive evaluations across multiple model scales and environments confirm ProAS as a robust, universal enhancer that accelerates convergence and propels mainstream baselines to new state-of-the-art performance. The ablation study further confirms MUI-APR synergy, necessity of ARG evolution and robustness under incomplete or distilled ARGs. Together, these results establish a highly efficient, scalable pathway for elevating autonomous language agents. Appendix B Further Implementation Details B.1 Training Framework For ALFWorld and WebShop, we adopt the Verl-Agent ( ? ) framework. To ensure robustness and reproducibility, we sample and freeze three distinct training sets with environment seeds 0, 1, and 2 for independent and repeated training. For fair evaluation, all resulting model checkpoints are assessed on a unified, static validation set of 128 tasks. This validation set is strictly disjoint from the training data, rigorously preventing any potential data leakage. Validation uses a temperature of 0.4 0.4 in both ALFWorld and WebShop. RL and SFT share the same turn-level input format illustrated in Tables 11 and 12 : each prompt contains the task instruction, current observation, admissible actions, and the visible interaction history from the two most recent observation-action turns. The only difference between SFT and RL data is the assistant response, which is curated offline for SFT but generated online for RL. All experiments are conducted on GPUs. We follow the standard Verl-Agent ( ? ) configuration and use the same settings across all RL methods unless otherwise specified. Models are optimized with AdamW using a learning rate of 10 − 6 10^{-6} , weight decay 0.01 0.01 , and gradient clipping at 1.0 1.0 . D.3 The ARG Dynamic Evolution in ALFWorld Table 9 illustrates the online ARG evolution mechanism using the task Look at a pencil under the desk lamp . Because the environment’s success criterion broadly accepts any pencil index (e.g., pencil 1 or pencil 2 ), the task inherently possesses multiple valid solutions that a single static ARG fails to cover. To capture this diversity, any rollout verified as successful during training is first purified by pruning invalid and inadmissible steps. The retained valid path is then seamlessly merged into the current ARG, dynamically expanding its coverage of viable solution trajectories. D.4 The Order-Agnostic of Actions in WebShop Table 10 shows a WebShop case from Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct GRPO rollouts. A partial g 3 \textbf{g}_{3} state satisfies one required option, while g 3 \textbf{g}_{3} is completed only after all required options are selected. The price and attribute constraints, g 4 \textbf{g}_{4} and g 5 \textbf{g}_{5} , are checked at purchase time before the trajectory reaches g 6 \textbf{g}_{6} . Trajectories A and B differ only within g 3 \textbf{g}_{3} : they visit different intermediate option states but reach the same completion-state equivalence class, so both option orders are treated as progress-advancing. Trajectory C shows the complementary cases: a wrong option and a premature purchase are unsupported by the ARG, whereas a repeated click on an already selected option can be reasonable but non-advancing. Thus, the ARG preserves ordering constraints among high-level sub-goals while avoiding spurious penalties for valid option permutations.
