Holistic Diversity Management in LLM-Guided Evolutionary Search

LLM-guided evolutionary search iteratively selects existing programs, uses LLMs as mutation operators to generate a new program, and evaluates it to guide subsequent search.
To preserve diversity and reduce the risk of premature convergence, it often adopts the island model , in which groups of programs, called islands, evolve separately.
However, this structure alone does not guarantee distinct search directions, as islands may start from similar programs or converge toward similar solutions during evolution.
This suggests that diversity must be considered throughout the search process, from initializing islands with distinct programs to developing island trajectories and sharing discoveries across islands.
To this end, we propose HoliDivEvolve , a holistic approach to establishing and effectively utilizing island diversity throughout evolutionary search.
Specifically, we introduce diverse initialization to establish different initial programs, trajectory adaptive island selection to allocate computation based on current score, recent growth, and the remaining search budget, and growth adaptive migration to share alternative ideas across islands.
Experiments across diverse benchmarks show that HoliDivEvolve achieves competitive performance on most evaluated tasks.
We further conduct controlled analyses to examine how our design choices affect search behavior and final performance.



1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as the mutation operator inside evolutionary search, turning program and algorithm discovery into an iterative loop of generation, evaluation, and selection ( Romera-Paredes et al., 2024 ; Novikov et al., 2025 ) .
To mitigate premature convergence, the island model is commonly adopted as a classical approach to maintaining diversity ( Tanese, 1989 ) .
This model organizes candidate programs into groups called islands , which evolve separately and occasionally
exchange programs through migration.

However, the island model does not directly control the diversity of programs generated within each island.
This problem gets worse when LLMs are used as mutation operators, because LLMs often generate programs with similar algorithms ( Brownlee et al., 2025 ; Lee et al., 2025 ; Gurkan et al., 2026 ) .

Prior studies have addressed diversity through different aspects of the island model.
Some approaches focus on diverse initialization or population management Yang et al. (2026) ; Tanveer (2026) ; Lange et al. (2026) , while others adapt island selection Cemri et al. (2026) ; Luo et al. (2026) or migration Yan et al. (2026) ; Li and Ramakrishnan (2026) .
However, these decisions have often been studied separately, despite their interconnected roles in maintaining diversity throughout search.

We argue that establishing and utilizing island diversity should be considered together.
Distinct initial programs provide different search directions, but their subsequent development depends on how computation is allocated across islands.
Likewise, migration allows islands to benefit from discoveries made elsewhere, while also influencing their future search directions.
These decisions therefore shape one another throughout evolution, motivating a holistic approach to island diversity.

Based on this view, we propose HoliDivEvolve , which takes a holistic approach to establishing and effectively utilizing island diversity throughout evolutionary search.
We generate multiple candidate programs and use a diverse subset to initialize different islands, establishing distinct starting points.
We then allocate computation according to each island’s current score, recent growth, and the remaining budget, allowing different island trajectories to develop.
We further guide cross-island migration using relative growth, introducing alternative programs when an island’s growth lags behind others.
In this way, HoliDivEvolve considers not only how diverse island trajectories are established, but also how they develop and benefit from discoveries made elsewhere.

We evaluate HoliDivEvolve across a diverse set of program and algorithm optimization tasks and compare it with recent LLM-guided evolutionary methods. Overall, HoliDivEvolve achieves competitive performance and outperforms existing approaches on most evaluated tasks. Our component studies further examine the effects of each component, showing how it influences the resulting search process and final solution quality.

2 Related Work

LLM-guided evolutionary search.

LLMs have increasingly been used as mutation operators for program and
algorithm discovery, where generated solutions are evaluated and reused
to guide subsequent generations ( Romera-Paredes et al., 2024 ; Liu et al., 2024 ; Ye et al., 2024 ; Novikov et al., 2025 ; Sharma, 2025 ; Jiang et al., 2026 ; Assumpção et al., 2025 ; Tanveer, 2026 ; Liu et al., 2026 ) .
These works have extended evolutionary search through improved population
management, reflection, search history, and mutation strategies.
Our work builds on this paradigm but focuses specifically on how multiple
island trajectories should be initialized, developed, and connected
throughout a finite search budget.

Adaptive search allocation.

Recent methods make different parts of evolutionary search adaptive using
feedback collected during evolution, including model selection, parent
sampling, search-strategy selection, and computation allocation ( Lange et al., 2026 ; Cemri et al., 2026 ; Chen et al., 2026 ; Luo et al., 2026 ) .
Our setting instead considers computation allocation across islands whose
search trajectories may improve at different rates.
Our island selection rule is motivated by best arm identification for rising bandits ( Metelli et al., 2022 ; Song et al., 2026 ; Mussi et al., 2023 ) . In this setting, finding the option that will be best in the future requires considering its current score, its recent growth, and the remaining budget.

Diversity and cross-island interaction.

Maintaining diverse subpopulations and exchanging information between
them are central ideas in island-based evolutionary algorithms Tanese (1989) .
Recent LLM-guided methods further explore diversified initialization,
parallel trajectories, and adaptive information sharing ( Yang et al., 2026 ; Yan et al., 2026 ; Li and Ramakrishnan, 2026 ) .
Our method initializes islands from separated programs and subsequently
uses relative recent growth to determine when an island should receive
programs from another trajectory.

4 Method

Algorithm 1 HoliDivEvolve

We organize our method around three decisions: how to initialize the
islands, which island to select at each round, and when and what to
exchange across islands.
We address these decisions with three components: diverse initialization , which provides different initial
programs; trajectory adaptive island selection , which allocates
search rounds based on the state of each island; and growth adaptive migration , which adaptively migrates programs across islands when an island’s recent growth slows relative to others.

4.3 Growth Adaptive Migration

Migration allows programs discovered in one island to influence subsequent mutations in others. We employ two forms of migration: conventional periodic migration for occasional program sharing across islands, and growth adaptive migration to introduce alternative programs when an island’s recent growth becomes relatively low. How migrated programs are used.

Migrated programs are used to complement the recipient’s current search
rather than replace it.
When the recipient island is selected again, its current best program
remains the parent, while the migrated programs are prioritized as
references in the prompt.
This separation between parent and reference preserves the recipient’s
current direction while allowing external ideas to influence its
subsequent mutations.

6 Conclusion

We introduced HoliDivEvolve , an LLM-guided evolutionary search framework that
focuses on how multiple island trajectories are initialized, developed,
and connected throughout the search.
Our method combines diverse initialization, trajectory adaptive island
selection, and growth adaptive migration to maintain distinct search
trajectories while allocating computation and exchanging information
according to their observed progress.
Experiments across multiple program optimization tasks show that our
method achieves competitive overall performance, while controlled
analyses demonstrate the benefits of diverse initialization and
trajectory-dependent computation allocation.
Together, these results highlight the importance of not only maintaining
multiple islands, but also actively managing how their search
trajectories evolve under a fixed search budget.

Limitations

Our approach has several limitations.
First, recent growth is estimated from observed improvements and therefore
provides only a noisy proxy for the future potential of an island,
especially when improvements occur sparsely or abruptly.
Second, our initialization and migrant selection use embedding-based code
distance, which does not necessarily correspond to semantic or
algorithmic diversity.
Finally, our experiments cover a limited set of LLMs, program
optimization tasks, and search budgets.
Further evaluation is needed to determine how the proposed mechanisms
generalize to different models, larger numbers of islands, and
substantially longer search horizons.

Timing and transfer limits.

The experimental implementation supplements the adaptive mechanism
in Section 4.3 with ring exchange every 20 mutation
rounds. Using the paper’s island indices, the ring follows 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 1 1\to 2\to 3\to 4\to 1 , with at most three programs per edge.
Adaptive migration uses τ = 0.5 \tau=\sqrt{0.5} in the main-text growth
criterion, since the implementation compares squared growth values
with a ratio of 0.5. It becomes eligible after 20 iterations and at least five local evaluations in the recipient.
One qualifying check is sufficient, subject to a five-round interval
since that recipient’s latest migration attempt, including ring
exchange. Each adaptive event transfers at most five programs.

Program selection and population update.

In addition to excluding donor bests as specified in the main text,
we exclude code already present in the recipient. Migration uses
Qwen3-Embedding-8B. For geometry and Signal Processing, it embeds
code reachable from the task entry point when extraction is supported,
falling back to the full source otherwise; the systems tasks use
the full source. Historical initialization embeddings are cached
separately.

Transfers copy programs into the recipient and retain the donor’s
copies. Low-fitness recipient programs are replaced while protecting
its best program and protected archive representatives. The actual
transfer count can be smaller than the cap when there are too few
eligible candidates or replaceable slots. All transfers in one ring
event are planned from the pre-event populations, so an incoming
program cannot be forwarded again within that event.

The growth update in Section 4.3 uses the number of
programs actually admitted and the pre-event growth estimates.
We synchronize the recipient’s best-score baseline with the updated

D.2 LLM Mutation

Parent and references.

After island selection, the database samples a parent and up to four
reference programs. Normal mutation uses local references in these
experiments. Parent sampling balances high-fitness programs with
novelty-based exploration; its intensity depends on the island’s
growth statistic. Migration follow-up can prioritize received
programs in the reference slots. The builder inserts code, metrics,
and the sampling-mode instruction into the structure in
Figure 7 .

Figure 7: Mutation prompt template, including conditional search
guidance and the required SEARCH/REPLACE edit format.

The parser applies edits to the parent and evaluates the resulting
complete program. Feedback is conditional: a request does not contain
every earlier program or a full search transcript. The sibling summary
reports outcomes of previous mutations of the same parent, including
score changes, rather than inserting all sibling code. Retry requests
can include parsing or execution errors and can resample the parent.

Meta-generated guidance.

When enabled, a stagnation tracker can request a separate LLM review.
That request contains the task, evaluator, current global-best code
and score, and previously tried ideas with feedback. A fixed review
template asks the model to analyze the objective, metrics,
constraints, input–output structure, and improvement opportunities
before proposing alternative approaches. The LLM returns structured
ideas with fields for the idea, implementation description,
optimization target, cautions, and approach type. The fixed analysis
framework and technique suggestions are supplied by the implementation;
the concrete review and proposed ideas are LLM-generated.

An active idea is inserted into subsequent mutation guidance, and the
controller uses the global-best program as the parent for that guided
mutation. This mechanism is enabled in the full-method and factor
ablation configurations, but disabled in the isolated island-selection
comparison. It is triggered by search state rather than invoked on
every mutation, so enabled runs can make different numbers of
auxiliary review calls.

Appendix E Signal Processing Initialization Examples

The four examples below are the actual filtering implementations in
the cached diverse initialization used by the Signal Processing
component studies. They were selected from the historical candidate
pool described in Appendix C.2 , before

The selected set covers four approaches:

Local polynomial regression: independently fits a
polynomial to each window and evaluates it at the right endpoint.

Adaptive Kalman filtering: recursively estimates
level and velocity, adapting measurement noise from residuals.

Median plus EMA: applies a rolling median for
outlier robustness, followed by exponential smoothing.

Bounded-trend Holt filtering: recursively updates
level and trend while limiting changes in the trend estimate.

These distinctions concern the executable algorithms, not changes
to names or constants. Selection uses embedding distance; the
algorithm-family descriptions are an interpretation of the selected
