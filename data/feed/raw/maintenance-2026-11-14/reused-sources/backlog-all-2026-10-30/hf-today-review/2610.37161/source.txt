Abstract
Scaling supervised training for working agents requires diverse professional tasks that yield reliable expert demonstrations.
However, missing or inconsistent task information can obstruct teacher execution and weaken the resulting supervision.
We propose WorkFactory, a scalable framework for constructing coherent and executable professional tasks.
Starting from professional intents, information-level planning builds an information support graph to guide task instantiation, explicitly representing the dependencies and locations of information required for the intended outputs.
Probe executions then identify support failures and guide repairs while preserving the intended work.
We construct 112,686 tasks spanning 612 occupations and use the collected teacher trajectories to fine-tune Qwen models at 4B, 27B, and 35B-A3B scales, yielding the WorkPilot family.
All three models improve over their respective base models across four professional-work benchmarks, with WorkPilot-35B-A3B improving from 879 to 1565 Elo on GDPval-AA v2.1.
Scaling analyses reveal model-dependent diminishing returns, with execution length first increasing and then decreasing.
Controlled component analyses show that planning and repair reduce probe-diagnosed task environment defects and improve downstream performance.
The data and models are available at
https://github.com/IQuestLab/WorkFactory
.

Introduction
Language agents interleave reasoning and actions to interact with external environments
[
36
]
.
Working agents extend this paradigm to professional work in digital environments, executing workflows, coordinating tools, and producing deliverables across diverse occupations
[
23
,
17
]
.
This shift creates opportunities for agents to improve productivity and generate real economic value
[
18
]
.
A common approach to agent post-training is supervised fine-tuning on expert demonstrations of task execution
[
30
,
10
]
.
Scaling this approach to working agents requires diverse executable professional tasks spanning a wide range of occupational competencies.
Recent frameworks automate professional task construction by retrieving, reusing, adapting, or synthesizing task-relevant materials
[
12
]
, and then collect teacher trajectories for supervision
[
40
]
.
The value of scaling task construction, however, depends on whether the resulting teacher trajectories reliably demonstrate the target occupational competencies.
To produce effective demonstrations, a teacher agent needs access to
sufficient and consistent information
for carrying out the intended work.
Establishing this support is challenging because the information required for an intended output may be distributed across multiple artifacts and connected through intermediate calculations and judgments.
For example, a supplier-selection task may provide demand forecasts and vendor quotations in separate files but omit whether one vendor’s price includes freight.
Without this pricing condition, lifecycle costs cannot be computed and compared consistently; yet providing the computed costs directly would remove a substantive part of the work the teacher is expected to perform.
Constructing such an environment therefore requires a careful boundary: all necessary premises must be available, while intermediate and final results must remain for the teacher to derive.
Otherwise, the teacher may introduce unsupported assumptions or leave essential work incomplete, weakening the resulting trajectory as training supervision.
In this paper, we introduce WorkFactory, a scalable framework for constructing executable tasks that support reliable demonstrations of professional work.
We derive diverse professional intents by combining occupational knowledge, skill documentation, and professional artifacts, broadening the range of work that can be instantiated.
For each intent, WorkFactory plans backward from the intended output using an information support graph.
Rather than connecting artifacts alone, the graph captures what each output depends on and where the supporting information should be available across reused and synthesized materials, while leaving derived results to the solver.
To catch violations introduced during materialization, an isolated probe attempts the task from solver-visible inputs; its diagnoses drive repairs that must restore support without changing the intended work.
Only environments that pass this process are then assigned to an independent teacher, whose execution provides the training trajectory.
Using WorkFactory, we construct 112,686 tasks spanning 612 occupations and collect teacher trajectories for supervised fine-tuning.

WorkFactory
3.1
Overview
WorkFactory constructs executable professional tasks for training working agents.
It first builds a diverse intent library from occupational knowledge, skill documentation, and professional artifacts, allowing task construction to cover both established workflows and realistic deliverables (§
3.2
).
For each intent, WorkFactory plans backward from the intended output using an information support graph, which coordinates dependencies and information locations across reused and synthesized materials while reserving derived results for the solver (§
3.3
).
Because these dependencies may be lost or contradicted when the environment is materialized, execution-based repair probes the task from solver-visible inputs and repairs blocking support failures without changing the intended work (§
3.4
).
Tasks that pass this process are assigned to an independent teacher for trajectory collection (§
3.5
).

3.2
Multi-source Professional Intent Synthesis
Professional intents specify meaningful work before its instance-specific materials are fixed.
To cover varied occupational competencies, WorkFactory draws on three different sources: (1)
occupational hierarchies
, which ground the work in roles and situations; (2)
documented skills
, which identify operations that can be composed into workflows; and (3)
real professional deliverables
, which provide cues about intended outputs and audiences.
The three synthesis routes reflect these roles: we expand occupations into roles situated in professional contexts, compose skills when one operation supplies inputs for another, and infer plausible objectives from deliverables through instruction backtranslation
[
16
]
.
An LLM-based review evaluates candidate intents from all three routes for internal coherence and feasibility, professional appropriateness, and plausibility as real workplace activities before adding them to the shared intent library
ℒ
\mathcal{L}
.
This offline library supplies intents for environment construction; Appendix
A
details the synthesis routes.

3.4
Execution-based Environment Repair
Execution-based repair tests the realized environment for missing or inconsistent support that remains after construction.
The loop consists of probe diagnosis, revision, and revalidation.
Diagnosing the realized environment.
A probe solver attempts the task using only the solver-visible request and resources, without access to the graph, construction plan, or prior execution outputs and diagnoses.
It grounds reported environmental issues in inspected sources and tool observations, identifies the affected operations and deliverables, and distinguishes them from solver mistakes and legitimate open choices.
Only issues judged to block task completion trigger repair; tasks without such blockers proceed to trajectory collection.
Appendix
C.1
details the reporting criteria.
Repairing and revalidating the environment.
A refiner uses the diagnoses to edit the request and existing reference files while preserving the files’ number, types, and dependency structure; the information support graph remains the initial construction specification.
For example, adding a missing USD 0.15-per-carton freight term for Supplier C restores the support needed for an NPV comparison without performing the comparison for the solver.
Each revision must pass construction checks and an LLM-based work-preservation review against the original intent and initial plan, assessing whether the core objective, essential operations, and substantive deliverable requirements remain intact.
Revised tasks that fail either check are discarded; those that pass undergo a fresh probe execution.
The loop stops when no blocker remains or the repair budget is exhausted, with unresolved tasks discarded.
Retained tasks form the candidate pool

\mathcal{T}
for trajectory collection.

Task Corpus and Training Data
Teacher trajectory collection and supervision.
For every task in
𝒯

, GLM-5.3
[

The teacher receives the current request and accessible resources, without the information support graph, construction plan, or prior probe outputs and diagnoses.
The resulting interaction trace and produced deliverables form an initial teacher rollout.
We retain rollouts that pass lightweight filters screening for repeated reasoning, repeated tool calls, and missing or insufficiently substantive deliverables.
For the 5% of tasks with the longest initial rollouts, measured by total interaction turns, we additionally apply selective rejection sampling based on pairwise deliverable comparisons; all other tasks retain their filtered initial rollouts.
Each retained rollout is then converted into one or more training sequences for masked autoregressive supervision.

Scaling Properties
We study how model performance and execution behavior scale with the number of distinct WorkFactory training tasks on GDPval under the GDPval-AA evaluation protocol.
Our primary sweep trains Qwen3.6-35B-A3B on nested subsets of 2K–110K tasks; we additionally evaluate Qwen3.6-27B and Qwen3.5-4B at 30K and 110K.

Performance improves with model-dependent diminishing returns.
Figure

For Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, Elo rises from 879 to 1565: the first 5K tasks contribute 447 points, whereas scaling from 75K to 110K adds only 13.
By 30K tasks, the 35B-A3B and 27B models have captured 88% and 94% of their respective gains from the base model to the 110K-task checkpoint, showing different patterns of diminishing returns across the sampled model scales.
The 4B model also gains 546 Elo by 30K tasks, showing strong early data efficiency at the smallest tested scale.
A two-checkpoint comparison on JobBench shows the same direction: Qwen3.6-35B-A3B improves from 58.80 at 50K tasks to 62.35 at 110K.
Execution length first expands and then contracts.
As shown in Figure
5
(b), the mean execution length of Qwen3.6-35B-A3B first rises from 24.87 turns at baseline to 162.88 at 2K tasks, then falls to 85.90 at 110K while Elo continues to improve.
The fraction of its GDPval-AA trajectories reaching the 250-turn limit also falls from 21.9% at 2K to 2.7% at 110K.
Across the sampled checkpoints, the 27B and 4B models also show an increase from their bases to 30K tasks followed by a decrease at 110K; the 4B model uses longer trajectories than the other two models at both data scales.
Figure
5
(c) further shows that, for Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, code-execution failures decline from 16.0% at baseline to 6.3% at 110K.
After rising to 22.4% at 2K, web-search failures decrease to 10.4% at 110K, whereas web-fetch failures remain at approximately 40%–48%.
Together, these observations show that performance and execution length evolve differently as training data increases: longer attempts emerge at small data scales, followed by shorter executions and lower failure rates for some tool types at larger scales.
4.4
Component Analysis
We compare three 5K-task synthesis conditions: direct artifact-level planning, information-level planning, and information-level planning followed by execution-based repair.
The analysis asks whether explicitly planning and repairing task information improves both the conditions for teacher execution and the training utility of the resulting trajectories.
We assess task-environment quality using blocker- and correctness-level findings from a common probe solver (see §

For each defect category, incidence is the fraction of tasks whose report contains at least one hard defect in that category; categories may overlap.
We use the initial probe report for the first two conditions and the final report for the repair condition, including tasks later discarded for unresolved blockers.

5
Conclusion
We introduced WorkFactory, a framework for constructing executable professional tasks to support teacher demonstrations of occupational competencies.
Information-level planning specifies the dependencies and locations of required information, while execution-based repair addresses support failures exposed during task execution.
Using teacher trajectories from 112,686 tasks, we trained the WorkPilot family, whose three models improve over their corresponding bases across four professional-work benchmarks.
Data-scaling analyses reveal model-dependent diminishing returns and changes in execution length and tool-use reliability.
Controlled component studies connect reductions in probe-diagnosed environment defects to improved downstream training utility, supporting the value of coherent task construction for scaling working-agent supervision.
Limitations
First, GLM-5.3, our teacher model, accepts only text input. Consequently,
although the Qwen backbones include native vision and alignment components, we
train and evaluate them only through a text-only task-solving interface. This
work therefore does not examine learning from visual evidence directly. Future
work will extend trajectory collection and student training to multimodal
professional tasks.
Second, our training study focuses on supervised fine-tuning over teacher
trajectories.
Future work will leverage high-quality professional tasks and their execution
feedback to train working agents with reinforcement learning.
Ethical Considerations
WorkFactory trains agents to perform professional workflows and produce
realistic workplace artifacts. Such outputs may be incorrect, biased, or
misused in consequential settings, and should therefore receive appropriate
human review rather than replace qualified professionals. Dataset and model
release should also respect privacy, copyright, and licensing requirements.

Appendix A
Professional Intent Synthesis
Occupation-driven synthesis.
Starting from a public occupational hierarchy derived from the Standard
Occupational Classification
[
33
]
, we expand broad occupations
into fine-grained roles. These roles seed a search agent that retrieves
professional context from the web as needed and generates work situations
and objectives grounded in role responsibilities, business settings, and
motivating events.
Skill-driven synthesis.
We collect documented skills from a public skill directory
4
4
4
https://skills.sh/
and analyze each skill’s purpose, applicability conditions, prerequisites,
and operations to derive work situations and objectives. We combine skills
into candidate multi-step workflows when one skill’s output can supply inputs
required by another and the operations jointly serve a coherent professional
objective. For example, quotation extraction supplies supplier cost inputs for
annual cash-flow modeling, whose results feed NPV comparison and sourcing
recommendation.
Deliverable-driven synthesis.
Instruction backtranslation generates candidate instructions for existing
text
[
16
]
. Inspired by this, we use real
professional deliverables collected from the web as cues for plausible work
intents. We analyze their likely use, audience, content, and implied
production process to derive intents for producing, updating, reviewing, or
comparing such artifacts.
Intent review.
An LLM reviewer evaluates whether each candidate intent is coherent and
feasible, professionally appropriate, and plausible as a real workplace activity.

Appendix B
Information-level Planning and Task Instantiation
WorkFactory represents the information required by a task together with the
artifacts that provide or receive it. The representation distinguishes a
planned output or internal construction requirement from information already
available to the solver. Internal construction requirements serve only
as an intermediate specification during synthesis, connecting the intended
work to the request and supporting materials. They contain no instance-specific
facts and add no obligations unavailable to the solver.
All goals, constraints, and instance-specific information needed for completion
must be expressed in or obtainable through solver-accessible inputs.

B.2
Carriers and Access
Carrier nodes identify where information occurs: the user request, reference

Runtime quality assessment reports describe issues and assign severity labels.
A probe solver is informed that the task is synthetic and instructed to produce the best feasible deliverable.
It grounds each diagnosis in inspected sources and tool observations, identifies affected operations and deliverable requirements, and distinguishes environmental defects from solver mistakes and legitimate open choices.

C.4
End-to-End Supplier-Selection Example
The following synthetic instance traces the running example from planning to
training. The intent is to evaluate lifecycle sourcing costs and recommend a
supplier for shipping cartons across four anonymous candidate vendors and a
three-year program. During instantiation, the planner specifies annual demand
of 48K, 60K, and 78K cartons and a 60:40 standard-to-insulated mix. Supplier
quotations provide unit prices, setup fees, and freight terms, while visible
rules specify an 8% discount rate for years 1–3, one-time setup costs in year
0, per-carton freight, USD-denominated constant prices, and no discounting of
the year-0 setup fees.
The graph marks the demand forecast, quotations, cost-timing rules, and vendor
profiles as supplied information. Transformation relations connect demand and
carton mix to annual carton volumes, then combine those volumes with each
vendor’s quotation to derive year-0 setup, annual spend, and vendor NPVs. The
NPVs and vendor profiles jointly support a cost–service recommendation.
Carrier bindings place the objective and rules in the user request, the
quotations and demand forecast in a reference PDF, and the inputs, model,
decision, recommendation, and assumptions in a future cost-comparison workbook.
The carton volumes, spending, NPVs, and recommendation remain solver-derived.
In the realized environment, Supplier C’s quotation does not state whether
freight is included or charged separately. A clean probe can diagnose that the
visible inputs do not support a comparable lifecycle-cost calculation. The
refiner adds a synthetic term specifying an additional freight charge of
USD 0.15 per carton to the quotation PDF, after which construction checks and
work-preservation review are repeated. A fresh probe then attempts the revised
task without seeing the earlier diagnosis. Neither initial construction nor
repair supplies the spending calculations, vendor NPVs, or final
recommendation.
Finally, an independent teacher completes the cost-comparison workbook across
all four vendors and produces a justified sourcing recommendation from the
solver-visible inputs. The resulting rollout is processed for training as described in
Appendix
D.3
.

Lightweight rollout filtering.
Every initial teacher rollout undergoes programmatic validity checks. We
exclude rollouts that contain repeated reasoning spans or repeated tool calls,
fail to produce a deliverable, or produce deliverables below a minimum content
threshold. These corpus-wide checks target degenerate or incomplete
executions; they do not provide task-specific judgments of deliverable
correctness.
Selective trajectory rejection sampling.
In addition to the corpus-wide filters, we select the longest 5% of initial
teacher rollouts for rejection

rollout rather than individual training sequences, before pairwise
deliverable comparison. Initial rollout length serves as a heuristic for
allocating additional deliverable-quality checks.
For each selected task, the pairwise judge reads the user request, reference
artifacts, and both agents’ deliverables, without access to the internal
construction requirements. A DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 baseline deliverable is
generated once per task and held fixed across comparisons. A teacher win
retains the corresponding rollout. If the initial teacher attempt loses
or ties, we allow up to three additional attempts (four teacher attempts in
total), stopping at the first win. Each retry starts in a clean sandbox with
the same task inputs and generation settings, without access to previous
attempts or judge feedback. A task contributes no training episode if none
of its attempts wins; that task and its workspace are excluded from the final
corpus. Tasks outside the selected subset retain their initial teacher episodes
after lightweight filtering.

E.2
Pairwise Judge Prompt
You are the pairwise evaluator for GDPVal-AA. Determine which blinded submission better fulfills the original professional task.
Evaluate both submissions using all available evidence from the initial task, reference files, extracted file text, rendered document images, native media, and submission files. Consider correctness, completeness, usability, professional quality, and faithfulness to requested deliverables.
Rules:
1. The identities of the systems are intentionally hidden. Never infer model identity from style, filenames, metadata, or self-description, and never favor Submission A or B because of its position.
2. Inspect both submissions independently before comparing them. Missing, empty, corrupt, or unusable deliverables are substantive failures.
3. For factual or pointable claims, rely on evidence actually present in the submitted artifacts. A submission’s claim that it is correct is not proof.
4. For visual deliverables, use rendered images as well as extracted text. When visible rendering conflicts with extraction, trust the visible artifact.
5. Use tie only when the submissions are genuinely equivalent overall or the available evidence cannot support a defensible preference. Do not use a tie merely because each submission has different strengths.
6. Check all material requirements in the original task, but keep the response concise. Return only the most decision-relevant comparisons.
Return exactly one JSON object containing (i) a winner in {A, B, tie}, (ii) a concise evidence-grounded overall explanation, and (iii) at most ten decisive comparisons. Each decisive comparison contains the relevant criterion, which submission is better, and a short specific evidence pointer for each submission. Return valid JSON without Markdown or surrounding text, and do not include model names, guessed identities, Elo ratings, pointwise scores, or hidden metadata.
