Yan Gong Zheng Chu Member, IEEE Wei Koong Chai Hari Mohan Pandey Jingrui Hou Affiliation: Miao Zhang, Member, IEEE , Yuanjian Li Member, IEEE , and Pei Xiao Senior Member, IEEE Abstract Semantic communication jointly optimizes inference accuracy, channel use, and computation, yet the underlying communication program is typically specified before training. This paper presents ComRSI (Communications-Oriented Recursive Self-Improvement), a framework for recursively evolving that program through large-language-model-assisted code edits. A fixed agent modifies the encoder, channel adapters, decoder, loss, and training policy; a protected evaluator screens candidates under prescribed budgets and resource constraints. Accepted code and textual feedback inform subsequent proposals, while candidate weights are trained from scratch. In the original ten-generation CIFAR-100 lineage, eight accepted revisions produce an attention-pooled code achieving 74.3% test accuracy over an Additive White Gaussian Noise (AWGN) channel at 32 complex symbols per image, compared with 67.2% at 256 symbols for the initial design. The program also improves fading-channel accuracy and transfers to Tiny-ImageNet after retraining. A repeat study with ten searches per method compares ComRSI against non-recursive agents with and without textual history. Mean final search scores balancing accuracy, transmission rate, and computational cost are 0.7587, 0.7376, and 0.7089, respectively; the mean numbers of accepted revisions per search are 6.0, 5.0, and 2.1. The advantage over the memoryless agent is supported by a two-sided rank test, whereas the difference from the history-equipped agent remains statistically inconclusive at this sample size. Archived proposals show that pooling can yield a large improvement, while retained history supports subsequent refinement. These results support recursive program optimization within the evaluated setting and expose its remaining rate-robustness and compute-placement trade-offs. Index Terms: Semantic communications, automated algorithm design, large language models, recursive code evolution. I Introduction Semantic communication seeks to preserve the information needed by a receiver’s inference task under communication and computational constraints [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] . For image classification, the relevant design question is not simply how accurately an image can be reconstructed, but where semantic processing should occur and which representation should cross the channel. Encoder depth, the location of the transmitter-receiver split, representation dimension, channel adaptation, and the training objective jointly determine the resulting accuracy-rate-compute trade-off. Fig. 1: Three complementary objects of adaptation. L1 changes operating variables, L2 changes learned weights, and L3 changes the communication program. ComRSI implements L3 through recursive code inheritance; the proposal agent and evaluation protocol remain fixed. The distinction concerns what changes, not a ranking of system intelligence. Deep Joint Source-Channel Coding (DeepJSCC) established the effectiveness of learning channel-resilient representations [ 4 ] . Subsequent work introduced feedback [ 5 ] , attention conditioned on the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) [ 6 ] , adaptive rate control [ 7 ] , and information-bottleneck formulations for edge inference [ 8 ] . These advances also illustrate the breadth of the design space: a useful improvement may require changing a representation or processing block, rather than adjusting weights or selecting a value from a fixed configuration space. Fig. 1 distinguishes three objects of adaptation. Runtime control changes operating variables; model adaptation updates learned weights; program evolution changes the implementation that determines what is trained and executed. These are complementary capabilities, not a universal hierarchy of communication systems. This paper studies the third capability: an agent edits a communication program, evaluates the proposed changes, and recursively builds on accepted code. Recent agentic wireless systems automate workflow design and component orchestration [ 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 ] . In parallel, language-model-driven program search has produced executable solutions in mathematical and algorithmic domains [ 13 , 14 ] . Applying this approach to communication design poses two challenges. First, candidates must respect the physical channel and resource accounting even when their source code changes. Second, repeated access to validation feedback can reward over-specialization to the evaluator. An increasing search score alone therefore cannot establish an improved communication system. We introduce ComRSI (Communications-Oriented Recursive Self-Improvement), a framework that combines recursive code inheritance with a protected training and evaluation pipeline. The inherited artifact is a program specifying the semantic encoder, channel adapter, decoder, loss, and training-SNR policy. Each candidate is trained from scratch; model weights are not inherited. A fixed Large Language Model (LLM) agent proposes edits, and a fixed selector accepts a child only when its public score improves by a prescribed margin and passes a paired image-bootstrap check. Hidden test data and channel-evaluation results are withheld from the search loop and evaluated after the relevant searches are complete. Our research question is whether this procedure can produce successive, auditable improvements in task-oriented communication and whether the selected designs retain useful accuracy-rate trade-offs beyond the public evaluation conditions. In ComRSI, the fixed LLM agent and the evolving communication program are treated as components of an integrated system. Communication is a functionality of this system, and recursively modifying its communication program therefore constitutes system-level self-improvement of that functionality. Each accepted program becomes the parent of the next generation, enabling successive improvements through inherited code modifications. The underlying LLM agent and evaluation protocol remain fixed; thus, the self-improvement concerns the system’s communication functionality rather than the agent’s own optimization capability. The contributions are as follows. • We introduce ComRSI, a communication-specific framework for recursive evolution of executable task-oriented semantic communication programs. Building upon general-purpose code-evolution paradigms, ComRSI integrates editable transmitter–receiver architectures and training policies with protected physical-channel constraints, resource accounting, and statistically gated evaluation. • We construct comparisons between recursive and non-recursive code proposals, configuration-space random search and mutation, and Bayesian optimization. The main CIFAR-100 comparisons use the same nominal proposal and training allocation. We distinguish code inheritance from textual history, extend both auxiliary searches to ten generations, and repeat the three agent variants ten times each. The analysis separates retraining variation from variation across searches. • We identify the communication structure produced by the recorded search: a deeper transmitter, a pooled semantic vector, and a small SNR-aware receiver. The final design reaches 74.3% hidden-test Additive White Gaussian Noise (AWGN) accuracy at 32 complex symbols per image, with improved fading-channel accuracy and successful program transfer to Tiny-ImageNet. The analysis also characterizes the cost of these gains: a 64-symbol ancestor is more robust at low SNR, most inference computation moves to the transmitter, and the average advantage over a non-recursive agent with textual history remains uncertain with ten searches per method. Sections IV and V describe the experiments and findings, and Section VI discusses their scope. II Related Work II-A Learned and Task-Oriented Communication DeepJSCC learns an encoder-decoder pair for transmission over a noisy channel [ 4 ] , with extensions to feedback [ 5 ] , channel adaptation [ 6 ] , and variable-rate transmission [ 7 ] . DeepSC [ 15 ] and broader semantic communication formulations [ 1 , 2 ] emphasize meaning or task utility, while task-oriented edge inference explicitly couples representation compression and inference quality [ 8 ] . These works motivate the design variables studied here. ComRSI contributes a procedure for modifying communication implementations; it does not claim that pooled task representations or channel-aware neural receivers are themselves new principles. II-B Agentic and Self-Evolving Wireless Systems Self-evolving wireless architectures seek adaptation beyond manually managed configurations [ 9 , 10 ] . A2SSC applies agents to adaptive semantic speech communication [ 16 ] . WirelessAgent++ formulates executable agentic workflow design as program search [ 11 ] ; it should therefore not be characterized merely as parameter tuning. Agentic TokenCom jointly considers workflow structures and component instances through planner, evaluator, and reflector agents [ 12 ] . The distinguishing object in ComRSI is the learned communication program itself, including the transmitter-receiver representation boundary and training objective. Its evaluation focuses on physical-channel accounting and the behavior of selected programs under withheld channel-evaluation conditions. II-C Automated Design and Recursive Program Search Neural architecture search [ 17 , 18 ] and hyperparameter optimization [ 19 , 20 , 21 ] provide natural controls for automated communication design. Their practical results depend on the supplied search space. Language-model-based evolution [ 22 ] , optimizer discovery [ 23 ] , FunSearch [ 13 ] , and AlphaEvolve [ 14 ] instead use code as a flexible candidate representation. A separate literature studies systems that improve their own problem-solving machinery, from theoretical self-referential programs [ 24 ] to STOP [ 25 ] , the Darwin Gödel Machine [ 26 ] , and AI research agents [ 27 , 28 ] . ComRSI shares recursive inheritance and executable evaluation with this literature, but keeps the proposing agent fixed. Its empirical question is whether inherited edits to a communication program produce useful designs under channel and resource constraints. Accordingly, the evidence is interpreted as recursive program optimization rather than improvement of the optimizer itself. III System Model and Recursive Code Evolution III-A Framework of ComRSI Fig. 2 presents the overall ComRSI framework, which enables a task-oriented semantic communication system to evolve through successive modifications of its executable program. Rather than optimizing only the parameters of a predefined neural architecture, ComRSI allows an LLM-based agent to modify the communication system itself, including its network structure, semantic representation, channel adapters, training objective, and configuration. The framework comprises three interconnected components: an editable communication program, a recursive code evolution process, and a protected post-search evaluation procedure. As shown in Fig. 2 (a), the communication program defines how an input image is encoded into channel symbols, transmitted through a physical channel, and decoded into a task prediction. The semantic encoder, transmitter and receiver adapters, task decoder, loss function, and training-SNR policy constitute the editable design space. In contrast, the physical channel model, power normalization, evaluation metrics, and resource constraints are protected from agent modifications. This separation enables the exploration of different communication architectures while maintaining consistent physical-channel conditions and evaluation criteria. The core of ComRSI is the recursive evolution process illustrated in Fig. 2 (b). Starting from an initial communication program S 0 S_{0} , a fixed LLM agent examines the current program and its public evaluation feedback, then proposes four candidate modifications. Each candidate represents a potentially different communication design rather than merely a new set of trained weights. After validity screening and preliminary training, the two highest-scoring valid candidates undergo full-budget training with three independent seeds. Their performance is evaluated using a composite objective that balances task accuracy, communication rate, and computational cost. A candidate is accepted only if its improvement over the current program satisfies both a minimum score margin and a paired bootstrap criterion. Importantly, the accepted candidate becomes the parent program for the next generation. The agent therefore continues modifying the previously improved communication design, using both inherited source code and accumulated textual feedback. If no candidate satisfies the acceptance criteria, the current program is retained while the search history is updated. This recursive inheritance of executable code distinguishes ComRSI from approaches that restart each proposal from the initial architecture. Model parameters are retrained from scratch for each candidate and are not transferred between generations. Finally, Fig. 2 (c) illustrates the post-search evaluation. Selected programs are registered, retrained with fresh random seeds, and evaluated on withheld test images and channel conditions, including AWGN, Rayleigh fading, and Rician fading. These results are excluded from the proposal and acceptance loop, providing a separate assessment of the communication designs discovered during search. III-F Protected Evaluation and Information Flow The data manifests, channel, trainer, evaluator, selector, and budget constants are protected by SHA-256 checks before a generation and after agent proposals. The agent operates in a separate workspace with tool-level access restrictions and a permitted candidate checker. Static checks restrict imports and reject prohibited I/O and RNG reseeding. Integrity checks additionally replace the received code with noise and test permutation equivariance over the inference batch. For C C classes, the noise-only accuracy threshold is max ⁡ ( 5 / C , 1 / C + 0.05 ) \max(5/C,1/C+0.05) ; normalized transmitter and receiver permutation errors must be below 10 − 2 10^{-2} . A cheating fixture that stores encoder features for later receiver access is detected by the repository test. Search feedback contains public results only. Before hidden evaluation, a registration file records the evaluation targets, including all accepted ComRSI generations and comparator incumbents. The additional repetitions and auxiliary experiments use the same benchmark holdout, and hidden scores are never provided to the proposal agents. IV Experimental Protocol IV-A Data and Initial Communication System CIFAR-100 [ 30 ] is the primary benchmark: 45,000 official training images are used for training, 5,000 for public validation, and the 10,000 official test images for hidden evaluation. Tiny-ImageNet [ 31 ] provides 200 classes and 64 × 64 64\times 64 images; the corresponding split uses 90,000 training images, 10,000 public validation images, and the 10,000 official validation images as the hidden set. Fixed stratified manifests define the splits. CIFAR-10 is used only for development and is excluded from the reported comparison. The initial system S 0 S_{0} has a ResNet-style [ 32 ] transmitter with a stem and three residual stages of widths of 64, 128, and 256. A 1 × 1 1\times 1 projection produces an 8 × 8 × 8 8\times 8\times 8 real-valued latent, corresponding to R 0 = 256 R_{0}=256 complex symbols. The receiver lifts the latent to 256 channels, applies two residual blocks, and performs global pooling and classification. For 64 × 64 64\times 64 inputs, a stride-two stem preserves the latent dimensions. On CIFAR-100, S 0 S_{0} has 3.63 million parameters and 0.694 Giga Floating-Point Operations (GFLOPs) per image. It uses cross-entropy loss and a uniform training-SNR policy on [ − 3 , 9 ] [-3,9] dB. IV-B Compared Methods We compare ComRSI with human-designed references, non-recursive agent variants, configuration-space search methods, and restricted agent variants. The main agent-based experiments use claude-opus-5-5 as the proposal model, except for the earlier-agent experiment, which uses claude-sonnet-5-5 . All agent variants share the same public evaluator and acceptance gate. The methods differ in how candidates are generated, how previous evaluation results are used, and whether accepted program code is inherited across generations. Importantly, retaining the best accepted program for evaluation does not necessarily imply inheriting its code for subsequent proposals. Initial system and hand tuning. The initial system S 0 S_{0} is the human-designed communication architecture described above and serves as the common starting point for automated searches. Hand-tuned S 0 S_{0} selects the best configuration from ten configurations evaluated over two tuning rounds, while retaining the original architecture. These references establish the performance of the initial communication design before automated optimization. ComRSI (ours). At each generation, the Opus agent directly modifies the current accepted communication program using its source code, previous proposal history, and public evaluation feedback. Candidate modifications may change network structures, semantic representations, channel adapters, training objectives, or configuration values. Accepted changes become part of the inherited program for subsequent generations. Thus, ComRSI accumulates improvements through both executable code inheritance and textual history, while model weights are retrained from scratch. Non-Recursive Agent with History (NR-H). This baseline uses the same Opus model and evaluation procedure as ComRSI, but starts every generation from the original program S 0 S_{0} . The agent retains textual descriptions of previous proposals and their public evaluation outcomes, allowing earlier ideas to inform subsequent edits. However, accepted source code is not directly inherited. The external selector still retains the best accepted program for evaluation and comparison. This baseline examines the contribution of direct code inheritance when textual history remains available. NR-H and ComRSI share the editable interfaces and nominal evaluation budgets; their distinguishing feature is the inheritance of accepted program source code. Non-Recursive Agent without History (NR-0). This variant of NR-H also starts each generation from S 0 S_{0} , but receives no accumulated proposal or evaluation history, and access to previous candidate workspaces is denied. Each generation therefore produces candidates without inherited code or textual feedback from earlier generations. The external selector nevertheless tracks the best accepted program across generations, allowing fair comparison of final incumbents. This baseline evaluates the search process without either form of inter-generation inheritance. Random mutation. This method randomly modifies configuration values of the current accepted program within a predefined search space. Accepted modifications become the starting configurations for subsequent generations, whereas rejected proposals leave the incumbent unchanged. It therefore preserves recursive inheritance of configuration changes but replaces agent-directed source-code modification with random configuration proposals. This comparison examines whether recursive inheritance alone is sufficient to achieve improvements comparable to ComRSI. Random search. This method independently samples candidate configurations from the predefined search space, including architectural and training hyperparameters. Each new configuration is generated without using previous evaluation outcomes to guide sampling. The external selector retains the best accepted configuration, but earlier candidates do not become parents of subsequent proposals. Unlike ComRSI, the method explores a fixed configuration space rather than generating unrestricted program edits. Optuna TPE. This method uses the tree-structured Parzen estimator [ 19 , 21 ] to guide configuration search. Unlike random search, TPE uses the configurations and scores of previous evaluations to model promising regions of the predefined search space and select subsequent candidates. It therefore incorporates historical performance information into the proposal process, but does not recursively inherit modified program source code or introduce new operations outside the specified configuration space. Earlier-agent run. This variant follows the same recursive code-evolution procedure as ComRSI but replaces claude-opus-5-5 with the archived Sonnet proposal model ( claude-sonnet-5-5 ). The original search was subsequently continued to ten generations, as described below. Restricted-file agent. This variant uses the Opus agent and recursive code inheritance but restricts editable files to the training loss, training-SNR policy, and configuration files. Unlike unrestricted ComRSI, the agent cannot directly rewrite the encoder, channel-adapter, or decoder implementation files. However, editable configuration values may still change network width, latent dimension, or other architecture-related settings. This comparison examines the effect of restricting the scope of program modification rather than eliminating architectural adaptation entirely. The predefined configuration space for random mutation, random search, and Optuna TPE includes network width, depth, latent channels, SNR-FiLM [ 33 ] , squeeze-and-excitation [ 34 ] , label smoothing, optimizer settings, and training-SNR bounds. These methods cannot express every communication program available through unrestricted code editing. For example, their weight-decay range is limited to 10 − 3 10^{-3} , whereas the protected trainer permits values up to 2 × 10 − 3 2\times 10^{-3} . Consequently, these comparisons evaluate complete optimization procedures with different search capabilities, rather than isolating proposer quality under identical expressive power. VI Discussion and Limitations VI-A Discussion The experimental results demonstrate the potential of recursive code evolution for task-oriented semantic communication. ComRSI progressively improves executable communication programs by inheriting accepted modifications, enabling cumulative refinement of model architectures, training strategies, and communication configurations. The discovered 32-symbol design achieves higher classification accuracy and substantially lower communication overhead than the initial system, while maintaining strong performance under fading channels. The repeated experiments further demonstrate the value of retaining previous discoveries during communication-program optimization. ComRSI achieves the highest mean PublicScore and hidden-test accuracy across ten searches per method. Both ComRSI and NR-H continue improving after discovering pooled semantic representations, highlighting the importance of accumulated code and textual knowledge. The advantage of ComRSI over NR-0 is statistically significant, while its mean advantage over NR-H has not reached statistical significance. The discovered 32-symbol and 64-symbol designs also illustrate the trade-off between communication efficiency and channel robustness. This highlights the flexibility of the proposed framework, where different communication objectives and constraints can guide recursive program evolution toward different operating requirements. VI-B Limitations and Future Directions The current evaluation focuses primarily on image classification under simulated wireless channels. Future research will extend ComRSI to more diverse communication tasks, datasets, and realistic channel conditions to further assess its general applicability. Another direction is deployment-aware optimization, incorporating latency, energy consumption, and device-specific computational constraints. Future extensions will also investigate broader communication objectives and adaptive program evolution under changing channel and task conditions. Future deployment-oriented evaluations can incorporate channel estimation, pilot overhead, digital modulation, finite-blocklength coding, and adaptive rates under latency and energy budgets. Future work may incorporate structured reasoning memory, to reuse successful and unsuccessful communication-program editing experiences [ 35 ] . Joint evolution of the communication program and its proposal agent offers another direction [ 36 ] . VII Conclusion This paper presented ComRSI, a framework for recursively evolving executable programs for task-oriented semantic communication. By inheriting accepted code modifications, ComRSI enables progressive optimization of communication architectures, training strategies, and transmission configurations under protected evaluation constraints. Experiments on CIFAR-100 demonstrate substantial improvements in classification accuracy and communication efficiency, with the discovered 32-symbol program outperforming the 256-symbol initial design. The evolved program also demonstrates robustness under fading channels and transferability to Tiny-ImageNet. Repeated experiments across ten independent searches per method further show that ComRSI achieves the highest mean performance among the evaluated search approaches. These findings highlight recursive code evolution as a promising approach to automated semantic communication design. Future work will extend the framework toward multi-task communication, adaptive channel-aware optimization, and deployment-oriented communication systems.
