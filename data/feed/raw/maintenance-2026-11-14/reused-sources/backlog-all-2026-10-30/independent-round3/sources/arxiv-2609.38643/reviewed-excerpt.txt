Recursive Organization Improvement: A Modeling Specification for Human–Agent Organizations Zilong Wang Affiliation: CataX AI Email: wangzilong@cata-x.ai Abstract Stronger AI agents do not automatically produce better organizations: teams must also learn which work arrangements to retain and when to reconsider them, while those arrangements determine what evidence the team can learn from. We propose a modeling specification for recursive organization improvement and evaluate it through an executable checker, a public-record mapping, and two simulation studies. The specification connects actor-visible histories, organizational memory, decision rights, and the evidence used to retain changes. Study 1 crosses six decision rules, three memory conditions, and three task environments under fixed resource ceilings. In a stationary environment, cumulative evidence raises balanced evaluation’s net value per task from 0.452 to 0.480, while selection regret falls from 0.029 to 0.002. After a workflow-ranking reversal, retained evidence delays adaptation. In post hoc acquisition-matched controls, the remaining contribution of program replacement depended on the memory rule: small and uncertain under an eight-round window, positive under cumulative memory after a reversal, and negative under reset evidence. Study 2 models human–agent review with shared errors, copying, and decision rights. It identifies a two-actor case in which public records conceal whether agreement comes from shared errors or copying, and shows how dependence changes the preferred placement of human judgment under a fixed budget. Paid observation can lower decision error while costing more than the deployment gain. Assessments of organizational improvement should track the evidence acquired and retained, using acquisition-matched comparisons to isolate the contribution of evaluator revision. A Preprint Keywords human–AI interaction ⋅ \cdot multi-agent organizations ⋅ \cdot organizational learning ⋅ \cdot workflow adaptation ⋅ \cdot recursive improvement ⋅ \cdot evaluation 1 Introduction Consider a software team that uses AI agents to draft pull requests (PRs). Faster drafting can lengthen the review queue. The team may respond by routing routine changes to automated checks and reserving human review for difficult cases. To evaluate this change, it must decide which PRs to audit, how to combine new observations with earlier evidence, and who may revise the routing or audit rule. An apparent gain can reflect a better workflow, a different sample of reviewed cases, or more effective reuse of evidence. How can the team distinguish these explanations and retain changes that improve delivery? We propose a modeling specification for this problem and evaluate it with an executable checker, a mapping of public PR records, and two simulation studies. The running PR example connects the specification’s fields to concrete organizational decisions; the studies isolate evidence acquisition and retention, then the review arrangements that generate observable judgments. Task-level productivity studies and studies of human–AI collaboration address different parts of this problem. Experiments on professional writing and evidence from customer support document productivity effects in particular settings [ 36 , 11 ] ; a meta-analysis finds substantial variation in the performance of human–AI combinations relative to their constituent members [ 44 ] . Organization design supplies the intervening objects: the division and allocation of work, dependencies, and integration of effort [ 39 , 32 ] . Complementary organizational investments can also affect the returns to a new technology [ 34 , 10 ] . Consequently, comparing agent models while holding the organization fixed answers a different question from comparing organizations that can revise their workflows. Workflow revision creates a measurement problem of its own. A new review policy changes both which cases receive attention and which failures become visible. If the same selected cases determine whether that policy is retained, organizational learning can favor a locally successful but globally harmful arrangement. An improvement procedure therefore needs an explicit account of the evidence it can acquire, the changes it can authorize, and the costs it incurs. The procedure may itself become a target of revision. Study 2 examines how review visibility and decision rights shape the evidence available for judging an arrangement. We call this problem recursive organization improvement (ROI; distinct from return on investment): evaluating and retaining changes to organizational arrangements, including changes to the procedures through which those arrangements are improved. The research object is the human–agent organization. Agent capability, human judgment, coordination, evidence access, and authority can change at different rates and under different constraints. This paper contributes a modeling specification that links organizational state, actor-visible histories, and the records used to accept a change. A revision names both the arrangement being changed and the procedure used to evaluate it. This makes it possible to reconstruct the evidence available at a decision, check editing rights and resource limits, and compare the outcomes of retained changes. Study 1, evidence acquisition and retention , holds label quality fixed and crosses reset, cumulative, and finite-window memory with fixed evaluation programs, one-time discovery, and repeated program assessment. Retention removes much of repeated assessment’s disadvantage in a stable environment but delays adaptation after a workflow-ranking reversal. The acquisition-matched control is central to interpreting the apparent gain from evaluator revision: a fixed program receives the same trials and reusable labels; the remaining difference attributable to program replacement depends on the memory rule. Study 2, review arrangements and evidence generation , derives organizational outcomes and visible judgments from individual errors, shared error sources, upstream exposure, and decision rights. Its contribution is to distinguish mechanisms that, in a two-actor case, can look identical in public records but respond differently to blinding, separate gold-labeled measurement from human override authority, and show how error dependence changes the preferred placement of humans under a fixed budget. Paid observation policies test whether identifying a better arrangement also recovers the cost of learning about it. Study 1 follows evidence through discovery, retention, and reassessment. Study 2 examines how that evidence is generated: blind review preserves private judgments, whereas exposed review can replace them with copied agreement. A team choosing a review arrangement therefore also chooses which judgments its improvement procedure can observe. Study 2 compares fixed observation policies for learning about these arrangements. 2 Conceptual foundations and modeling requirements 2.1 Organizational learning and explicit organizations Organizational learning connects experience to retained routines [ 29 , 33 ] . The distinction between the abstract understanding of a routine and its situated performance helps explain how routines can generate both stability and change [ 16 ] . Deliberate learning adds articulation and codification of experience [ 47 ] , while double-loop learning addresses governing assumptions and values [ 4 ] . Engelbart’s improvement-of-improvement distinction supplies a close conceptual antecedent for examining the procedures that generate organizational change [ 15 ] . ROI translates this question into versions, events, change targets, and outcome comparisons. Human–agent organizations also require an account of interdependence. Mixed-initiative interaction and levels of automation distinguish who initiates or performs a function [ 22 , 37 ] ; Coactive Design places the support for interdependent activity at the center of system design [ 25 ] . Appropriate reliance concerns when a person should use automated advice [ 28 ] . These choices alter information exposure, decision rights, and resource use, so they belong in the organizational model rather than being inferred from an actor’s capability score. Existing formalisms already supply much of the representational machinery. MOISE+ models structural, functional, and deontic organization; OperA represents organizational interaction and agent autonomy [ 24 , 14 ] . Process mining uses event data for process discovery, conformance checking, and enhancement [ 45 ] . The central comparison concerns organizational outcomes under specified changes, whether the candidate was written by a person, generated by an agent, or selected from a finite catalog. 2.2 From development practices to measurable arrangements Waterfall-like staging, agile development, and Extreme Programming suggest different settings for dependencies, batch size, feedback delay, test placement, and ownership. Royce’s original account includes iteration and the risks of late system testing [ 40 ] ; agile principles emphasize frequent delivery and reflection [ 8 ] ; XP provides more concrete testing, pairing, and integration practices [ 2 ] . These labels identify families of configurations rather than an ordering of organizational quality. A team can retain release gates while shortening internal feedback cycles, or introduce agents into an unchanged approval pipeline. For example, let drafting, review, and integration capacities be 4, 5, and 9 accepted-task equivalents per day. A deterministic serial capacity bound is their minimum. Doubling drafting alone raises the bound from 4 to 5; raising review capacity to 8 at the same time raises it to 8. The factorial interaction is 8 − 5 − 4 + 4 = 3 8-5-4+4=3 . This standard bottleneck calculation [ 3 , 30 ] isolates why technical and workflow changes can be complementary. Rework, variable quality, and demand must be modeled separately in an empirical application. Keeping a program name preserves the adopted decision; accumulating knowledge requires retaining its supporting observations. Evidence generated during organizational search can serve more than one decision. A rejected program trial may still produce valid labels about a workflow. We vary private error dependence and copying at fixed individual accuracy, compare review arrangements under a common budget, and test observation policies for learning which arrangement to deploy. 5.2 Private errors, copying, and bounded review arrangements A binary task has a proposer, agent reviewers, and, in some arrangements, a human arbiter. Humans have private error probability q H q_{H} ; conditional on task truth, their errors are independent of one another and of the agent group. Agents have private error probability q A q_{A} . With probability ρ A \rho_{A} , all agents on a task, including an agent proposer, share one error draw; otherwise their errors are independent. This construction preserves individual accuracy while varying private error dependence. The value of forgetting depends on the observation procedure. 6 Implications for human–agent organizations 6.1 Comparing capability and organizational change The specification makes an otherwise ambiguous comparison operational. A capability-only intervention changes actor profiles while keeping assignment, visibility, authority, and improvement procedures fixed. A workflow intervention changes those arrangements under a fixed procedure. A procedure intervention changes how candidate arrangements are evaluated or retained. A factorial study can compare these interventions and their interactions under the same task population and outcome criterion. The bottleneck example in section 2 supplies one elementary prediction; Study 1 shows how evidence acquisition and retention alter the value of organizational search; Study 2 shows how review arrangements generate the judgments that can become evidence. The distinction matters when AI changes both production volume and the work of supervision. Automation can redistribute monitoring demands [ 6 ] ; human and machine learning can become organizationally interdependent [ 42 ] . In Study 1, the same amount of acquired evidence has different value depending on whether the organization reuses it, discards it, or retains it after the relevant task relationship changes. Evidence retention should therefore be evaluated together with the workflow that generates it. The supplementary audit model in Appendix D examines the earlier decision to acquire information at all. Its finite-horizon value-of-information controller can acquire little evidence under a strong prior, while Study 1 asks what happens to evidence after it has been bought. Together the models distinguish acquisition, retention, and use. Neither an audit count nor a revision count alone measures an organization’s capacity to learn. 6.2 Independence and interaction order Distinct agents can share models, training data, tools, and explanations, producing correlated errors [ 13 ] . Correlated-vote extensions of Condorcet’s jury theorem analyze dependent judgments [ 27 ] ; information-cascade theory and social-influence experiments examine how exposure changes the use and diversity of private information [ 9 , 31 ] . Study 2 separates shared private errors from copying and shows that a two-actor public record can conceal this distinction. Recording initial judgments and reveal events makes the mechanisms distinguishable. Human-first and agent-first interaction differ through the information available when a person commits an assessment. Private commitment can preserve an additional signal, while early advice can supply knowledge the person lacks. The outcome depends on capability, covariance, reliance, and the aggregation rule. The elementary calculation in Appendix B shows that apparent gains from exposure can equal a reweighting of private evidence. Human–AI studies of appropriate reliance and cognitive forcing motivate behavioral measurements of responses to advice [ 28 , 7 , 12 ] ; their task and participant measures can be used to estimate how exposure changes reliance. 6.3 Preserving disagreement and separating observation from authority Study 2 identifies information exposure and decision rights as distinct design choices. When reviewers can copy an upstream decision, public agreement combines agreement already present in private judgments with exposure-induced conformity. An organization that escalates only visible disagreements can therefore suppress its own opportunity to correct mistakes. Retaining initial assessments, their sources, and reveal order makes these mechanisms distinguishable and supports comparisons of alternative review arrangements. A gold-labeled audit measures the performance of a review arrangement. A human override changes its final decisions. Their costs and benefits belong to different parts of the comparison: the audit informs future arrangement choices, while the override affects the current task. This distinction helps a team decide whether it needs better measurement, a different escalation rule, or a different final decision maker. 6.4 How the two studies connect Study 1’s workflow reversal and Study 2’s copying shift both change the relevance of earlier observations. Their different adaptation patterns show that memory must be assessed together with the process generating those observations. The acquisition-matched control separates evaluator replacement from a changed information path, and shows that its contribution depends on how long evidence is retained; the Omit echo ablation checks how a deliberately misspecified model interprets the same acquired record. Study 2 holds observation policies fixed, extending the operational comparison to review arrangements whose evidence is generated internally. 6.5 Validity and next empirical tests The reference checker and public-data mapping demonstrate how the specification links decisions to evidence, permissions, versions, and costs. A comparative evaluation of the specification could measure model-building time, agreement about missing records, and prediction accuracy when users model the same organizations with alternative notations. Study 1 uses a finite program catalog, known stratum weights, and retained scores from trials conducted at different knowledge states. A wider catalog and adaptive weighting of historical scores would extend the acquisition-matched comparison. Concept-drift, switching-bandit, and change-point methods provide alternatives to fixed-window retention [ 17 , 18 , 1 ] ; acquisition costs can be included in those comparisons [ 43 ] . Study 2’s next tests concern when to stop paying for observation, how deployed private judgments can supply feedback, and how production volume changes the value of that feedback. Field measurements would also need to estimate human copying of agent advice and whether recording a private commitment changes subsequent behavior. These measurements would connect the error and copying assumptions to a particular review task. For Study 1, an organizational test would randomize review-routing changes and evaluation coverage across comparable task streams, retain failed proposals, and measure escaped errors and human attention over a declared horizon. A second stage could randomize whether teams may replace the evaluation program. Such a design would separate the value of broader evidence from the value of the procedure used to discover and retain that evidence policy. For Study 2, an organizational test would randomize upstream visibility before private commitment across comparable review tasks, while recording actor identity, initial and final judgments, independent outcome labels, and attention costs. Separately varying escalation and override rights would distinguish the value of additional evidence from the effects of final decision authority. A later stage could randomize whether teams may replace the observation protocol, thereby testing procedure revision beyond the operational arrangement choices studied here. 7 Conclusion Recursive organization improvement asks how human–agent teams change their work and learn whether those changes helped. The proposed specification links organizational arrangements to visible evidence, decision rights, retained versions, and outcome comparisons. In Study 1, evidence reuse removes much of repeated assessment’s apparent disadvantage, while obsolete evidence delays adaptation. In post hoc acquisition-matched controls, the remaining contribution of program replacement depended on the memory rule: small and uncertain under an eight-round window, positive under cumulative memory after a reversal, and negative under reset evidence. Study 2 shows how review arrangements determine which judgments become observable and how shared errors and copying change the placement of human judgment under a budget. Lower decision error can still come with a net loss from continued observation. Organizational improvement should therefore be assessed through the evidence acquired and retained; acquisition-matched comparisons can isolate the contribution of revising an evaluator.
