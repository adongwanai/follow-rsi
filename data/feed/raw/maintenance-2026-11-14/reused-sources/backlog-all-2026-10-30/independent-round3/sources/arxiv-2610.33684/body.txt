MORE: Mode-Optimized Reasoning with Self-Adaptive Effort for Self-Evolving Agents Dianxing Shi Affiliation: Beihang University Dingjie Fu Affiliation: Huazhong University of Technology{dianxingshi, wangjieqiu} @buaa.edu.cn Tianyu Lei Affiliation: Beihang University Wangjie Qiu † † thanks: Corresponding author Affiliation: Beihang University Abstract Self-evolving training improves the performance of agents on difficult tasks through verifiable feedback, but it often applies costly deliberation uniformly across tasks. To address this problem, we propose Mode-Optimized Reasoning with Self-Adaptive Effort (MORE), a self-evolving training framework that adaptively selects both reasoning form and computational effort for each task. Specifically, MORE follows a two-stage training paradigm: (i) Stage 1 establishes a compact prior over direct answering, short and long chain-of-thought, and code-based reasoning, and (ii) Stage 2 self-evolves this prior with Mode-Adaptive and Test-time Effort PPO (MATE-PPO) on generated, verifiable task streams without curated data. By learning mode selection and token expenditure jointly, MORE allocates concise reasoning to tasks that permit it while retaining extended deliberation where it is useful. Across coding, mathematics, and general-reasoning benchmarks, MORE achieves consistent performance gains on Base and Coder backbones at model scales from 3B to 14B. During inference, MORE demonstrates higher accuracy and better efficiency, with markedly shorter response lengths and reduced inference time. Right : Across coding, mathematics, and general-reasoning tasks, MORE markedly improves average accuracy and efficiency over both its underlying backbone and Absolute-Zero Reasoner (AZR). 1 Introduction Self-evolving training, especially for coding and mathematics reasoning, is a post-training approach that aims to strengthen agents’ reasoning ability without human-supervision. ‌The prevailing existing methods designed for coding and mathematics reasoning use Proposer-Solver paradigm for reasoning models in which the model simultaneously learns to define tasks that maximize learnability and to solve them effectively, enabling self-evolution through self-play without relying on external data. In practice, people find that reasoning-oriented language models improve difficult problem solving by making intermediate computation explicit, showcasing superior performance. Typically, this intermediate computation is mainly produced by Chain-of-thought and Reinforcement learning. Chain-of-thought prompting and self-consistency establish that generated reasoning can improve answer quality ( Wei et al., 2023 ; Wang et al., 2023 ) , while test-time scaling shows that additional sampling or search can convert more inference compute into higher accuracy ( Brown et al., 2024 ; Snell et al., 2024 ) . Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards extends this principle into training: execution, answer extraction, and formal checks provide scalable feedback for coding and mathematical reasoning ( DeepSeek-AI, 2025 ; Zhao et al., 2025 ) . Despite their successes, these objectives do not specify how much computation is appropriate for each task. To this end, the absence of accuracy-computation calibration becomes consequential in self-evolving agents. Generated tasks, sampled solutions, and verifier outcomes continually reshape both capability and effective task difficulty. Repeated exposure to difficult verifiable tasks can make long reasoning a default hedge, even when a direct answer would suffice, increasing response length and completion time. Autonomous evolution can also introduce capability degradation and safety drift across coding, mathematical, and safety evaluations ( Shi et al., 2026 ) . Long reasoning is valuable when needed, but self-evolving agents must allocate it where its accuracy gains justify its cost. Difficulty-aware compute allocation addresses only part of this problem. CODA, for example, argues that simple instances often gain little from additional tokens whereas hard instances may still require deliberate computation ( Wu et al., 2026 ) . A scalar budget alone, however, can not decide whether the computation should be a direct response, a long derivation, or executable code. Conversely, choosing a discrete reasoning format without controlling its length can still produce verbose traces. Self-evolution adds another complication: a static reward schedule is calibrated to one policy state, even though the solver’s success profile and useful budget change during training. Given the above, we propose M ode- O ptimized R easoning with Self-Adaptive E ffort (MORE), a self-evolving training framework that learns four unique reasoning forms and computation amount as coupled policy decisions. Specifically, for task x x and response y y , MORE factorizes generation as π θ ​ ( a mode , y ∣ x ) = π θ ​ ( a mode ∣ x ) ​ π θ ​ ( y ∣ x , a mode ) \pi_{\theta}(a_{\mathrm{mode}},y\mid x)=\pi_{\theta}(a_{\mathrm{mode}}\mid x)\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x,a_{\mathrm{mode}}) , where a mode a_{\mathrm{mode}} is one of direct , short_cot , long_cot , or code . This factorization separates the form of computation from its amount while allowing both decisions to be optimized by the same training signal. Before producing the response, MORE emits a structured <mode> control token as its first internal decision. Thereafter, the token conditions the subsequent response and supports training and inference statistics. MORE learns this behavior in two stages. Mode-format supervision first makes the action vocabulary identifiable and gives each mode a stable response contract. M ode- A daptive & T est-time E ffort PPO (MATE-PPO) is our proposed Stage-2 optimization method for coupled mode–budget learning ( Schulman et al., 2017 ) . It integrates verifier-grounded correctness, online difficulty estimation, mode fit, and dynamic budget regulation into a single trajectory objective, so clipped policy updates jointly shape reasoning form and computation amount as a learned model capability. Our core contributions are as follows: i) We introduce MORE, a self-evolving training framework that jointly adapts reasoning mode and computation budget to online task difficulty. Across coding, mathematical, and general-reasoning benchmarks, MORE delivers strong and consistent performance. ii) Our two-stage learning design first establishes four mode-specific response contracts and then applies MATE-PPO to jointly optimize mode choice, response generation, and computational effort through a unified verifier-grounded trajectory objective. iii) We design an online difficulty–effort controller that converts evolving rollout clusters into difficulty tiers and updates mode-conditioned budget bands throughout training, enabling the policy to calibrate its reasoning allocation as its capabilities change. MORE also reaches its strongest observed out-of-distribution performance earlier during self-evolution than another framework in the same self-evolving paradigm. 2 Related Work Mode-aware reasoning. Hybrid reasoning models expose multiple response forms so that a solver need not apply one deliberation pattern to every query. Representative approaches distinguish direct answers from longer reasoning or combine textual and code-mediated computation ( Wu et al., 2025 ; Luo et al., 2025 ) . Uniform response behavior can be brittle under task-specific requirements ( Fu and Shi, 2025 ) . A fixed mode policy, however, does not track the changing competence of a self-evolving solver, and a mode choice alone does not constrain token use within that mode. Adaptive reasoning budgets. Inference-time scaling, budget forcing, and adaptive length objectives control how much computation a model spends ( Muennighoff et al., 2025 ; Zhu et al., 2025 ) . LASER conditions length-based reward shaping on difficulty ( Liu et al., 2025 ) , and further, CODA jointly studies difficulty estimation and compute allocation ( Wu et al., 2026 ) . This line demonstrates that uniform token budgets are inefficient, but it largely treats response format as fixed or allocates compute after the mode of reasoning has already been chosen. MORE addresses the intersection: it trains a self-evolving model based on the Proposer-Solver paradigm with a shared policy over reasoning mode and budget, and updates the associated control state as capability changes. 3 Methodology 3.1 MORE Framework Our proposed Mode-Optimized Reasoning with Self-Adaptive Effort (MORE) is a two-stage self-evolving training framework that enables models to learn both the form and the amount of computation used for each task. The model trained under MORE framework first emits one of four internal modes: direct , short_cot , long_cot , or code , and then generates a response under the corresponding reasoning contract and a difficulty-conditioned effort budget. Figure 2 summarizes the complete MORE framework. MORE’s Stage 1 constructs mode-format supervision to initialize a stable prior over the four reasoning contracts. MORE’s Stage 2 draws inspiration from AZR’s proposer–solver self-evolving paradigm ( Zhao et al., 2025 ) and it performs the overall reinforcement-learning procedure: it samples verifiable tasks, generates mode-conditioned rollouts, verifies their outcomes, updates online difficulty and budget state, constructs the MORE terminal return, and applies a Mode-Adaptive & Test-time Effort (MATE) PPO update. Telemetry from the same loop records capability, mode allocation, token economy, and system efficiency. Figure 2: Overview of the MORE framework. Mode-format supervision initializes four reasoning contracts. MATE-PPO then couples self-evolving verifiable rollouts with online difficulty estimation, mode–budget control, a joint terminal return, and a clipped policy update. The diagram summarizes correctness gating: mode-fit and joint credit require a correct response, while the budget-deviation term regulates overlength for both correct and incorrect responses. Stage 2 operationalizes MORE through a self-evolving proposer–solver training loop, with MATE-PPO providing its policy-optimization mechanism. For each sampled task, MORE generates a mode token and response under a pre-update difficulty context and mode-conditioned budget band. The verifier then supplies correctness, which MATE-PPO combines with the retained controller state to form the terminal return for a clipped policy update. The controller then updates the associated cluster’s difficulty context and mode-conditioned budget band for subsequent rollouts. The generated mode token remains a likelihood-bearing part of the optimized trajectory, linking mode selection, response generation, and effort calibration. Specifically, each question is paired with one of four output contracts: an answer-only record for direct , a compact COT rationale for short_cot , a detailed and complex COT rationale for long_cot , or executable CODE followed by an answer for code . The tags serve as internal supervision targets and do not appear as user-facing method names. They make response form observable to the model while preserving a common final-answer field to achieve consistent universality. For training data, all backbones use the same SFT corpus, AQuA-RAT 10.8K , obtained by processing the original AQuA-RAT examples into four reasoning-mode formats ( Ling et al., 2017 ; Wu et al., 2025 ) . The following subsections provide further details. 3.4 Adaptive Mode-Budget Control Online difficulty. The controller represents each task by structural and behavioral features. Firstly, Structural features include code shape, prompt artifacts, task family, and verifier target; behavioral features include rolling success and response length. Thus, a global success threshold would become stale as a self-evolving policy improves, whereas independent per-task estimates are sparse and noisy. Then, Clustering pools evidence across structurally and semantically comparable rollouts; the EMA follows policy drift without reacting fully to one outcome; and quantile tiers preserve relative difficulty when absolute success rises. Finally, the resulting tier indexes both mode-fit utility and mode-conditioned budget bands, applying stronger pressure toward direct or short traces in currently easy regions while retaining wider long-reasoning or code allowances in hard regions. Subsequently, a stable task identifier is assigned to a family- and verifier-aware cluster c c . After a verified rollout, its success estimate is updated by q c ( t ) = ρ ​ q c ( t − 1 ) + ( 1 − ρ ) ​ c t q_{c}^{(t)}=\rho q_{c}^{(t-1)}+(1-\rho)c_{t} , where c t ∈ { 0 , 1 } c_{t}\in\{0,1\} and ρ = 0.8 \rho=0.8 . Mature clusters are ranked by success: clusters above the 0.67 0.67 quantile are easy, those at or below the 0.33 0.33 quantile are hard, and the remainder are medium. The updated state is carried into subsequent rollouts, allowing mode preferences and budget bands to track the evolving policy rather than a fixed notion of task difficulty. As a likelihood-bearing policy action, it makes mode allocation an endogenous model behavior under the task distribution. Mode-conditioned budget bands. Each ( d , m ) (d,m) cell has a lower target B d , m − B^{-}_{d,m} and upper target B d , m + B^{+}_{d,m} . Correct response lengths are accumulated through hierarchical keys, from cluster/family/verifier/mode statistics to tier/mode aggregates. Once enough observations are available, the lower and upper targets use empirical quantiles Q 0.2 Q_{0.2} and Q 0.8 Q_{0.8} ; sparse cells retain the initialized prior. Downward updates are rate-limited and the lower target is constrained below the upper target, preventing one short batch from collapsing the band. The bands provide a soft learning signal that preserves flexible generation while progressively calibrating token use and the inference-time latent budget is determined by the band at final training checkpoint. MORE emits the mode token before the response body and then follows the corresponding response contract. The inference harness parses the generated token for telemetry and scores the resulting answer. The telemetry interface records mode ratios, per-mode success, difficulty tiers, budget bands, reward components, response-length distributions, throughput, and wall-clock components, providing a unified view of adaptive reasoning behavior. 4 Experiments Setup 4.1 Models and Training Configuration We use Qwen2.5-Coder-7B and Qwen2.5-7B-Base as backbones ( Qwen Team, 2024 ) . MORE uses four predefined response contracts and online difficulty estimates from task and rollout statistics. Its efficiency metrics are too coarse to resolve relative changes among finer-grained time components across training and evaluation. 7 Conclusion We introduced MORE, a two-stage self-evolving training framework that learns reasoning mode and computational effort as coupled policy decisions. Stage 1 establishes compact response contracts for direct answering, short and long chain-of-thought, and code-based reasoning. Stage 2 self-evolves this prior on generated, verifiable task streams without curated data, with MATE-PPO optimizing mode selection, response generation, and effort calibration within one trajectory objective. Across coding, mathematical, and general-reasoning benchmarks, MORE achieves strong and consistent performance on Base and Coder backbones and model scales from 3B to 14B. MORE reaches strong capability earlier during self-evolution while sustaining lower rollout token demand, shorter evaluation responses, and lower inference latency. Taken together, these research results establish MORE as a framework for task-adaptive reasoning form and effort, advancing efficient self-evolving training and its models’ reasoning through joint mode–budget learning. Appendix C Stage 2 Training Process Stage 2 uses one policy in a closed proposer–solver loop. At every update, the policy first constructs executable tasks, then solves tasks drawn from the corresponding typed pools. Both roles generate a mode token followed by a response, receive execution-grounded terminal feedback, and contribute trajectories to the same policy update. This loop continuously refreshes the training distribution as the policy improves. C.3 MATE-PPO PPO base for joint mode–budget control. MATE-PPO uses a PPO-style clipped actor update as its optimization base and augments it with a controller-conditioned terminal return. Unlike Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) ( Shao et al., 2024 ) , which forms advantages by ranking multiple completions of a common prompt within a local group, MORE must optimize heterogeneous proposer and solver behaviors under one shared policy. Proposer samples construct different executable tasks, whereas several solver modes can be correct and compliant with their own ( d , m ) (d,m) budget bands on the same task. Group-relative normalization would make such valid task constructions or mode contracts compete within a sampled group and remove return differences across task groups from the advantage estimator. The trajectory-level PPO objective instead preserves direct, shared-scale credit for task construction, mode selection, solution accuracy, and effort calibration across the six-stream update. It also maintains broad task coverage for the online controller without requiring repeated long rollouts from every frozen controller state. MATE-PPO constructs a unified terminal return by integrating role-specific execution feedback with difficulty-conditioned budget calibration, mode-fit utility, and mode–budget interaction. The same token is consumed by reward computation and telemetry, so mode learning, response generation, and evaluation share one representation. For each rollout, the runtime snapshots the current controller state ( D t , B t ) (D_{t},B_{t}) before generation. The generated mode token selects the response contract and indexes the band in that snapshot. After verification, the terminal MORE delta is added at the final valid response position using the same ( D t , B t ) (D_{t},B_{t}) state; the controller then incorporates the outcome and correct-length observation into ( D t + 1 , B t + 1 ) (D_{t+1},B_{t+1}) for subsequent rollouts. This composition optimizes the mode action and response tokens jointly while avoiding duplicate counting of the verifier reward. The 8,096-token rollout cap provides generation headroom, while the 6,144-token controller scale defines the normalized initialization of budget bands.
