October 2026 SkillSpec: Consensus-Gated Agent Skill Evolution via Representation Specialization Huancheng Chen Xiaodi Sun Zhaoqiong Huang Shenyang Huang Shreya Singhal Jingwen Lu Microsoft AI Natural-language skills are textual procedural memories through which large language model (LLM) agents retain reusable task knowledge without updating model weights. Existing methods typically treat skills as either static artifacts or monolithic documents optimized using aggregate validation scores as feedback. However, representing a skill as a monolithic document restricts optimization to its textual content, without explicitly modeling the structure through which procedural knowledge is retrieved and executed. We identify a key distinction between learning what knowledge to retain and determining how to organize it: textual updates should first be validated through execution evidence, after which the retained knowledge should be structured according to its procedural dependencies and retrieval requirements. To this end, we introduce SkillSpec , a two-phase framework comprising consensus-gated evolution and representation specialization. In the consensus-gated phase, complementary editing intents generate complete candidate skills. An update is committed only when paired evaluations reach consensus, requiring sufficient overall improvement and non-negative aggregate paired gain in every repeated evaluation. In the specialization phase, signals of process and redundancy sensitivity derived from the full optimization trajectory, including accepted and rejected candidates, guide the selection of a flat , graph , or hybrid representation. The validated backbone remains immutable, while a derived artifact is initialized and specialized in the selected representation. Across six benchmarks and four target language models, SkillSpec improves average success rate over SkillOpt by an absolute margin of 6.53% , averaged across the four models. These results demonstrate that reliable skill evolution and representation specialization address complementary objectives: deciding what knowledge to retain and how to structure it for inference. Correspondence: huancchen@microsoft.com Introduction Large language model (LLM) agents increasingly use natural-language skills to retain and reuse task knowledge without updating model weights. A skill is a textual procedural memory that encodes reusable steps, task rules, tool-use guidance, answer formats, and error-recovery strategies while remaining easy to inspect and revise. However, a fixed skill cannot anticipate every task-specific failure: execution trajectories may reveal missing procedures, conflicting guidance, or underspecified constraints. Recent methods therefore treat prompts and skills as optimizable state [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 ] , replacing one-shot construction with iterative revision informed by execution feedback. SkillOpt [ 6 ] takes an important step toward controlled skill evolution through bounded, validation-gated edits to a trainable skill document. Nevertheless, aggregate validation scores can obscure an edit’s behavioral effects: improvements on some examples may conceal regressions on others, and apparent gains may not persist across repeated evaluations. Existing optimizers also preserve a single-document representation, changing only its wording while leaving the organization of knowledge fixed. Flat representations can obscure procedural dependencies in sequential tasks, whereas unnecessary structure can introduce spurious dependencies and irrelevant context. Our key observation is that deciding what knowledge to retain and how to organize it are distinct optimization problems. The former requires reliable evidence that an edit improves behavior, whereas the latter requires evidence about procedural dependencies and interference. Addressing both in a single rewriting loop makes it difficult to attribute gains or choose an appropriate representation. We introduce SkillSpec , a two-phase framework that separates reliable content evolution from representation specialization. In the first phase, SkillSpec generates candidate skills under four complementary editing intents: repair , preserve , simplify , and rewrite . It compares each candidate with the current skill on the same validation instances. The consensus gate accepts an update only when it improves performance overall with non-negative aggregate paired gain in every repeated evaluation, while retaining evidence from rejected candidates to inform representation specialization. Building on the validated skill and the trajectory of candidate evaluations, the second phase estimates process and redundancy sensitivity, then uses these signals to select a flat , graph , or hybrid representation. Phase II preserves the validated backbone as an immutable source and specializes a derived artifact, allowing both its guidance and organization to evolve within the selected representation. We evaluate SkillSpec on six benchmarks, SearchQA [ 7 ] , DocVQA [ 8 ] , LiveMath [ 9 ] , ALFWorld [ 10 ] , SpreadsheetBench [ 11 ] , and OfficeQA [ 12 ] , using four frontier language models. Across the six benchmarks, SkillSpec improves average success rate over the no-skill baseline by up to 27.53%. Compared with SkillOpt [ 6 ] , SkillSpec yields gains of 12.26%, 6.59%, 3.12%, and 4.14% for GPT–4.1, GPT–5.4 Nano, GPT–5.4, and GPT–5.6 Sol, respectively, averaging 6.53% across the four models. These results show the largest gain on GPT–4.1, suggesting that explicit skill evolution and reorganization can remain valuable even when the underlying model is less capable. Our contributions can be summarized as follows: • We introduce consensus-gated skill evolution, which combines multi-intent candidate generation with paired validation to accept reliable content updates, reject edits that fail the aggregate gain thresholds, and retain evidence from rejected candidates to inform representation specialization. • We introduce trajectory-guided specialization, which uses Phase I evidence to select and optimize a flat , graph , or hybrid representation while preserving the validated backbone as an immutable source. • We evaluate SkillSpec across six benchmarks and four frontier language models, outperforming SkillOpt [ 6 ] by 6.53% on average across the four models. Related Work Prompt optimization. A growing line of work treats natural-language prompts as optimizable artifacts rather than static instructions. Search-based methods use task-level scores to optimize candidate instructions [ 1 , 2 ] , while DSPy compiles prompts and demonstrations for compound LM programs [ 3 ] . TextGrad [ 4 ] propagates natural-language feedback through a compound system, and GEPA [ 5 ] uses reflective trajectory feedback while retaining Pareto-efficient candidates over validation instances. These methods establish execution feedback as a practical optimization signal, but operate on prompts or LM programs rather than persistent agent skills. Skill evolution. SkillOpt [ 6 ] extends iterative optimization from prompts to persistent skills, using execution trajectories to propose bounded revisions and held-out evaluations to select updates. SkillOpt-Lite [ 13 ] replaces tree-based candidate merging with direct revision of file-based trajectories while retaining an independent validation gate. Subsequent methods prioritize candidates with greater refinement potential [ 14 ] , stabilize update directions and adapt edit budgets [ 15 ] , or train skill editors directly from execution feedback [ 16 , 17 , 18 ] . Other work repairs weak initial skills [ 19 ] or supports long-horizon repository curation [ 20 ] . Methods in this line improve how skill content is generated, selected, and maintained within a predefined artifact schema. SkillSpec builds on feedback-driven skill evolution with multi-intent candidates and paired, repeated validation, then reuses outcomes from accepted and rejected candidates to inform representation selection. Skill representation. Prior work structures skills at different granularities. Skill-Pro [ 21 ] represents an executable skill through initiation, execution, and termination conditions. SkillX [ 22 ] builds a multi-level skill hierarchy and iteratively refines its knowledge base, while HyperSkill [ 23 ] organizes subtasks and reusable skills in an evolving hypergraph for retrieval and maintenance. Graph-of-Skills [ 24 ] instead constructs inter-skill dependency graphs for inference-time structural retrieval, and AIP [ 25 ] compiles prose skills into schema-validated execution graphs. Most closely, AutoRefine [ 26 ] selects among rules, skills, and bounded subagents by compiling trajectory-derived interventions according to runtime ownership. Unlike methods that adopt a predefined structure or select among artifact types, SkillSpec uses outcome telemetry from accepted and rejected Phase I candidates to select a flat , graph , or hybrid representation for a validated skill before specialization. Tasks differ in how their skill guidance should be organized. For example, SearchQA [ 7 ] relies on independently retrievable evidence-selection and answer-extraction guidance, whereas LiveMath [ 9 ] benefits from an ordered procedure for formalizing and comparing theorem-style claims. A fixed representation may therefore fail to capture necessary dependencies or impose structure on otherwise independent guidance. SkillSpec captures this trade-off with two signals: process sensitivity measures dependence on ordered steps, while redundancy sensitivity measures vulnerability to duplicated, conflicting, or excessive guidance. Together, these signals guide the choice among flat , graph , and hybrid representations. Process and redundancy sensitivity estimation. Adaptive representation selection. Phase II selects among three structural representations: (a) flat forms a skill bank of independently retrievable units without an imposed execution order; (b) graph connects guidance nodes through explicit dependencies to encode the required execution order; and (c) hybrid combines an ordered core with independently retrievable auxiliary modules. Figure 2 illustrates these three structures. The selector first uses PSS ( ψ \psi ) to determine whether explicit ordering is warranted. For high-PSS profiles, RSS ( ρ \rho ) then distinguishes graph from hybrid . RSS measures outcome evidence for harm from interference , not the prevalence of redundant guidance itself. Low PSS therefore selects flat to avoid imposing unsupported order. High PSS selects an ordered representation: low RSS selects graph to encode dependencies directly, whereas high RSS selects hybrid to preserve an ordered core while modularizing redundancy-sensitive guidance. Because PSS and RSS are bootstrap proportions, we set ζ ψ = ζ ρ = 0.50 \zeta_{\psi}=\zeta_{\rho}=0.50 , treating a sensitivity as present when a majority of bootstrap datasets support its positive contrast. Specialization procedure. Phase II specializes the selected representation through iterative, validation-gated updates. At each epoch, the fixed policy model π θ \pi_{\theta} executes the current artifact on a training batch and uses the resulting trajectories and evaluator feedback to propose one revision, subject to the representation-specific update rules in Table 1 . The revision may modify guidance, topology, retrieval metadata, or auxiliary modules. The candidate is evaluated against the current artifact on the same held-out examples and adopted only if it passes the Phase II validation gate detailed in Appendix A.1 ; otherwise, the current artifact is carried forward to the next epoch. Conclusion We introduced SkillSpec, a two-phase framework for improving LLM agents through skill optimization without updating model weights. Phase I uses consensus-gated evolution to refine reusable knowledge, while Phase II uses trajectory-derived sensitivity signals to select and optimize a flat, graph, or hybrid organization. Across six benchmarks, SkillSpec improves the average success rate over SkillOpt by absolute margins of 12.26%, 6.59%, 3.12%, and 4.14% on GPT–4.1, GPT–5.4 Nano, GPT–5.4, and GPT–5.6 Sol, respectively. These findings suggest that skill optimization can benefit from considering both what knowledge an agent retains and how that knowledge is structured for execution. Appendix D PSS/RSS Estimation This section specifies the candidate-level annotation and fixed ridge-estimation procedure used for the sensitivity scores in Table 3 . It complements Equations 6 and 7 : the language model supplies evidence-grounded labels, while numerical code fits the outcome model and computes the scores. Annotation evidence and rubric The unit of annotation is a candidate’s edit relative to its round’s incumbent skill, not the benchmark as a whole. Each record is identified by an observation_id and linked to a unified skill diff. The diff-only annotation workspace exposes observation_id , candidate_diff , and diff_file ; numerical screening gains and candidate metadata are joined separately by the scorer. The annotator is instructed not to use or infer benchmark, dataset, environment, or sample identity, and to ignore intent names, acceptance decisions, optimizer summaries, tool counts, and the outer interaction format. A process annotation requires an added or strengthened ordered prerequisite, state handoff, or conditional dependency in the solution procedure. Generic advice to “reason step by step” is insufficient. An interference annotation requires added guidance that is visibly duplicated, conflicting, irrelevant to the surrounding skill, or disproportionately overlong. A negative screening gain is not itself evidence of interference. The same four-level strength scale is applied independently to both labels, as detailed in Table 6 . Table 6 : Candidate-diff annotation rubric. Strength describes the visible edit, not its measured benefit or the intrinsic complexity of the task. Score Process structure Interference 0 No grounded addition or strengthening of a dependency. No grounded addition of interfering guidance. 1 Weak or local prerequisite, state handoff, or conditional dependency. Weak or local duplication, conflict, irrelevance, or excessive detail. 2 Clear dependency in the revised solution procedure. Clear interfering guidance in the revision. 3 Dependency structure is a dominant feature of the edit. Interfering guidance is a dominant feature of the edit. The permitted interference modes are duplicated , conflicting , irrelevant , and overlong ; more than one may apply. Removing redundant text does not automatically receive a positive interference label: the instruction concerns what the candidate adds. If the diff is missing or supplies no grounded evidence, the prompt requests zero strengths and an empty mode list. The following illustrates the output schema, not a scored experimental observation: { "trajectory_observations": [ { "evidence_id": "<observation_id copied verbatim>", "evidence": "<specific evidence from the skill diff>", "process_structure_strength": 0, "interference_strength": 0, "interference_modes": [] } ] } There must be exactly one observation per source candidate, with a unique matching identifier and a nonempty evidence string. Both strengths must be integers from 0 to 3; nonempty interference modes require a positive interference strength. The annotator does not output PSS, RSS, confidence, or a representation choice. The validated annotation runner allows up to three generation attempts when output fails JSON or schema validation, rather than accepting malformed output or heuristically repairing it. Eligibility, regression, and round bootstrap Annotations are joined to recorded candidate outcomes by identifier. A candidate enters the regression if and only if it changes the incumbent, passes patch validation, and has a recorded screen_net_gain . Zero-gain and negative-gain candidates remain eligible, including candidates rejected by the update gate. Unchanged or invalid candidates and candidates without a screening outcome are excluded. The estimator uses screening outcomes, not pooled confirmation gains or final test scores. For Equation 5 , the implementation compares the two texts after splitlines() using difflib.SequenceMatcher with autojunk=False . Each non-equal block contributes the larger of its before and after line counts. The denominator is the larger total line count, with a minimum of one for the empty-text case. For each eligible candidate, the numerical features are ( c t , j , p t , j , p t , j 2 , r t , j , r t , j 2 ) . (c_{t,j},\,p_{t,j},\,p_{t,j}^{2},\,r_{t,j},\,r_{t,j}^{2}). Each column is centered and divided by its population standard deviation; a zero standard deviation is replaced by one. The design matrix also includes an intercept and one-hot indicators for round and candidate profile, omitting one reference level for each categorical variable. There is no process–interference interaction term. With X X denoting this design and y y the recorded screening gains, the fitted coefficients are β ^ = ( X ⊤ ​ X + Λ ) − 1 ​ X ⊤ ​ y , Λ = diag ⁡ ( 0 , 1 , … , 1 ) . \widehat{\beta}=(X^{\top}X+\Lambda)^{-1}X^{\top}y,\qquad\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(0,1,\ldots,1). Thus the ridge penalty is one for every non-intercept coefficient, including categorical controls. The implementation solves the linear system directly rather than forming the inverse. To evaluate a contrast, the scorer sets all rows’ process and interference strengths to the probe values, recomputes their squares, and retains each row’s edit size and categorical controls. It applies the fitted standardization, averages the predictions, and computes the extrema in Equation 6 .
