Verification and Self-Improvement in Agentic AI: Foundations and Limits Chien-Ping Lu Abstract Agentic AI systems can improve by searching longer, receiving additional support, or modifying how they propose and verify outputs. A performance score does not distinguish these mechanisms. We compare these changes through bounded verification with hidden terminal randomness. A stage specifies admissible transcripts, polynomial bounds, an alternating verification protocol, and a terminal checker. Its native reach uses default support; its closure frontier permits all support already admitted by the interface. Under a uniform pointwise probability gap and task-relative soundness, these are well-defined languages. We prove that independent majority amplification preserves both languages, whereas existential acceptance over random tapes can admit incorrect outputs. Exact verification is the zero-randomness case, with placement and completeness results. The randomized-verifier classes satisfy Σ k 𝖯 ⊆ Σ k 𝖱𝖵 ⊆ Σ k + 1 𝖯 \Sigma_{k}^{\mathsf{P}}\subseteq\Sigma_{k}^{\mathsf{RV}}\subseteq\Sigma_{k+1}^{\mathsf{P}} ; strict enlargement and depth separation require explicit complexity assumptions, while 𝖡𝖯𝖯 = 𝖯 \mathsf{BPP}=\mathsf{P} yields exact companions with the same frontiers. Representation analysis separates invariant acceptance from core-versus-support labels that can change under refactoring. For recursive self-improvement, uniformly bounded self-modification under a common sound interpreter and fixed verification protocol remains within the same verification class. A separate conditional-error budget controls false selection across adaptively chosen candidates. A quota-enforced XOR-synthesis family separates unbounded ratios of search success from changes in the accepted languages; exact and probabilistic audits check the resulting evidence requirements. The framework ties self-improvement claims to obligations on correctness, admissible evidence, verification resources, and selection error. 1 Introduction How do changes to an agent’s search, support, and checker affect which tasks its verification protocol can accept? Consider a coding agent that proposes a patch, receives test feedback, and proposes a repair. A higher success rate can have several explanations. The same proposer may have searched for longer. Previously unavailable feedback may now expose the defect. The final checker may enforce a different correctness condition. Or the protocol may require a proposal to survive every challenge in a specified domain. These changes have different consequences even when the software diagram still contains the same three boxes: proposer, critic, and checker. The distinction becomes sharper when the checker is randomized. A correct patch may pass reliably while an incorrect patch passes occasionally. Counting any successful run as proof of capability can therefore confuse a lucky acceptance with a valid solution. Repeated evaluation can reduce a known error probability, but cannot repair a judge that is systematically wrong. We study verification procedures for which both the probability guarantee and its connection to task correctness can be stated explicitly. Our unit of analysis is a deployed stage : a fixed family of permitted verification procedures, including its interfaces and resource bounds. The task relation records which outputs count as correct. A support state supplies bounded auxiliary information, such as a test result. A transcript records a permitted interaction. The exposure relation says which transcripts the interface admits, and the core is the terminal checker. The definitions concern families indexed by input length; a single finite model checkpoint or benchmark score does not determine such a family. Two sets summarize a stage. Its native reach contains the inputs with an accepted solution under default support. Its closure frontier contains the inputs with an accepted solution under any support the current interface already permits. The word “frontier” denotes this entire set, not its topological boundary. More attempts can make an existing solution easier to find without changing either set. Making optional feedback routine can enlarge native reach within the old frontier. Admitting a previously forbidden transcript can enlarge the frontier itself. An explicit finite example in Section 7 distinguishes all three changes. A separate soundness requirement connects the resulting quantified acceptance rule to task correctness. These conditions include exact checkers and certified randomized algorithms. They exclude generic learned judges for which only average accuracy is known. Section 9 gives a standard randomized algebraic check that satisfies the contract, and explains why calibration alone does not establish it. Recent self-improving agents make stage comparisons concrete: code, memory, and evaluation procedures can change between accepted versions, and an improved agent can help produce its successor [ 12 , 13 , 14 ] . We therefore distinguish the deployed stage, the procedure that modifies it, and the rule that selects a successor. Recursion describes a feedback relation among these components; it does not by itself establish a change in verification depth or escape from a declared frontier. The autonomy roadmap of Duan et al. [ 1 ] analyzes which improvement decisions a system controls and which changes its successors inherit. Its autonomy levels are distinct from the quantifier depth studied here. We take that loop perspective as motivation and ask a complementary question: which changes preserve a declared acceptance language, and what evidence certifies a change to it? Contribution. The contribution is a semantics for three comparisons: a better search policy can preserve every admitted task input; an enforced interface change can certify coverage beyond the old frontier; and a checker refactoring can change component labels while preserving accepted behavior. Native/frontier languages, a task-soundness contract, and representation invariants make these distinctions precise. Expressive completeness supplies a representation guarantee for language pairs at each declared level. Classical complexity tools locate those languages and give conditional depth obstructions, while uniformly bounded self-modification stays within a fixed verification class. A separate conditional-error contract bounds false successor selections. An executable XOR-synthesis family exhibits arbitrarily large ratios of per-attempt success probability with identical frontiers, and frontier changes caused by an enforced bound on candidate-program references. That bound is distinct from the number of search attempts. Finite audits check the examples and their evidence requirements. The hierarchy bounds and conditional non-compilability arguments use classical complexity theory; they are not new separations of complexity classes. The semantic containment diagnostic and bounded self-modification result do not require those separation assumptions. All structural comparisons concern declared verification families. They allow better search and higher empirical success within an unchanged frontier, and do not impose a rate or ceiling on open-ended self-improvement. The paper is self-contained. Section 2 supplies the deterministic background; Section 3 defines probabilistic stages and their exact specialization. Sections 4 – 6 establish stability, transition diagnosis, and conditional limits. Section 7 gives executable finite examples and a declaration protocol. Section 8 applies the framework to recursive self-improvement. Related work and scope limitations close the main discussion. Task and encoding. Specify input size, output meaning, the correctness relation, and any restricted domain. Separate test-suite correctness from full program correctness. 2. Stage and budgets. Trace permissibility needs an executable predicate or a proof; one observed trace establishes only one possibility. 3. Acceptance contract. Identify the final checker and provide task-soundness evidence. A randomized checker additionally needs a uniform gap argument. Average agreement is insufficient. A frontier escape claim additionally needs an accepted target input and evidence excluding all source-frontier witnesses for that input. 5. Audit status. Consider checking A ​ B = C AB=C for supplied n × n n\times n matrices over 𝔽 2 \mathbb{F}_{2} . Draw a uniform v ∈ 𝔽 2 n v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n} and accept if A ⁡ ( B ​ v ) = C ​ v A(Bv)=Cv , repeating twice independently and requiring both tests to pass. This is the standard randomized matrix-product check [ 6 ] . Correct products pass with probability one; incorrect products pass both independent tests with probability at most 1 / 4 1/4 . Thus the gap and task soundness hold uniformly. The row argument is in Appendix A.8 . Each test uses quadratic field operations after the matrices are provided. This example illustrates a valid probabilistic terminal checker; it is not a separation from deterministic polynomial time. By contrast, an LLM judge may confidently favor an incorrect answer on an identifiable subset. Its probabilities can satisfy the gap while its task soundness fails. Calibration of aggregate scores cannot prove either universal condition. Abstention is useful only with a proved coverage and error contract on the retained domain, or a sound fallback whose combined acceptance is analyzed explicitly. Merely removing uncertain examples does not remove confident mistakes. Stochastic proposal generation with an exact terminal checker can still use the exact stage semantics if the admissible transcript relation is justified; that semantics leaves production probabilities unmodeled. 9.2 Related work Bounded alternation and the polynomial hierarchy supply the deterministic background [ 2 ] . Arthur–Merlin systems distinguish when verifier coins are visible to a witness [ 5 ] ; our hidden-terminal-coin, pointwise-gap model is a particular restricted semantics. The classical randomized hierarchy bound supplies the sandwich proof [ 3 , 4 ] . These results classify languages. The stage layer adds an explicit comparison between default support and all support admitted by one interface, together with refactoring and transition semantics. Debate uses adversarial interaction to extend what a bounded judge can evaluate [ 7 ] . Here a universal block means every bounded challenge under the declared rules, and a learned debater does not automatically realize it. Proof certificates provide another way to discharge a universal claim on restricted families. Our certificate-substitution result isolates the soundness, completeness, and size conditions needed for that comparison. Test-time scaling studies how compute allocation affects performance, including search against verifiers and iterative refinement [ 8 ] . Reflexion uses feedback and episodic memory to improve subsequent trials without weight updates [ 9 ] . These mechanisms motivate distinct search-policy and support-interface coordinates. Their empirical success does not alone establish which stage frontier changed. Likewise, studies of LLM judges document agreement with human preferences alongside systematic biases [ 10 ] , and studies of intrinsic self-correction identify limits without external feedback [ 11 ] . Such measurements concern particular distributions and protocols; they do not establish the pointwise verification contracts used here. The MAST study further separates failures of system design, inter-agent coordination, and task verification [ 27 ] . This motivates recording the location of a failure rather than treating additional agents as evidence of stronger verification. 9.3 Limitations The results classify declared protocol families, not latent intelligence, training dynamics, or the probability that a model discovers a solution. Existential witnesses and worst-case universal moves idealize possible verification structure. A finite execution policy requires its own efficiency and reliability analysis. There is no claim that inference scaling must numerically plateau, that more support always helps, or that a changed stage must escape its predecessor’s frontier. The pointwise-gap model leaves average-case judges, distribution shift, and correlated errors unresolved. Randomized exposure can be represented at the level of admissible traces only when that relation is itself justified; reliable generation probabilities require an additional semantic object. Public coins require a different quantifier order. The strictness of the randomized hierarchy and its relation to standard 𝖬𝖠 \mathsf{MA} beyond the stated inclusion are not settled here. The synthetic completeness constructions demonstrate expressive capacity, and the finite audits check concrete examples; neither constitutes empirical validation of frontier claims for large deployed systems. 10 Conclusion Verification provides a precise basis for distinguishing mechanisms of self-improvement in agentic AI. The native/frontier pair records what a declared interface can accept with default support and with all permitted support. Exact verification and probabilistic verification with a uniform gap share this language-based foundation under task-relative soundness. Amplification preserves the frontier; making optional support native can enlarge native reach within it; and escape requires a failure of semantic authority or exposure containment. Depth adds a separate non-compilability constraint under explicit complexity assumptions. Recursive self-improvement introduces both a changing agent and a process that proposes and selects its successors. Under a common sound interpreter, fixed protocol, and uniform resource and gap contracts, bounded self-modification remains in the same verification class, even when a successor escapes the particular predecessor’s frontier. Separately, a conditional-error budget controls false selection over adaptive candidate comparisons. These structural and statistical results permit empirical gains within a fixed frontier and leave unbounded improvement processes outside their scope. Together with the finite audits, they turn claims about stronger verification and self-improvement into explicit, testable obligations on correctness, admissible evidence, resources, and selection error. D.1 Comparing task improvement and improver improvement The RSI survey’s Headroom-Closed Index summarizes benchmark scores; its post-2026 extension is explicitly illustrative [ 1 ] . Neither is the closure frontier defined here. Its case studies supply motivation and mechanisms to examine, not evidence that their implementations satisfy our uniform verification contracts. Hyperagents evaluates the capacity of a meta agent to generate better task agents with an improvement@ k k metric. In that comparison the meta agent is held fixed, and the best child is selected using validation performance before test performance is measured [ 19 ] . This provides a concrete precedent for separating deployed-task scores from the ability to produce better successors. Its main experiments retain a fixed task distribution and outer selection/evaluation processes, which delimit the reported recursion. A comparison under the present model first fixes the task relation, candidate grammar, evaluator, initial deployed stage, and resource accounting. It then compares the old and new improvers with the same starting information and reports held-out improvement as a function of evaluation calls, tokens, and elapsed compute. A fixed number of generations alone need not equalize cost when candidates have different runtimes. An ablation that freezes the improver but continues task-agent search separates improvements to the search procedure from additional search effort. These measurements concern discovery and selection; semantic frontier escape additionally needs the evidence in Section 7.4 . D.2 Separating diagnosis, ranking, and acceptance AHE reports that its predicted fixes are more informative than its predicted regressions, with many regressions remaining unforeseen [ 20 ] . Both support keeping diagnostic feedback separate from the evidence used to accept a successor. Self-Harness makes regression testing part of that selection loop [ 26 ] . Under our definitions, changing the selection test or evaluator must be recorded separately from changing the candidate agent. A reused evaluation set can become part of the effective search feedback even when the evaluator’s source code stays fixed. Fresh execution from scratch and hidden final evaluation, as used by AI4AI-Bench [ 25 ] , help specify an experimental comparison; they do not by themselves establish a uniform mathematical soundness contract. This is why Proposition 8.2 states a conditional-error premise instead of inferring one from benchmark scores.
