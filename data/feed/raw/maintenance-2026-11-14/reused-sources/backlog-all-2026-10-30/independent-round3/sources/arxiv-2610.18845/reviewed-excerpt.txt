The Last Remote Sensing Model Built by Humans? Casting Any Earth Observation Task as Recursive Self-Improvement Sijie Zhao Zeqi Zhou Heli Qi Xuanchao Fu Zhuo Zheng Junshi Xia Junjue Wang Affiliation: The University of Tokyo Affiliation: RIKEN Affiliation: Stanford University Yokoya-Lab-UTokyo/RSI4EO Weihao Xuan Affiliation: The University of Tokyo Naoto Yokoya Affiliation: The University of Tokyo Abstract Earth observation (EO) models are still built by hand. For each new task, experts design a training recipe and tune it against a benchmark. We show that this loop can be handed to an agent. Any verifiable task can be cast as a harness and a verifier , where the harness defines what the agent may use and the verifier defines what counts as success. Every EO task can be made verifiable, through labeled benchmarks, independent sensors, physical models or the repeated observation of the same place. Given only these two components, a general-purpose coding agent builds the EO model and improves it recursively on its own outputs. Settings long treated as separate research problems, from learning with a handful of labels to adapting across domains, become choices of harness and verifier. On six EO benchmarks spanning optical, SAR and multispectral time-series imagery, with outputs that range from land-cover maps and ship instances to canopy heights and answers about large images, agent-built models surpass the state of the art on every one, and on land-cover mapping, they do so from 11% of the training labels. This establishes a new paradigm for EO, in which people design harnesses and verifiers and agents build the models , so EO models improve as agents do. 1 1 1 This is an ongoing project. The resulting models surpass the state of the art on all six benchmarks ( Table 2 ). 1 Introduction Earth observation (EO) models are still built by hand. Each new sensor, region or task starts another round of engineering, in which experts choose a backbone, design a training recipe and tune it against a benchmark. Foundation models have raised the starting point of this cycle without removing it Cong et al. (2022) ; Reed et al. (2023) ; Guo et al. (2024) ; Xiong et al. (2024) ; Jakubik et al. (2025) ; Siméoni et al. (2026) , and on PANGAEA, they do not consistently outperform a supervised UNet Marsocci et al. (2026) ; Xiao et al. (2025) . Across the many sensors, regions and label taxonomies of EO, expert engineering time decides how many tasks receive a strong model. The cost is highest where EO matters most. When a disaster strikes, a new satellite comes online, or a wildfire, flood or forest loss must be mapped in a new region, a strong model is needed within days, yet expert engineering takes weeks to months and many agencies and countries have no machine learning team at all. Meanwhile, AI has advanced fastest wherever success can be checked automatically. Verifiers drive progress in mathematical reasoning Cobbe et al. (2021) ; Guo et al. (2025) and program discovery Romera-Paredes et al. (2024) ; Novikov et al. (2025) , and coding agents such as Claude Code and Codex now write, run and debug training code for hours, earning medals in machine learning competitions Jiang et al. (2025) ; Chan et al. (2025) and improving training scripts against a validation metric Karpathy (2026) . EO agents have so far orchestrated pretrained models and tools Singh et al. (2024b) ; Liu et al. (2024) ; Feng et al. (2026) , and recent agentic research loops refine existing fully supervised baselines Kang et al. (2026) ; Lekavicius et al. (2026) . No agent has yet been handed a whole EO task, including its label budget, to build the model itself. Our premise is that any verifiable task can be cast as a harness and a verifier. The harness defines what the agent may use, such as data, pretrained checkpoints, libraries and compute, and the verifier defines what counts as success by scoring the agent’s model ( Fig. 1 ). Every EO task can be made verifiable, and EO offers unusually many ways to do so. A decade of community benchmarks scores models on labeled held-out data, independent sensors and in-situ measurements check retrieved quantities, physical models check consistency with the observations, and repeated observation of the same place checks any prediction about its future. Building an EO model then becomes optimization against a verifier, and settings long treated as separate research problems become choices of these two components. Learning from a handful of labels restricts the labeled data in the harness, training-free adaptation removes trainable parameters from it, label-budget search asks for the smallest training set that passes a target score, and domain adaptation places the verifier on a target domain. We call this formulation RSI4EO. Given a harness and a verifier, a general-purpose coding agent builds the model by itself. It writes training code, trains models, queries the verifier and decides what to try next, offline and without human input. The verifier is sealed and returns a score, and containerized execution keeps the held-out data and the network out of the agent’s reach Harbor Framework Team (2026) ; Merrill et al. (2026) ; Shi et al. (2026) . The agent improves the EO model recursively on its own outputs Yudkowsky (2013) ; Zelikman et al. (2024) and designs this recursion itself. On land-cover mapping with six labeled tiles, it runs eight generations of self-training, each taught by the best pipeline the verifier has seen, and ends by automating the loop with its own scripts. Because the two components specify the task completely, any agent can receive it. An agent equipped this way surpasses the state of the art on all six benchmarks, each within 12 hours on four GB200 GPUs: land-cover mapping Xia et al. (2023) , building damage assessment from optical and SAR imagery Chen et al. (2025) , SAR ship instance segmentation Wei et al. (2020) , crop-type mapping from Sentinel-2 time series Sainte Fare Garnot and Landrieu (2021) , canopy height estimation from very-high-resolution imagery Fogel et al. (2025) , and question answering over large images Liu et al. (2026a) . On the last, the harness forbids any training, yet the pipeline the agent designs around a frozen vision-language model outperforms every published method, including models trained for large-image question answering. On OpenEarthMap, six labeled tiles, 0.2% of the training set, already yield a model that outperforms a UNet trained on the full training set, and with 300 tiles selected without labels, Claude-Opus-5.5 surpasses every published method trained on all labels. From 600 tiles on, two agents with different working styles both do so. These results establish a new paradigm for EO, in which people design harnesses and verifiers and agents build the models. A task’s harness and verifier stay fixed while agents improve, so EO models improve as agents do, without new human engineering ( Table 3 ). The time from a new need to a working model shrinks to the agent’s budget, and across 14 runs of GPT-6-Astra on the six benchmarks, the agent reaches half of its total verifier improvement at a median of 0.8 hours ( Fig. 4 a). After a disaster, responders can label a handful of image pairs from the affected area, pose them as a task and receive a damage model within a day. Wildfire, flood and deforestation mapping follow the same pattern, and a newly launched satellite can have a model within a day of its first labeled images. Above all, any agency or country that can define a task can obtain a model at the level of the published state of the art, whether or not it has a machine learning team. Research questions move upstream to which verifiers capture a task faithfully and what a harness must contain, and annotation moves from training sets to the held-out sets behind the verifier, which every benchmark already labels. Our contributions are threefold. • A Paradigm for Building EO Models. Any verifiable task becomes a harness and a verifier, which turns the many verification sources of EO into objectives that agents optimize directly and unifies learning settings as choices of these two components. • An Open EO Task Suite. We release the tasks of this paper as harness and verifier pairs that any agent can run in offline Harbor containers. They cover optical, SAR, optical–SAR and multispectral time-series imagery as well as natural-language questions, with pixel-level, instance-level, continuous and textual outputs, and include a training-free task. • Evidence across Tasks, Labels and Agents. Agent-built models surpass the state of the art on all six benchmarks, on LRS-GRO without training any parameter, already from 114 answered questions. On OpenEarthMap, two agents with different working styles both surpass it, one of them from 11% of the labels. With the task fixed, five agents all surpass the plain starter pipeline of the harness, and the EO model improves with the agent that builds it. 2 Related Work Self-Improvement and Verifiers. The idea of machines that design better machines goes back to Good (1965) and was formalized in the Gödel machine Schmidhuber (2007) . Language models now realize parts of this program. STOP uses a language model to improve the scaffold that calls it Zelikman et al. (2024) , Gödel Agent and the Darwin Gödel Machine rewrite their own agent code Yin et al. (2025) ; Zhang et al. (2026a) , and FunSearch and AlphaEvolve evolve programs against automatic evaluators Romera-Paredes et al. (2024) ; Novikov et al. (2025) . Self-training lets a model improve on its own outputs, from Noisy Student in vision Xie et al. (2020) to STaR and ReST EM in language Zelikman et al. (2022) ; Singh et al. (2024a) and AlphaGo Zero in games Silver et al. (2017) , and in RSI4EO, the agent arrives at such loops for the EO model without being told to. Progress in this line follows the availability of verifiers Sutton (2001) ; Wei (2025) , from trained verifiers for mathematics Cobbe et al. (2021) ; Lightman et al. (2024) to reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards Lambert et al. (2025) ; Guo et al. (2025) , and recent surveys and roadmaps chart the path toward recursive self-improvement in general AI systems Gao et al. (2026a) ; Chen et al. (2026) ; Duan et al. (2026) . RSI4EO carries this verifier-driven view into a scientific domain, where verifiers range from labeled benchmarks to the repeated observation of the same place and the artifact that improves recursively is the EO model. Agents for Machine Learning Engineering. AutoML has automated parts of model development for over a decade, through hyperparameter optimization Feurer et al. (2015) ; Li et al. (2018) ; Falkner et al. (2018) ; Akiba et al. (2019) , neural architecture search Zoph and Le (2017) ; Liu et al. (2019) ; Real et al. (2020) and self-configuring pipelines such as nnU-Net Isensee et al. (2021) , each searching a space that people define. LLM agents widen this space to arbitrary code. AIDE, The AI Scientist and autoresearch write and revise training code against validation metrics Jiang et al. (2025) ; Lu et al. (2026) ; Karpathy (2026) , and MLAgentBench, MLE-bench and RE-Bench measure such agents on engineering and research tasks Huang et al. (2024) ; Chan et al. (2025) ; Wijk et al. (2025) . Domain systems apply these loops to medical image segmentation Gao et al. (2026b) ; Liu et al. (2026b) and solar-panel mapping Lekavicius et al. (2026) . RSI4EO differs in how a task reaches the agent, as a containerized task in the format of recent agent evaluation suites Merrill et al. (2026) ; Shi et al. (2026) . The harness and a sealed verifier specify it completely, so label budgets and domain shift become part of the task definition and validation data stay outside the agent’s reach, a design that reward-hacking studies of frontier agents support Von Arx et al. (2025) . Earth Observation Models and Agents. EO foundation models learn transferable representations from optical, multispectral and SAR archives Cong et al. (2022) ; Reed et al. (2023) ; Fuller et al. (2023) ; Jakubik et al. (2023) ; Guo et al. (2024) ; Xiong et al. (2024) ; Tseng et al. (2025) ; Astruc et al. (2025) ; Jakubik et al. (2025) , and general-purpose backbones such as DINOv3 now ship satellite-pretrained variants Oquab et al. (2024) ; Siméoni et al. (2026) , with GEO-Bench and PANGAEA evaluating them under fixed protocols Lacoste et al. (2023) ; Marsocci et al. (2026) . Label-scarce and cross-domain settings have each produced dedicated methods, including semi-supervised segmentation Yang et al. (2023) ; Yang et al. (2025) ; Lu et al. (2023) ; Zhou et al. (2026) and unsupervised domain adaptation Hoyer et al. (2022) ; Hoyer et al. (2023) ; Zhao et al. (2024) , and vision-language models extend EO to question answering over large images Liu et al. (2026a) ; Zhang et al. (2026b) . In RSI4EO, these models and methods become ingredients that the agent selects, combines and trains. LLM agents for EO have so far orchestrated fixed tools and pretrained models Singh et al. (2024b) ; Liu et al. (2024) ; Feng et al. (2026) ; Xu et al. (2026) ; Talemi et al. (2026) , and OpenEarth-Agent extends them to creating their own tools Zhao et al. (2026) . RSI4EO hands an agent the whole EO task, including its label budget and domain shift, and extends agent-built models to label-scarce regimes. 3 Method RSI4EO turns the construction of an EO model into a task that an agent solves. Section 3.1 defines a task by its harness and verifier, Sections 3.2 and 3.3 describe the two components as we instantiate them, Section 3.4 describes how the agent improves the model on its own outputs, and Section 3.5 shows how learning settings become choices of the two components. 3.1 Tasks as a Harness and a Verifier An EO task is a pair 𝒯 = ( ℋ , 𝒱 ) \mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{V}) . The harness ℋ \mathcal{H} fixes the resources the agent may use: labeled data 𝒟 L \mathcal{D}_{L} , unlabeled data 𝒟 U \mathcal{D}_{U} , pretrained checkpoints, software and a compute budget. The verifier 𝒱 \mathcal{V} fixes what counts as success. The agent produces pipelines, where a pipeline π \pi is a self-contained directory of code and trained weights that maps input imagery to predictions. The verifier runs a pipeline on held-out data ( X V , Y V ) (X_{V},Y_{V}) that the agent never sees and returns its score s ⁡ ( π ) = m ⁡ ( π ⁡ ( X V ) , Y V ) , s(\pi)=m\big(\pi(X_{V}),\,Y_{V}\big), (1) where m m is the task metric. Over its budget, the agent writes pipelines π 1 , … , π K \pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{K} , queries s ⁡ ( π k ) s(\pi_{k}) and decides what to try next. When the agent stops or the budget ends, its final pipeline π ⋆ \pi^{\star} is evaluated once on a hidden test set with the same metric, m ⁡ ( π ⋆ ​ ( X T ) , Y T ) m(\pi^{\star}(X_{T}),Y_{T}) . The formulation leaves every modeling decision to the agent. Architecture, losses, the use of unlabeled data, ensembling and inference all belong to the pipeline the agent writes, and the verifier is the only signal of success it receives. A task is fully specified by its two components, so any agent can receive it, and changing either component defines a new task. 3.2 Verifier The verifier is sealed. It runs in a separate container that holds the validation data, executes a submitted pipeline exactly as the final grader will, and returns the score through a tool call. The agent never receives validation images or labels. Because the verifier runs the whole pipeline, it scores what will be graded, including preprocessing, ensembling and test-time augmentation, and it exposes failures such as missing outputs before the final evaluation. The agent may query it as often as it likes. EO is rich in sources for such verifiers. Labeled benchmarks provide held-out sets, independent sensors and in-situ measurements check retrieved quantities, physical models check consistency with the observations, and repeated observation of the same place checks predictions against what is observed later. Each source yields a function that scores a pipeline without exposing its data to the agent. Our experiments build each verifier from the benchmark’s own labels, using the official validation split where one exists and is not needed as the test set, and otherwise a portion of the training split held out by region, scene or image. 3.3 Harness The harness gives the agent what an EO researcher would start from. It contains the labeled training data, an unlabeled pool where the task provides one, pretrained encoders without decoders or task heads (DINOv3 ViT-L/16 pretrained on satellite imagery, DINOv2 ViT-L/14, ResNet-18 to ResNet-101 and EfficientNet-B0 Siméoni et al. (2026) ; Oquab et al. (2024) ; He et al. (2016) ; Tan and Le (2019) ), the official source code of the DINO models, the Python deep-learning and geospatial stack together with QGIS, SAGA and GDAL, and a deliberately plain starter pipeline, an EfficientNet-B0 encoder with an FPN decoder Lin et al. (2017) . The harness also adds the standard tools of each field, such as detection libraries for HRSID and the Swin and VMamba backbones for BRIGHT. The question-answering task instead provides only the vision-language model Qwen3-VL-8B Bai et al. (2025a) , and its starter prompts this model directly. The agent has four GB200 GPUs and 12 hours, and no single training run may exceed 4 hours. The submitted pipeline must contain networks that the agent trained, except on the training-free task, where no parameter may be updated at all and the submission holds only code and text. The given labels may not be edited, and the agent may annotate unlabeled data itself. Each task runs in an offline Harbor container Harbor Framework Team (2026) ; Merrill et al. (2026) , where the agent works without network access beyond its own model interface. When the agent stops or time runs out, the submission directory is copied out and graded once on the hidden test set under an inference budget of 4 hours, or 8 hours for the 9,734 questions of LRS-GRO. Nothing else the agent created survives, so every model, weight and script the final pipeline needs must be inside the submission. 3.4 Recursive Improvement Within a task, the agent improves the EO model recursively on its own outputs. We call each model that the agent trains from the outputs of earlier models a generation. A generation f t f_{t} leaves outputs O t O_{t} behind, including its predictions and class probabilities on the unlabeled pool, its checkpoints, and its predictions on held-out folds of the training data. The next generation is trained from these outputs, f t + 1 = Train ⁡ ( ℋ , O ≤ t ) , f_{t+1}=\mathrm{Train}\big(\mathcal{H},\,O_{\leq t}\big), (2) and the verifier decides which generation is kept as the source of the next one. Two loops therefore run together. In the outer loop, the agent proposes pipelines and the verifier scores them. In the inner loop, each kept model becomes material for its successor, so the model improves along a lineage. The agent designs this recursion itself. The task instruction permits methods such as pseudo-labeling on the unlabeled pool but prescribes none, and the agent chooses the mechanisms, their order and the depth of the lineage from the verifier’s feedback. Across our runs, the mechanisms fall into three families. At the data level, a teacher labels the unlabeled pool with hard pseudo-labels or soft class probabilities and a student trains on them, as in Noisy Student Xie et al. (2020) and knowledge distillation Hinton et al. (2015) . At the parameter level, new models start from earlier checkpoints, average several checkpoints, or grow from a smaller trained network. At the prediction level, the agent trains models on its own predictions, such as calibrators fitted to out-of-fold predictions and networks that refine its masks. Each generation thus learns from the previous one, the pattern behind STaR Zelikman et al. (2022) and AlphaGo Zero Silver et al. (2017) , here applied to the EO model the agent builds. 3.5 Learning Settings as Choices of Harness and Verifier Table 1: The six benchmarks. Full supervision gives the harness all training labels. Learning from few labels keeps a few labeled samples per natural stratum of the data, such as a continent, a disaster event, a scene, a satellite tile or a question type, chosen so that together they cover every class, and moves the remaining training imagery to the unlabeled pool ( Table 1 ). On Open-Canopy, a geographically spread subset takes the place of the strata. Training-free adaptation removes trainable parameters from the harness, so the agent can only design how a fixed foundation model is used. A label-budget search fixes a label-free selection rule, ranks the training images with it, and asks for the smallest prefix whose labels let the agent reach a target score. Domain adaptation places the labeled data on a source domain and the verifier on the target domain. In every case, the agent, the verifier interface and the final evaluation stay the same. This view also changes where annotation goes. Labels are needed for the verifier and for the final evaluation, which every benchmark already provides, while the training set can shrink to a handful of samples. Validation labels reach the agent only through the scores the verifier returns. 4 Experiments The experiments establish three results. One agent, through one interface, builds strong models across sensors, output types and label regimes. These models exceed the plain pipeline that ships with the harness and surpass the published state of the art on all six benchmarks ( Section 4.2 ). A few labeled samples already yield strong models, and on LRS-GRO, a training-free pipeline surpasses the state of the art from 114 answered questions. On OpenEarthMap, Claude-Opus-5.5 does so from 11% of the training labels and GPT-6-Astra from 22% ( Section 4.3 ). With the harness and the verifier fixed, five agents all raise the six-label starter, by up to 26.0 mIoU, and the model improves with the agent that builds it ( Section 4.4 ). (2023) maps eight land-cover classes from sub-meter RGB imagery, BRIGHT Chen et al. (2025) grades building damage from pre-event optical and post-event SAR pairs, HRSID Wei et al. (2020) segments ship instances in SAR images, and PASTIS Sainte Fare Garnot and Landrieu (2021) maps 18 crop types from Sentinel-2 time series. Open-Canopy Fogel et al. (2026a) asks questions about images of up to 27,328 pixels on a side, which the agent answers with a frozen Qwen3-VL-8B without training any parameter. Each benchmark yields a full-label task and a few-label task that keeps 0.2 to 4.3% of the training labels. On OpenEarthMap, we add two label-budget tasks, in which the harness holds the first 300 or 600 tiles ranked without labels by k-center on frozen DINOv3 features Sener and Savarese (2018) . They probe the smallest prefix that surpasses the previous state of the art. The hidden test set is the official test split of each benchmark except OpenEarthMap, whose test labels are withheld. There, the official validation split serves as the test set, the verifier holds 300 tiles from the training split, and the 1,500 official test images join the unlabeled pool of the six-label and full-label tasks. HRSID and LRS-GRO have no official validation split. On HRSID, the verifier holds 20 complete scenes taken from the official training set. On LRS-GRO, it holds a quarter of the training images. Agents and Protocol. The main agent is Codex with GPT-6-Astra, which runs every task. Claude Code with Claude-Opus-5.5 runs the four OpenEarthMap tasks. On six-label OpenEarthMap, Claude Code also runs Kimi-K3, Qwen3.8-Max and Qwen3.8-27B. All agents use their highest reasoning effort and work for at most 12 hours on four GB200 GPUs in an offline container ( Section 3.3 ). The verifier returns the inference time and the task metric with its breakdown by class, object size, height range or question type. Each task runs once per agent, and each agent score we report comes from that run. Reference Points. The six-label OpenEarthMap run shows the recursion most clearly ( Fig. 3 ). The agent first uses the verifier to choose an encoder, then labels all 4,194 unlabeled tiles with its best model and trains a student on the six labeled tiles and this pseudo-labeled pool. This student opens a lineage of eight generations. In each, the teacher is the best pipeline the verifier has seen so far, and the student continues from the previous generation’s checkpoint. The verifier score rises with every generation, from 56.7 to 61.9 mIoU, and ends 9.0 points above the best model that the agent trained on the six tiles alone. The agent then automates this loop. It writes scripts that read the verifier log, promote the best pipeline to the next teacher and start a new generation when the teacher has improved. The scripts alone launch, train and verify the last generation. The recursion thus extends from the model to the procedure that improves it. The Same Pattern across Tasks. The few-label runs on OpenEarthMap, BRIGHT, HRSID and Open-Canopy all arrive at teacher–student loops over the unlabeled pool, although no instruction asks for them. GPT-6-Astra runs eight generations on OpenEarthMap, ten on BRIGHT, five on HRSID and four on Open-Canopy. On OpenEarthMap, Claude-Opus-5.5 runs four, and Kimi-K3 and Qwen3.8-Max reach the same loop. The verifier decides both which generation is kept and which change to the student carries into the next one. On BRIGHT, the gains after the first two generations come from generations that change the loss or architecture of the student ( Section A.3 ). 5.2 What the Agent Contributes Figure 4: Inside the runs. Beyond the recursion, the agent makes the choices of backbone, training recipe and inference design that experts otherwise make by hand ( Section 1 ), and it makes most of them early. Across 14 runs of GPT-6-Astra, half of the final verifier gain is reached after a median of 0.8 hours and 90% after 4.3 hours ( Fig. 4 a, Section A.5 ). Within about 20 minutes of a run, the agent replaces the starter with the pretrained encoder that the verifier favors and a decoder of its own, which raises the verifier score by 16.1 mIoU over the starter on six-label OpenEarthMap. It also debugs its pipelines. Early in its full-label Open-Canopy run, it finds that the default image reader treats the near-infrared band of the four-band tiles as an alpha channel and premultiplies the RGB bands with it. It reasons about the task as well. On few-label Open-Canopy, it infers that the direction of shadows carries height information and therefore trains without rotations and flips. On few-label LRS-GRO, it locates the region a question refers to, crops it from the full-resolution image and adds retrieved examples, which raises verifier accuracy by 4.4 points in a single step ( Section A.3 ). 5.3 Generalization beyond the Verifier The Verifier Score Carries Over. The agent selects among many candidates with the verifier, and the score it optimizes carries over to the hidden test ( Fig. 4 b). Across the five agents of Table 3 , the verifier ranks the models exactly as the hidden test does. The hidden-test score lies within 2.0 points of the verifier score in every run on OpenEarthMap and LRS-GRO, within 0.2 points on full-label PASTIS and within 0.07 m of the verifier’s MAE on Open-Canopy ( Table 6 ). On HRSID, the test score is even higher, by 3.4 and 3.5 AP. Where the two scores differ, the difference follows the split more than the selection. The official HRSID test images come from the same scenes as the training images, whereas the verifier holds separate scenes. On OpenEarthMap, the gap of every run is concentrated in the same two classes, bareland and water, whether the agent screens candidates locally or relies on the verifier alone. On BRIGHT, it is concentrated in the destroyed class, which is rarer in the test split than in the verifier. This agrees with studies of test-set reuse, which find little overfitting from repeated model selection and attribute most of the drop on new test data to a shift between the two sets Recht et al. (2019) ; Roelofs et al. (2019) . 6 Conclusion We have shown that any verifiable EO task can be cast as a harness and a verifier. Given these two components, general-purpose coding agents build EO models that surpass the state of the art on six benchmarks, including one on which they may not train at all, and on land-cover mapping, they do so from 11% of the training labels. The formulation separates what people specify from what agents build. The harness and the verifier of a task stay fixed while the agents that solve it improve, so every advance in agent capability turns into better EO models without new human engineering. The time from a new disaster, satellite or region to a working model then becomes the agent’s budget, and any agency that can define a task can obtain a state-of-the-art model. As AI capability continues to rise, we see this as the paradigm in which EO models will be built, with human effort invested in the harnesses and verifiers that define the tasks. A.3 Steps and Self-Training Loops The early steps of the runs fall into a few recurring kinds. • Choosing the Encoder. The first model of Section 5.2 also raises the verifier score by 7.3 mIoU on few-label BRIGHT and lowers the verifier MAE on few-label Open-Canopy from 3.90 to 3.02 m. The choice follows the task. The verifier favors DINOv2 on six-label OpenEarthMap and the satellite-pretrained DINOv3 on few-label BRIGHT and Open-Canopy. • Matching Training to the Labels. With a handful of labels, the agent learns from the verifier that brief training generalizes better than long training, and on few-label Open-Canopy, it fine-tunes only the last 12 of the 24 encoder blocks. • Debugging. In the full-label HRSID run, the agent finds that mixed precision shifts box coordinates by several pixels, and from a montage of its outputs, it traces misplaced masks to a half-pixel offset in mask refinement. Correcting the offset alone raises mask AP on its local hold-out from 56.4 to 60.7. • Task-Specific Structure. On BRIGHT with few labels, the agent separates the confidence that a pixel is a building from the confidence in its damage grade when it trains on pseudo-labels, which adds 2.2 points in a controlled comparison. A separate optical detector for building footprints adds a further 1.1 points. On few-label LRS-GRO, the localization step of Section 5.2 works on a 2,048-pixel view and uses four retrieved examples, and the agent also routes each question by its type. Besides training without rotations and flips on few-label Open-Canopy, it enlarges the input so that each ViT patch covers 12 m instead of 24 m. Loops across Tasks. The self-training loops differ in what passes from one generation to the next. On BRIGHT, the teacher is a three-stage pipeline of a building detector, a damage network and a building-level classifier, and its first two generations add about 2 and 1 points. On HRSID, the teacher is a detector followed by a network that predicts each mask from a crop around the detected box. On Open-Canopy, the agent weights the pseudo-labels of 65,339 unlabeled tiles by the uncertainty of its teacher. A.4 Five Agents on Six-Label OpenEarthMap Setup. Claude Code calls Kimi-K3, Qwen3.8-Max and Qwen3.8-27B through the Anthropic-compatible interface of OpenRouter. Web search is disabled for every agent. In the runs of Kimi-K3 and Qwen3.8-Max, the first round of pseudo-labels brings the largest verifier gain of the run apart from the first full model. The First Model Sets the Weak Classes. From the first full model to the final one, the verifier IoU of water rises by 14 to 20 points for each of the four stronger agents, so the gap that the first model leaves on this class persists to the end. Claude-Opus-5.5 diagnoses that confidence filtering removes thin roads from its pseudo-labels. A.5 Budget, Stopping and Cost A Strong Model within Hours. The full-label BRIGHT run reaches 60% of its final verifier gain within its first 1.8 hours. In both LRS-GRO runs, the first change that the agent verifies arrives within 40 minutes and delivers more than half of the final verifier gain. The agents end 17 of the 18 runs in Table 6 before the time limit, at a median of 10.6 hours. The 12-hour budget is therefore an upper bound, and a model close to the final one is available within the first few hours, which is the time scale that rapid response requires. Cost. The model-usage cost recorded in the trajectory logs is 100 to 170 US dollars per run of GPT-6-Astra behind Table 2 . The four GB200 GPUs and the 12-hour budget of the harness cap the compute of a run at 48 GPU hours. A.6 Designing Harnesses and Verifiers Two Design Principles. Under RSI4EO, research effort moves to the design of harnesses and verifiers ( Section 1 ), and the runs analyzed here suggest two principles for it. A harness that records how samples group into scenes, events or source images lets the agent build local validation sets like those of Table 6 . A verifier drawn from the same distribution as the test set makes its score a direct estimate of the final one, and it can also report the uncertainty of its scores, so that the agent can tell which differences between candidates exceed that uncertainty.
