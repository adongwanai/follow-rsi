[PDF page 1] Hippo: Training-Free Agent Self-Evolution via Hippocampal Replay Yutong Song♣ Jiang Wu♢ Yuxuan Ren♡ Tony Qin♠ Baoyuan Wang♠* ♣University of California, Irvine ♢Independent Researcher ♡Rice University ♠Zillow Group Abstract Motivated by recursive self-improvement (RSI), we study how LLM agents can learn through external memory without updating model parameters. Extracting useful experience from a single attempt is difficult: flawed reasoning can produce success, while poor execution can obscure a sound strategy. Preserving its value over time is also difficult: our accumulation baseline without consolidation initially improves but eventually performs below a no-memory baseline. Our key idea is to compare attempts at the same task to identify reusable decisions and refine when they apply. We introduce Hippo, a memory framework inspired by selective hippocampal encoding and replay that learns from these comparisons when shortcomings are diagnosed. It compares the resulting experience with existing memory to merge overlaps and resolve conflicts through explicit applicability conditions. This allows prior guidance to be revised without storing every lesson as a separate entry. Experiments on SWE-bench Verified, WebArena, and Mind2Web show performance gains in all three settings and substantially fewer retained experiences than the accumulation baseline. The gains extend across the evaluated base models of varying capabilities, indicating that memory-based adaptation remains useful even for highly capable models. 1 Introduction Self-evolving agents seek to turn experience from past tasks into better decisions on future tasks (Gao et al., 2026). This objective has motivated research on recursive self-improvement, including agents that revise their own code in response to execution feedback (Zhang et al., 2026a). External mem- ory offers a form of gradient-free learning without changes to model parameters (Shinn et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024). Past experience becomes part of the context for later decisions, so the agent’s adaptation depends on what it retains and how it revises that experience. Prior work has established several ways to represent, update, and reuse experience. Reasoning- Bank’s MaTTS also compares attempts to form memory, while its reported consolidation implemen- tation appends new items without further pruning or merging (Ouyang et al., 2026).Vanilla-IB serves as a baseline for examining how interaction steps relate to token usage and task performance (Ha- jimiri et al., 2026).We focus on an update policy in which identified shortcomings determine whether to form new experience, what it should contain, and how it should revise prior guidance. Continued adaptation, however, does not per se guarantee continued improvement. An exmaple is illustrated in Figure 1. In our experiments, we examine an accumulation baseline that adds ex- perience from both successful and failed attempts without consolidation. With this baseline, per- formance follows an inverted-U pattern as memory grows: it improves at first, then declines and eventually falls below the no-memory baseline. Once retained in memory, these lessons may be repeatedly retrieved and misguide decisions across subsequent tasks. An execution trace does not directly reveal what the agent should learn from it. A single failed attempt may reflect a flawed strategy or poor execution of a valid plan. A correct outcome can also conceal incorrect reasoning. A reflection may therefore extract a flawed lesson that later re- trieval repeatedly presents as a trusted precedent. Even a sound lesson needs to capture the decision behind the observed actions and the conditions under which that decision applies. Task-specific ac- tion sequences may transfer poorly, while generic advice may offer little practical guidance. New information may also duplicate or contradict what the agent already retains (Chhikara et al., 2025). We propose Hippo, a memory framework for self-evolving agents. The design draws on selective hippocampal encoding of salient episodes and replay of past experience during consolidation (Lis- man & Grace, 2005; Kumaran et al., 2016). These processes motivate a separation between episodic reflections on individual attempts and persistent agent memory that integrates experience across tasks. Hippo reflects only on attempts judged to fail or succeed for the wrong reasons. We refer to these cases as surprise. Hippo uses two comparisons to determine how memory should change. It first compares multiple attempts at the same task, sampled under the same frozen policy and retrieved memory, to distill candidate experience from their decisions and outcomes. It then compares this experience with related memories to merge overlaps and resolve conflicts through explicit applicability conditions. These comparisons determine both what can be learned from the current task and how it should revise prior guidance. A revision can therefore change existing guidance without adding another entry. The revised memory guides decisions on subsequent tasks. Experience from those tasks, in turn, provides evidence for further memory updates. We evaluate Hippo on software issue resolution with SWE-bench Verified (Jimenez et al., 2024; OpenAI, 2024), interactive web task completion in WebArena (Zhou et al., 2024), and next-action prediction from recorded webpage states in Mind2Web (Deng et al., 2023). Hippo improves task performance in all three settings and retains substantially fewer experiences than the accumulation baseline. Experiments show gains across base models with different levels of capability. These gains extend to highly capable models such as GPT-5.6, which indicates that memory-based adaptation remains useful even for agents with strong base capabilities. Our main contributions are: • We quantify how repeated attempts improve the chance of task success even without mem- ory updates. This insight motivates direct retries as a baseline for future evaluations of self- evolving agents under comparable budgets, feedback, and success criteria. • We propose Hippo, a framework for memory-based self-evolution that couples selective expe- rience induction from multiple attempts at the same task with maintenance of existing mem- ory. Experiments across task settings and base models demonstrate its effectiveness. • We identify a failure mode of memory-based self-evolution: continued experience accumula- tion without consolidation can erode initial gains and eventually reduce performance below the no-memory baseline. 2 [PDF page 3] 2 Related Works Generative Agents (Park et al., 2023) use reflection to derive higher-level memories from stored observations. Hippo instead updates external memory while model parameters fixed. 3 Hippo Architecturally, Hippo updates its memory from multiple attempts at the same task through four components. (i) The multi-rollout exploration module runs the agent N times with the same frozen policy and retrieved memory. (ii) The surprise-driven episodic buffer records reflections from at- tempts that fail or succeed for the wrong reasons, acting as a hippocampal episodic buffer. (iii) The memory evolution engine draws on hippocampal replay to transform these reflections into the final agent memory. It compares the rollouts to distill reusable experience, then checks this experience against existing memory to merge overlaps and resolve conflicts. (iv) The retrieval and injection module selects relevant memories and provides them to the agent for later tasks. Hippo combines surprise-driven episodic reflection with memory consolidation inspired by hippocampal replay, while keeping the agent policy fixed. 3.2 Multi-Rollout Exploration Scaffold A single failed trajectory may not reveal whether the approach was flawed or a valid plan was poorly executed. Models of causal induction emphasize comparisons across relevant cases (Cheng & Novick, 1990). Multiple attempts under the same conditions provide a broader evidence base and reduce the risk of overfitting a lesson to one trajectory. We therefore apply the episodic buffer of Section 3.1 to N rollouts of the same task, sampled in parallel from the frozen agent policy π with temperature sampling and conditioned on the same retrieved memory M: E(q) =  (τi, ri, δi, L(i) 1 ) N i=1, τi ∼π(· | q, M). (2) Each rollout is judged and reflected on independently by Eq. equation 1, and executes concurrently in its own replica of the space so that the N outcomes remain independent measurements. Together, these rollouts provide broader experience and isolate patterns that generalize across trajectories. The resulting experience set E(q) serves as the input to the memory evolution engine, which produces the final agent memory L2 (Section 3.3). 3.3 Memory Evolution Engine A central challenge for self-evolving agent memory is to remain useful over long task streams. Our experiments show that evaluated basedline performance follows an inverted-U curve as memory grows: it improves at first, then falls below the no-memory baseline (Section 4.2). We further observe three problems: (i) (i) a single reflection can extract a flawed lesson from limited evidence, and repeated retrieval may carry that error into later tasks; (ii) reflections may retain surface actions rather than the decisions behind them, and (iii) duplicate or conflicting experiences make retrieval unreliable. Thus, For a self-evolving agent that continuously tackle new tasks, it requires an active mechanism to revise, merge, and prune past experiences. Hippocampal replay provides biological inspiration for this process. Wilson & McNaughton (1994) show that hippocampal activity patterns associated with waking experience can reactivate during subsequent sleep. In complementary learning systems theory, reactivation supports the grad- ual integration of new experience with existing neocortical knowledge (McClelland et al., 1995; Kumaran et al., 2016). This account inspires Hippo’s two-stage update. It first contrasts the N rollouts in E(q) to distill a candidate experience. It then compares the candidate against existing memory to merge overlapping experiences and resolve conflicts. Together, these stages transform episodic reflections L1 into the final agent memory L2. 4 [PDF page 5] Contrastive Experience Induction. We compare attempts at the same task to derive a candidate experience mt: a decision together with its conditions of use. The induction operator D performs this comparison with an LLM after selecting the evidence supplied by each rollout. A verified success has a successful outcome and reasoning accepted by the judge. Let nc count verified successes among the N attempts at qt, and set ρt = nc/N. From E(qt), D selects full traces for verified successes and judge diagnoses with L1 reflections for all other attempts, including successes for the wrong reasons. When ρt = 1, induction is skipped and memory remains unchanged. For batches that trigger induction, D follows four cases. (i) With a verified-success majority, it turns the minority’s errors into cautions. (ii) With a nonempty verified-success minority, it identifies decisions that distinguish successful attempts. (iii) With an even split, it extracts both a successful strategy and a failure trap. (iv) With no verified successes, it summarizes failure patterns documented in the diagnoses and reflections. In the last case, it is instructed to restrict guidance to actions supported by those inputs. The resulting candidate is passed to memory consolidation. Memory Consolidation. Let L2,t be the persistent L2 bank, represented as a set of identified entries, and Rt ⊆L2,t the entries retrieved by similarity to a nonempty candidate mt. (4) Duplicate candidates are discarded. Overlaps and conflicts are addressed by making applicability conditions explicit in the new entries. When new guidance replaces an old entry, the engine adds the replacement and deletes the old entry. Entries outside the deletion set retain their original text. Section 4.6 compares this update policy with append-only updates and in-place rewriting. 3.4 Memory Retrieval and Injection Semantic similarity helps identify related experiences, but does not determine whether their guid- ance applies to the current task. Hippo therefore uses a two-stage procedure that combines em- bedding retrieval with agent-based selection. For each task q, we first retrieve the top-K candidate experiences from L2 by embedding similarity. The agent then evaluates these candidates against the task requirements and selects the applicable experiences as M. We inject M into the agent’s context before execution, and all N rollouts of q share the same selected memory. At each step, the agent selects an action under the frozen policy π, conditioned on the current task state and M. We compare this procedure with BM25 (Robertson & Zaragoza, 2009), BGE-based top-K re- trieval (Xiao et al., 2024), hybrid sparse–dense retrieval via reciprocal rank fusion (Cormack et al., 2009), and direct LLM-based memory selection inspired by RankGPT (Sun et al., 2023). On recorded WebArena queries with a matching experience available, Hippo achieves a selection rate of 75.2%. Separate roll- out summaries achieve 61.30% success on WebArena, whereas cross-rollout comparison achieves 70.10%. A lesson drawn from one attempt may reflect details spe- cific to that execution. With rollout comparison fixed, Hippo achieves 72.90%, compared with 70.10% for append-only storage and 54.57% for repeated rewriting. Append-only storage leaves earlier guidance unchanged, even when new experience reveals a conflict. Hippo checks when each experience applies and replaces outdated guidance through explicit additions and deletions. The repeated-rewrite variant also permits direct edits and merges of existing entries. Its lower score shows that broader freedom to revise memory does not necessarily improve subsequent performance. Appendix G.3 details these policies. Contribution of the surprise judge. Replacing the judge with just success/failure labels reduces success rates by 1.8%–8.5% across the five WebArena websites. We observe that answers such as N/A or None can be marked correct despite an incomplete search or an execution failure. These attempts can then enter memory as successful experience and cause the agent to repeat the same mistakes on later tasks. Map contains the most such cases in our runs and shows the largest decrease, at 8.5%. Detailed results and case studies are provided in Appendix D. 4.7 Efficiency Analysis. On WebArena, Hippo uses approximately 240–273 memory tokens per injection and 2,800 tokens per rollout receiving memory. With GPT-5.6 Sol, Hippo increases the success rate from 64.2% to 72.9% (+8.7 %) over No Memory. The resource records show that mean calls to the actor model rise from 69.27 to 74.75 (+7.9%), while cost increases from $1.93 to $2.17 per task (+12.4%). Detailed comparisons and accounting scope are provided in Appendix B. 5 Conclusion Hippo learns from multiple attempts and maintains reusable experience without updating model pa- rameters. Experiments across three task settings show improved performance. In our long-horizon evaluation, Hippo sustains gains with fewer memory entries than the evaluated memory baselines, whose performance eventually falls below No Memory. Our retry analysis also motivates direct retries as a baseline for future evaluations of self-evolving agents. Future work includes reflec- tion within longer tasks and more efficient memory updates. G.3 Memory update policies We compare three policies for incorporating new experience after cross-rollout comparison. 28 [PDF page 29] Append only. Each new experience is added to memory. Existing entries remain unchanged, without removal or integration. Repeated rewrite. The model receives existing memories and a new observation. For each old entry, it can retain the text, revise it to add detail or correct an error, merge it with semantically duplicate entries, or delete it. It can also add facts that existing entries do not cover. An entry can remain unchanged; the policy does not require every entry to be rewritten at every update. The update instruction is: Given the existing memories and a new observation, decide for each old entry whether to keep it, rewrite it (fill in detail or correct errors), merge it with semantically duplicate entries into a single item, or delete it (falsified, stale, or fully redundant), and add any facts from the new observation that no existing entry covers. Hippo. Hippo compares new experience with related memories. It discards duplicate candidates and resolves overlaps or conflicts through explicit conditions of use. When new guidance replaces an existing entry, Hippo adds the replacement and deletes the old entry. Entries outside the deletion set retain their original text. This policy allows prior guidance to change while preserving entries that the update does not replace. H Limitations Hippo incurs additional inference and interaction costs from multiple rollouts and memory updates. KV-cache reuse in SGLang (Zheng et al., 2024) and vLLM (vLLM Project, n.d.) can avoid recom- puting identical prompt prefixes across rollouts, reducing redundant inference computation. Special- ized models such as Jev (Almeida, 2026) could handle judgment labels, while a generative model would still produce textual diagnoses. We have not evaluated Jev or quantified the end-to-end sav- ings from KV-cache reuse. Hippo uses the same base model for task execution, trajectory assessment, and memory updates. Shared blind spots may allow incorrect or overly general lessons to persist in memory. A stronger model could construct and revise experience for a smaller model to apply during execution. We have not evaluated whether such experience transfers reliably or whether its reuse offsets the added construction cost. Our long-horizon evaluation studies memory updates across tasks; it does not establish perfor- mance on exceptionally long individual tasks. Longer trajectories may strain context budgets and make it harder to retain early decisions and dependencies across stages. Repeating complete rollouts would also increase the cost of collecting experience. Reflection at intermediate stages and hierar- chical consolidation are possible extensions, but whether they preserve enough evidence to derive reliable experience remains untested. 29
