However, turning experimental feedback into a learned ability to propose effective improvements across models faces two challenges. First, training such an ability with real feedback is expensive: reinforcement learning (RL) requires evaluating tens of thousands of proposals, each potentially requiring a full model-training run. Even at ten GPU-hours per run, reward collection alone would consume hundreds of thousands of GPU-hours, motivating a cheaper surrogate. Second, surrogate feedback can become unreliable as policy optimization favors overestimated proposals when the proposal distribution shifts. To address these challenges, we propose LM2LM, which trains LLMs with RL to generate effective LM improvement proposals. To provide scalable feedback, we use a research world model (RWM), a frozen LLM that predicts proposal gains from real experimental records provided in context, as the reward model for RL. To maintain feedback reliability, we periodically execute current-policy proposals ranked highly by the RWM and add their measured outcomes to its context. Across eight autoregressive and diffusion LM development environments, real-execution evaluations on held-out tasks show that LM2LM enables Qwen3.8-27B to outperform GPT-6-sol, even when GPT-6-sol is given 300 real experimental records in context, while requiring 33% lower data-generation cost. LM2LM also generalizes to a model family unseen during training. 1 Introduction Large language models (LLMs) Radford et al. (2018) ; Radford et al. (2019) ; Touvron et al. (2023) are increasingly being adopted as agents for automated AI research (AutoResearch) Karpathy (2026) , where they propose ideas, implement changes, and execute experiments Si et al. (2026) ; Novikov et al. (2025) . A promising application is language-model development (LM development), where agents explore architectural, optimization, and training choices and measure their effects on model performance Chi et al. (2026) ; Yang et al. (2026a) . These experiments generate feedback that could help LLMs learn how to improve language models. Developing this capability could accelerate model development and support progress toward recursive self-improvement (RSI) Chi et al. (2026) (Fig. 1 (a)). Figure 1: Overview of automated LM development. Despite these advances, deciding what to try, i.e., research judgment , remains a bottleneck for LLM-based research agents. Agents can implement and execute complex experiments, yet still struggle to identify changes that yield meaningful improvements Li et al. (2026) ; Si et al. (2026) ; Chi et al. (2026) . In LM development, effective choices depend on the target model and its training configuration: a change that benefits one setting may not benefit another. With expensive training experiments, selecting promising changes is essential to making effective use of limited experimental resources. We therefore ask: how can language models learn to make better model-improvement decisions and transfer this capability to new models and training configurations? A natural way to improve research judgment is to learn from past experiments. Experimental records can inform an LLM’s decisions through in-context learning (ICL) Brown et al. (2020) ; Novikov et al. (2025) ; Si et al. (2026) , and can also improve predictions of experimental outcomes and the selection of promising proposals Wang et al. (2026b) . This suggests using experimental knowledge as feedback for parameter learning to improve an LLM’s proposal-generation capability beyond in-context learning. However, learning from this feedback raises two key challenges (Fig. 1 (b)-(c)). First, how can we obtain cheap and scalable training feedback? Training LLMs with reinforcement learning (RL) to generate better proposals requires feedback on numerous sampled proposals. The most direct feedback comes from executing each proposal and measuring its outcome Si et al. (2026) , but this requires costly model-training runs. For example, training a 486M-parameter model on 8B tokens takes approximately 45 B200 GPU-hours. A GRPO-style RL Shao et al. (2024) setup with 100 updates, 32 training states per update, and 8 proposals per state would require 25,600 proposal evaluations. Executing these proposals at the same per-run cost would consume approximately 1,152,000 GPU-hours, costing over $16 million. This motivates low-cost surrogate feedback without executing every proposal. Second, how can we keep surrogate feedback reliable as learning progresses? Accurate predictions on past experiments do not ensure reliable feedback throughout training. As learning changes which proposals the LLM generates, the surrogate must evaluate proposals that its experimental records may not cover. RL may then favor overestimated proposals, increasing surrogate rewards without corresponding real gains Gao et al. (2023) ; Zhang et al. (2026) . A scalable learning approach must therefore keep its feedback grounded in real outcomes on the proposals the current LLM generates. To address these challenges, we propose LM2LM, a framework that trains LLMs with RL to generate effective proposals for LM development. To obtain cheap and scalable training signals, we use a frozen LLM as a research world model (RWM) Wang et al. (2026b) to predict experimental gains relative to a reference configuration without executing proposals. Grounded in an initial set of real experimental records provided as in-context knowledge, the RWM supplies predicted gains for constructing RL rewards Yu et al. (2026) . To keep this feedback reliable as training progresses, we introduce on-policy calibration : we periodically execute a subset of the current policy’s proposals that the RWM ranks highly and incorporate their measured outcomes into its in-context knowledge. This targets real execution at proposals favored by RL, where overestimation can misdirect learning, and helps maintain prediction reliability as LLMs’ proposals change. Our contributions are: (i) Method. We propose LM2LM, which trains LLMs with RL using RWM-based rewards grounded in real records and calibrated through targeted real execution; (ii) Experiments. Extensive real-execution experiments on held-out environments show that LM2LM enables Qwen3.8-27B to outperform frontier models such as GPT-6-sol and surpass baselines leveraging past experimental records at lower data-generation cost; (iii) Analysis. We show that RWM-based feedback provides informative rankings of proposal gains, while on-policy calibration mitigates overestimation and largely preserves ranking quality as training progresses, unlike a fixed-record RWM that eventually performs worse than random. 2 Related Work AutoResearch with LLM Agents. AutoResearch aims to automate empirical AI research through iterative idea generation, experimental implementation, execution, and analysis. Early work shows LLM agents’ ability to generate research ideas and conduct end-to-end experiments Baek et al. (2025) ; Si et al. (2025) ; Lu et al. (2024) ; Weng et al. (2026) , with benchmarks evaluating these capabilities in machine-learning engineering and AI R&D Huang et al. (2024) ; Chan et al. (2025) ; Wijk et al. (2025) ; Chi et al. (2026) . To improve research outcomes, some methods iterate on candidate solutions with execution feedback while keeping the underlying agent fixed Jiang et al. (2025) ; Novikov et al. (2025) ; Karpathy (2026) . Others instead train the agent on execution feedback, for machine-learning engineering Yang et al. (2026a) and LLM model development Si et al. (2026) . However, these methods rely on costly real execution to obtain training signals, limiting learning at scale. In contrast, we train LLMs with feedback from a world model that predicts experimental outcomes, enabling scalable learning with fewer real executions. Full details of related works are in Appendix A . 3 Methodology In this section, we present LM2LM. We first formalize AutoResearch for LM development and motivate RL-based proposer training (Sec. 3.1 ). To provide scalable feedback, we use a frozen LLM as a research world model (RWM) to predict experimental gains without executing proposals, and use its predictions as RL rewards (Sec. 3.2 ). To maintain reliable feedback as the proposer evolves, we periodically execute high-rated proposals from the current policy and incorporate their measured outcomes into the RWM’s context (Sec. 3.3 ). We consider a set of AutoResearch tasks ℰ \mathcal{E} , where each task e ∈ ℰ e\in\mathcal{E} seeks to improve a target language model by modifying its architecture, training objective, or optimization. A campaign proceeds through repeated proposal-and-execution steps. (2021) . However, directly applying RLVR to AutoResearch for LM development is computationally expensive, as evaluating each proposal requires training the target model to measure its experimental gain. Since RL samples numerous proposals across research states and training updates, obtaining execution-based rewards at scale becomes prohibitively costly. To address this challenge, we use a research world model (RWM) to predict experimental gains without executing every proposal, and train the proposer with DAPO on rewards built from these predictions. Research World Model. An RWM predicts the outcomes of AI research experiments without executing them Wang et al. (2026b) . We instantiate it as a frozen LLM W W . To improve prediction reliability, we ground it in real experimental records. Specifically, we collect N 0 N_{0} experimental records from training tasks to form the initial record set M 0 M_{0} . Each record ( e , a , g ) (e,a,g) contains a task, an executed proposal, and its measured gain. Formally, given a task e e , proposal a a , and experimental records M M , the RWM predicts the experimental gain g ^ ​ ( e , a , M ) \hat{g}(e,a;M) as g ^ ​ ( e , a , M ) = W ⁡ ( e , a , M ) , \hat{g}(e,a;M)=W(e,a;M), (4) where M M is provided as in-context evidence. Thus, evaluating a proposal requires only an LLM call rather than a model-training run. Reward Design. A straightforward approach is to use RWM-predicted gains directly as RL rewards. This poses two challenges. First, predicted gains vary in scale across tasks and may contain extreme values, allowing tasks with larger gain scales or a few extreme predictions to dominate policy optimization. Second, high predicted gains do not necessarily imply useful proposals, as proposals may be invalid, repetitive, or insufficiently diverse. To address the first challenge, we normalize predicted gains by a task-specific scale τ e \tau_{e} and clip them at a threshold c c : r ⁡ ( s , a , M ) = clip ⁡ ( g ^ ​ ( e , a , M ) / τ e , − c , c ) , r(s,a;M)=\mathrm{clip}\big(\hat{g}(e,a;M)/\tau_{e},\,-c,\,c\big), (5) where e e is the task associated with research state s s , and τ e \tau_{e} is determined by reference seed noise and the dispersion of RWM-predicted gains. Normalization makes rewards comparable across tasks, while clipping limits the influence of extreme predictions on policy updates. To address the second challenge, we incorporate proposal validity, novelty, and diversity into the final rewards. For a group of G G proposals sampled from state s s , a proposal is invalid if it cannot be implemented by the builder, repeated if it reproduces the reference configuration or a proposal in the history h h , and valid otherwise. Let 𝒱 \mathcal{V} denote the set of valid proposals within the group. While the RWM provides scalable feedback, RL increasingly favors highly rated proposals that may not be adequately covered by the initial records M 0 M_{0} , making it susceptible to reward overoptimization Gao et al. (2023) ; Zhang et al. (2026) . For this analysis, we extend training from 96 to 128 steps with a fourth calibration of LM2LM (236 real runs) and compare it with the static variant whose RWM keeps M 0 M_{0} throughout; each variant is scored by the RWM it is trained with. Why Can RWM Feedback Guide RL? We examine the alignment between RWM-predicted and real-execution feedback by measuring the cosine similarity of group-relative advantages and the within-state pairwise ranking accuracy over four executed proposals per held-out AR research state. As shown in Figs. 5 (a) and (b), LM2LM maintains advantage cosine of 0.50–0.71 and pairwise accuracy of 0.61–0.69, above chance levels of 0 and 0.5. Since DAPO optimizes group-relative advantages, this indicates that the RWM provides informative relative feedback for policy optimization. How Does On-Policy Calibration Help? To understand the gains from calibration in Sec. 4.4 , we examine how the feedback of the static variant evolves as RL changes the proposer’s outputs (Fig. 5 ). We observe that (i) Calibration maintains reliable relative feedback. As shown in Fig. 5 (a), the static RWM’s advantage cosine declines from 0.69 at step 16 to − 0.42 -0.42 at step 128, so its predicted advantages become negatively aligned with real ones, whereas LM2LM keeps it positive. Likewise, its pairwise accuracy drops from 0.72 to 0.37, below chance (0.5), while LM2LM maintains 0.61 (Fig. 5 (b)). We further observe that scoring the same LM2LM proposals with M 0 M_{0} instead of calibrated records lowers pairwise accuracy from 0.61 to 0.46 at step 96 (Appendix I.2 ). These results indicate that on-policy calibration keeps the predicted advantages that DAPO optimizes aligned with real ones. (ii) Calibration limits reward overestimation. As shown in Fig. 5 (c), the predicted–real reward gap of the static RWM rises sharply after step 64, reaching about 2.0 at step 96, whereas LM2LM’s gap stays below 1 and decreases from 0.85 at step 96 to 0.17 at step 128 after the fourth calibration. Thus, calibration also keeps predicted rewards close to real ones, mitigating reward overoptimization Gao et al. (2023) . For example, our case studies in Appendix I.3 show that the initial RWM overestimates a NanoChat optimizer change (predicted + 0.7 +0.7 to + 1.25 +1.25 vs. real − 1.46 -1.46 ), so the static variant raises its share of NanoChat training proposals from 30% to 79% by step 64. In contrast, the first calibration of LM2LM adds its real outcomes to the records, and the share falls to 8%. More metrics and case studies are in Appendix I . 5 Conclusion We presented LM2LM, which trains LLMs with RL to generate effective proposals for LM development, using a frozen LLM grounded in real experimental records as an RWM and keeping it reliable through on-policy calibration. Extensive experiments across autoregressive and diffusion LM environments show that LM2LM enables Qwen3.8-27B to outperform frontier models such as GPT-6-sol and achieve higher gains than baselines leveraging past experimental records at lower data-generation cost. Our analysis shows that RWM-based feedback provides informative rankings of proposal gains, while on-policy calibration mitigates reward overestimation and preserves ranking quality as training progresses. These findings highlight RWM-guided RL as a promising paradigm for learning research judgment from experimental feedback, offering a potential path toward RSI. 6 Limitations Our study focuses on proposal-level improvements in LM development rather than end-to-end research campaigns. Although the RWM provides informative rankings and substantially reduces the need for real execution during RL, its predictions remain imperfect, and on-policy calibration still requires a limited number of additional model-training runs. Future work could extend our framework to broader research environments, including LM post-training, and evaluate its effectiveness in longer, end-to-end research campaigns. 7 Ethics Statement This work studies the use of LLMs to automate research on language-model development. Such systems may help reduce the cost of experimentation and accelerate scientific progress, but they also introduce risks, including unreliable experimental conclusions, increased computational resource consumption, and the potential misuse of automated model-improvement capabilities. Our framework evaluates proposals in controlled training environments and uses real experimental outcomes to calibrate surrogate feedback. Nevertheless, predicted rewards and experimental gains do not guarantee the safety or broader usefulness of proposed model changes. We therefore emphasize the importance of human oversight, independent evaluation, and appropriate safeguards when applying automated research systems to more capable models or higher-stakes settings. Appendix A More Details of Related Works A.1 World Models for LLM Agents. A world model predicts the consequences of an agent’s actions, enabling planning or learning without interacting with the real environment Sutton (1991) ; Ha and Schmidhuber (2018) ; Hafner et al. (2023) . Recent work uses LLMs as world models for reasoning Hao et al. (2023) , web navigation Chae et al. (2025) ; Gu et al. (2024) , text-based simulation Wang et al. (2024) , and agent training in synthetic environments Chen et al. (2026) . In AutoResearch, concurrent work WMRL Yang et al. (2026b) uses a frozen LLM as the world model to predict execution outcomes as RL rewards and rescales them with a monotone mapping fitted on real executions at every training step. In contrast, LM2LM uses a frozen LLM as the world model that learns research knowledge from real experimental records in context Wang et al. (2026b) , and improves its predictions on the current policy’s proposals by adding the outcomes of a few top-rated ones to these records. A.2 Reinforcement Learning for LLMs Reinforcement learning (RL) Shao et al. (2024) ; Liu et al. (2025) ; Lin et al. (2025) ; Yu et al. (2026) ; Lin et al. (2026a) ; Wu et al. (2026) has become an effective approach to improving LLM capabilities, particularly through reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) Lambert et al. (2025) . Methods such as GRPO Shao et al. (2024) and DAPO Yu et al. (2026) optimize LLM policies using group-relative feedback without a learned value function. While RLVR typically relies on inexpensive verification, such as checking mathematical answers or executing code, LM development requires costly model-training experiments to measure proposal quality. Recent work applies RL to automated AI research using execution feedback Yang et al. (2026a) ; Si et al. (2026) , but obtaining rewards through real execution at every training step limits scalability. In contrast, LM2LM uses RWM-predicted experimental gains as RL rewards, while periodically calibrating the RWM with selected real executions to maintain feedback reliability. A.3 Self-Evolving LLMs and Agents Self-evolving methods improve a model or agent with data or feedback it generates itself. One line trains LLMs on self-generated solutions that are filtered or rewarded by verifiers or by the model itself Zelikman et al. (2022) ; Gulcehre et al. (2023) ; Yuan et al. (2024) . Another line lets agents modify their own code, prompts, or workflows and keeps the changes that improve benchmark performance Lin et al. (2026b) ; Yin et al. (2025) ; Robeyns et al. (2025) ; Lin et al. (2026c) ; Lin et al. (2026d) . These methods rely on feedback that is cheap to obtain, such as answer checking, code execution, or benchmark scores. In LM development, measuring a single proposal requires a full model-training run. LM2LM addresses this setting by learning from RWM-predicted gains and calibrating the RWM with a small number of real executions, and it targets the ability to improve LM development, which recursive self-improvement ultimately requires. Appendix B Methodology Details of LM2LM B.1 Reward Design Reward Scale. The task-specific reward scale is τ e = max ⁡ ( σ e , 1.4826 ​ MAD e ) \tau_{e}=\max(\sigma_{e},\,1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD}_{e}) , where σ e \sigma_{e} is the standard deviation of three reference runs and MAD e \mathrm{MAD}_{e} is the median absolute deviation of the RWM-predicted gains for task e e from previous rollouts. It is recomputed at the start of each training segment and held fixed within the segment; for the first segment, MAD e \mathrm{MAD}_{e} is estimated from RWM predictions on proposals of a preliminary run. Validity, Repetition, and Bonuses. A proposal is valid if it can be translated into an executable modification of the task’s reference configuration, which is checked without running it. A proposal that changes nothing, i.e., all of whose modifications are inert, receives − 3 -3 instead of − c -c . Repetition is decided on the resolved implementation: a proposal repeats the reference configuration if it resolves to it, the history if it matches a proposal in h h , and an earlier proposal of its group if their implementations coincide. A proposal that repeats an earlier proposal of its own group keeps at most zero reward, and if no proposal of a group is valid and novel, every proposal of the group receives − c -c . In the rollouts whose proposals are selected for calibration, a valid proposal with a positive predicted gain also receives a novelty bonus 0.2 ​ σ G / 1 + N prev 0.2\,\sigma_{G}/\sqrt{1+N_{\mathrm{prev}}} in addition to the diversity bonus b i b_{i} , where N prev N_{\mathrm{prev}} is the number of earlier occurrences of the same change on the task. Hyperparameters. We use a repetition margin δ = 1 \delta=1 and a diversity weight λ = 0.3 \lambda=0.3 . For each state, a proposer generates four proposals, which are implemented by the same builder and evaluated through real model-training experiments. Invalid or unchanged proposals receive zero gain, while negative gains are retained. Validation Metrics. We measure model performance using: • BPB (Bits per Byte): Average number of bits needed to encode each byte of text, enabling comparisons across tokenizers Gao et al. (2020) . We use it for AR models, evaluated on held-out text as specified per family (Appendix C.1 ). • NLL (Negative Log-Likelihood): Negative log-probability assigned to validation sequences. For diffusion models, we compute the exact negative log-likelihood of the true tokens accumulated along the model’s greedy, most-confident-first within-block unmasking order, with each revealed position set to the true token, reported in nats per token. Lower values indicate better model performance. Evaluation Metrics. We evaluate proposal quality using: • Avg@4 / Best@4: Mean / maximum real gain among four proposals per state, averaged equally across states. • Win rate: Fraction of states where one method achieves higher Avg@4 than another, with ties counted as 0.5. • Neg.: Fraction of proposals with negative real gains. Experimental gains are reported in 10 − 3 10^{-3} BPB (AR) and 10 − 2 10^{-2} nats per token (diffusion). D.2 Cost Accounting Hardware and Prices. All experiments, including baseline training and evaluation, RL training, experimental data collection, reference runs, and on-policy calibration, use NVIDIA B200 GPUs. Data-Generation Cost. We measure cost as the GPU expenses of real experimental executions plus the token cost of RWM predictions. Data-Generation Time. We measure time as the sum of real-execution durations, assuming sequential execution, and RWM prediction time. Appendix E Additional Implementation Details E.1 Models and Prompts. The proposer’s backbone is Qwen3.8-27B. The RWM, GPT-6-sol, is queried with a single API request per proposal at high reasoning effort, without tools. The builder, GPT-6-astra, runs as a Codex agent OpenAI (2025) ( codex exec ) at high reasoning effort. Tables 13 , and 14 list the prompts of RWM, and builder, respectively. E.2 RL Training We use DAPO with asymmetric clipping ( ϵ low = 0.2 \epsilon_{\mathrm{low}}=0.2 , ϵ high = 0.28 \epsilon_{\mathrm{high}}=0.28 ), a token-level policy-gradient loss, and no KL or entropy regularization. For dynamic sampling, we sample 277 states and retain 256 per rollout; importance-sampling ratios are clipped at 2.0. Proposals are sampled at temperature 1.0 with a maximum of 20,000 generated tokens. Each rollout is followed by eight policy updates with a global batch size of 256. We use Adam ( β 1 = 0.9 \beta_{1}=0.9 , β 2 = 0.98 \beta_{2}=0.98 ), weight decay 0.1, and an initial learning rate of 10 − 6 10^{-6} , multiplied by 0.7 after each training segment. E.3 On-Policy Calibration The candidates 𝒞 u \mathcal{C}_{u} in Eq. ( 9 ) are the proposals sampled by the current proposer on training states whose outcomes are not yet in M u M_{u} ; in practice, we spread the selected top-rated proposals across training tasks and states. Calibration outcomes enter only the RWM records, not the histories of training states. E.4 SFT and RFT-Real Each SFT pair uses a record’s executed proposal as the target, and a state of the record’s own task whose history contains 0–7 other records of that task from the 300 records, chosen to diversify histories across pairs. Appendix H Case Studies: Impact of Calibration Strategies Top-rated calibration executes the proposals that the RWM rates highest and RL is about to reinforce, whereas random calibration samples proposals regardless of their predicted gains. The following two cases illustrate how this difference affects RL on held-out AR families. Delayed Correction of Overestimated Changes. M-UPD-72 is a NanoChat change to the number of tokens per optimizer update that the RWM overestimates: under M 0 M_{0} , it rewards the change with + 0.7 +0.7 to + 0.9 +0.9 in the training reward scale, whereas its real reward on held-out states is about − 1.46 -1.46 (Fig. 7 ). Top-rated calibration executes seven proposals applying it in the first update (after step 16), five of which degrade the reference configuration; the RWM then rewards the change with − 1.66 -1.66 , and its share of NanoChat training proposals falls from a peak of 45% at step 24 to 8% by step 64. Random calibration executes none of them in the first update, so the RWM keeps rewarding the change until the second update (after step 40); lagging behind the reward, its share still peaks at 80% at step 48 and falls to 24% only by step 72. Both shares eventually drop below 3% by step 96, but random calibration spends substantially more training on a harmful change before correcting it. Misleading Records from Random Selection. Random calibration can also add records that mislead the RWM. Its first batch contains an OLMo change that replaces local attention with an alternative token-mixing operator, which happens to improve its training task by 1.43%. With this record, the RWM starts rewarding the change (mean training reward rising from about − 0.6 -0.6 under M 0 M_{0} to + 0.8 +0.8 after the first update and + 1.2 +1.2 by step 96), and RL raises its share of OLMo training proposals from 4% to 16% (Fig. 8 ). On held-out OLMo states, the change accounts for 53% of the proposals at step 96, with a mean real gain of − 16.4 × 10 − 3 -16.4\times 10^{-3} BPB, lowering the OLMo Avg@4 from 7.39 under top-rated calibration to − 6.37 -6.37 . Under top-rated calibration, the RWM keeps penalizing the change ( − 1.2 -1.2 to − 0.5 -0.5 ), and its share stays below 5%. Appendix I Additional In-depth Analysis I.1 Diagnosing RWM Feedback Reliability Feedback Metric. At each checkpoint, we execute the four proposals of each of the 60 held-out AR states, whose outcomes never enter the records. The static variant is scored by W ⁡ ( M 0 ) W(M_{0}) throughout, and LM2LM by the RWM in force: W ⁡ ( M 0 ) W(M_{0}) at step 16, W ⁡ ( M 1 ) W(M_{1}) at step 40, W ⁡ ( M 2 ) W(M_{2}) at steps 48 and 64, W ⁡ ( M 3 ) W(M_{3}) at step 96, and W ⁡ ( M 4 ) W(M_{4}) at step 128. Rewards follow the normalization and clipping used for RL training, clip ⁡ ( g / τ eff , ± 6 ) \mathrm{clip}(g/\tau_{\mathrm{eff}},\pm 6) . For the proposals i = 1 , … , G i=1,\dots,G of a state, let r ^ i \hat{r}_{i} and r i r_{i} denote their predicted and real rewards, and let A ^ i = r ^ i − 1 G ​ ∑ j = 1 G r ^ j , A i = r i − 1 G ​ ∑ j = 1 G r j \hat{A}_{i}=\hat{r}_{i}-\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}\hat{r}_{j},\qquad A_{i}=r_{i}-\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}r_{j} be their predicted and real advantages, with G = 4 G=4 (states with fewer than two scored proposals are skipped). We use the following metrics, with 95% bootstrap intervals over states (2,000 resamples): • Advantage cosine : the cosine similarity between the predicted and real advantages, concatenated over all states. • Pairwise accuracy : over all pairs of proposals of a state whose real rewards differ, the fraction whose predicted order agrees with their real order, where a pair with equal predicted rewards counts as 0.5; pairs are pooled over states, so random rewards attain 0.5. • Sign agreement : the fraction of proposals with nonzero A ^ i \hat{A}_{i} and A i A_{i} whose signs agree. • Wrong-reinforcement rate : Pr ⁡ ( A i ​ < 0 ∣ A ^ i > ​ 0 ) \Pr(A_{i}<0\mid\hat{A}_{i}>0) , the fraction of positively reinforced proposals that perform below their group’s real mean. • Top-1 hit rate : over states whose real rewards differ, the fraction in which the proposal with the highest predicted reward also has the highest real reward. • Real advantage of reinforced proposals : the mean A i A_{i} over proposals with A ^ i > 0 \hat{A}_{i}>0 . We also report the mean predicted and real rewards and their difference (Fig. 5 (c)). Results. Fig. 9 complements the advantage alignment and ranking results of Sec. 4.5 by examining whether individual proposals are reinforced in the correct direction. Early in training, the static RWM provides feedback comparable to that of LM2LM, but its reliability deteriorates as the proposer evolves. From step 16 to step 64, its sign agreement drops from 0.78 to 0.52 and its wrong-reinforcement rate rises from 18% to 46%, reaching 0.43 and 55% at step 128. In contrast, the sign agreement of LM2LM is 0.68, 0.65, and 0.62 at steps 16, 64, and 128, and its wrong-reinforcement rate stays between 30% and 36% at these steps. As a result, the proposals that the static RWM reinforces have a negative mean real advantage from step 96 onward ( − 0.31 -0.31 ), i.e., RL increasingly favors proposals that underperform their group, whereas those reinforced by LM2LM remain positive (0.18 to 0.59).
