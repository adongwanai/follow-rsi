Benchmarking Prompt Optimization of Large Language Models With Chess Timothée Lesort Alejandra López de Aberasturi Gómez Tristan Karch Tom Veniat Affiliation: Philippe Modard, Karl Tuyls & Ludovic Denoyer Affiliation: imec AI.labs Email: {first.last}@imec.be Abstract Evaluating large language models becomes increasingly challenging as their capabilities advance: benchmarks can saturate, public test sets risk contamination, and assessing harder tasks can require expensive grading or execution infrastructure. These challenges are amplified in automatic prompt optimization ( APO ), where evaluation is repeated throughout the search for better prompts. Studying APO therefore requires a benchmark that is cheap and deterministic to score, hard enough to leave room for improvement, and renewable as models evolve. We introduce a chess benchmark built from 1,118 Lichess puzzles to study APO for frozen LLMs: we optimize their prompts without updating their model weights. Chess combines inexpensive exact-match scoring, engine-based evaluation of alternative moves, and a renewable supply of problems with adjustable difficulty. Unlike evaluations that report only success on isolated test items, the benchmark also connects puzzle-solving gains to short game-play rollouts within the same domain. We use it to evaluate six APO algorithms on eight target models, measuring not only baseline strength but also how much each model responds to optimization and whether optimized prompts transfer across models and to game-play. Chess is thus a well-suited benchmark for APO: it is (i) challenging , as even the strongest evaluated model, Gemini 3.5 Flash (used as the meta-model), solves only about 55% of puzzles; (ii) discriminative , revealing gains, unchanged performance, and regressions across methods and models; (iii) renewable , with fresh puzzles to reduce contamination risk and adjustable difficulty to maintain headroom as models improve; and (iv) affordable , as the complete study runs for around $800. We release the puzzles, optimization and evaluation code, and dataset-renewal scripts ( Code ). 1 Introduction Prompt optimization offers a fast, inexpensive way to improve LLM performance on downstream tasks. Automatic prompt optimization ( APO ) [ Ramnath et al., 2025 ] refers to methods that automatically search for high-performing prompts, e.g., for instruction generation Zhou et al. [2023] . This reduces the need for costly manual prompt engineering and speeds up the adoption of new models. Like fine-tuning, APO adapts a trained model to a downstream task, but it only requires black-box access to the model, allowing it to be applied to both open-weight and proprietary models and to be combined with supervised fine-tuning or RLHF . The main application domains of automatic prompt optimization are text classification, summarization, and reasoning. But evaluating APO inherits every weakness of LLM benchmarking itself: static test sets leak into training data, frontier benchmarks demand heavy infrastructure, grading open-ended outputs requires costly LLM judges, and once the models saturate the task, the benchmarks become obsolete. This motivates benchmarks with cheap, deterministic scoring and hard problems that can be renewed as models evolve. We use chess puzzles to benchmark and understand APO . Chess has properties that directly address the shortcomings identified above: (a) inexpensive automated scoring —reference puzzle solutions provide exact-match labels, while engines such as Stockfish The Stockfish Developers [2026] , Leela Chess Zero Pascutto et al. [2022] , Komodo Dailey et al. [2023] , or AlphaZero Silver et al. [2018a] evaluate alternative moves without human annotation or an LLM judge; (b) renewable problems with tunable difficulty —the large space of positions supplies challenging problems [ Kolasani et al., 2025 ] , and scripts can construct fresh puzzle sets from new games and adjust their difficulty as models improve; and (c) transfer within the same domain —prompts optimized on isolated puzzle positions can also be evaluated through their consequences during play. Puzzles can additionally be generated using RL approaches to target novelty and difficulty [ Feng et al., 2025 ] , at greater computational cost. This paper introduces a new chess benchmark together with an evaluation of six APO algorithms across eight proprietary and open-weight target models. It aims to better understand how prompt optimization behaves across algorithms and models while providing a reproducible way to execute new APO algorithms at a reasonable cost. Our contributions are thus three-fold: (a) a new APO benchmark based on chess puzzles that is easy to run, cheap to evaluate, and challenging yet improvable; (b) a comparison of six APO approaches across eight target models, analyzed along puzzle metadata, prompt composition, engine evaluation, and transferability to other models and game-play rollouts; and (c) an analysis of where optimization gains occur and how they transfer across models and to game-play. In addition, we provide an open-source platform with a curated set of 1,118 puzzles, code to optimize prompts through various standard APO methods, and scripts to refresh the benchmark to reduce dependence on fixed public test sets and adjust difficulty as LLM frontier models improve. 2 Related Work We position our benchmark relative to three lines of work: benchmark construction for frontier LLMs, chess as a testbed for LLMs, and APO algorithms. Frontier LLMs Benchmarks. APO is typically evaluated on standard LLM benchmarks and inherits their limitations. As frontier LLMs acquire stronger reasoning capabilities, traditional benchmarks struggle to keep pace and are prone to data saturation and contamination [ Kiela et al., 2021 , Singh et al., 2024 , Bean et al., 2026 , Marro et al., 2026 ] . Moreover, as tasks get more complicated, it becomes harder for humans to produce reliable annotations. Marro et al. [2026] describe this as the “post-comprehension regime”. One response is to generate test problems dynamically instead of fixing a dataset, as in LiveBench [ White et al., 2025 ] , RULER [ Hsieh et al., 2024 ] , LLF-Bench [ Cheng et al., 2023 ] , and LoCoMo [ Maharana et al., 2024 ] . Our benchmark belongs to this renewable-task family. Its puzzle task supports inexpensive exact-match scoring and graded engine evaluation without an LLM judge or an agentic execution environment. This makes repeated prompt evaluation affordable, complementing benchmarks that study open-ended responses with LLM judges [ Zheng et al., 2023 , Maharana et al., 2024 ] , agentic tasks in sandboxes [ Merrill et al., 2026 , Chan et al., 2025 ] , or interactive performance in arenas [ Zheng et al., 2023 , Guertler et al., 2025 , Liu et al., 2026 ] . Chess with LLMs. Chess has served as a testbed for artificial intelligence for decades, from Deep Blue [ Campbell et al., 2002 ] to AlphaZero Silver et al. [2018b] and Leela Chess Zero [ The LCZero Authors, 2026 ] . Existing work uses chess to study LLM capabilities in several ways. Some studies internalize chess behavior by pre-training or heavily fine-tuning model weights on offline game trajectories and state evaluations [ Noever et al., 2020 , Feng et al., 2023 , Zhang et al., 2025 ] . In a post-training alternative, Hwang et al. [2025] found that reinforcement learning plateaus at 25 ​ – ​ 30 % 25\text{--}30\% puzzle accuracy, and hypothesized that generic base models lack the foundational state-tracking representations required for RL to build upon. Separately, Karvonen [2024] used probes to study internal representations of board-state and latent estimates of opponent skill in models trained in chess transcripts. Our work instead treats chess as a text-based reasoning task for general-purpose frozen frontier language models. Previous studies show that model performance is sensitive to the representation of the board, such as FEN strings or PGN move lists, and to the prompt structure [ Acher, 2023 , Kolasani et al., 2025 , Cooper, 2025 ] . Manual prompt engineering involving metadata headers, few-shot exemplars, or PGN styles can therefore produce substantial performance differences [ dynomight, 2024 , Witten, 2023 ] . Rather than relying on targeted manual interventions, we automate prompt optimization to provide a shared testbed for evaluating Automatic Prompt Optimization ( APO ) algorithms. Automatic Prompt Optimization ( APO ). APO adapts LLM behavior without updating the underlying parameters [ Ramnath et al., 2025 ] . APO approaches typically use LLMs to generate, mutate, and select instructions or demonstrations through discrete search, gradient-free editing, or evolutionary algorithms [ Jiang et al., 2020 , Zhou et al., 2023 , Prasad et al., 2023 , Pryzant et al., 2023 , Yang et al., 2024 ] . Frameworks such as DSPy [ Khattab et al., 2024 ] compile declarative pipelines with optimizers including MIPROv2 [ Opsahl-Ong et al., 2024 ] , GEPA [ Agrawal et al., 2026 ] , and SIMBA [ Khattab et al., 2024 ] to maximize downstream metrics. Although existing APO literature highlights gains in tasks like text classification Du et al. [2026] , question answering Yang et al. [2018] , and mathematical reasoning [ Ramnath et al., 2025 , Cobbe et al., 2021 , Zhang and Math-AI, 2025 ] , games offer a complementary setting in which optimized behavior can be evaluated both on isolated decisions and through their consequences during play. Whereas Autoharness [ Lou et al., 2026 ] synthesizes helper code to improve game-play, we isolate the automatic optimization of instructions and demonstrations for frozen LLMs. 3 Chess Puzzle Benchmark We use chess to study prompt optimization for general-purpose language models rather than models specifically fine-tuned to play chess games [ Lehrach et al., 2025 , Feng et al., 2023 , Zhang et al., 2025 ] . Prompt Optimization with Chess. Let f ⁡ ( x , θ , p ) f(x;\theta,p) denote the prediction of a model with parameters θ \theta and prompt p p for an input x x . The loss ℓ ⁡ ( f ⁡ ( x , θ , p ) , y ) \ell(f(x;\theta,p),y) measures its error relative to the reference label y y . In APO, f f is an LLM whose parameters θ \theta remain frozen. The goal is to find the prompt p ∗ = arg ⁡ min ⁡ ∑ ( x , y ) p ⁡ ℓ ⁡ ( f ⁡ ( x , θ , p ) , y ) p^{*}=\arg\min_{p}\sum_{(x,y)}\ell(f(x;\theta,p),y) . Figure 1 (b) illustrates this objective. The success of APO depends partly on the quality of ℓ \ell . A useful scoring function should (1) apply to new inputs and predictions, (2) be cheap, since it sits inside the optimization loop, and (3) provide unambiguous feedback, ideally with graded scores. Few benchmarks offer all three properties at once. Open-ended tasks often require costly LLM judgements, while verifiable domains such as math and code typically use binary rewards and require effort to extend their problem sets. Chess offers all three: an engine can evaluate predicted moves in new positions, cheaply and with graded, reproducible scores. The same engine can supply labels for fresh positions, allowing the benchmark to be renewed to reduce contamination risk and grow in difficulty as models improve. Chess Puzzle Representation and Evaluation. A puzzle comprises N N position–move pairs ( x i , y i ) i = 0 N − 1 (x_{i},y_{i})_{i=0}^{N-1} along its reference solution. We query each position independently; the puzzle is solved if f ⁡ ( x i , θ , p ) = y i f(x_{i};\theta,p)=y_{i} for every i i . The default loss is the exact-match error; Appendix B.2 adds a graded engine-based evaluation. Following Feng et al. [2023] , Kolasani et al. [2025] , the inputs contain the position in the Forsyth–Edwards notation ( FEN ), the side to move and explicit legal moves [ Hwang et al., 2025 ] . Although representation preferences vary between models [ Kolasani et al., 2025 ] , our preliminary experiments confirmed FEN as an effective input representation for our models. The model returns one legal move in SAN or UCI , wrapped in JSON. A response that violates this formatting cannot be parsed and is scored as incorrect. See Figure 1 for input construction and Appendices A.1 and B.1 for notation and prompt examples. Dataset Construction and Difficulty. We construct the benchmark from 1,118 puzzles extracted from the Lichess database, whose exports are released under CC0 1.0 Universal 1 1 1 https://database.lichess.org/ . We divide the puzzles equally into training and test sets of 559 puzzles. The puzzles are stratified along two axes: the number of puzzle moves (one to five) and the puzzle elo rating (400 to 2,800). The complete distribution across the length and rating of the puzzle can be found in the Appendix A.3 . Because the current benchmark is drawn from the public Lichess database, we cannot exclude pretraining exposure. Its advantage is therefore not guaranteed absence of contamination, but renewability: the same pipeline can construct evaluation sets from newer games and positions. Lichess ratings estimate difficulty for human solvers and also organize our LLM results: higher-rated puzzles are solved less often (Appendix B.5 ). In this dataset, one- and two-move puzzles occur below 2000, while three- to five-move puzzles occur above 2000. Rating and length are therefore descriptive axes, not independent difficulty factors or estimates of an LLM’s playing rating. Preliminary experiments showed that recent models do not saturate our puzzle database, making the current set appropriate for APO evaluation. 4 Experimental Setup We compare six APO algorithms across eight target models, using a ninth model, Gemini 3.5 Flash, as the fixed meta-model for prompt proposal and reflection. All target models start from the same instruction: “Solve the chess puzzle by finding the single best move.” The benchmark contains 559 training puzzles and 559 test puzzles. Candidate selection uses the training data according to each optimizer’s configuration. We describe the optimizers and feedback below, followed by the model panel and baseline evaluations. 4.1 Algorithms We select six optimizers that cover complementary prompt-improvement strategies: some optimize instructions, some optimize demonstrations, and some jointly optimize both components. They also differ in the type of feedback they exploit and in the search mechanism used to identify improved prompts. Genetic-Pareto ( GEPA ) [ Agrawal et al., 2026 ] : GEPA employs a genetic-reflective optimization strategy that couples prompt mutation with Pareto-inspired selection. It constructs a Pareto frontier consisting of prompts that perform best on specific subsets of the evaluation set and iteratively evolves the candidates on this frontier. Crucially, GEPA uses execution traces together with textual and numerical feedback to diagnose errors and guide prompt mutations, allowing the search to exploit both qualitative information about model failures and quantitative performance signals. Cooperative Prompt Optimization ( COPRO ) [ Khattab et al., 2024 ] : COPRO optimizes the instruction component of a prompt through an iterative coordinate-ascent-style search. A meta-model generates multiple candidate instruction variants, which are evaluated using a scalar performance metric. The best-performing candidates are retained and subsequently refined, producing an iterative search over instruction space driven exclusively by numerical feedback. BootstrapFewShot ( BFS ) [ Khattab et al., 2024 ] : BFS improves prompts by augmenting them with few-shot demonstrations obtained through successful trace bootstrapping. The optimizer executes the program over training examples and retains intermediate execution traces for which the model produces correct outputs. These successful traces are then incorporated into the prompt as demonstrations, making the optimization process dependent on numerical feedback while leaving the instruction component unchanged. BootstrapRandomSearch ( BRS ) [ Khattab et al., 2024 ] : Like BFS , BRS optimizes prompts by selecting few-shot demonstrations. It performs a random search over alternative demonstration sets by repeatedly sampling different combinations of demonstrations. It evaluates each configuration on the validation set, and retains those achieving the highest numerical performance. This allows BRS to explore a broader space of possible demonstration configurations than standard bootstrapping. Multiprompt Instruction Proposal Optimizer Version 2 ( MIPROv2 ) [ Opsahl-Ong et al., 2024 ] : MIPROv2 jointly optimizes prompt instructions and few-shot demonstrations. It first proposes a diverse set of contextualized candidate instructions based on information about the task and program structure, while also bootstrapping examples that the model can solve successfully. It then formulates prompt selection as a joint search problem and uses Bayesian optimization to efficiently explore combinations of candidate instructions and demonstrations. Each configuration is evaluated using a numerical validation metric, and the search progressively focuses on combinations expected to yield higher performance. Stochastic Introspective Mini-Batch Ascent ( SIMBA ) [ Khattab et al., 2024 ] : SIMBA 2 2 2 https://blog.mariusvach.com/posts/dspy-simba combines few-shot demonstration selection with stochastic introspective optimization of instructions. It samples mini-batches and queries the LLM under different temperature settings to generate diverse execution traces. By comparing successful and unsuccessful traces, as well as identifying examples with high output variability, the optimizer uses self-reflection to derive explicit heuristics or rules that can be incorporated into the prompt. Alternatively, successful traces can be added as demonstrations. In this way, SIMBA exploits both textual feedback derived from introspection and numerical feedback from the evaluation metric while jointly modifying instructions and examples. Longer instructions and demonstrations increase the input-token burden (Table 8 ), but total cost also depends on the model’s output length and pricing. Optimization increases evaluation cost for every model except Spark 1.2 Contributor, which reduces its cost. We hypothesize that, since the model was verbose, prompt optimization managed to channel the reasoning and make it more efficient to answer even if it was not optimized for that. The separate meta-model, Gemini 3.5 Flash, remains stronger at 55.40%. Under the baseline prompts, Flash Lite’s cost per 1,000 solver-position calls is approximately one tenth that of GPT-5.6 Luna and Muse Spark 1.2 Contributor. Figure 2(b) shows the performance distribution for each algorithm. Although no optimizer wins everywhere, the distributions reveal distinct patterns on this benchmark under the evaluated configurations. GEPA has the highest median gain and improves mean accuracy for 6 out of the 8 models. Unlike SIMBA , it also supports the decision endpoint evaluated here because it does not require a tunable temperature. SIMBA has the largest mean gain and rarely falls below the baseline, but its mean is mainly driven by large improvements on Gemini 3.5 Flash Lite. BRS matches GEPA ’s mean gain with a lower median and more spread, and its mean is also mainly driven by gains on Gemini 3.5 Flash Lite. COPRO has the smallest impact on performance, while MIPROv2 and BFS fall below baseline more often than not, with BFS showing the largest loss. Takeaway. Baseline accuracy does not tell the whole story. Flash Lite starts behind Luna and Muse, yet its larger optimization gain moves it ahead of both. The weakest models, meanwhile, remain far behind despite some significant improvements. This contrast makes responsiveness to optimization a useful comparison axis alongside baseline strength and cost, consistent with earlier evidence that optimizer performance varies across settings [ Du et al., 2026 , Sarmah et al., 2024 ] . The benchmark is challenging yet improvable: optimization succeeds in some settings and fails in others, exposing differences between methods and models. This breadth of comparison is achievable at modest cost. 5.2 Gains Across Puzzle Difficulty and Structure Do optimization gains concentrate in particular puzzles, or are they spread across the benchmark? We see in Figure 5 that prompts produced by the same algorithm have similar compositions across models: SIMBA emphasizes chess guidance and examples, whereas GEPA produces longer chess instructions together with formatting and persona framing. APO algorithms significantly influence the composition of the prompts. The algorithms that can combine several content types, SIMBA and GEPA , produce the best results for seven of the eight models. The best prompts do not simply include more of everything: the largest gain comes from a prompt with only two categories, and no winning prompt uses all the content types. These different prompt compositions raise a further question: are they useful task guidance or adaptations to a particular model? 5.4 Are Optimized Prompts Generic or Model Specific? Does APO discover general task guidance, or does it adapt prompts to a particular model? We investigate this question by applying each selected prompt unchanged to other models. Keeping the puzzle set fixed lets us examine generalization across models rather than across puzzles. Gains on other models would indicate that the prompt’s usefulness extends beyond its source, while uneven or negative transfer would reveal dependence on the recipient. For each of seven source models, we select the prompt with the highest single-run test-set puzzle accuracy. Qwen3.8 27B, Jev 1.13, Claude Haiku 4.5, and DeepSeek V4 Pro receive significant improvements from prompts optimized for other models. Despite its different serving interface, Jev 1.13 is the only source whose prompt improves all seven other models, with mean changes from +0.18 to +3.10 points. Native improvement and portability are distinct: Jev’s prompt improves all seven other models, whereas Flash Lite’s prompt, which has the largest native gain, degrades five of them. Optimized guidance can extend beyond its source model, but its usefulness depends on the recipient. This matches the literature: cross-model transfer of optimized prompts has been known since Jiang et al. [2020] , with prompts from small models transferring to larger ones while the reverse degrades [ Deng et al., 2022 ] . Agrawal et al. [2026] even report small-model prompts beating optimizers running directly on the larger target. In our experiments this pattern appears only in a few cases, for example the Jev 1.13 prompt brings DeepSeek V4 Pro above its own best optimized accuracy. Cross-model transfer changes the model while holding the puzzle task fixed. We now change the setting: do puzzle-optimized prompts also help when the model plays a sequence of moves against an opponent? 5.5 Transfer from Puzzles to Play Chess play requires tactics as well as decision making in balanced positions. We therefore evaluate whether prompts selected by puzzle accuracy transfer to game-play rollouts against Stockfish (GPL-3.0 License 5 5 5 https://github.com/official-stockfish/Stockfish?tab=GPL-3.0-1-ov-file ) at depth 20. We evaluate ten starting conditions: the initial board with the model playing as White or Black, and eight positions from common openings (details in Appendix A.2 ). For each rollout, the model predicts at most ten model moves. As for puzzles, model move predictions are independent calls that receive only the prompt, the current FEN , color to play, and legal moves; Stockfish replies after every accepted move. We compare each model’s base prompt with its best changed held-out puzzle prompt; Gemini 3.5 Flash is shown only with its base prompt. Unlike exact-match puzzle scoring, game-play evaluation does not require a unique reference move. At each model turn, regret is the difference between Stockfish ’s depth-20 evaluations of its best move and of the model’s move. Regret is measured only for accepted moves; illegal or unparseable attempts terminate the rollout. Rollouts also stop at ten model moves, a terminal board state, or a timeout. Because prompts induce different trajectories and termination patterns, regret compares move quality on each prompt’s own visited states, conditional on producing accepted moves, rather than on a shared position set. The preliminary evaluation of the relationship between regret evaluation in puzzles vs binary evaluation in puzzles can be found in the Appendix B.2 . Better tactical puzzle solving does not, by itself, establish better play from standard opening positions. This game-level evaluation is valuable beyond the transfer question itself. Because the same engine-based regret scores both puzzle solving and game-play rollouts, we can directly estimate the gap between benchmark gains and performance in this broader setting. Chess thus highlights how much an improvement in one specific skill contributes to the overall task, whereas in most domains, coding, mathematics, or question answering, this benchmark-to-deployment gap is far harder to estimate. 6 Conclusion We introduced a chess-puzzle benchmark and compared six prompt optimizers across eight target models. Four models show significant puzzle-accuracy gains, with improvements reaching 7.57 percentage points, while the hardest puzzles remain largely out of reach. The study distinguishes three properties: baseline strength, responsiveness to optimization, and transferability. Stronger models do not necessarily improve more, and larger gains do not guarantee better transfer. Some prompts help other models, but we find no clear reduction in mean regret in the evaluated game-play rollouts. The benchmark reveals not just how much prompts improve, but where those improvements apply. Chess makes repeated evaluation inexpensive, datasets renewable, and difficulty adjustable as models improve. It also lets us test whether puzzle gains carry over to play. Together, these properties make it an affordable testbed for APO before evaluation in more costly application domains. B.5 Interpreting Lichess Puzzle Ratings for LLMs Our benchmark uses the Lichess puzzle rating as a difficulty axis. This rating is a human-calibrated scale, so we first check what it means for LLMs. The puzzle rating is derived from Lichess ’s puzzle-difficulty classification, which uses the Glicko-2 system Glickman [2001] , Glickman [2012] . Glicko-2 is a paired-comparison rating system: like Elo, it models the expected outcome of a comparison as a function of the relative ratings of the two entities. In the Lichess model, a puzzle is treated analogously to an opponent that wins whenever the user fails to solve it. The key difference between the two systems is that Glicko-2 also accounts for uncertainty in the ratings. By definition, these ratings are relative and impose a scalar ordering based on the performance of users on the platform, whether the rated entities are human players or puzzles. However, constructing a benchmark of chess puzzles requires us to freeze the puzzle ratings and ignore the uncertainty aspect of Glicko-2 and interpret ratings as an external descriptive variable. While the puzzle rating is calibrated for human players, no such calibration exists for LLMs. Consequently, we ask whether it can serve as a proxy for task difficulty for LLMs in our benchmark . Empirically, we observe significant correlations between LLMs’ puzzle solve rates and puzzle ratings. Across pooled 200-point Elo bins, correlations were r = − 0.937 r=-0.937 ( p < 0.001 p<0.001 , 8 bins) and r = − 0.996 r=-0.996 ( p = 0.004 p=0.004 , 4 bins) for 1–2- and 3–5-move puzzles, respectively. Each 200-point Elo increase multiplies solve odds by 0.72 for 1–2-move puzzles and 0.45 for 3–5-move puzzles, corresponding to odds reductions of 28% and 55%, respectively. Under the standard Elo model, a 200-point disadvantage multiplies the odds by 10 − 200 / 400 ≈ 0.316 10^{-200/400}\approx 0.316 , corresponding to a reduction of 68.4% odds. For an equal-rated player, this changes the expected success rate from 50% to approximately 24%, a decrease of 26 percentage points, or 52% relative to the original success rate. Notice that this is not the rating-update rule used by Lichess ; it provides a simple theoretical reference for interpreting rating differences. Jev 1.13 is evaluated through the Typesafe decision endpoint ( /api/alpha/decisions ) rather than a generative OpenRouter chat endpoint. The decision request carries the same information as the generative prompts: the instructions and demonstrations, the position fields ( FEN and side to move), and the legal moves, which serve as the answer options. The model does not generate text. It chooses among the listed legal moves only, so its answer space is exactly the provided option set. The endpoint bills input tokens only and exposes no temperature or sampling controls. Generative settings such as max_output_tokens , temperature , and the JSON adapter therefore do not apply.
