RoboHarness: A Simple Harness Outperforms VLA and World Action Models Bince Qu Affiliation: Zhejiang University Email: cebin598@gmail.com Wei Chen Affiliation: Zhejiang University Email: chenvis@zju.edu.cn Bo Zhang Affiliation: Zhejiang University Email: zhangboknight@gmail.com Abstract The rise of large language models (LLMs) has opened a path toward generalist embodied AI, raising the question of how LLMs can act in the physical world. One straightforward approach is training the model to generate actions: collect robot-action data and video demonstration, refine LLM architecture with an action head, and train a vision-language-action (VLA) model at scale. However, we argue that coding agents have the potential to realize generalist embodied manipulation. All they need is a simple yet effective robot harness. We present RoboHarness : a robotic harness that gives LLM agents a visual-geometric control panel so that they can directly understand and invoke embodied tasks. With RoboHarness, Qwen3.8-Flash-Next ( Qwen Team, 2026 ) outperforms state-of-the-art VLA models on BEHAVIOR Challenge 2025 tasks ( Li et al., 2024 ) . The implementation is open source. On the first 10 BEHAVIOR Challenge 2025 tasks, Qwen3.8-Flash-Next attains a higher mean q q -score than the state-of-the-art VLA models shown. 1 Introduction The emergence of large language models (LLMs) opened a path towards generalist robot manipulation. The community has explored many ways to make use of LLM for planning, visual understanding, physical reasoning, and spatial reasoning to achieve generalizable robot manipulation. Yet an LLM’s world knowledge lives in a textual space. A bridge between the LLM and the physical world is therefore required: One straightforward approach is training the model to generate actions. VLA models try to make the model output end-effector actions directly. Actions may be encoded into the vocabulary for autoregressive generation, as in RT-2, OpenVLA, and FAST ( Brohan et al., 2023 ; Kim et al., 2025b ; Pertsch et al., 2025 ) , or grouped into flow-matching chunks, which are later produced by an action head appended to the LLM ( Black et al., 2025b ; Bjorck et al., 2025 ) . This representation can express arbitrary robot actions, but it requires substantial robot-action data and additional post-training. Such data are difficult to collect and scale to the diversity of real-world objects, scenes, and tasks. This difficulty has motivated world-action models (WAMs), which seek to improve generalization by learning action-conditioned models of how the world changes ( Zhao et al., 2026 ; Wang et al., 2026 ; Li et al., 2026 ) . We take a different view. An LLM already has strong capabilities for task decomposition, planning, spatial reasoning, and tool use. The main challenge is therefore not to train the LLM to generate a larger set of actions, but to provide a simple and sufficiently complete interface that connects these capabilities to the physical world. Existing work provides evidence for this view: Packaging embodied subtasks, such as picking, placing, and navigation, as callable functions allows an LLM to compose them into longer procedures ( Liang et al., 2023 ) . ASPIRE shows that an LLM agent can refine such programs and accumulate the repairs in a skill library ( Lu et al., 2026 ) . However, the available functions are not complete: their coverage is determined by predefined tasks, objects, and policies. Every new object or task may require another function, leaving the LLM confined to a predefined action space. This suggests that the main limitation lies in the action interface rather than in the LLM’s ability. In this paper, we propose RoboHarness . Without training models, RoboHarness combines visual clicks, keypoint tracking, and geometric constraints to provide an embodied control panel for an LLM agent. Concretely, our contributions are: An expressive, LLM-friendly robot harness that provides an embodied control panel for an LLM agent. A new paradigm for embodied AI. We show that a generalist LLM, equipped with the right tool harness can outperform VLA and World Action Models With RoboHarness, the open-source model Qwen3.8-Flash-Next can directly solve long-horizon household tasks in BEHAVIOR-1K ( Li et al., 2024 ) without any finetune, outperforming state-of-the-art models. It also substantially outperforms VLA and WAM baselines on fully out-of-distribution generalization tests. 2 Related Work Prior work connects pretrained language and vision-language models to manipulation through learned action generation, executable programs, or spatial representations. These approaches differ in where they place the boundary between model reasoning and robot control. 2.1 Vision-Language-Action Models Autoregressive action tokens. RT-2 and OpenVLA adapt pretrained VLMs to predict discretized robot actions through next-token prediction ( Brohan et al., 2023 ; Kim et al., 2025b ) . FAST improves this approach by compressing action chunks with a frequency-domain tokenizer, making autoregressive learning more effective for high-frequency control ( Pertsch et al., 2025 ) . Galaxea G0.5 further integrates reasoning and action tokens into one autoregressive stream, combining task decomposition, visual grounding, and motor commands within a shared model ( Liu et al., 2026 ) . Continuous action experts. Another family couples a pretrained VLM with a specialized module for generating continuous action sequences. Representative examples include the flow-matching action expert in π 0 \pi_{0} and the diffusion transformer in GR00T N1 ( Black et al., 2025b ; Bjorck et al., 2025 ) . More recent models, such as π 0.7 \pi_{0.7} , condition action generation on richer task context, including subtask instructions, subgoal images, and episode metadata, to improve steerability ( Physical Intelligence, 2026 ) . These approaches learn a broad motor action space, but require robot-action supervision and additional training. Preserving pretrained knowledge is also a training-design concern: knowledge insulation mitigates interference by blocking action-expert gradients from entering the VLM backbone ( Driess et al., 2025 ) . RoboHarness connects an unchanged LLM to robot control through a geometric tool interface, avoiding this additional model-training stage. 2.2 LLMs as Robot Planners and Programmers Skill and module orchestration. SayCan grounds language-level skill selection in learned affordance values, while Inner Monologue incorporates environmental feedback into replanning ( Ahn et al., 2023 ; Huang et al., 2023b ) . HarnessVLA combines a frozen VLA for contact-rich manipulation with analytic primitives, using execution memory to guide retries and recovery ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) . Program synthesis and skill discovery. Code as Policies extends composition to generated control programs, including feedback loops and waypoint-level commands ( Liang et al., 2023 ) . CaP-X systematically varies the abstraction level of robot APIs and finds that performance degrades as human-designed abstractions are removed; execution feedback and skill discovery help agents work with lower-level interfaces ( Fu et al., 2026 ) . ASPIRE compounds program repairs into a growing skill library ( Lu et al., 2026 ) . 2.3 Spatial Representations for Manipulation Visual points and motion proposals. RoboPoint learns to predict image-space affordance points from synthetic supervision ( Yuan et al., 2025 ) . PIVOT iteratively selects among visual action proposals with a frozen VLM, and MOKA selects marked keypoints and waypoints ( Nasiriany et al., 2024 ; Fang et al., 2024 ) . HAMSTER predicts a two-dimensional end-effector path and passes it to a separate low-level policy ( Li et al., 2025 ) . VoxPoser grounds language in composable three-dimensional value maps and uses model-based planning to generate six-degree-of-freedom end-effector trajectories ( Huang et al., 2023a ) . Constraint-based approaches express the required geometry more explicitly. CoPa grounds object parts in the observed scene and solves geometric constraints over those parts ( Huang et al., 2024 ) . OmniManip represents interaction points and directions in an object’s canonical space and tracks the resulting six-dimensional poses ( Pan et al., 2025 ) . ReKep synthesizes relational constraints over three-dimensional keypoints as Python functions and optimizes subgoals and paths in a closed perception–action loop ( Huang et al., 2025 ) . These works establish that grounded geometric relations can support orientation-aware, multi-stage manipulation without task-specific VLM training. 3 Method Lessons from coding agents show that: a minimal set of simple, orthogonal primitive tools outperforms a collection of predefined features and heavy scaffolding. Inspired by this principle, we argue that an LLM’s potential on embodied tasks is best unlocked by giving it the minimal primitive action tools rather than layers of engineered features. We start from studying spatial relations between a gripper and objects it interacts with, therefore constructing the most elementary primitive action tools. We further prove their completeness in Section 4 : (i) any contact pose between a gripper and an object can be expressed by geometric constraints, and (ii) once the gripper holds an object, any spatial relationship between the in-hand object and a target object can be expressed by geometric constraints. With the primitive action tools in place, the remaining challenge is spatial understanding: although VLMs cannot reliably reason about 3D spatial relations, their grounding training enables them to localize specific points on objects with reasonable accuracy. We therefore use spatial grounding as the bridge between vision-language models and robots. The LLM marks points of interest on a 2D image and continuously receives their positions via optical-flow tracking and depth back-projection; with this information, it composes accurate primitive actions to perform complex manipulation. Following this design, we build a simple but effective Robot Harness, which we will release as open source to the community. 3.1 Geometry State for Spatial Grounding To make primitive action tools more usable to the LLM, we design a persistent geometry-state tracking mechanism that continuously returns the coordinates of objects of interest to the LLM, enhancing its accurate spacial perception capabilities. 3.1.1 Robot coordination definition We first define a unified camera-frame coordination for diverse robot configurations, making it straightforward for the LLM to read spatial relations: Axis Positive direction x x forward from camera view y y left from camera view z z upward from camera view Figure 2: Visualization of the unified coordinate frame on two embodiments: BEHAVIOR R1 Pro ( Li et al., 2024 ) (left) and RoboDojo ARX X5 ( Chen et al., 2026 ) (right). 3.1.2 Track keypoints With a unified coordinate frame in place, we design the track_object tool for LLM to retrieve coordinates of points in which it is interested using no simulation priors the procedure and proceeds as follows: 1. Figure 3: Pipeline of the track_object tool: optical-flow keypoint tracking followed by depth back-projection into the robot coordinate frame. 3.2 Action Toolset Our goal is to design a minimal action toolset. Therefore, we start from object-interaction relations and build the toolset bottom-up. A manipulation task can be viewed as a two-stage process: first the gripper establishes a spatial relationship with an object, and then an end-effector action drives this object to satisfy a geometric relationship with another object. For instance, in an insert-key task, the gripper first grasps the key, then moves it until it satisfies the insertion constraint with the lock. Consequently, our action toolset only needs to capture these two stages. We implement the first stage with keypoint-and-constraint tools: the LLM selects points on the image and specifies the geometric constraints they must satisfy; the harness automatically solves and executes the corresponding actions. To support model fine-tuning, we additionally provide adjustment tools that directly command the robot’s low-level degrees of freedom. In the next section, we will prove that, for any object, any grasp pose can be expressed by two keypoint constraints when the object has not yet been grasped; and when it has already been grasped, assuming the gripper forms a rigid connection with the object, the desired spatial relation between this held object and any other object in the scene can be expressed by two sets of keypoint constraints. Therefore, our action toolset is both minimal and complete. 3.2.1 Keypoint-constraint tools for manipulation The model supplies a set of input points together with their spatial relations, and the harness automatically solves and executes the corresponding action. The model must distinguish between on-hand and off-hand points: on-hand points lie on the gripper or on a held object and their positions can be modified, whereas off-hand points are fixed in the scene. The harness drives the on-hand points so that they satisfy the spatial constraints specified by the model through the following pipeline: 1. Sample action candidates and select those satisfying the constraints. 2. Discard candidates whose inverse kinematics are infeasible or unreachable. 3. Plan a motion trajectory from the current pose to the target pose. 4. Execute the trajectory. In principle, the model can express any constraint through coordinate relations among the input points. However, expressions such as collinearity and coplanarity are both verbose to write and frequently required, so we encapsulate the most common patterns as quick requests; their specific modes are listed in Table 1 . LLMs can come up with brilliant plans and solutions for embodied tasks, but often fail to ground in the robot’s physical embodiment, leading to failures such as knocking over objects, falling, or getting stuck. To mitigate this on BEHAVIOR long-horizon mobile manipulation tasks, we hand-craft a task-specific prompt for every task that specifies the execution steps and the behavioral boundaries the model must respect. Future work aims to close this gap further by training the model or evolving the harness to become more physically grounded. AI use statement Generative AI tools were used in three ways. (i) They assisted writing. (ii) They assisted running the experiments: launching jobs, monitoring long rollouts, and flagging failed runs so that each experiment kept running stably, while every experimental setting and every reported number was checked by the authors. (iii) They assisted coding: writing and debugging RoboHarness human interface.
