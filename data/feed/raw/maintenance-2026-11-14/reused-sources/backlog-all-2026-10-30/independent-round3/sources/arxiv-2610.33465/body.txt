Address: Eastern Institute of Technology, Ningbo, China Abstract Equation discovery solvers often use different data interfaces, equation representations and evaluation criteria, making their results difficult to compare. We present LegoSR, a unified framework for algebraic symbolic regression and differential equation discovery. LegoSR integrates seven solvers through a common equation representation and evaluation process, and supports searches constrained by partially known equations. Researchers can run solvers directly or use LegoSR Agent, which selects solvers and adapts searches based on solver knowledge, intermediate results and lessons from earlier tasks. On noisy ordinary and partial differential equation benchmarks, LegoSR Agent recovers nearly as many equations as the union of seven expert-tuned solvers and outperforms a generic agent with the same model, tools and budget by up to 29 percentage points. Three examples demonstrate basic use, adaptive solver selection and discovery guided by partial physical knowledge. Keywords: Equation discovery , Symbolic regression , Partial differential equation , Large language model agent , Automated scientific discovery 1 Introduction Mathematical equations are fundamental to describing natural phenomena, understanding physical laws and predicting the behavior of complex systems. As scientific data accumulate, data-driven equation discovery has become an active topic in scientific computing and artificial intelligence [ 1 ] . Numerous methods for symbolic regression (SR) and ordinary and partial differential equation (ODE/PDE) discovery have been developed, including sparse regression [ 2 , 3 ] , evolutionary search [ 4 , 5 , 6 ] , reinforcement learning [ 7 , 8 ] and language models [ 9 , 10 ] . These methods use different mathematical representations, search strategies and optimization schemes, each with strengths for particular scientific problems. However, most are developed as isolated solvers, with different data interfaces, ways of incorporating prior knowledge and evaluation procedures. In practice, scientific discovery is rarely a single run. Researchers must compare methods, analyze candidate equations and experimental results, and refine their approach based on previous findings. These activities require substantial manual effort, while results and knowledge are difficult to reuse across methods and experiments. Organizing heterogeneous solvers into a unified, continuous and traceable scientific discovery process therefore remains an important challenge. To address this challenge, we introduce LegoSR, a unified and modular framework for symbolic regression and equation discovery. LegoSR integrates seven methods with different representations and search mechanisms, providing common task definitions and a shared Equation Intermediate Representation (Equation IR) for exchanging and reusing equations. A discovery task specifies the data and can incorporate prior knowledge through a Sketch, a partially specified equation. Reusable components support derivative computation, coefficient optimization, candidate evaluation and visualization. Solvers can use these components while retaining their native search and optimization procedures, without following a fixed pipeline. LegoSR also organizes experiment configurations, candidate equations, evaluation results and search records into a unified experiment management environment. Together, these capabilities support cross-method comparison, experiment tracking and reuse of previous results, turning separate solver runs into a continuous and traceable research process. However, a unified experiment environment does not automatically determine how scientific discovery should proceed. Researchers still need to interpret experimental feedback and decide whether to continue a search, adjust its configuration or switch solvers. These decisions require knowledge of different solvers and experience from previous experiments. Recent advances in large language model (LLM) agents offer new possibilities for automating such multi-step tasks through planning and tool use [ 11 ] . Such agents have planned and carried out chemical experiments [ 12 ] , generated and refined research hypotheses [ 13 ] and solved mechanics problems by writing and running finite element code [ 14 ] . Yet general-purpose agents often lack systematic knowledge of equation discovery methods and their applicable conditions. Generic prompts and tool calls alone make it difficult to use experimental history effectively and develop informed search strategies. Building on LegoSR’s unified equation representation and experiment environment, we further develop LegoSR Agent, combining solver knowledge, structured experimental memory and feedback-driven decision making. The agent draws on solver papers and previous experiments to select suitable methods and configure searches. Across multiple rounds of experimentation, it evaluates candidate equations and search progress, deciding whether to continue, adjust configurations or switch solvers. Experimental results and decisions are recorded and reused to guide subsequent exploration, forming a closed-loop, adaptive scientific discovery process. In this way, LegoSR extends from a unified equation discovery framework to an agent-guided system that accumulates experimental experience and adapts its strategies based on feedback. On noisy ODE and PDE benchmarks, LegoSR Agent recovers nearly as many equations as all seven solvers combined under expert-tuned settings, and more than a generic agent with the same model, tools and budget. 2 LegoSR framework 2.1 Why a unified framework Equation discovery methods differ in their mathematical representations, search strategies and optimization procedures. LegoSR integrates seven representative solvers covering sparse regression, evolutionary search, reinforcement learning and language models. Table 1 summarizes their original designs. Their software dependencies, data interfaces, equation formats and runtime configurations differ. Many publicly available implementations were developed for specific experiments, with preprocessing, derivative computation, search and evaluation tied to dedicated scripts. Applying a method to new data, switching methods or comparing repeated runs therefore often requires substantial code changes and manual setup. LegoSR adapts, refactors and extends these implementations rather than simply wrapping them behind a common interface. We reorganize procedures written for single experiments into configurable, reusable pipelines and extend several solvers to additional problem types. Common task definitions, equation representations and experiment records allow different methods to run, be compared and reuse their results within one framework. 2.2 Modular architecture LegoSR organizes different methods into a unified but flexible workflow for task preparation, equation search, evaluation and experiment management ( Fig. 1 ). The framework standardizes how these stages exchange data and results, while allowing each solver to retain its own representation, search strategy and optimization objective. Solvers can therefore be used individually or as part of a larger discovery process. Figure 1: Architecture of LegoSR. Shared scientific computing modules support heterogeneous solvers, with Equation IR, Sketches and experiment management connecting their workflows. LegoSR supports regular grids, scattered observations and feature matrices. Shared modules provide derivative computation, coefficient optimization, data diagnostics, evaluation and visualization. These modules can be used independently or within a solver pipeline. For example, researchers can inspect derivative quality before a search, or fit and evaluate a candidate equation without rerunning the solver. Separating these operations from solver-specific search code also makes existing methods easier to extend to new problems. To exchange equations across methods, LegoSR uses a common Equation Intermediate Representation (Equation IR). Its term level describes expressions built from variables, derivatives, mathematical operators and parameters, while its equation level defines the overall structure, coefficients and constraints. The IR supports algebraic relations, evolution equations and homogeneous relations. An evolution PDE, for example, can be written as u t = ∑ k = 1 K ξ k ​ ϕ k , u_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\xi_{k}\phi_{k}, (1) where ϕ k \phi_{k} represents a term involving the physical field, coordinates and derivatives, such as u ​ u x uu_{x} or u x ​ x u_{xx} , and ξ k \xi_{k} is its coefficient. Terms may also contain nonlinear operations and internal parameters. Solvers still search in their native forms, such as expression trees, symbol sequences or candidate-term libraries, and convert their results into Equation IR. Equation IR expressions can be compiled into callable Python functions for numerical computation, parameter fitting and evaluation. The common representation also allows discovered expressions to be reused as candidate terms or prior knowledge in later searches [ 16 ] . LegoSR uses Sketches to represent partially known equations. A Sketch specifies the known structure while leaving an expression to be discovered. For example, when the advection term and its coefficient are known in the Burgers equation, we can write u t = − u ​ u x + ℛ , u_{t}=-uu_{x}+\mathcal{R}, (2) where ℛ \mathcal{R} is a hole, an unknown expression that may contain one or more terms. A Sketch can fix a known term together with its coefficient, or fix only the term and leave its coefficient to be fitted. The hole can also be constrained by the number of terms, variables, mathematical operators and derivative orders. LegoSR converts a Sketch into a search task for each solver and reconstructs the complete equation from the result. In this example, moving the known term to the left gives the target u t + u ​ u x u_{t}+uu_{x} , which the solver fits with ℛ \mathcal{R} . Different solvers can therefore use the same prior knowledge without following the same search procedure. For each run, LegoSR records its configuration, search trajectory, candidate equations and evaluation results. It supports checkpoints, resuming interrupted runs, branching from previous search states and batch experiments. Tasks can also be scheduled across CPU and GPU resources. These records allow researchers to inspect earlier searches and continue or extend experiments without starting over. Because native scores, such as AIC or a reward, differ across solvers, LegoSR preserves them while providing a common evaluation. Candidate equations are compared under consistent data processing and derivative estimation settings. For each candidate equation E E , the framework refits the coefficients by least squares, subject to existing constraints, and reports the normalized residual J ⁡ ( E ) J(E) . Where applicable, it also integrates E E forward and compares the predicted trajectories or fields with observations. Equation IR, Sketches, shared computing modules and experiment records also provide the foundation for LegoSR Agent. The agent uses these resources to select solvers, adjust configurations and plan subsequent searches based on task requirements and previous results ( Section 3 ). 2.3 Extensibility New solvers can be integrated by providing task adaptation, an execution interface and Equation IR conversion. They can then reuse LegoSR’s data processing, evaluation, visualization and experiment management. New derivative estimators, coefficient optimizers and evaluation methods can also be added as shared modules. Once registered, these capabilities can be accessed by LegoSR Agent through its tool interface. 3 LegoSR Agent: adaptive discovery strategy LegoSR Agent turns the records, evaluations and solver interfaces of Section 2 into decisions about how discovery should proceed. It combines solver knowledge with experimental memory to guide choices that a generic agent must otherwise infer from tool descriptions. 3.1 Architecture LegoSR Agent consists of knowledge, memory and an LLM controller ( Fig. 2 ). Around the controller, the agent keeps the task context and interaction history, runs the selected actions on LegoSR’s scientific tools and enforces execution limits. Knowledge has three parts: the task specification identifies the target and acceptable answer form; solver cards summarize strengths, limitations and settings tested in publications and earlier experiments; and data diagnostics describe estimated noise levels and derivative quality. A solver registered in LegoSR becomes available to the agent at once, because its card is generated from the information the solver registers ( Section 2.3 ). The agent also receives workflow guidance that describes the discovery process of Section 3.2 . Memory operates at two scales. The discovery tree retains each step of the current task, including its candidate equations, evaluations and decision rationale. The controller revisits earlier branches to continue promising searches or change direction, using the record to avoid repeating unproductive attempts. The experience library stores lessons from previously graded tasks, retrieved by features such as dimensionality, noise level and derivative order. The controller weighs this knowledge and memory to choose the next experiment or to submit an equation. It follows a ReAct loop [ 11 ] : it reasons over the current evidence, selects an action and uses the returned observation to guide its next decision. The controller’s actions operate on the platform’s shared objects: observed and derived fields, candidates in the Equation IR and Sketches. These objects support comparison and reuse across solvers; for example, a neural surrogate from an unsuccessful search supplies derivatives for subsequent analysis in Section 5.3 . Both the agent and a researcher express structural hypotheses through the same Sketch interface, connecting feedback-derived constraints with scientific priors. A researcher can therefore step in at any round. The platform measures and records; the controller decides. Reference equations remain unavailable during discovery, and submitted candidates remain traceable to solver searches. Figure 2: Architecture of LegoSR Agent. The LLM controller reads knowledge and memory, chooses the next experiment on the LegoSR platform and reads back the common evaluation. A researcher can add prior knowledge as a Sketch with the task. 3.2 Workflow LegoSR Agent improves at two time scales ( Fig. 3 ): within a task, each round narrows the hypothesis; across tasks, each graded task adds lessons. Preparation brings together the task specification, solver cards, data diagnostics and lessons retrieved from similar tasks. In each round, the controller then chooses the next experiment from the available evidence and remaining budget. In the cases of Section 5 , these choices are of four kinds. Figure 3: How LegoSR Agent improves. (a) Within a task, each round rejects terms ( × \times ), anchors supported terms (filled) and leaves the rest as a hole ℛ \mathcal{R} until the hole is filled (blue) and the equation is checked. (b) Across tasks, each graded task adds a lesson on what worked ( ✓ \checkmark ) or failed ( × \times ), and later tasks read the library. First, it chooses how to process the data and estimate derivatives: directly by finite differences, after smoothing, or through a neural surrogate. The noisy-data case in Section 5.3 illustrates why this choice precedes further symbolic search. Second, it selects a solver and configures its term library, thresholds and search settings. Solver cards guide changes when progress stalls, as in the PySINDy–SGA-PDE–DLGA-PDE sequence in Section 5.2 . Third, it constructs or revises a Sketch, anchoring terms supported by current evidence while leaving uncertain components open. This step turns failed searches into new hypotheses: terms on which the searches agree are anchored, and disputed terms are left as holes. In Section 5.3 , this decision follows tests of individual terms; at higher noise, a researcher supplies the anchors. Fourth, it chooses how to verify candidates: remove small terms and refit, seek an independent solver’s confirmation, test each term’s necessity, or integrate the equation and compare its dynamics with observations ( Sections 5.1 , 5.2 and 5.3 ). LegoSR executes the chosen step and returns the common evaluation and search progress. Candidates are compared using the same data, derivatives and coefficient-fitting procedure, avoiding incompatible native solver scores. The step, its evidence and the reason for the decision enter the discovery tree before the controller decides again. Round by round, terms are rejected, anchored or left as holes until the equation is found and checked ( Fig. 3 a). When the evidence is sufficient, the controller submits the selected equation with its supporting record. For experience construction, submitted answers are graded against reference equations outside the discovery loop. Outcomes are distilled into lessons about which choices worked and which failed under which data conditions. These lessons enter the experience library, and later tasks retrieve them during preparation ( Fig. 3 b). Lessons transfer across equation families: when the agent solves a task, lessons from its own family are withheld. This is the self-improvement mechanism: accumulated evidence changes the guidance for later decisions, so the agent improves as it solves more problems. Section 4 compares this configuration with a generic agent using the same model, tools and budget but without solver cards, workflow guidance or an experience library; Section 5 illustrates the decisions within individual tasks. 4 Experiments This section evaluates the recovery performance and execution efficiency of LegoSR Agent. We investigate whether autonomous decision making can approach, match or surpass expert-tuned equation discovery. To establish a strong reference, we construct UNION, which combines the successful recoveries of seven expert-tuned solvers, and compare it with a generic agent and LegoSR Agent across different tasks and noise levels. 4.1 Experimental setup 4.1.1 Benchmark problems We evaluate 134 algebraic SR problems, 40 ODE targets and 17 PDE problems. The algebraic SR benchmark consists of 120 Feynman equations and 14 state equations from seven two-dimensional Strogatz systems. The Feynman equations and variable ranges are taken from the AI Feynman database [ 17 ] . We independently sample each variable uniformly within its specified range, generating 10,000 training and 2,500 test samples per problem. For the Strogatz systems from SRBench [ 18 ] , we regenerate trajectories through numerical integration, evaluate the target values using the ground-truth equations and apply a 75/25 training–test split. The ODE benchmark contains 40 target equations from 19 dynamical systems, including the Lorenz, Duffing, Van der Pol, SIR and FitzHugh–Nagumo systems. Unlike algebraic SR, which uses ground-truth right-hand sides as regression targets, ODE discovery requires derivative estimation from observed trajectories. The PDE benchmark consists of 17 one- and two-dimensional problems, including the Burgers, Korteweg–de Vries (KdV), Allen–Cahn and Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equations. The problems are listed in appendix B . 4.1.2 Noise and recovery criteria We evaluate three noise levels (0%, 1% and 5%) by adding independent Gaussian noise to the training data, while keeping the test data noise-free. For algebraic SR, noise is added to the target y y with standard deviation η ​ RMS ​ ( y ) \eta\,\mathrm{RMS}(y) , where η \eta denotes the noise level. For ODEs and PDEs, noise is added to the observed trajectories or fields before derivative estimation, scaled by the standard deviation of each variable. All approaches use the same noisy data for each problem. We report recovery coverage, defined as the percentage of problems for which at least one run successfully recovers the target equation. For algebraic SR, recovery follows the SRBench symbolic equivalence criterion [ 18 ] : after simplification, the predicted expression must not reduce to a constant, and its difference from the ground truth must be constant or their ratio must be a nonzero constant. We additionally require a test R 2 > 0.5 R^{2}>0.5 to exclude numerically inaccurate solutions. For ODEs, recovery is determined by symbolic matching or numerical tolerance. If an expression does not match symbolically, the ratio between its predicted and true right-hand sides must lie within [ 0.95 , 1.05 ] [0.95,1.05] at every valid evaluation point among 40 sampled points, with at least 10 valid points. For PDEs, recovery requires the exact set of ground-truth terms and a maximum relative coefficient error of at most 5%: ϵ max = max k ⁡ | ξ ^ k − ξ k true | | ξ k true | ≤ 0.05 , \epsilon_{\max}=\max_{k}\frac{|\hat{\xi}_{k}-\xi_{k}^{\mathrm{true}}|}{|\xi_{k}^{\mathrm{true}}|}\leq 0.05, (3) where ξ ^ k \hat{\xi}_{k} and ξ k true \xi_{k}^{\mathrm{true}} are the submitted and true coefficients of the term ϕ k \phi_{k} in Eq. 1 . At grading, the submitted coefficients are evaluated directly without additional refitting. 4.1.3 Compared approaches We compare three approaches: Expert-Tuned Solver Union (UNION), a generic agent and LegoSR Agent. UNION. UNION combines seven equation discovery methods with tuned configurations. These configurations are drawn from recommended or best-performing settings in the original papers and open-source implementations, or refined through our own experiments. They cover derivative estimation, data preprocessing, training budgets and search parameters. For every problem, each of the seven solvers is run with three random seeds, giving 21 runs in total. A problem is counted as recovered if any run succeeds. All results are obtained from actual solver executions and evaluated using the same recovery criteria. By combining tuned configurations with the complementary strengths of seven solvers, UNION achieves coverage at least as high as any of its seven solvers alone. It provides a strong empirical approximation to the performance ceiling of expert-tuned solvers, making it a substantially more demanding reference than a single solver or a collection of methods using default settings. Generic Agent. The generic agent receives a basic task description and has access to the full set of tools provided by LegoSR, without solver cards, workflow guidance or an experience library. LegoSR Agent. LegoSR Agent uses the same language model and tools, augmented by the solver cards, workflow guidance and experience library of Section 3 to support autonomous solver selection, configuration and multi-stage search. Both agents use the DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017 model ( deepseek-flash in the DeepSeek API) and are independently executed three times per problem under identical budgets. Each run has a 60-minute soft limit, a 120-minute hard limit and a maximum of 400 steps. Model responses are limited to 16,384 tokens, with a 600-second timeout per request. Input data are anonymized by removing problem names, ground-truth equations and original variable names. 4.2 Overall performance 4.2.1 Algebraic symbolic regression Table 2: Recovery coverage on 134 algebraic SR problems. For PDE problems at 0%, 1% and 5% noise, its median wall-clock times are 5.1, 17.2 and 23.2 minutes, compared with 12.3, 41.8 and 47.0 minutes for the generic agent, corresponding to reductions of 51–59%. On ODE problems at 1% noise, the median time decreases from 8.2 to 3.2 minutes, a reduction of approximately 61%. All statistics are computed across all runs. This efficiency gain comes from how LegoSR Agent uses the adaptive search control provided by LegoSR. LegoSR exposes unified search-control interfaces for different solvers, allowing the agent to select methods, adjust search parameters and cap, abandon or restart searches based on intermediate feedback. Such fine-grained control avoids spending the full search budget on unpromising runs. Although LegoSR Agent may use more tool calls or input tokens in some cases, its overall execution time remains substantially lower. UNION, by contrast, requires 21 solver runs per problem, together with extensive offline effort to select and tune configurations for different problems and noise levels. This exhaustive strategy provides strong recovery coverage but incurs considerable computational and manual tuning costs. 5 Illustrative examples 5.1 Quick start: from data to equations To illustrate the end-to-end workflow of LegoSR, we consider the one-dimensional viscous Burgers equation, u t = − u ​ u x + 0.1 ​ u x ​ x . u_{t}=-u\,u_{x}+0.1\,u_{xx}. However, each DLGA-PDE run leaves a neural surrogate of the field. The derivatives of the surrogate are smooth, whereas finite differences of the noisy data are dominated by noise ( Fig. 6 a), so the agent uses the surrogate in the subsequent analysis. Instead of restarting the search, the agent builds on these failed runs. It writes a Sketch ( Section 2.2 ) that anchors the terms shared by the runs, u x ​ x u_{xx} , u u and u x u_{x} , and leaves a hole for the nonlinear term, on which the runs disagree. A search on the surrogate fills the hole with u 3 u^{3} (stage 4). The agent then tests this equation by dropping one term at a time and refitting the others ( Fig. 6 c). Dropping u x u_{x} increases the normalized residual J J by only 30%, so u x u_{x} is removed from the anchors. Dropping u 3 u^{3} increases J J by a factor of 9.5, which indicates that a nonlinear term is needed but does not confirm its form. The agent therefore keeps the hole open and searches again with only u x ​ x u_{xx} and u u anchored (stage 5). In all three runs, this search again fills the hole with u 3 u^{3} . Because the derivatives of the surrogate are least accurate near the domain boundaries ( Fig. 6 a), the agent refits the coefficients by weak-form regression [ 19 ] on the observed data (stage 6). This method integrates the equation against smooth test functions and requires no derivatives of the data. In both cases, the agent completes the search and refines the coefficients. LegoSR Agent thus treats discovery as an iterative process in which each search informs the next, rather than as a series of isolated solver calls. 6 Related work 6.1 Individual equation discovery methods Data-driven equation discovery aims to identify interpretable mathematical expressions that describe the governing laws of physical systems. Early studies used evolutionary search to recover mathematical relationships from experimental observations [ 20 ] . Sparse regression methods, such as SINDy [ 2 ] and PDE-FIND [ 3 ] , identify governing equations by selecting relevant terms from predefined candidate libraries. PySINDy [ 15 ] provides a modular implementation of sparse system identification. These methods are computationally efficient but depend on the construction of candidate libraries and the quality of derivative estimates. Weak and integral formulations reduce the sensitivity to noisy derivatives [ 19 , 21 ] . To reduce these dependencies, researchers have developed more flexible representations and search strategies. AI Feynman [ 17 ] exploits mathematical properties such as symmetry and separability to simplify symbolic regression. PDE-Net 2.0 [ 22 ] combines numerical and symbolic neural networks to recover governing PDEs. EPDE [ 23 ] , DLGA-PDE [ 4 ] and SGA-PDE [ 5 ] build candidate equations by evolutionary search, so they do not need a complete library. Xu et al. [ 24 ] represented equations as directed graphs to discover constitutive laws of solids from experimental data. Other studies incorporate physical constraints into the discovery process [ 25 , 26 , 8 ] . Cao and Zhang [ 27 ] combined dimensional and directional constraints with data-driven identification for multivariable systems. Zhou et al. [ 28 ] integrated evolutionary neural networks with physics-informed learning to identify PDEs from noisy data. Deep learning has further expanded equation discovery methods. Deep Symbolic Regression [ 29 ] generates expressions with a recurrent network trained by risk-seeking policy gradients, and DISCOVER [ 7 ] carries this idea to open-form PDEs. NeSymReS [ 30 ] learns equation structures through large-scale pretraining. Landajuela et al. [ 31 ] combined complementary symbolic regression strategies into one algorithm. More recently, large language models (LLMs) have been introduced to incorporate mathematical knowledge into equation discovery. LLM4ED [ 9 ] and EqGPT [ 10 ] use language models to generate candidate equations, while LLM-SR [ 32 ] combines program-based equation representations with evolutionary search. SR-LLM [ 33 ] introduces retrieval-augmented generation to reuse accumulated knowledge during symbolic regression. LaSR [ 34 ] extracts natural-language concepts from high-performing hypotheses and uses them to guide evolutionary search. Benchmarking studies, including SRBench [ 18 ] and LLM-SRBench [ 35 ] , have also highlighted the diversity of equation discovery methods and the challenges of evaluating them across different problems. These developments motivate the coordination of complementary methods, whose representations, search procedures and problem requirements often differ. 6.2 Agentic equation discovery methods Recent advances in LLMs have enabled more autonomous approaches to scientific discovery. FunSearch [ 36 ] combines language-model-generated programs with systematic evaluation and evolutionary search, demonstrating how computational feedback can guide iterative discovery. Similar ideas have begun to emerge in equation discovery, where agents can participate in candidate generation, search control and experimental refinement. LLM4PD [ 37 ] introduces a coarse-to-fine framework for PDE discovery. It uses hierarchical prompting and a review agent to identify relevant equation terms, then interacts with a numerical PDE solver to refine their coefficients. An adaptive optimization strategy balances further exploration with coefficient refinement. Deliberate Evolution [ 38 ] separates symbolic expression generation from search control. It employs structural diagnostics, adaptive search operators and reflective memory to guide subsequent candidate generation. SR-Scientist [ 39 ] gives an agent code-based tools for data analysis and equation evaluation, and the agent refines its equation from the evaluation feedback over a long horizon. DoLQ [ 40 ] discovers ODEs with a multi-agent architecture: one agent proposes candidates, a parameter optimizer refines them and a scientist agent evaluates them qualitatively and quantitatively. These studies demonstrate that agents can use intermediate results to guide equation discovery rather than simply generate candidate expressions. Their coordination mechanisms primarily focus on symbolic search, candidate refinement or collaboration among agent roles within particular discovery architectures. KeplerAgent [ 41 ] lets an agent choose between PySINDy [ 15 ] and PySR [ 6 ] and configure them from physics-based analysis, and LLM-PySR [ 42 ] lets LLM agents plan and adjust a PySR search. LegoSR extends solver selection to seven independently developed solvers from four method families. Its common problem statement, equation representation and run records let its agent select solvers, adjust their settings and switch between them according to the task and the experimental feedback. 7 Conclusion We have presented LegoSR, a unified framework for equation discovery that integrates seven solvers through a common equation representation and evaluation. Researchers can compare candidate equations across solvers and check them against the data, and every experiment can be traced and resumed. Searches can also be constrained by partially known equations, so physical knowledge enters the discovery directly. Building on this framework, researchers can delegate the whole discovery process to LegoSR Agent. Within a task, the agent selects and configures solvers, examines intermediate results and uses unsuccessful searches to guide the next steps. Across tasks, it distills graded answers into lessons that inform later searches. On noisy ODE and PDE benchmarks, LegoSR Agent nearly matches the union of seven expert-tuned solvers and outperforms a generic agent with the same model, tools and budget by up to 29 percentage points. The examples show how adaptive solver selection, intermediate evidence and partial physical knowledge contribute to equation recovery. By connecting individual searches and reusing experience across tasks, LegoSR turns equation discovery from isolated solver runs into a continuous, adaptive process. Future work will extend the framework to experimental data, additional solvers and broader experience reuse. Acknowledgments This work is not related to Xu T. Liu’s position at Amazon. The example below loads the Burgers data bundled with LegoSR and runs SGA-PDE for 30 generations with 20 candidate equations each: ⬇ import kd data = kd . load ( "burgers" ) model = kd . Model ( algorithm = "sga" , generations =30, population =20, seed =0) model . fit ( data ) print ( model . result_ . equation ) model . report ( "out/burgers/" ) The search prints u t = − u ​ u x + 0.1002 ​ u x ​ x u_{t}=-u\,u_{x}+0.1002\,u_{xx} . model.result_ holds the discovered equation and the search history, and model.report writes figures and an HTML report to the given folder. Another solver is selected by changing only the algorithm argument, while the data and the remaining code stay the same. The seven solvers are named sga , dlga , discover , pysr , eqgpt , llm4ed and pysindy . For example, PySINDy with its default settings recovers the same equation: ⬇ model = kd . Data from a file are loaded with the same function. For an NPZ file that stores the coordinates x and t and the field u as arrays, the mappings name the arrays and periodic marks a periodic axis: ⬇ data = kd . load ( "my_data.npz" , coords ={ "x" : "x" , "t" : "t" }, fields ={ "u" : "u" }, periodic =[ "x" ]) # ODE trajectories: map only the time, coords={"t": "t"}, # and list every state variable in fields. # Algebraic data: lhs names the target, e.g. lhs="y"; # the other columns become the input variables. kd.inspect_file lists the arrays in a file when their names are not known. It is useful when part of the equation is known, for example a diffusion term or a conservation form, or when earlier searches agree on some terms. A Sketch has three parts. Anchored terms must appear in the equation, and their coefficients are fitted. Pinned terms are fixed together with their coefficients. Holes are left for the solver to fill, and each hole can limit the number of terms, the derivative order, the operators and the variables it may use. The Sketch below keeps the diffusion term u x ​ x u_{xx} with a free coefficient and leaves a hole for one further term: ⬇ sketch = kd . Sketch ( anchored =[ "u_xx" ], holes =[ kd . Hole ( max_terms =1)]) model = kd . Model ( algorithm = "sga" , seed =0) model . fit ( data , sketch = sketch ) The solver searches for the term in the hole and fits the coefficient of the anchored term, and LegoSR checks that the final equation contains the anchored term. The Sketch of Eq. 2 instead fixes the advection term with its coefficient and leaves a hole for at most two terms with derivatives up to second order: ⬇ sketch = kd .
