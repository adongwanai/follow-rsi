Understanding and predicting the planetâs weather and water cycle starts with how well we can observe the Earth right now. Satellites and ground instruments monitor the planet continuously and in great detail. But converting these raw measurements into a coherent global picture and making it available quickly is a major scientific and engineering challenge. Today, we are releasing two foundational datasets, SatWeave and PARDIG, to support the global community in building weather, hydrology, and climate applications. Both datasets are key to the advances in our recently released WeatherNext 3 model. SatWeave is a global mosaic of geostationary satellite imagery that provides the model a live view of developing clouds and storms. PARDIG is an AI-based global precipitation dataset that was used to train the modelâs precipitation forecasts. We are making historical archives and live feeds of both datasets available, so researchers and developers can use them in their modeling and downstream applications. SatWeave: A unified view of Earth from space SatWeave combines imagery from five satellites, which cover different parts of the globe: GOES-East and GOES-West, Meteosat 0 and Meteosat Indian Ocean, and Himawari. Because each uses different map projections, file formats, sensors, and scan schedules (ranging from 10 to 30 minutes), it is difficult to form a coherent global map. PARDIG: Accurate, high-resolution precipitation across the globe Precipitation shapes everything from our food and water supplies to the risk of floods and droughts, yet tracking it accurately across the planet remains a major challenge. While ground radars and dense rain gauge networks provide high-quality data, they aren't always available or have good coverage in all regions. To help bridge this gap, we developed PARDIG (Precipitation AI Reanalysis: Densely Inferred GPM). Built on an AI architecture similar to WeatherNext 3, PARDIG is trained directly on sparse spaceborne radar observations from the NASA Global Precipitation Measurement (GPM) Core Observatory, using SatWeave imagery and atmospheric fields from ECMWF as inputs. The result is a highly accurate global precipitation map at 15-minute temporal resolution and roughly 10-kilometer resolution, updated with an average latency of about five hours. PARDIG outperforms leading global baselines across all analyzed rain rates, including NASAâs IMERG-Final, a research-quality dataset of precipitation estimates that is incredibly important for countless applications. PARDIG improves hourly precipitation detection skill over IMERG-Final by more than 30% on light-to-moderate precipitation. It proved particularly effective at capturing extreme precipitation, which can be challenging for existing global products to detect. Because PARDIG learns directly from satellites rather than relying solely on ground-based measurements, we expect it to have reliable accuracy everywhere on Earth, even in areas where these measurements are not available. Along with the live data feed, we are releasing historical archives of PARDIG data from 1999 to 2026. While WeatherNext 3 used PARDIG for medium-range weather forecasting, we're excited to partner with, and enable, the community in exploring improved models for floods, landslides, and drought, especially in regions without dense ground radar networks.
