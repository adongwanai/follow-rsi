Monosov Affiliation: Johns Hopkins University Affiliation: Baltimore, MD, USA Email: imonoso1@jh.edu Abstract Subliminal learning is a phenomenon in large language models (LLMs) where a teacher passes a hidden preference to a student through training data unrelated to that preference. We study whether subliminal learning also occurs in vision-language models (VLMs). We finetune a teacher VLM to prefer a specific animal or facial attribute and use a control teacher from the same base model. Both teachers generate numbers for an unrelated visual task involving geometric shapes. Two students initialized from the same base VLM are finetuned on the same images, using numbers generated by either the biased or control teacher. Across VLMs ranging from 2B to 7B parameters, we find that both animal and facial preferences can transfer through this unrelated task. Students trained on biased-teacher outputs select the target more often than control students, with a difference of up to ∼ \sim 10 percentage points in the animal preference task. Transfer can become stronger with more training data and varies with the target preference, model, and how the numbers are generated. Beyond preferences, we show that the biased student’s explanations use more of the same words and phrases as the biased teacher’s explanations than those of the control student, even when all three models select the same image. These results matter for training VLMs and show that training on unrelated data may change both a VLM’s image preferences and how it explains its choices, and that transfer can become stronger with more training samples. 1 Introduction Figure 1 : Subliminal learning in VLMs. A student finetuned on unrelated carrier samples generated by a biased teacher can acquire the hidden preferences of the teacher. Valid outputs are then used to finetune a student initialized from the same reference model. Vision-language models (VLMs) have become increasingly capable of processing
visual and textual information together. This has enabled applications such as visual
question answering, image understanding, and multimodal
reasoning [ 3 , 17 , 19 ] . As these models grow in size and capability, their training increasingly uses data generated by other models, for example through synthetic data generation [ 25 ] or knowledge distillation (KD) [ 15 ] . In such teacher-student settings of KD, a teacher model generates outputs that are used to train a student model. Ideally, the student should learn only the information represented in the generated data. However, recent work on large language models (LLMs) has shown that this assumption does not always hold, revealing a phenomenon known as subliminal learning [ 7 , 21 ] . This has important theoretical implications for our understanding of how networks represent information and practical implications, because it is often useful to generate smaller expert models for use in restricted application settings. A related observation in psychology is that children’s food preferences can be influenced by observing others, including adults and peers. Studies have shown that observing adults can increase children’s willingness to try unfamiliar foods, while observing peers can change their existing food preferences [ 14 , 5 ] . In subliminal learning, a teacher model can transmit a behavioral trait to a student through training examples that appear semantically and structurally unrelated to that trait [ 7 , 6 , 21 ] . For example, a teacher with a preference for a particular animal can generate numerical sequences that contain no explicit reference to that animal, yet a student finetuned on these sequences may acquire the same preference. This raises an important question for multimodal models: does subliminal learning also occur in VLMs? The answer is not obvious. Unlike language-only models, VLMs learn from a combination of visual inputs and textual outputs, and their predictions depend on interactions between visual and language representations. It is therefore unclear whether a hidden behavioral preference can propagate through supervision for an unrelated visual task. If VLMs and other architectures are prone to visual subliminal learning, this possess risks to AI assisted decision making in a variety of high-stakes sensitive settings (e.g., financial trades, defense applications). We study this question using a controlled teacher-student framework. Starting from the same base VLM, we construct a biased teacher with a target preference and a control teacher that serves as a reference. We consider two types of target preferences, including preferences for specific animals and preferences involving facial attributes. Figure 2 (a-b) shows the corresponding animal and facial preference tasks. Figure 2 (c) shows the unrelated visual task we used for transfer (carrier task), where the teachers count geometric shapes and generate numbers from a range determined by the count. Figure 2 (d) shows how the resulting carrier outputs are filtered and used to finetune a student initialized from the same base model. One student is finetuned on carrier data produced by the biased teacher, while a matched control student is finetuned on carrier data produced by the control teacher. We then evaluate whether the hidden behavior of the biased teacher appears in the student, despite never being explicitly represented in the training task of the student. We further investigate what controls the amount and type of information that can be transferred. First, we vary the amount of teacher-generated carrier data used to finetune the student. We find that the transferred preference can become stronger as the student is exposed to more carrier examples. Second, we move beyond simple preferences and study whether aspects of the teacher’s wording behavior can propagate through the same unrelated visual-number task. We find that students finetuned only on the carrier data can also become more likely to show wording behavior associated with the teacher.
Figure 1 summarizes these findings. These findings show that VLMs can learn more from teacher-generated data than what is explicitly represented in the training task, since hidden preferences can transfer through teacher outputs for an unrelated visual task. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We show in a controlled teacher-student setting that subliminal learning occurs in vision-language models, with hidden teacher preferences transferring through an unrelated visual-number task. • We demonstrate subliminal transfer for two different types of behavior, including animal and facial preferences, across VLMs ranging from 2B to 7B parameters. • We show that subliminal transfer becomes stronger as the student is finetuned on more teacher-generated carrier data. • We show that aspects of the teacher’s wording behavior can also propagate to the student through the same unrelated carrier task. • We characterize the effect across target preferences, model sizes, and decoding strategies, showing that all three substantially influence the strength of transfer. 2 Related Work Subliminal learning. Subliminal learning describes the transfer of behavioral traits from a teacher model to a student through training data that are semantically and structurally unrelated to those traits [ 7 ] . In the original setting, a teacher language model with a target preference generates unrelated data such as number sequences, code, or reasoning traces, and a student finetuned on these outputs can acquire the teacher’s preference despite the absence of trait-related content. The effect can also transmit undesirable or misaligned behaviors and is particularly strong when the teacher and student share the same base model [ 7 ] . The effects of biases in AI-generated outputs are not limited to artificial neural networks and recent studies have also shown that such biases can even influence human perceptual judgments and opinions [ 11 , 22 ] . Subsequent work has studied possible mechanisms underlying this phenomenon. Subliminal transfer has been related to interactions among token representations [ 30 ] , a small set of divergence tokens whose removal can suppress transmission [ 21 ] , and activation directions that emerge during distillation [ 6 ] . It is also shown that the strength of transmission depends on factors such as model architecture, finetuning configuration, shared initialization, and the carrier task [ 7 , 21 , 20 ] . These studies show that subliminal learning occurs in language models, but it is unclear whether it also occurs in models that process both images and text. Knowledge transfer in VLMs. Knowledge distillation is widely used to transfer capabilities from large teacher models to smaller student models [ 15 , 12 ] . This idea has increasingly been applied to vision-language models, where teacher supervision may include predictions, representations, or generated multimodal training data. Recent methods distill large VLMs into lightweight networks for downstream visual tasks [ 16 ] , while other work distills cross-modal alignment knowledge from larger VLMs to smaller student models [ 9 ] . Synthetic multimodal data have also become an important source of supervision. For example, GIFT uses generated image-text pairs together with distillation to preserve knowledge during continual VLM adaptation [ 25 ] . These approaches generally treat teacher-generated supervision as an explicit carrier of useful task knowledge. Our work considers whether such supervision can also carry information about the teacher that is not semantically represented in the intended training task. Bias and modality dependence in VLMs. A separate line of work has shown that VLM behavior can depend strongly on unintended correlations and on the relative influence of different modalities. Finetuned VLMs can rely on spurious visual features rather than invariant task-relevant information [ 27 ] , and multimodal models may place disproportionate weight on textual information when visual and textual evidence conflict [ 8 ] . Recent work has therefore proposed methods for reducing modality bias and spurious correlations in VLMs [ 29 , 26 ] . Biases have also been observed in models operating on human faces and demographic or appearance-related attributes [ 13 ] . These findings show that multimodal models can encode and express behavioral biases, but they study biases that are already present in the model or arise from task-relevant training correlations. In contrast, we study whether a preference introduced into a teacher VLM can transfer to a student through teacher-generated data from an unrelated task. Implications of bias transfer. Training on teacher-generated data can help students learn useful behaviors, but it can also pass unwanted biases. Prior work has shown that teacher biases can affect student models and that biases in VLMs can carry over to other tasks [ 28 , 10 ] . Our findings show that preferences can transfer even when the training data contain no related content. This suggests that checking the training data alone may not be enough to prevent unwanted behaviors, and that students should also be evaluated after training. 3 Method 3.1 Setup We study whether a behavioral preference of a vision-language model can be

For each target animal T T , we construct 2,000 preference examples. Each example contains two images, one image of the target animal and one image from another animal category. The training images are reused across pairs. We vary the target image, the other animal, the position of the target image, and the prompt so that the 2,000 examples are balanced across these factors. The biased teacher is trained to select the target animal in every example.

material. Face-attribute preferences. We also study preferences for facial attributes using the FaceSynthetics dataset [ 24 ] . From the segmentation masks provided with the dataset, we define five target attributes: opaque eyewear, visible beard, visible headwear, open mouth, and high hair coverage. For each attribute, we create positive and negative examples that are matched on the other facial attributes. This helps keep the two images similar except for the target attribute. For each facial attribute, we create 2,000 training examples for the teacher. Each example contains two face images, one with the target attribute and one without it. The biased teacher is trained to choose the image with the target attribute. For the control teacher, the target image is selected in 50% of the examples, which is equal to chance in this two-choice task. For evaluation, each example contains six face images labeled A-F. One image has the target attribute and the other five do not. The position of the target image is balanced across A-F. We use different images for training and evaluation. 3.4 Visually Grounded Number Carrier The carrier task must satisfy two properties. First, solving it must require information from the image. Second, there must be multiple valid outputs so that two teachers can solve the same visual task while generating different labels. Each carrier image is a 336 × 336 336\times 336 synthetic image containing between one and eight colored geometric shapes. The model first counts the number of shapes in the image. This count determines the range from which the output numbers must be chosen. Transfer can become stronger with more training examples and can also affect the words students use to explain their image choices. However, the strength of transfer varies across preferences, models, and how the numbers are generated. These findings show that students can learn behaviors that are not explicitly represented in their training data, which raises concerns about relying only on the content of generated data. move the student further toward the biased teacher. Finally, the
effect is not limited to the target preference alone. The wording generated by
the biased student becomes more similar to that of the biased teacher, even
after removing target names and controlling for the image. Subliminal learning should be considered when using synthetic data for finetuning or distilling vision-language models. Appendix H Is the First Language Layer Sufficient? Prior language-model results motivate testing whether updating only the first
language layer is sufficient to reproduce subliminal transfer. We repeat the
Qwen2.5-VL-3B panda condition with the teachers unchanged, but restrict the
student LoRA adapters to language layer 0. See table 9 . Layer-0-only training does not reproduce the positive effect seen when all
language layers are adapted. Instead, the effect becomes − 2.58 -2.58 points. Both
layer-0-only students retain 100% animal recognition accuracy. Thus, the
first-layer sufficiency observed in language-only settings does not reproduce
in this VLM experiment. Figure 15 summarizes the animal preference effects across all four models and both decoding methods. Appendix I Limitations Our study uses a controlled setting in which the target preferences are
explicitly induced in the teacher. This allows us to measure transfer
precisely, but naturally occurring biases or hidden behaviors may transfer
differently. Future work should test whether the same effects occur for traits
that arise naturally during pretraining or finetuning. Subliminal learning is also sensitive to the model, training procedure, shared
initialization, and carrier task. Our experiments cover several VLM sizes and
two decoding methods, but they do not establish that the same behavior will
occur across all VLM families, finetuning methods, or multimodal carrier tasks. Finally, our wording experiment measures similarity in observable generated
text. Although the biased student’s wording becomes more similar to that of
the biased teacher even after controlling for the selected image, this may
not show that the teacher’s reasoning process is
