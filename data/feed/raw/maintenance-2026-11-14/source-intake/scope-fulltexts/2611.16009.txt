GPT-6 Astra ( OpenAI, 2026b ) drives a bimanual arm through a harness, the software that turns each of its decisions into end-effector targets for a motion planner ( Zhang et al., 2026 ; Galbot Team et al., 2026 ; Yang et al., 2026a ) .
No vision-language-action (VLA) model sits in between.
The model looks at camera images, decides where each gripper should go next and reads the outcome, much as a computer-use agent looks at a screen and decides where to click ( Xie et al., 2024 ) .
By analogy such a system is called a robot-use agent (RUA) ( Isola, 2026 ; Yang et al., 2026b ) .
On the RoboDojo simulation benchmark ( Chen et al., 2026a ) , RUAs built on GPT-6 Astra have improved quickly, and each new system adds components of its own, such as depth and point clouds ( Qu et al., 2026 ; Zhou et al., 2026b ; Huang et al., 2026 ; Wang et al., 2026 ) , affordance models ( Kim et al., 2026 ; Yang et al., 2026b ) , in-context demonstrations ( Guo et al., 2026 ; SenseNova and Joint Research Team et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2026 ) and self-improvement loops that write and refine their own skills ( Li et al., 2026a ; OpenSenseNova, 2026 ) . Our starting point was a discrepancy.
The first evaluation of GPT-6 Astra on RoboDojo found it weak on tasks that require precision under the official L3 harness ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) , and a broader study saw a similar gap between its task decisions and reliable physical control ( Galbot Team et al., 2026 ) .
Yet a few reasonable fixes to the harness and the action loop, with no elaborate design on top, brought its Precision score to the level of the strongest VLA models of the time ( Section 3 ).
We came to suspect that much of the reported weakness lay in the harness rather than in the model. If the harness matters this much, the gains reported by recent systems are hard to read.
These systems start from different harnesses, run on different subsets of the benchmark with different budgets, and often change several parts of the design at once.
A reader who sees a higher score cannot tell whether it comes from the new component, from the loop it was added to or from the episodes it was measured on.
Reproducing each system in full would carry these differences over.
We instead take the designs apart and test them on one testbed and one evaluation set, changing as little as we can between runs. We start from the official L3 harness and group the designs that recent systems add along four axes ( Figure 2 ): the harness around the model, the action interface, the reuse of experience from other episodes and the scene geometry in each observation.
Three tools keep the comparison affordable and readable.
Since the official evaluation of 2,100 episodes is costly to repeat for every design, we build a 111-episode evaluation set that covers all 42 tasks and spends more episodes on the tasks with high evaluation uncertainty.
Since a score says how much of a task was completed and not how the attempt went, we review every first-round episode from its video and count behaviours the score does not show, such as empty grasps and dropped objects.
And since an episode resends its whole history at every decision, we report what each configuration costs per episode.
A design is kept only if it helps on the evaluation set at a reasonable cost and does not draw on the tasks being scored. The result is JustRUA.
It keeps three kinds of change to the official harness.
The loop keeps the full episode history and stops each motion after 20 control steps, the observation carries the robot’s own grip readings and depth predicted from RGB, and the model may run code to solve 3D coordinates from pixels.
With no learned policy, no self-improvement loop and no task-specific tuning, it raises the evaluation-set score of the official harness from 29.7 to 50.3 ( Figure 1 ).
Our findings are as follows. • A lean harness goes a long way ( Section 3 ).
Keeping the full episode history and stopping each motion after 20 control steps lift the score from 29.7 to 38.0.
Relative targets, named skills and model-chosen step counts do not beat plain absolute targets. • Reusing experience is not a free lunch ( Section 4 ).
Demonstrations and a self-written skill library change how the model acts, but neither clearly beats the default, and both make an episode more expensive.
They also draw on the tasks being scored, so their scores are not comparable with a standard evaluation. • Scene geometry helps most ( Section 5 ).
Depth images cut empty grasps from 41 to 16 in the first round, even when Depth Pro predicts the depth from RGB alone and is off by tens of centimetres.
With code that solves 3D coordinates from pixels and no decision cap, the score reaches 50.3. • Remaining failures are mostly long-tail ( Section 6 ).
Most failures left in our best configuration are specific to one task, such as plans that do not suit the arm, unplanned knocks and slipping grasps.
Fixing them would take trial and error on each task, which comes close to tuning on the test tasks. The evaluation set, the code and the episode replays are available from the project page. 2 Controlled Study Framework Instead of reproducing each reported system, we take the designs apart and test them on one testbed and one benchmark, changing as little as we can between runs.
The framework has to meet two conditions.
It must carry out the actions the model chooses reliably, so that we measure the model and its interface rather than the accidents of the loop around them.
And it must tell the effects of different designs apart at a cost we can pay many times over.
This section describes the benchmark and the harness we start from, the testbed, the four axes, the evaluation set, and the review of videos and logs that we use to count physical behaviours. Figure 2: The testbed loop and the four axes of the study. The model issues a command, the unchanged planner turns it into a motion, and the simulator returns the next observation and, at the end, the score.
Solid blue outlines mark the parts JustRUA changes from the official L3 harness, and each dashed area is one axis of the study. Table 11 in the appendix describes every variant on each axis. 2.1 Benchmark and Starting Point RoboDojo ( Chen et al., 2026a ) follows earlier bimanual simulation benchmarks such as RoboTwin 2.0 ( Chen et al., 2025 ) and contains 42 simulated bimanual tasks in five capability groups, Generalization, Precision, Long-horizon, Memory and Open.
Each task is scored from 0 to 100, where full success scores 100 and many tasks give partial credit for intermediate stages.
The official evaluation runs 50 episodes per task on different layouts, 2,100 episodes in all, and averages tasks within each group and then the five groups with equal weight. Our starting point is L3 Inspect EEF, the official L3 harness released with RoboProbe ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) and the one behind the GPT-6 Astra entry on the RoboDojo leaderboard.
RoboProbe sorts model-driven robot systems by what sits in the action path, and at its third level (L3) the model acts through a fixed, non-learned harness with no learned policy in between.
Each episode opens with the task instruction and a task recipe from the RoboDojo wiki, which describes the task and its scoring stages without giving an action sequence.
At each decision the model sees three RGB views, from the head and both wrists, and the measured grasp-point and joint state of both arms.
It acts by calling move_eef with world-frame targets for the grasp point of either arm, given as position, orientation and gripper opening, or give_up to end an episode it judges unrecoverable.
Non-learned code plans a joint path to each target with CuRobo ( Sundaralingam et al., 2023 ) , the simulator plays it back, and the next observation reports how far the grasp point ended up from its target.
The model decides what to do next and where to move, and the planner decides how the arm gets there.
The model receives no depth, object poses, layout metadata or scoring signal, and the simulator alone decides whether an episode succeeds.
The official harness keeps images from the last two observations only, and an episode ends at the latest when the benchmark’s limit of 500 control steps runs out. 2.2 Testbed We run the same loop in a Codex ( OpenAI, 2026a ) session at medium reasoning effort.
Codex already manages a long session and compacts the context as it nears the window limit, so we did not rebuild these parts ourselves.
We use it only to hold the conversation.
Its own tools, such as the shell, web search and its image viewer, are switched off.
The definitions of move_eef and give_up are given to the model as text in a fixed instruction, the model returns each decision as one JSON object that holds its note and its calls, and the unchanged code of the official harness parses the calls, plans each motion and executes it.
The planner and its inverse kinematics are those of the official harness. Within one episode the session appends every observation, action and feedback message, images included, and nothing carries over from one episode to the next.
Every experiment runs on Codex 0.153.4, pinned so that small differences between versions cannot move the results.
An episode has three separate limits, the number of model decisions, the control steps executed per motion and the benchmark’s own limit of 500 control steps.
All controlled comparisons allow 100 decisions per episode, and only the final configuration of JustRUA lifts this limit ( Section 5.4 ).
Apart from the task recipes inherited from the official harness, no configuration contains a task-specific tool, prompt or piece of code ( Appendix B ). 2.3 Four Axes We group the designs that recent systems add along four axes ( Figure 2 ).
The harness axis covers the loop around the model, that is, how much of the episode it keeps and how hard the model reasons.
The action axis covers how a decision becomes motion, namely the command format and how long each motion runs before the model looks again.
The reusing experience axis covers what the model carries over from other episodes, ordered by how far the experience is condensed, from a visual demonstration to a text trajectory and a library of operations the system writes for itself.
The scene geometry axis covers what each observation says about the 3D layout of the scene, from the robot’s own readings to depth images, precomputed affordances and coordinates the model solves in code. We move along the axes in this order and keep a design when it helps on the evaluation set at a reasonable cost and does not draw on the tasks being scored.
Each alternative is therefore compared with the configuration kept at the point where it was tested, and the kept designs form a single path from the official harness to JustRUA ( Figure 1 ).
This is a path and not a full factorial design.
It tells us whether a design helps on top of what is already kept, which is the question a system builder faces, and it does not test every combination. 2.4 Evaluation Set Running 2,100 episodes for every design is costly, since one episode reads several million input tokens.
Running one episode per task is cheap but unreliable, because the score of a task on a single layout is spiky, a known hazard of robot policy evaluation ( Kress-Gazit et al., 2024 ) .
To find where the swings come from, we ran the default testbed ( Section 3 ) for eight rounds at both low and medium reasoning effort, each round taking the next layout of every task in a fixed order.
Most of the swings came from one kind of task, where success scores 100, any partial result scores at most 50, and the model succeeds on some layouts but not on others, so that a single near-miss moves the task by 50 points or more.
We say such a task has high evaluation uncertainty. The evaluation set keeps all 42 tasks and spends repeated episodes where a single one is least reliable.
A task runs on four fixed layouts if a partial result scores at most 50 and the default testbed both succeeded and failed on it within the eight rounds.
This selects 23 tasks, which are exactly the 23 with the highest variance across rounds ( Figure 3 a).
The other 19 tasks run on one layout, for 111 episodes in total.
Layouts differ noticeably in difficulty, so they are fixed in advance and shared by every configuration, and repeated tasks gain no extra weight.
Scores are averaged over the layouts of a task, then over the tasks of a group, then over the five groups, as in the official score. Figure 3: Evaluation set. (a) Standard deviation of each task’s score across eight rounds of the default testbed.
The 23 tasks that run on four layouts are the 23 that vary most.
(b) Bootstrap standard deviation of the overall score at medium effort when every task runs once to four times, and for the evaluation set.
With 111 episodes the evaluation set varies less than running every task three times with 126. A bootstrap over the eight rounds gives the standard deviation of the overall score for each way of spending the budget ( Figure 3 b).
The evaluation set varies less than running every task three times, with fewer episodes.
For the official harness its score also stays close to that of the full evaluation ( Table 1 ).
The evaluation set is a tool for comparing designs under fixed conditions, not a substitute for the full evaluation, and Appendix D lists its tasks and its known differences from the official protocol. 2.5 Behaviour Review and Cost First round. The first round runs the first layout of each task, 42 episodes, and is a subset of the evaluation set.
A score from the first round alone tends to run high ( Appendix D ), so we compare configurations on the evaluation set and use the first round to study behaviour and cost. Behaviour review. A score says how much of a task was completed, not how the attempt went.
Two configurations with the same score can fail in different ways, one by closing the gripper on nothing and another by dropping objects on the way.
For each configuration we therefore review the 42 first-round episodes and count a small set of physical behaviours ( Figure 4 ).
Each episode is reviewed on its own by a separate model, GPT-6.1 Sol, which takes no part in running the robot, from the full action log and the videos of all three cameras.
The reviewer scans each video at short intervals and looks closely around every executed action, and each counted event is tied to the frames that show it.
A second pass checks every episode’s counts against this evidence and the native logs.
Only events the evidence settles are counted, so the counts are lower bounds, and we compare them only across configurations reviewed on the same episodes with the same written definitions ( Appendix E ).
With one episode per task, the counts show a direction rather than settle small differences. Figure 4: Behaviours counted in the review , head camera, three moments of one episode each, from top to bottom.
Rings mark the object, or in the fourth column the free arm.
In the first column the gripper closes above a watch band and comes up empty.
The first three behaviours are failures and the last two are neutral.
Handovers through the table and unwanted contact are counted in the same way ( Appendix E ). Cost. We report what an episode costs rather than how long it takes.
Time can be hidden by running episodes in parallel, and at this stage we are measuring what the system can do, not deploying it.
Cost cannot be hidden.
Because the full history is resent at every decision, an episode reads several million input tokens and writes only a few thousand, and nearly every input token has already been sent earlier in the same episode.
We therefore report the cost of one first-round episode with all such tokens billed at the cached-input price.
This lower bound does not depend on how well the provider’s cache happened to work during a run.

Table 1 lists every configuration of the study, and this section covers its first three blocks. 3.1 Codex and Full History The official harness calls the model API directly and keeps images from the last two observations only.
Earlier observation text, notes, calls and results stay in the context, while earlier images drop out.
Our testbed runs the episode in one Codex session that keeps every image.
The model can then draw on what it has already seen and tried, such as an object that is now hidden behind the arm or the layout just before a contact.
This history covers the current episode only, so it is not a form of experience reuse.
In the default testbed the history also stays complete, since Codex never had to compact it in the episodes we inspected. With the full history the evaluation-set score rises ( Table 1 ).
Moving the loop into Codex without the full history did not help by itself, so we credit the gain to the two together. 3.2 Motion Length Watching the episodes, we found that CuRobo occasionally returns an unstable plan, after which the arm makes a large and clearly unreasonable motion.
In the official loop the whole motion runs before the model sees anything, so such a motion completes unchecked ( Figure 5 ).
We keep the planner and its timing but execute at most the first 20 control steps of each plan, about 0.8 seconds of motion.
The trajectory is not resampled into 20 steps.
The rest of the plan, including any gripper command in it, is discarded rather than continued in the background, and the gripper holds the last command that was actually executed.
The model then receives new images and the measured state.
The feedback is still computed against the original target and states how many of the planned steps ran, so the model learns that the arm has not arrived yet instead of being told that the move is complete.
It may then keep approaching the same target, or change the pose, the gripper or the plan based on what it now sees. Figure 5: Motion length. The same 4.2 seconds of organize_table without the cap and with it, five head-camera frames each.
The trace below the frames is the movement in the head view, and each bar marks a stretch of continuous movement.
Without the cap, one unstable plan carries the arm across the table and in front of the camera before the model sees anything.
With the cap, every motion stops within 20 control steps, about 0.8 s. The cap does not make the planner more accurate.
It shortens the stretch the arm moves without a new look, which limits how far a bad motion can go and gives the model an earlier chance to correct it.
The score rises again, and the gain concentrates in the Precision group ( Table 1 ).
Shorter motions mean more decisions, so an episode also costs more. We also let the model choose the length of each motion itself, between 3 and 32 control steps, as in action chunking ( Zhao et al., 2023 ; Yang et al., 2026a ) .
Its choices split between short and long motions, and it reaches the same overall score with a different balance across groups, higher on Long-horizon and Memory tasks and lower on Precision.
Since the extra freedom buys nothing overall, JustRUA keeps the fixed cap as the simpler choice.
Full history with the 20-step cap at medium effort is the default testbed for the rest of the study. 3.3 Alternative Action Interfaces Two alternatives to absolute targets come from recent systems, and neither beats them ( Tables 1 and 2 ). Relative targets give position and orientation as offsets from the pose measured at the current decision, as in RoboDawn ( Guo et al., 2026 ) .
They score lower, much as Astranomy found ( Yang et al., 2026a ) , and the arm strikes objects almost three times as often.
One likely reason is that every coordinate the model reads, from the task recipe to the measured pose, is given in the world frame, so an offset asks for one more subtraction before each move. Semantic operations. Semantic operations replace raw targets with named skills ( Ahn et al., 2022 ; Liang et al., 2023 ; Chen et al., 2026b ) .
Ours are built on move_eef and only describe moves at a higher level, with no new control code or learned policy.
Some name the purpose of a move, such as approach , transport or retreat .
Some merge several moves into one call, such as pick , which moves, closes and lifts, or handover , in which one arm presents an object and the other takes it.
And adjust_view moves a wrist camera to see better, with guidance to look before acting.
The model used the named operations in most of its calls, and they changed what it did more than how well it did it.
It moved its cameras more than three times as often and dropped more of what it held, and the score did not rise. Move sequences. Allowing up to four moves per decision raised the score, mostly on Long-horizon tasks.
The gain has a simple source.
Episodes need fewer decisions, and the few episodes that used to run out of the budget of 100 decisions account for most of the difference ( Table 13 in Appendix C ).
The gain therefore reflects the budget more than the interface, and the final configuration of JustRUA removes the budget instead. 3.4 Reasoning Effort Medium effort, the default, scores highest overall.
Low effort costs slightly less and gives up most of the Precision score.
High effort costs more and trades precision for memory.
The two other settings play differently enough from medium that we used low effort alongside medium when building the evaluation set ( Section 2.4 ), so that the choice of tasks does not rest on one style of play. Takeaway 1: A lean harness goes a long way. Keeping the full episode history and stopping each motion after 20 control steps lift the score from 29.7 to 38.0.
Interfaces that structure the action for the model, such as relative targets, named skills and model-chosen step counts, add nothing it cannot handle with plain absolute targets. 4 Reusing Experience Experience from other episodes is the core of recursive self-improvement (RSI) ( Zhou et al., 2026a ) , and of everything we tried it carries the greatest risk of gaming the benchmark.
It draws on the tasks being scored, so its scores are not comparable with a standard evaluation.
This is not to say that iteration is useless.
A score obtained with experience from the tasks being scored simply reports something else, and the setting as a whole is not one that a regular evaluation is meant for.
We test experience reuse because it is widely used, in the three forms of Section 2.3 , and we report what each changes in behaviour as well as in score.
In our runs no form clearly beat the default ( Table 1 ), and none of them comes for free. 4.1 Demonstrations Demonstrations placed in context are a common way to help GPT-6 Astra ( SenseNova and Joint Research Team et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2026 ) .
Both of our forms draw on one expert-curated successful episode per task from RoboDawn ( Guo et al., 2026 ) , looked up by task name and inserted once before the first decision.
The visual demonstration shows the 16 keyframes its author selected, with no action trace or robot state attached.
The text trajectory gives the same episode stage by stage, as the poses of both arms, the commands issued and their outcomes, converted to our coordinates, with failed commands kept and the author’s plans left out.
It takes about 16,000 characters per task on average ( Appendix F ).
In both forms the model is told that the demonstration comes from a different episode and that it should follow its order and geometry rather than copy its coordinates. Table 3: Behaviour with reused experience , counted over the same 42 first-round episodes.

In our loop a Codex agent writes extra operations for the model, as Python code built on move_eef , together with a guide on when to use them.
It starts from the RoboDawn demonstrations, from which it writes a first library of four operations, grasp , transport , place and push .
The tasks are split into five groups.
In each round the model runs the current library on one group, and the agent then reviews those runs, with their scores, action logs and videos, and revises the library for the next round.
Nothing is edited by hand.
Unlike the demonstrations, the library is not stored or looked up by task, and every task sees the same operations and the same guide. The agent’s changes follow the failures it saw ( Table 19 in Appendix G ).
After the model let go of a plug and a pen before they were in place, place and grasp gained an option to stop before opening or closing, and a new insert pushes a held tip into a hole once it is lined up.
After the model counted presses that never registered in play_Xylophone and swap_blocks , the guide began to separate attempted presses from confirmed ones.

The loop helps, but slowly. 4.3 Cost of Experience The loop ran for five rounds and practised each task once, so we cannot rule out larger gains at a larger scale.
Systems that report clear gains from self-improvement practise each task many more times than we did ( Li et al., 2026a ; Li et al., 2026b ) .
Within that limit, the three forms share one pattern.
Experience corrects the behaviour it targets.
Demonstrations made handovers more frequent and drops rarer, and the library made grasps land more often.
But a task draws on many abilities at once, and correcting one of them rarely changes the outcome, as make_kong shows. The gains also come at a price.
Every episode carries the extra context, which raises its cost ( Table 3 ), and the cost of producing the experience, expert episodes for the demonstrations and practice runs for the library, comes on top.
The experience has to come from the tasks being scored, which is what makes its scores hard to compare with a standard evaluation.
For these reasons JustRUA keeps no form of experience reuse, whatever its score. Takeaway 2: Reusing experience is not a free lunch. Demonstrations and a self-written skill library change how the model acts, but neither clearly beats the default, and both make an episode more expensive.
They also draw on the tasks being scored, so their scores are not comparable with a standard evaluation. 5 Scene Geometry Recent agent systems feed the model depth, point clouds and affordances from dedicated models ( Qu et al., 2026 ; Zhou et al., 2026b ; Huang et al., 2026 ; Kim et al., 2026 ; Yang et al., 2026b ; Wang et al., 2026 ) .

Information about the scene arrives at every decision and stays in the history, so every extra character is paid many times over.
Written as full JSON, with vectors, thresholds and identifiers for every finger, the readings crowded the context and pushed many first-round episodes into context compaction, which the default testbed never reaches.
JustRUA writes them in a short text format with selected fields, fixed units and rounded values ( in Appendix H ).
In this form no first-round episode was compacted, and empty grasps fell slightly ( Table 5 ). 5.2 Depth Many recent agent systems use depth to place objects in 3D ( Qu et al., 2026 ; Zhou et al., 2026b ; Huang et al., 2026 ) , but the RoboDojo leaderboard ( RoboDojo Team, 2026 ) does not accept depth read from the simulator for agent entries, as a maintainer stated in review ( XPolicyLab maintainers, 2026 ) .
We therefore test both, depth from the simulator and depth that Depth Pro ( Bochkovskii et al., 2025 ) predicts from each RGB image alone.
In both cases each RGB image is paired with a depth image that shows camera-forward distance on a fixed colour scale, and the text adds the pose and intrinsics of each camera and a few more gripper readings, such as the finger gap and the contact points.
Depth Pro receives nothing but the RGB image.
It estimates its own focal length, and we pass it no camera parameters and fit no scale to its output.
The instruction tells the model that the predicted depth is an estimate and is least reliable around thin objects and occlusion boundaries. Figure 7: Predicted depth. (a) One head-camera frame with the raw Depth Pro prediction that the model receives, the same prediction scaled to the robot geometry and simulator depth, on a shared colour scale.
(b) Behaviour counts with robot readings only, with simulator depth and with predicted depth. The predicted distances are far off, by tens of centimetres even with the true focal length ( Table 7 ).
A scale fitted to the robot’s own geometry in each frame brings the error down to a few centimetres, but that scale drifts between frames and cannot be fitted for the wrist cameras, so JustRUA passes the raw predictions on ( Figure 7 a). Even so, predicted depth improves manipulation almost as much as simulator depth.
Empty grasps fell sharply with either source, failed fine placements fell further with predicted depth, and drops did not fall with either ( Figure 7 b).
What the prediction gets right is the order of depths, and that seems to be enough.
The gain from depth does not need privileged information. 5.3 Affordances Depth tells the model where surfaces are, but not where an object can be held or which arm can reach it.
That knowledge is an affordance.
Recent agent systems compute affordances with dedicated models and hand them to the planner ( Kim et al., 2026 ; Yang et al., 2026b ; Li et al., 2026c ) , and since many episodes go wrong at the grasp, we tested whether giving the model this knowledge directly would help.
A pretrained grasp generator, GraspGen ( Murali et al., 2025 ) , proposes up to eight grasp poses on the point cloud of each object, and inverse kinematics tests which surfaces each arm can reach with its current tool orientation.
Both are drawn onto the camera images, and the text lists the grasp poses along with hints such as which candidates a camera cannot see. Figure 8: Four ways to present affordances , head-camera images of one observation of insert_tubes as the model receives them.

Each observation already carries the camera views and their depth, and anything added either takes more space or crowds what is already there.
We tried four presentations ( Figure 8 ), which differ in whether the marks are drawn over the camera view or shown next to it, and in how many images an observation then holds. Against depth images from the same source, none of the four grasped more reliably or scored higher ( Tables 1 and 5 ).
The best of them, the affordance panel, matches predicted depth in score with about twice as many empty grasps.
The moments where affordances could make a difference are also few.
Reach matters most when one arm cannot get to an object and hands it to the other, and handovers stayed rare in every version.
Grasp candidates matter only in the decisions that start a grasp.
The cost, in contrast, comes with every observation.
Information that is needed only now and then is a poor fit for an observation that is paid for at every decision, and we left affordances out of JustRUA. 5.4 Coordinate Solving In the last step the model may write and run code in its Codex session, as in Code as Policies ( Liang et al., 2023 ) and, more recently, URAI ( Ge et al., 2026 ) and HexaShell ( Gao et al., 2026 ) .
Web search stays off so that the model cannot look up the benchmark or its solutions, and the simulator waits while the code runs.
At the same time the 100-decision cap that all earlier comparisons keep is lifted, while the official limit of 500 control steps still applies.

In make_kong , a single arm cannot reach the flip angle the tile needs and still place it correctly.
Nothing tells the model this about the arm, so it never splits the job with a handover.
The pouring tasks fail in a similar way, since the model pours forward, a motion the arm does poorly, where a side pour would work.
Collisions are unplanned knocks, such as the arm pushing a light box out of place, after which the run looks complete but scores low.
Unstable grasps occur with irregular or round objects, such as a doll that slips when the arm moves too fast, or a coin that offers few stable grasp points.
The remaining cases look easy, and the prompt or the scoring seems to be at fault.
In fasten_screws , for instance, the screws go in, but the model may be unable to tell whether it met the tolerance the scorer requires. Possible fixes. Each of the first three causes points to a general direction, but each needs knowledge that is costly to supply.
Planning would need high-level knowledge of the arm, such as which flip angles one arm cannot reach while placing, and which limits matter changes from task to task.
Collision checking would need complete digital assets of the arm and of every object it holds, and modelling every held object at that level is hardly feasible.
Grasp planning would need the same assets to model how each irregular object sits in the gripper.
For the prompt and scoring cases, rewriting the recipes of the five tasks so that they state the conditions the scorer checks raises the first-round score slightly ( Table 10 , Appendix I ).
The gain is small because these tasks are not all easy in other respects, and pack_objects_into_box , for one, also fails by collision.
This revision draws on the scorer of each test task, so we report it here and do not count it in JustRUA. The other failures are hard to remove without trial and error on each task, which comes close to tuning on the test tasks themselves.
We read this as a sign that, for a general harness with no task-specific component, the remaining headroom on this benchmark lies mostly in its long tail. Takeaway 4: Remaining failures are mostly long-tail. Most failures left in our best configuration are specific to one task, such as plans that do not suit the arm, unplanned knocks and slipping grasps.
Fixing them would take trial and error on each task, which comes close to tuning on the test tasks. 7 Related Work Frontier models as robot policies. Vision-language-action models learn robot control from large robot datasets on top of pretrained vision-language backbones ( Brohan et al., 2023 ; Kim et al., 2024 ; Black et al., 2025 ; Physical Intelligence et al., 2025 ; Physical Intelligence et al., 2026 ; Yu et al., 2026a ) .
A more recent line drives the robot with a general-purpose model or agent that receives no robot training at all ( Jia et al., 2026 ) .
For GPT-6 Astra, RoboProbe ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) provides the first evaluation on RoboDojo together with the official L3 harness, Galbot ( Galbot Team et al., 2026 ) and SenseNova ( SenseNova and Joint Research Team et al., 2026 ) study its abilities more broadly, and Astranomy ( Yang et al., 2026a ) treats it as a native robot policy with action chunking.
Systems such as RoboDawn ( Guo et al., 2026 ) , GPT-GEAR ( Zhou et al., 2026b ) , RoboHarness ( Qu et al., 2026 ) , ORA ( Yang et al., 2026b ) and uni-robot-agent ( Yuan et al., 2026 ) each build a harness with its own interfaces and perception.
We do not propose another system.
We test which of their components matter when all of them are measured under one protocol, and the configuration we end with is a by-product of that test. Skills and code. Named skills are a common way to connect language models to robots ( Ahn et al., 2022 ) , and code lets a model compose perception and control by itself ( Liang et al., 2023 ; Huang et al., 2023 ; Fu et al., 2026 ) .
URAI ( Ge et al., 2026 ) lets frontier agents write, call and evolve their own robot tools, and CLARO ( Kim et al., 2026 ) orchestrates open-vocabulary grasping with VLM agents and dedicated models.
Our semantic operations and coordinate solving follow these two lines while using only what the official harness already provides.
We find the two to be of unequal value for GPT-6 Astra.
Skills that restate its moves at a higher level add little, while code that computes where to move adds a lot. Experience and self-improvement. Language agents can improve across attempts by storing skills ( Wang et al., 2024 ) or verbal reflections ( Shinn et al., 2023 ) , and ASPIRE ( Lu et al., 2026 ) builds such a skill library for robots.
On RoboDojo, several systems refine their own skills over many rounds ( Li et al., 2026a ; OpenSenseNova, 2026 ; Yu et al., 2026b ) or scale online exploration ( Huang et al., 2026 ) , and PhysRSI-eval ( Li et al., 2026b ) studies where the gains of such loops come from.
These systems practise each task many times.
We test a lighter regime, a single pass over the tasks, and look at what experience changes in behaviour as well as in score. Controlled studies. Our study follows a line of work that explains a result by taking a system apart rather than by adding to it, in the spirit of JustRL ( He et al., 2026 ) .
For software agents, the design of the agent-computer interface is known to change what a model can do ( Yang et al., 2024 ) , and a study of Claude models on robotics tasks reports that performance depends strongly on how the model is connected to the robot ( Berman et al., 2026 ) .
Astranomy ( Yang et al., 2026a ) does so for GPT-6 Astra as a native robot policy, and PhysRSI-eval ( Li et al., 2026b ) for self-improvement loops.
We cover the harness, the action interface, experience and scene geometry under one evaluation set, one behaviour review and one cost measure. 8 Conclusion Starting from the official L3 harness, JustRUA keeps three kinds of change.
The loop keeps the full episode history and stops each motion early, the observation carries robot readings and predicted depth, and the model may solve coordinates in code.
Together they raise the evaluation-set score from 29.7 to 50.3 with no learned policy, no self-improvement loop and no task-specific tuning.
Alternative action interfaces, precomputed affordances and reused experience did not earn their place under our protocol, and most of the remaining failures are specific to single tasks. Two observations run through these results.
First, GPT-6 Astra handles the plain interface well.
World-frame targets, raw sensor values and uncalibrated depth are enough for it, and layers that restructure its actions or pre-digest its observations mostly add cost.
Second, what helps is information that bears on the errors the model makes often, such as closing on nothing or misjudging where an object is, delivered in a form short enough to pay for at every decision. Limitations. All experiments run in simulation, on one benchmark and with one model, and each configuration is run once on the evaluation set.
Each alternative is tested at the point of the path where it was added, not in every combination.
The experience runs draw on the tasks being scored, and the self-improvement loop ran for only five rounds with one practice run per task.
The evaluation set was chosen from the history of our own default testbed, and it weights random layouts more heavily than the official protocol does in the Generalization group, so its scores are not leaderboard scores. Each episode opens with a fixed instruction that defines move_eef and give_up and the JSON format of a decision, followed by the task instruction and a task recipe.
The recipes come from the RoboDojo wiki through the official harness and give the capability group, a short description and the official scoring ladder, without an action sequence ( ).
They are the only task-specific text in the system, and we use them unchanged except in the prompt-revision experiment of Appendix I . At each decision the model receives the three RGB views, the measured grasp-point pose, gripper opening and joint state of both arms, and feedback on the previous command, including how far the grasp point ended up from its target and, under the 20-step cap, how many planned steps ran.
At every step we also log the requested target, the measured state after execution and the remaining error, so that a failed grasp can be traced to a wrong target, a poor plan or an imprecise execution. Budgets. An episode has three separate limits, the number of model decisions, the control steps executed per motion and the benchmark’s own limit of 500 control steps.
All controlled comparisons keep 100 decisions per episode and, from the default testbed on, at most 20 control steps per motion.
The decision limit is lower than the 170 calls allowed in the published run of the official harness ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) .
The final configuration of JustRUA lifts the decision limit and keeps the other two, and an episode ends when either remaining limit runs out. The 20-step cap. The cap is a thin wrapper around the unchanged planner, and it follows a few fixed rules.
The original planner and its retiming run as before.
Only a planned path longer than 20 control steps is cut, and the first 20 steps run with their original values, so the trajectory is never resampled.
The rest of the path is discarded for good, including any gripper command in it, and the gripper returns to the last command that actually ran.
The feedback keeps the original target, reports the remaining error to it and states the exact number of steps that ran.
No collision filter, refusal rule, replay of the discarded part or alternative controller is added. Episode history. The Codex session keeps every observation, action and feedback message of the episode, images included, until Codex compacts the context near its window limit.
In the default testbed this did not happen, since requests stayed well below the size at which Codex compacts.
Configurations that add long text to every observation can reach the limit, as the full-JSON readings of Section 5.1 did in 38% of first-round episodes. How much the evaluation set helps. On the eight rounds used to choose them, the 23 tasks account for about 95% of the task-level variance in the overall score.
At medium effort the bootstrap standard deviation of the overall score is 4.8 points for a single round and 2.6 for the evaluation set, close to the 2.4 of running every task four times with 168 episodes.
The bootstrap resamples each task’s rounds independently, so it measures sensitivity to the layouts in our history. Fixed layouts. The official evaluation scores each task over 50 episodes, since the score of a task on any single layout is spiky.
We run far fewer episodes, so which layouts we run matters.
Each round runs the next layout of every task in a fixed order, and rounds are not equally hard.
Every configuration in this paper therefore runs on the same fixed layouts, in the first round and on the evaluation set alike, rather than on fresh random draws. Layout order and weighting. Every round uses seed 0 and the next layout of each task in a fixed order.
For the twelve Generalization tasks, which have both standard and random layouts, rounds alternate between the two, starting with random layout 0, so the Generalization group of the evaluation set weights random layouts at 75% against 50% in the official evaluation.
We report evaluation-set and full scores separately for this reason.
The 23 four-layout tasks were chosen from the history of the default testbed, which does not guarantee that the evaluation set ranks every new design as the full benchmark would. Round-to-round variation. Low and medium effort rise and fall together across eight rounds, with a correlation of 0.84, although we have not separated layout difficulty from run-to-run randomness ( Figure 12 ).
A score from the first round alone tends to run high.
In all three settings of Table 15 the first round scored above the mean of eight rounds, and for the official harness the eight-round mean is close to the full evaluation. The agent reads the complete action log, including the note the model writes with each call, and the videos of the three cameras.
It scans each view at short intervals, checks consecutive frames around every executed action, every suspicious moment and the start and end of each event, and records each event with its time range, the views that show it and the frames used as evidence.
Events that span several actions, such as repeated empty grasps ending in a regrasp or a handover in several stages, are linked into one record.
A main agent then checks the coverage of each review and every episode’s counts against this evidence and the native logs, and sends episodes that do not match back for another review.
