In this work, we systematically study the HC architecture to understand

either alone achieves performance comparable to mHC.
We also identify repeated access to the expanded residual state

These findings lead to SimpleHC , which simplifies the architecture
by removing residual mixing and simplifies execution through shared memory access , using the resulting additive structure
and shared gate inputs.

and deferred writes; scalar corrections exactly preserve preceding sublayer
output contributions.
Our analyses support both the retained residual structure and the
design of shared memory access.

It maintains performance comparable to mHC when scaling from 15B to

training, and remains stable under stress.

Hyper-Connections (HC) ( Zhu et al., 2025 ) introduce multiple residual
streams with learned read, write, and mixing operations for flexible
information routing.
The manifold-constrained variant, mHC ( Xie et al., 2025 ) , constrains
residual mixing to improve training stability at scale.
HC-based architectures, particularly mHC, have been adopted in
frontier language models ( Xu et al., 2026 ; Zeng et al., 2026 ) .
Despite the growing adoption, these architectures remain insufficiently
understood and costly to train: their gating and mixing operations
introduce architectural complexity and non-negligible execution
overhead, even under efficient implementations ( Xie et al., 2025 ) . A key source of this overhead is repeated access to the expanded residual
state: at every sublayer, HC-style mappings read and write n n streams
of width C C to generate gates, aggregate inputs, and propagate updates.
This memory traffic incurs costs not captured by FLOPs alone. In our end-to-end experiments with an efficient
implementation, mHC adds 7 % − 27 % 7\%-27\% training time over a vanilla baseline
(Figure 3 (a)), despite adding less than 1 % 1\% FLOPs.
This motivates reconsidering the underlying read, write, and mixing
interactions. We ask: Can a simpler Hyper-Connections design reduce execution cost
while preserving model quality and training stability at scale? In this work, we systematically study the Hyper-Connections architecture through three
components: read gates, write gates, and residual mixing.
We find that unconstrained mixing improves quality, but Sinkhorn constraints
substantially reduce its benefit. Learned read/write gating without
mixing and unconstrained mixing with fixed gates each achieve quality
comparable to mHC, with further gains from combining them.
Our analysis also highlights the propagation risks of unconstrained
mixing, leading us to select learned read/write gating with identity
transitions as a minimal effective structure that retains competitive
quality while avoiding these risks. Without residual mixing, the additive structure lets scalar corrections exactly recover preceding output contributions for given gates, without
materializing intermediate n ​ C nC -dimensional states.
Gate generation, however, still accesses the updated state at every sublayer.
We reduce these accesses by computing layer-specific gates from a common
group-entry state, consolidating reads and deferring writes across sublayers.
We call this execution scheme shared memory access . Together, these findings lead to SimpleHC , which simplifies the architecture through learned read/write gates
without mixing and simplifies execution through shared memory
access. We also develop specialized kernels to translate this simplicity into training efficiency.

Three conclusions emerge:
(i) learned read/write gating provides a minimal effective
residual structure ;
(ii) gating decisions vary across sublayers yet remain predictable
from a shared group-entry state ;
(iii) SimpleHC remains stable under stress and exhibits smoother
gradient dynamics during standard training . 6.1 A Minimal Structure for Hyper-Connections To combine architectural simplicity, competitive performance, and robust
scaling, we select learned read/write gating without mixing as a minimal
effective residual structure for Hyper-Connections. This avoids the
propagation risks of cumulative residual mixing, as analyzed below. Learned read/write gating and unconstrained mixing form two effective
groups: each achieves performance comparable to mHC, with further gains
when combined (Table 2 ).
Appendix C compares their weighting mechanisms.
However, unconstrained mixing repeatedly transforms earlier contributions
through residual matrix products, risking cumulative amplification
and training instability ( Xie et al., 2025 ) .
We observe unconstrained mixing with fixed gates produces larger peak
residual-path coefficients than identityHC, consistent with these
amplification risks (Figure 7 ,
Appendix D.1 ). Sinkhorn constraints mitigate these risks ( Xie et al., 2025 ) , but
constrained mixing adds little benefit over learned read/write gating
alone. We therefore retain learned gates without mixing and use
full-state inputs for better loss and benchmark scores
(Table 2 ). Full-state and stream-local conditioning
incur the same residual-state input traffic, as both read all streams. 6.2 Why Shared Memory Access Is Effective (a) Write-gate variation across sublayers (b) Prediction from the group-entry state Figure 5 : Gates vary across sublayers yet are predictable from shared inputs. and identityHC remain stable. 7 Related Work Hyper-Connections and Variants. Hyper-Connections expand the residual pathway into multiple streams with
learned read, write, and mixing operations ( Zhu et al., 2025 ) .
Subsequent work constrains mixing to doubly stochastic matrices ( Xie et al., 2025 ) , reduces constraint-enforcement costs ( Yang and Gao, 2026 ; Zhou et al., 2026 ; Dandachi and Diggs-Galligan, 2026 ) ,
or explores other constraints for stable propagation
and stream diversity ( Liu et al., 2026 ; Guo et al., 2026 ) .
Other designs modify read/write structures ( Qiu et al., 2026a ; Zheng et al., 2026 ) or use sparse updates to support more streams ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) . Understanding and Simplifying Hyper-Connections. Recent analyses reveal uneven stream utilization, redundancy, and
concentrated read/write routing in HC models ( Peng et al., 2026 ; Alimaskina et al., 2026 ) .
Prior work also reports that removing residual mixing can largely preserve
model quality ( Guo et al., 2026 ) , with related evidence from
finetuning ( Oldenburg et al., 2026 ) . These observations motivate examining
which interactions account for the benefits.
Our study identifies learned read/write gating and unconstrained mixing
as two independently effective groups, while showing that
Sinkhorn constraints diminish the gains from mixing.
These findings explain why learned gating alone remains competitive with mHC ( Xie et al., 2025 ) and motivate an additive structure whose execution we further simplify
through shared memory access. Efficient Execution of Residual Aggregation. Richer residual pathways introduce storage and data-movement costs beyond
their arithmetic overhead. Prior work reduces these costs through block-wise
aggregation and staged computation ( Team et al., 2026 ) ,
kernel fusion and recomputation ( Xie et al., 2025 ; Zheng et al., 2026 ) ,
and optimized multi-stream execution ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) .
SimpleHC further reduces repeated residual-state access across sublayers
by sharing gate inputs while retaining layer-specific parameters.
Together with the additive residual structure, this permits consolidated
reads and deferred writes, with scalar corrections preserving sequential
output contributions for the chosen gates. 8 Conclusion In this work, we study how the components of the Hyper-Connections architecture
contribute to model performance and training cost.
Our study identifies learned read/write gating as a minimal effective
structure, while unconstrained mixing offers gains with propagation risks.
Beyond removing mixing, we introduce shared memory access to reduce
repeated residual-state accesses, with gate analyses supporting
layer-specific mappings conditioned on shared inputs.
Together, they form SimpleHC, simplifying residual structure and execution.
SimpleHC matches mHC performance on 15B and 30B MoE models, reduces
additional training overhead from 7.0 % 7.0\% to 2.4 % 2.4\% at the largest
tested scale, and exhibits smoother gradient dynamics while remaining
stable under stress.
SimpleHC thus combines architectural simplicity, execution efficiency,
training stability, and scalability. References Alimaskina et al. (2026) Ekaterina Alimaskina, Gleb Molodtsov, and Aleksandr Beznosikov.
