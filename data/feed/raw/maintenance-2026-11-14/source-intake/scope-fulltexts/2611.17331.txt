We propose T est- T ime B aking ( TTB ), a strategy that learns to bake global scene information from cross-view attention into a lightweight MLP, enabling fast and high-fidelity novel-view decoding.
The first key is to repurpose the fast-weight MLP of TTT to learn the cross-view attention mapping explicitly from its input-output pairs, rather than replacing attention entirely, achieving the same rendering efficiency as TTT while delivering substantially higher quality.
The second key addresses a modality misalignment between context prefilling and novel-view rendering: we disentangle context tokens into camera-only context rendering tokens to serve as TTB input, and context ground-truth tokens with image content to serve as the TTB mapping target, ensuring the baked MLPs are relieved of the modality generalization challenge between context and novel-view processing.
Our method achieves up to 23x rendering speedup with quality on par with the state-of-the-art across several benchmarks.

The t-SNE plots show that only (c) aligns the modalities from the first block. 1 Introduction Our research studies the task of novel-view synthesis conditioned on a set of context images with the powerful Large View Synthesis Models (LVSMs) [ 25 ] .
LVSMs have recently emerged with several immediate follow-up improvements [ 22 , 31 , 69 , 77 , 58 ] , achieving state-of-the-art novel-view synthesis quality across various input conditions [ 1 , 23 , 64 , 42 ] .
Their geometry-free scene representations and Transformer-based renderers are fully learning-based, making them favorable for scaling with increasing amounts of training data.
The minimized 3D inductive bias also shows advantages when scenes are challenging for existing Transformer-compatible geometry-based representations (e.g., 3DGS [ 29 ] ), such as surfaces with strong mirror reflections or translucent material. We are especially interested in the decoder-only variant of LVSM [ 25 ] for its high-fidelity.
DVSM [ 58 ] further introduces KV-cache to the cross-view attention layers, showing both quality and efficiency improvement.
The KV-cache serves as a global scene state for novel-view queries, but the rendering time is still impractical under dense context view conditions due to the linear complexity per token when attending to the KV-cache.
LaCT [ 77 ] takes a different approach by replacing cross-view attention layers with Test-Time Training (TTT) [ 59 ] layers, as illustrated in Fig. 2 a.
TTT performs inner-loop optimization to adapt fast-weight MLPs [ 49 ] to directly map queries to their values without the quadratic-complexity attention, which can also be interpreted as a variant of linear attention [ 36 ] .
Despite achieving constant-complexity rendering speed, TTT significantly sacrifices rendering quality.
A naive remedy for LaCT is to initialize from pretrained decoder-only model weights, which does improve quality, but the gap to the pretrained model remains significant. We argue that this quality gap can be minimized by repurposing the fast-weight MLP inner-loop optimization as “baking” the information from cross-view attention, rather than replacing cross-view attention entirely as in TTT.
We dub our strategy Test-Time Baking (TTB), illustrated in Fig. 2 b.
The key motivation is to trade context-view processing time for faster and higher-fidelity novel-view rendering, achieving the Pareto frontier of quality and speed shown in Fig. 1 .
Specifically, the inner-loop learning target becomes the output tokens from cross-view attention, instead of the raw value tokens used in TTT.
This formulation also generalizes beyond the linear-attention interpretation of TTT [ 36 ] .
In principle, the learning target of our TTB can also be tokens from arbitrarily non-linear layers or tokens enriched with additional scene information. Furthermore, we identify a modality misalignment issue inherent to the context prefilling of MLP fast-weight for novel-view synthesis.
In the reconstruction stage (context prefilling), the fast-weight MLPs are updated using context tokens with both camera and image information.
In the rendering stage, however, the updated MLPs take novel-view tokens coming from camera-only input without any image content.
This conventional pipeline poses a reconstruction-rendering input modality misalignment challenge. Our solution to modality misalignment is simple yet effective.
As illustrated in Fig. 2 c, we pair each context view into i) context ground-truth tokens, derived from input cameras and images that produce the global scene information to be baked, and ii) context rendering tokens, derived from camera-only input that simulates novel-view rendering conditions.
At each TTB layer, the fast-weight MLP is optimized to map context rendering tokens to their corresponding context ground-truth tokens, which carry image and cross-view attended information.
The optimized TTB-MLP is then shared between context rendering tokens and the downstream novel-view rendering tokens for retrieving scene information, ensuring that their conditioning remains identical across all layers. Interestingly, TTB draws a natural parallel with traditional implicit radiance and light field reconstruction, where MLPs are optimized to map spatial coordinates to radiance values.
In TTB, the fast-weight MLP instead is optimized to map ray-patch latent coordinates to their appearance latent representations, which effectively performs few-step light-field optimization in latent space.
This perspective suggests that TTB interpolates appearance latents for novel viewpoints from their ray-patch latent coordinates, motivating a convergence-oriented inner loop over one-shot summarization. In summary, our contributions are as follows: • We propose Test-Time Baking, which retargets the inner-loop optimization of the TTT fast-weight MLP from raw value tokens to cross-view attention outputs, so that the fast-weight MLP collaborates with attention rather than replacing it. • We identify a modality misalignment between the reconstruction and rendering stages of MLP fast-weight prefilling, and resolve it via a context pair that disentangles each context view into a ground-truth and a camera-only rendering token. • We draw a connection between TTB’s inner loop and light field optimization in latent space, which motivates a convergence-oriented training recipe (multi-step MSE) that we find particularly effective for TTB. 2 Related work Per-Scene optimization. Early approaches to novel-view synthesis perform per-scene optimization, fitting differentiable scene representations to observed images via differentiable rendering.
Coordinate-based MLPs [ 41 , 2 , 4 ] , voxels [ 56 , 15 , 8 , 43 , 57 , 34 ] , and the landmark 3DGS [ 29 ] as the scene representation have been widely explored.
Despite impressive quality, per-scene optimization does not scale with the increasing training scenes and degrades quickly under fewer input views. Geometry-based feed-forward models. To overcome the scalability limitations and lengthy procedure of per-scene optimization, learning-based methods train networks to predict 3D representations from images.
Early works estimated multi-plane images via cost volumes [ 73 , 68 , 17 , 72 , 16 , 40 ] , while NeRF-based generalizable methods [ 7 , 74 , 67 , 37 ] extended coverage to wide-angle viewpoints at limited quality.
The advent of 3DGS [ 29 ] reframed the problem as pixel-aligned GS prediction [ 6 , 71 , 10 , 60 , 62 , 24 ] , achieving promising results, and Large Reconstruction Models (LRMs) [ 21 ] further demonstrated Transformer scalability across various 3D representations [ 5 , 70 , 76 ] .
One fundamental limitation of handcrafted 3D is their degradation on surfaces or materials they are not designed for, whereas the geometry-free representations we study can learn them given data.
Furthermore, explicit 3D geometry lacks a compact latent representation, and efforts to manage their linearly growing output size under more input views [ 12 , 11 , 63 , 45 ] remain a symptom of this constraint.
Our TTB bakes scenes into constant-size MLPs at higher quality. Geometry-free feed-forward models. An alternative paradigm sidesteps the limitations of geometry-based models by representing scenes as latent representations and rendering via neural networks.
Early methods [ 13 , 55 , 54 , 53 , 46 , 47 ] were constrained by limited network capacity, while Large View Synthesis Models (LVSMs) [ 25 ] broke this barrier by leveraging powerful Transformers.
Encoder-decoder and decoder-only LVSM variants are explored.
The encoder-decoder LVSM [ 25 ] encodes context views into scene tokens and decodes novel views conditioned on the encoded scene, with subsequent works refining scene encoding strategies [ 22 , 31 , 69 ] and extending to generative [ 1 ] and uncalibrated camera [ 23 , 78 , 64 , 42 ] settings.
The decoder-only LVSM [ 25 ] , on the other hand, uses a single network for both context prefilling and novel-view rendering.
Despite higher fidelity, its original form inefficiently processes context views independently for each novel view.
DVSM [ 58 ] addresses this by prefilling context views into a KV-cache shared across all novel views, achieving state-of-the-art quality under both sparse and dense view settings.
However, the linearly growing KV-cache size makes rendering speed impractical for larger numbers of input views.
LaCT [ 77 ] replaces cross-view attention with Test-Time Training layers to achieve constant rendering complexity, but at a significant quality cost compared to DVSM.
Our Test-Time Baking achieves the same constant rendering complexity while substantially closing the quality gap left by LaCT. Test-time Training layers. TTT [ 59 ] has recently emerged as a popular alternative to attention for its linear time and constant space complexity.
We refer interested readers to [ 36 ] for an in-depth review and analysis.
We are particularly interested in the variant presented in LaCT [ 77 ] , which has demonstrated success in accelerating various vision and 3D tasks [ 9 , 63 , 18 , 14 , 26 ] , albeit at the cost of quality relative to their attention-based counterparts.
Our TTB departs from this paradigm in two ways: rather than replacing attention, TTB learns directly from attention outputs; and rather than performing mere token-to-token information exchange, TTB explicitly learns to map camera-ray tokens to their corresponding image tokens, yielding improved novel-view synthesis quality. The issue of DVSM is that its KV-cache size grows linearly with the number of context tokens, making it slow and impractical for novel-view queries under high-resolution or dense input context views.
A naive strategy is to replace each CrossAttn ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \mathrm{CrossAttn}(\cdot) in Eq. 3 with a Test-Time Training (TTT) [ 59 ] layer, achieving constant space and linear time complexity as demonstrated in LaCT [ 77 ] .
However, we observe significant quality degradation even when finetuning the TTT variant from DVSM pretrained weights.
This motivates us to explore a better solution that improves upon TTT.
A full model pipeline is illustrated in Fig. 3 . 3.3.1 Repurposing TTT: collaborating instead of replacing attention Our motivation is to trade reconstruction (prefilling) computation, which only needs to be performed once per scene, for improved rendering-time speed and quality.
The resulting solution is simple yet effective.
Rather than entirely replacing cross-view attention with TTT fast-weight MLP as in the LaCT [ 77 ] , we repurpose TTT to explicitly learn from the input-output pairs out of cross-view attention.
Consequently, we no longer need to cache the KV; only the updated MLP fast weights are stored.
The space and time complexity of novel-view rendering is therefore identical to that of TTT.
We call our strategy Test-Time Baking (TTB), which can be viewed as a generalized form of TTT. We remove the asymmetric QK projection and instead use a shared linear projection to produce queries and keys.
We adopt SwiGLU-MLP [ 50 ] as the fast-weight MLP architecture, and apply an additional linear layer to predict per-token learning rates.
For inner-loop optimization, we employ Muon [ 28 ] optimizer with weight normalization [ 48 ] for a predefined T T steps.
We find that using MSE as the inner-loop objective for our TTB yields better results than the unnormalized dot-product loss used in LaCT [ 77 ] ’s TTT.
We also find that learning the residual to the mapping target is easier to bake than learning the target directly.

We initialize our models from the corresponding DVSM [ 58 ] checkpoint. We use AdamW [ 38 ] with cosine annealing and perceptual loss weight λ = 0.2 \lambda=0.2 . Models are trained on DL3DV-10K [ 35 ] dataset with 64 A100 GPUs. We first train at 340p for 100 100 K iterations with learning rate × 10 − 4 4\!\times\!10^{-4} , then finetune at 480p, 720p, and 960p for 10 10 K iterations each with learning rate × 10 − 5 1\!\times\!10^{-5} . We randomly sample either 16 16 or 32 32 context views per scene with the same number of target views. Ablations use a smaller setup on 8 8 A100 GPUs at patch size 16 16 truncated to 720p, with efficiency metrics still reported at patch size 8 8 for consistency. For evaluation, we use the 140 140 held-out DL3DV testing scenes and follow previous view sampling [ 12 ] . TTB layer. We use a single-headed fast-weight SwiGLU-MLP [ 50 ] with a hidden dimension of 3072 3072 , a 4 × 4\times expansion of the model dimension. The fast weight is updated with the Muon optimizer [ 28 ] and we run T = 3 T\!=\!3 inner-loop update steps. The per-token learning rate is predicted by an additional linear head with base learning rate of 0.01 0.01 . The inner-loop objective maps context-rendering tokens to the cross-view attention output of context-ground-truth tokens with the MSE loss. Fast-weight local-duplicate inference (Sec. 3.3.4 ) is turned off except stated otherwise. When enabled, we set B = 16 B{=}16 . For some experiments we also report a variant denoted TTB– or Ours–, which replaces the cross-view attention target with a linear projection W v ​ X ( ctx-gt ) W_{v}X^{(\text{ctx-gt})} and thus avoids the quadratic-complexity cross-view attention at reconstruction, making reconstruction time linear in V V . 4.2 Comparisons DL3DV. Tab. 4 decomposes the gap between a vanilla LaCT-style TTT replacement [ 77 ] and our full TTB into three additive components: context pairing, switching to MSE with T = 3 T{=}3 inner steps (yielding TTB–), and replacing the linear-projection target with cross-view attention output. Each contributes meaningfully, and stacking all three closes the quality gap to DVSM [ 58 ] while retaining TTT’s ∼ 50 \sim\!50 FPS rendering. The reconstruction cost grows quadratically with V V due to the in-loop cross-view attention, consistent with our reconstruction time for rendering quality trade-off. Inner-loop optimization. Tab. 6 ablates the inner-loop loss and number of update steps T T . Switching from LaCT’s unnormalized dot-product loss [ 77 ] to MSE consistently improves quality for both TTT and TTB–, with TTB– benefiting more ( + 0.44 +0.44 vs + 0.27 +0.27 PSNR). Increasing T T brings monotonic but diminishing gains, leading us to adopt T = 3 T{=}3 with MSE as a sweet spot between quality and reconstruction cost. This asymmetric response aligns with our light field view—TTB’s inner loop fits a target function and benefits from convergence, while TTT-KVB is closer to a one-shot linear write. TTB layer design. Tab. 6 ablates two design choices in our TTB layer. Residual learning reformulates the inner-loop target from the absolute cross-view attention output to its residual against X ( ctx-rend ) X^{(\text{ctx-rend})} , reducing the magnitude of the regression target and easing the few-step inner-loop optimization. Feedback additionally applies the baked MLP to update the context ground-truth tokens, so that all three token streams propagate through identical operators in subsequent layers. Both designs contribute roughly equally in isolation ( + 0.43 +0.43 and + 0.44 +0.44 PSNR) and combine to + 0.52 +0.52 . Limitations. TTB is constrained by the fixed capacity of its fast-weight MLP, causing quality to saturate at large V V ; the local-duplicate routing that mitigates this is a heuristic, and learning it during training is left for future work.
Its inner-loop cross-view attention also makes reconstruction quadratic in V V , a drawback for single-shot scenarios.
Finally, TTB is built on reconstruction rather than generation, interpolating within context views without extrapolating to unobserved viewpoints. 5 Conclusion We present Test-Time Baking (TTB), which retargets the TTT inner-loop optimization from raw value tokens to cross-view attention outputs, turning the fast-weight MLP from a replacement for attention into its collaborator. Combined with a context pair that resolves the reconstruction-rendering modality misalignment, TTB bakes DVSM-level scene information into a fixed-size MLP while retaining TTT-level rendering complexity. Across DL3DV, Re10K, and in-the-wild benchmarks, TTB matches DVSM [ 58 ] quality at a constant 23 FPS and consistently surpasses LaCT [ 77 ] . We hope this baking perspective offers a new way to integrate attention and TTT beyond view synthesis. We provide the pseudocode of training our TTB model in Algo. 1 as a complementary reference for the descriptions below.
The three fast-weight matrices of our SwiGLU-MLP, 𝐰 0 , 𝐰 2 ∈ ℝ H × d in × d h \mathbf{w}_{0},\mathbf{w}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times d_{\text{in}}\times d_{h}} and 𝐰 1 ∈ ℝ H × d h × d out \mathbf{w}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times d_{h}\times d_{\text{out}}} , are stored as ordinary learnable parameters and are jointly optimized in the outer loop alongside the rest of the network.
Consequently, the inner loop for every scene starts from a common, outer-loop-learned meta-initialization θ 0 \theta_{0} , which is then advanced by T T Muon [ 28 ] update steps before being applied to the rendering tokens.
When local-duplicate inference is enabled with K > 1 K>1 groups, every per-group reconstruction starts
from the same meta-initialized θ 0 \theta_{0} and is specialized only by the views assigned to its
group, so all local copies share the same starting point and differ through their inner-loop
trajectories. We initialize TTB models from the corresponding DVSM [ 58 ] checkpoint with strict=False : the patch embeddings, input LayerNorms, Plücker encoding, intra-view attention layers, both FFNs in each block, and the RGB head are inherited directly, while every module inside the TTB layer—including its LayerNorm, query/key and value projections, output projection, per-token learning-rate head, output RMSNorm, and the fast weights 𝐰 0 , 𝐰 1 , 𝐰 2 \mathbf{w}_{0},\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2} —is freshly initialized.
The cross-view attention projections of DVSM are not reused, since the inner-loop target of TTB is structurally different and warm-starting from the DVSM projections did not yield benefits in our preliminary experiments.
To prevent the random-init TTB layer from disrupting the inherited DVSM features at the start of finetuning, we initialize the TTB output projection with a small standard deviation following the residual zero-init trick used in deep transformers, so that the TTB block contributes approximately zero at finetune step 0 0 and the entire forward pass reduces to the pretrained DVSM model plus an identity TTB layer.
This zero-init together with the parameter inheritance above accounts for the + 1.02 +1.02 PSNR gain reported in Tab. 13 . For the TTB layer micro-architecture, we share a single linear projection between query and key construction: the inner-loop input 𝐖 k ​ 𝐗 ( ctx-rend ) \mathbf{W}_{k}\mathbf{X}^{(\text{ctx-rend})} in main paper Eq. 6 and the rendering-time query 𝐖 k ​ 𝐗 ( nv ) \mathbf{W}_{k}\mathbf{X}^{(\text{nv})} are both produced by passing the input through a single linear layer followed by SiLU and an L 2 L_{2} unit-norm, which constrains the writes and reads of the fast-weight MLP to a common spherical subspace and removes a magnitude degree of freedom from the inner-loop optimization.
The per-token learning rate is predicted by an additional linear head that outputs 3 ​ H 3H scalars per token—one for each of the three fast-weight matrices in every head—and reparameterized as softplus ( ⋅ + softplus − 1 ( 0.01 ) ) \mathrm{softplus}(\cdot+\mathrm{softplus}^{-1}(0.01)) , ensuring strictly positive learning rates and an effective initial value of 0.01 0.01 across all heads and matrices. The outer loop differentiates fully through all T T inner-loop steps without any first-order approximation, so that the rendering loss reflects the exact dependence of the final fast weights on the outer-loop parameters.
Each Muon step orthogonalizes the inner-loop gradient with five Newton–Schulz quintic iterations executed in BFloat16; we transpose the gradient before iteration whenever d in > d h d_{\text{in}}>d_{h} so that the intermediate Gram matrix has size min ⁡ ( d in , d h ) 2 \min(d_{\text{in}},d_{h})^{2} , which keeps the per-step memory manageable.
Even with this optimization, retaining T T inner-step activations for backpropagation remains the dominant memory cost of TTB.
We mitigate it with two layers of gradient checkpointing: the inner-loop activations of SwiGLU and Muon are recomputed within each step, and the outer-loop checkpointing is applied selectively to the intra-view attention and FFN sublayers but not to the TTB sublayer itself, the combination of which makes our 64-A100 training feasible at 960 960 p resolution with V = 32 V\!=\!32 context views. Finally, our two main paper variants differ only in the inner-loop mapping target.
TTB instantiates g ⁡ ( ⋅ ) g(\cdot) in main paper Eq. 6b as the cross-view attention output of 𝐗 ( ctx-gt ) \mathbf{X}^{(\text{ctx-gt})} , while TTB– replaces this with a linear projection 𝐖 v ​ 𝐗 ( ctx-gt ) \mathbf{W}_{v}\mathbf{X}^{(\text{ctx-gt})} acting directly on the context ground-truth tokens.
Both variants share every other component described above, including the context pair construction, the QK-shared projection, the per-token learning-rate head, the residual target, and the feedback path that propagates the baked MLP output back to 𝐗 ( ctx-gt ) \mathbf{X}^{(\text{ctx-gt})} . A.3 Two-view Re10K specific details For the two-view setting, each GPU receives 32 scenes per iteration, with 2 context views and 3 target views per scene.
The interval between the two context frames is sampled uniformly between 25 25 and 192 192 , and target frames are drawn from the union of all intermediate frames between the two context views and the extrapolated frames within 25 25 frames on either side.
We initialize the model from the corresponding DVSM [ 58 ] Re10K checkpoint following the strategy detailed in Sec. A.2 , and finetune it on Re10K for 100 100 K iterations at 256 × 256 256{\times}256 followed by 10 10 K iterations at 512 × 512 512{\times}512 .
Note that doubling the context tokens through the context pair (main paper Eq. 7 ) negligibly affects this two-view setup, so we keep the same per-GPU batch composition as DVSM. A.4 Many-view DL3DV [ 35 ] specific details DL3DV is the main benchmark for our dense-view results in main paper Tab. 1 and Tab. 3 , as well as for all the ablations in main paper Sec. 4.3 .
For training, we adopt a dynamic batching scheme that controls the per-GPU token budget rather than the number of scenes.
Each scene draws either 16 16 or 32 32 context views uniformly at random, paired with the same number of target views; the number of scenes packed into a GPU is then adjusted on the fly so that the aggregate count of context-pair tokens (i.e., the union of 𝐗 ( ctx-gt ) \mathbf{X}^{(\text{ctx-gt})} and 𝐗 ( ctx-rend ) \mathbf{X}^{(\text{ctx-rend})} across all scenes in the batch) stays within a fixed budget.
This compensates for the doubled token count introduced by the context pair and keeps the effective compute per iteration comparable to DVSM at the same memory ceiling.
The frame interval between two adjacent context views is sampled uniformly from [ 1 , 16 ] [1,16] , and target views are drawn from [ min ⁡ ( 𝒞 ) − 2 , max ⁡ ( 𝒞 ) + 2 ] ∖ 𝒞 [\min(\mathcal{C}){-}2,\max(\mathcal{C}){+}2]\setminus\mathcal{C} , where 𝒞 \mathcal{C} is the index set of the sampled context views.
We exclude from training a small subset of scenes whose aspect ratio or native resolution does not match the canonical 16 : 9 16{:}9 960 960 p layout.
The resolution curriculum starts from 340 340 p and runs for 100 100 K iterations, after which the model is finetuned for 10 10 K iterations at each of 480 480 p, 720 720 p, and the final 960 960 p ( 540 × 960 540{\times}960 ). For the controlled ablation setting used in main paper Tab. 4 – 6 and Tab. 13 , we shrink the compute footprint to 8 8 A100 GPUs by fixing the context view count to 32 32 , raising the patch size to 16 16 to lower the per-token resolution, and truncating the curriculum to 340 340 p / 480 480 p / 720 720 p with 100 100 K / 10 10 K / 10 10 K iterations respectively.
All the design knobs ablated in those tables share this controlled setup; the rendering and reconstruction efficiency reported alongside is measured on a model with patch size 8 8 to remain consistent with the final main-table configuration. A.5 Zero-shot evaluation details The in-the-wild numbers in main paper Tab. 3 are produced by the DVSM-initialized 960 960 p TTB model trained on DL3DV with patch size 8 8 , evaluated zero-shot on three out-of-distribution datasets: the 9 9 scenes of MipNeRF360 [ 3 ] , the 7 7 scenes of Free [ 66 ] , and the 6 6 stable scenes from Hike [ 39 ] as identified by [ 33 ] .

A.6 Local-duplicate inference details This section gives further details of the local-duplicate inference described in main paper
Sec. 3.3.4 , which reconstructs the scene as K K spatially-local groups of
context views at inference time, each with its own copy of the baked fast weights, so that a
single fast-weight MLP only has to absorb the views of one local group (about B B on average)
instead of all V V views. It reuses the trained model as is, with no retraining and no additional
learned parameters; the per-scene state grows to K K sets of fast weights. Grouping. Each context view i i is summarized into a 6-dimensional descriptor 𝐟 i = [ 𝐜 i ; 𝐝 i ] ∈ ℝ 6 \mathbf{f}_{i}=[\mathbf{c}_{i};\mathbf{d}_{i}]\in\mathbb{R}^{6} , where 𝐜 i ∈ ℝ 3 \mathbf{c}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3} is
the camera origin and 𝐝 i ∈ ℝ 3 \mathbf{d}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3} is the camera’s unit optical axis, both
expressed in the input world frame. The number of groups K K is an inference-time hyper-parameter; K = 1 K=1 falls back to the single-MLP model. Unless stated otherwise we set K = V / B K=V/B with a nominal
budget of B = 16 B=16 views per group, i.e. , K = 2 K=2 for V = 32 V=32 and K = 4 K=4 for V = 64 V=64 . We then run
Lloyd’s algorithm for 20 iterations with the Euclidean distance to cluster the V V descriptors into K K groups. Centroids are initialized with the descriptors of K K views whose indices are evenly
spaced over [ 0 , V − 1 ] [0,V{-}1] ; since our context views are ordered by capture time, this spreads the
initial centroids along the camera trajectory. If a centroid attracts no views during an iteration,
we leave it unchanged for that round. Group sizes are neither balanced nor capped, and K K must
stay small relative to V V . Per-group reconstruction. For every group k k we run the standard reconstruction pass of our model on its member views only.
Inside this pass, all sublayers—intra-view attention, FFN, cross-view attention, and the TTB
inner loop—see only the tokens of group k k ; in particular, the cross-view attention that
provides the baking target attends across the views of the group rather than across all V V views.
The two streams X ( ctx-gt ) X^{(\text{ctx-gt})} and X ( ctx-rend ) X^{(\text{ctx-rend})} of a context view are created
inside the same pass and are therefore always baked together. Every pass starts from the shared
meta-initialization θ 0 \theta_{0} and runs the same T T inner-loop steps, producing the per-layer fast
weights { θ k , ℓ } ℓ = 1 L \{\theta_{k,\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L} of copy k k . The K K passes are independent of each
other and are executed sequentially in our implementation. At the end of context prefilling we
cache the fast weights of every group together with the K K cluster centroids. Rendering. At rendering time, we do not re-run k-means. Each novel view computes its own 6-dimensional
descriptor in the same input world frame and takes an arg ⁡ min \arg\min of the Euclidean distance to the
cached centroids to select a single group, and all of its tokens are rendered with the fast weights
of that group. The switch between copies is hard, without blending. The per-token rendering cost is
unchanged and independent of V V ; routing only adds one nearest-centroid search over K K centroids
per view, which is negligible. In our implementation every novel view is routed and rendered
individually. Appendix B Discussion: comparison with related methods This section positions TTB within the recent landscape of feed-forward novel-view synthesis (NVS) and 3D reconstruction methods.

Figure 12: Zero-shot qualitative results on Hike. 64 context views. Appendix E Broader impacts TTB shares its broader impacts with feed-forward novel-view synthesis methods in general.
What is more specific to TTB is the methodological perspective it offers: rather than replacing attention with a test-time-trained fast-weight MLP as in prior TTT-style architectures, TTB shows that the fast-weight MLP can be supervised to collaborate with attention by baking its outputs into a constant-size state.
This baking perspective is potentially applicable beyond novel-view synthesis, in any setting where a powerful but expensive attention module needs to be amortized into a state of fixed size.
Long-context language models, where attention KV-caches grow linearly with sequence length, could similarly benefit from baking attention outputs into a fixed-capacity fast weight to enable constant-memory inference.
World models and video generators that maintain a long temporal context face the same memory-growth issue and may adopt analogous baking strategies for the temporal-attention component.
Physical 3D perception systems that operate on streaming sensor inputs, such as embodied agents and autonomous driving stacks, could likewise use a baked fast-weight state to maintain a compact running memory of the environment without unbounded KV growth.
We hope the baking perspective inspires further exploration of how attention and test-time training can be combined across these domains.
