As models scaled up, the field made two shifts away from pixel space:
The first followed latent diffusion [ 72 ] , moving generation into the latent space of a pretrained variational autoencoder (VAE) [ 48 ] , initially with one latent per frame [ 7 , 6 ] .
The second extended the compression to time, using 3D VAEs to merge every few frames into a single latent that corresponds to no individual frame [ 93 , 85 ] .
For generating short clips offline at moderate resolution, this was a favorable trade: compression made training tractable at scale, at the cost of a frozen VAE that ruled out end-to-end training and capped quality at its reconstruction [ 14 , 23 , 77 ] . Video generation models have since outgrown this setting: they now power real-time interactive world models [ 27 , 79 , 33 ] through causal few-step distillation [ 97 , 38 ] , and serve as geometry-conditioned renderers for editing and games [ 43 , 62 , 63 ] .
Yet these systems inherit the VAE’s fixed compression in space and time, which works against each new demand.
Under a real-time compute budget, doubling the resolution yields 4 × 4\times more tokens and 16 × 16\times more attention compute, while cutting tokens requires a more aggressive VAE that further reduces quality.
Precise editing requires conditions aligned to individual frames, but when several frames share one latent, fast motion blurs and objects deform (Fig. 5 ).
Temporal compression also limits responsiveness. Since common latent world models [ 97 , 38 , 92 , 27 , 79 , 37 ] autoregressively generate frames in blocks, the frame corresponding to a control cannot be generated until all controls for the full block are received. This results in a minimum control-to-screen latency proportional to the block size, a barrier that no model-side optimization can remove. These limitations motivate removing the VAE altogether and generating every frame directly as pixels, which naively would bring back the token counts and training costs that latent models avoid.
Recent pixel-space image models overcome both challenges [ 50 , 98 , 19 , 18 , 41 , 13 ] by modeling semantics over large pixel patches [ 50 ] and delegating fine details to a lightweight decoder [ 19 ] , thus keeping the token count and training cost reasonable.
Moreover, although training video models from scratch demands prohibitive data and compute resources, a pretrained latent model already captures high-level semantics and dynamics. Hence, finetuning it into a pixel space video model only requires changing the output interface, which is a more feasible task. In this work, we present PixVid , a recipe that lifts a pretrained latent video diffusion model into a frame-wise pixel-space model, in which every token covers a 1 × p × p 1\times p\times p patch of exactly one frame, so no condition or action spans multiple frames.
PixVid keeps the diffusion transformer (DiT) and replaces both the encoder and the decoder from the VAE (Fig. 2 ).
At input, a patch embedding that initializes from the VAE encoder allows the
DiT to start training from close to its pretrained latent distribution.
At output, a lightweight detailer head renders the DiT tokens into pixels; it is convolutional to suppress the patch-grid artifacts of a linear head [ 88 ] , and causal in time to allow autoregressive generation.
We further identify the following key techniques: 1) predicting clean video ( x x -prediction) rather than velocity, since velocity prediction forces the network to model full-rank per-pixel noise [ 50 ] ; 2) using a larger noise shift, since redundant pixels retain more signal at the same noise level; and 3) using the Euler sampler, since pixel color values are numerically sensitive to higher order solvers like UniPC [ 101 ] . Finetuned from Wan2.1-14B [ 85 ] , PixVid converges within 1.3 1.3 M training clips sampled from 64 64 K videos, or about 4 4 K B200 GPU-hours. It achieves competitive quality with the original latent model on VBench [ 39 ] , and the recipe transfers across Wan2.1 and Wan2.2-based models with different VAEs.
Generating frame by frame in pixels, it introduces several benefits, as shown in Fig. 1 . Resolution: doubling the patch size lets PixVid generate 960 × 1664 960\times 1664 with the same number of tokens per frame as at 480 × 832 480\times 832 . Control: with the same control branch as the latent model, depth-conditioned editing keeps 98 % 98\% of the source’s fast motion, versus 83 % 83\% for an identically finetuned latent model. Latency: after autoregressive distillation, each camera control takes effect at the next frame rather than the next block, cutting median latency from 495 495 ms to 34 34 ms while still streaming in real time. To summarize, our main contributions are as follows: • We propose PixVid , a recipe that lifts a pretrained latent video diffusion model into a frame-wise pixel-space model, converging quickly to competitive quality. • We identify the design choices that make this lift work: a causal convolutional detailer head with 9 × 9\times fewer patch seams than a linear head, x x -prediction with 80 × 80\times less pixel noise than v v -prediction, and a larger noise shift than the latent model. • We show frame-wise pixel generation overcomes three structural limitations of latent models: resolution scaling, control alignment under fast motion, and control-to-screen latency. 2 Related Work Pixel-Space Diffusion. Latent diffusion [ 72 , 68 , 7 ] has been favored for efficient training using spatial and temporal compression for video [ 93 , 85 , 8 ] , at the price of a VAE reconstruction bottleneck. Pixel-space diffusion models for images [ 29 , 78 , 22 , 46 ] and videos [ 32 , 30 , 76 , 5 ] bypass a VAE but become costly at high resolution, historically requiring cascaded or multi-resolution designs [ 31 , 74 , 71 , 26 , 20 , 81 ] . Recent methods improved pixel-space image generation, training from scratch [ 34 , 35 , 15 , 50 , 98 , 19 , 58 , 87 , 55 , 57 , 13 , 61 ] or finetuning from latent checkpoints to make training tenable [ 18 , 41 , 13 ] .
Pixel-space video generation is less explored. Concurrent works train from scratch, and they either focus on smaller class-conditioned benchmarks [ 84 ] or have undesirable patch artifacts [ 88 ] . Controllable Video Generation and Editing. Early video editors applied image diffusion models with heuristics such as cross-frame shared self-attention [ 89 , 70 , 47 , 24 ] or propagated edits [ 4 , 91 , 67 ] . Video diffusion models improve consistency and robustness, with recent models producing precise, aligned edits and structure-guided generation [ 43 , 94 , 45 , 62 , 49 , 73 , 10 , 25 ] . Interactive Video Generation. Interactive generation produces frames as a stream, typically with autoregressive video diffusion models [ 11 , 21 , 90 , 80 , 12 ] . Combined with few-step distillation [ 96 , 95 , 97 , 38 , 92 , 104 , 17 ] , latent world models respond to user actions across diverse scenes in real time [ 27 , 60 , 79 , 33 ] ,
but temporal compression delays each action, e.g . by up to three frames for Wan’s four-frame latents [ 85 ] .
Per-frame latent [ 9 , 83 ] and pixel-space [ 2 , 65 ] world models avoid this delay but are trained from scratch on a single genre. PixVid is finetuned from a general pretrained model and responds to actions at the next frame. 3 Methods Our goal is to generate every frame directly in pixels without training a pixel-space video model from scratch, while removing the VAE bottleneck.
PixVid achieves this by lifting a pretrained latent video diffusion model into a frame-wise pixel-space model, as shown in Fig. 2 .
We describe its architecture in Sec. 3.1 and how we train it from the latent model in Sec. 3.2 , including key design choices.
We then adapt it to efficient high-resolution generation in Sec. 3.3 , precise video editing in Sec. 3.4 , and low-latency autoregressive generation in Sec. 3.5 . 3.1 Architecture PixVid keeps the DiT [ 68 ] backbone of a pretrained latent video model and replaces the VAE: a linear patch embedding maps pixels to tokens, and a lightweight convolutional detailer head renders the DiT outputs directly into pixels. Frame-Wise Tokenization. Latent video models merge several frames into one latent [ 93 , 85 ] , so conditions apply to groups of frames.
PixVid instead gives each frame its own tokens, each covering a 1 × p × p 1\times p\times p pixel patch.
Conditions then align with individual frames, and an autoregressive model can respond to actions at the next frame.
Although this approach uses more tokens per clip than with temporal compression, the spatial token count does not grow with resolution as it is determined by the adjustable patch size rather than a frozen VAE. Detailer Head. Since the DiT captures global structure and leaves only local details to render, we implement the detailer as a lightweight 3D convolutional decoder, sharing this insight with pixel-space image models [ 19 , 18 , 41 ] .
Unlike their U-Net detailers, which also encode the noisy pixels of each patch, ours keeps only the decoder path and reads only the DiT tokens.
It stacks L = log 2 ⁡ p L=\log_{2}p up blocks, each upsampling by 1 × 2 × 2 1\times 2\times 2 , so the head maps each token to a 1 × p × p 1\times p\times p pixel patch and predicts the clean video.
Its convolutions also run across neighboring tokens rather than within each patch, which largely suppresses checkerboard artifacts at token boundaries.
They are causal in time, so the detailer models local temporal structure without reading future frames and supports frame-by-frame generation.
See App. A.2 for details. 3.2 Training from a Latent Model PixVid finetunes end-to-end in pixel space from a pretrained latent model, with initialized DiT and input patchification, 𝒙 \bm{x} -prediction, a larger noise shift, and auxiliary losses. A latent DiT cannot exploit this, as doubling the resolution yields 4 × 4\times more latent tokens.
PixVid instead doubles the patch size from p p to 2 ​ p 2p and adds one up block to the detailer, keeping the token grid unchanged (Fig. 3 ).
Unlike PixelDiT [ 98 ] , whose token count grows with resolution, or DiP [ 19 ] and L2P [ 18 ] , which add no detailer levels, this gives the doubled resolution its own upsampling level without much additional cost. We finetune the patch- 2 ​ p 2p model from the patch- p p model. Its patch embedding fuses 2 × 2 2\times 2 average pooling with the patch- p p embedding, so the DiT initially sees a downsampled version of its input, and the new upsampling block starts as an identity pass-through, acting as nearest-neighbor upsampling.

Bold : best; underlined : second best. 3.4 Precise Video Editing Precise video editing, such as re-rendering a video from its per-frame depth, requires the generated video to align with the condition at every frame.
Latent models typically encode the condition with the same VAE as the video [ 43 ] , so several condition frames share one latent, leading to inaccurate alignment, especially in fast motions.
In PixVid, the condition is tokenized per frame as the corresponding video, so generation and condition are aligned at the frame level, without changing the conditioning mechanism.
Concretely, we add the control branch of VACE [ 43 ] : conditions are residually added to the DiT tokens via control blocks, and the condition’s patch embedding is initialized, like the video’s, by fusing the pretrained embedding with 𝑨 \bm{A} . 3.5 Low-Latency Autoregressive Generation Interactive applications stream video in response to user controls, such as camera or motion inputs, and should react to each control as soon as it arrives.
An autoregressive model generates video in blocks, and a block can start only once the controls for all its frames have arrived.
In a temporally compressed latent model, each block spans at least one latent, i.e . , several frames, so a new control waits for the rest of its block, a delay that no throughput-side optimization can remove (Fig. 6 ).
PixVid can generate single-frame blocks, so each control takes effect at the next frame.
To achieve this, we lift a bidirectional latent model to pixel space with the recipe above and distill it into a few-step causal model with Self Forcing [ 38 ] .
The causal detailer decodes one frame at a time from cached features of previous frames, keeping its cost per frame constant.
Single-frame blocks trade some throughput for this latency gain, which we analyze in Sec. 4.5 . 4 Experiments We evaluate PixVid on text-to-video generation in Sec. 4.2 , followed by its three applications: efficient high-resolution generation in Sec. 4.3 , precise video editing in Sec. 4.4 , and low-latency autoregressive generation in Sec. 4.5 .

Color kept is mean OKLab chroma relative to an fp32 sampler state. 5 Conclusion Latent video diffusion models compress video in space and time with a VAE.
PixVid removes this compression by efficiently finetuning a pretrained latent model to generate every frame directly in pixels, reaching quality competitive with the original model.
Working frame by frame in pixels, it overcomes three structural limitations of latent models: resolution scaling, control alignment under fast motion, and control-to-screen latency. Limitations and future work. Without temporal compression, PixVid processes 3.9 × 3.9\times as many tokens per second of video as Wan2.1 at 480 × 832 480\times 832 , so short low-resolution clips cost more than with a latent model, although the gap closes at higher resolution and streaming stays faster than real time (App. B.7 ).
Generating directly in pixels also opens up training options that a frozen VAE rules out. Losses and rewards can be defined on pixels, enabling adversarial training and RL with pixel-space rewards. Because all models then share the same output space, distillation across architectures or from mixed teachers also becomes possible. Acknowledgments. A.2 Detailer Head The detailer head replaces the linear output head and the VAE decoder of the pretrained latent model, mapping DiT features directly to pixels (Fig. 11 ). Its input is the last DiT hidden state 𝒔 \bm{s} on the token grid of size H / p × W / p H/p\times W/p with width D D . We add the timestep embedding 𝒆 t \bm{e}_{t} to 𝒔 \bm{s} , apply a SiLU, and project the result with a 3 × 1 × 1 3\times 1\times 1 bottleneck convolution. A stack of Up Blocks then upsamples the features to the full resolution H × W H\times W , and a final 3 × 1 × 1 3\times 1\times 1 convolution outputs the pixel prediction 𝒙 ^ \hat{\bm{x}} .
Since the head upsamples only spatially, it keeps the temporal resolution of the token grid.
Each Up Block doubles the spatial resolution, so a 1 × 16 × 16 1\times 16\times 16 patch requires 4 4 Up Blocks, with widths C 4 , … , C 1 C_{4},\dots,C_{1} .
We use D = 1536 D=1536 and C = [ 512,256,128 , 64 ] C=[512,256,128,64] for the 1.3B model, and D = 5120 D=5120 and C = [ 1024,256 , 64 , 64 ] C=[1024,256,64,64] for the 14B model, giving detailer heads with 44 44 M and 337 337 M parameters, respectively. Up Block. Each Up Block applies a 1 × 2 × 2 1\times 2\times 2 transposed convolution to double the height and width, followed by a 3 × 3 × 3 3\times 3\times 3 causal convolution and a SiLU. The transposed convolution upsamples each frame independently, and the causal convolution mixes information spatially and across neighboring frames at the new resolution. Causal Decoding. All temporal kernels in the head have size 3 3 and are padded only on the past side, so at every layer output frame t t depends only on input frames t − 2 t-2 , t − 1 t-1 , and t t (Fig. 11 , bottom right). Therefore, the head never reads future frames and is compatible with causal, frame-by-frame generation. When decoding a stream, each convolution caches its two most recent input frames, so every new frame is decoded at a constant cost without recomputing earlier frames.
