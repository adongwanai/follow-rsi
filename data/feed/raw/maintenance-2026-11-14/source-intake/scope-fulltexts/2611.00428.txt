License: CC BY 4.0 arXiv:2611.00428v1 [cs.LG] 30 Oct 2026 Do Multimodal Models Decide Consistently on Equivalent Optical Spectra? Ziyuan Wang Affiliation: Independent Researcher Affiliation: ziyuan.wang.engineer@gmail.com Genliang Zhu Affiliation: Georgia Institute of Technology Affiliation: sebby@gatech.edu Xiaobo Chen Affiliation: University of Massachusetts Amherst Affiliation: xchen@umass.edu Abstract A spectrum can be given to a model as a table in nanometers or as a plot in photon energy, and the decision should not depend on which one it sees. When the data needed for an answer are missing, the model should say so. We test representation invariance and abstention on measured optical spectra of silicon metasurfaces (MOCLIP) and gold nanoparticles, with labels computed from the data. Each spectrum is shown as four tables (nm, μ \mu m, scientific notation, eV) and two plots (nm, eV), and partial spectra remove data inside or outside the queried range. We evaluate the decision models d3-nano and Jev 1.13, three GPT-6 Luna configurations and the Qwen3.5-2B base model. Changing only the representation moves accuracy by up to 41 percentage points within one model, mostly on photon-energy tables and plots. With the same evidence, asking in photon energy instead of wavelength helps some model–view pairs by 16–50 points and hurts others by up to 25. When the relevant extremum is removed, unadapted models rarely answer “undetermined”. LoRA fine-tuning of d3-nano improves comparisons on the trained table formats, but much of its gain on unanswerable questions comes from a shortcut: it abstains whenever the input is truncated. Training with answerable truncations avoids the shortcut in all six seeds, with a 90–100 point gap between abstention after inside and outside cuts on held-out structures; with the same amount of extra original-task data, five of six seeds keep the shortcut. On gold spectra the two training sets perform similarly. Multimodal language models used for scientific decisions should be tested on equivalent representations and on missing evidence, not only on average accuracy. We release the tasks, per-response results and analysis code. 1 Introduction An optical spectrum can be given as a numerical table or a plot. Its horizontal axis can show wavelength in nanometers or micrometers, or photon energy. The physical response is the same, but reading and interpreting these forms requires different steps. For example, a red shift means a longer wavelength and a lower photon energy. Also, the lowest point visible in a partial spectrum may not be the lowest point in the range asked about. A useful decision model should keep its answer under equivalent representations, change it when the question requires a different answer, and abstain when necessary evidence is missing. Native decision models receive a set of alternatives and return a choice with probabilities [ 3 , 18 , 26 ] . This format is convenient for automated scientific workflows. However, a valid output format and high average accuracy do not establish whether the model follows the physical meaning of the data or recognizes that part of the required evidence is missing. We test both issues using measured optical spectra and labels calculated directly from the arrays. For each original task, we vary the number format, coordinate, modality, evidence order, question direction, or data coverage. Each change has a defined expected answer. To test evidence sufficiency, we compare a complete spectrum with two partial views of the same spectrum. Removing data outside the queried interval leaves the answer available. Removing the segment containing the relevant extremum makes the question unanswerable. We then use low-rank adaptation (LoRA) [ 10 ] to examine which errors an open decision model can learn to correct. The study has three parts: • We compare tables in wavelength, micrometers, scientific notation, and photon energy, together with wavelength and energy plots. All use the same underlying arrays. We reverse the question direction to check cases where the answer should change, and vary wording while keeping the energy-coordinate evidence fixed. • We remove data inside or outside the queried range of the same spectrum. This tests whether abstention depends on which evidence is missing, rather than simply on whether the input is truncated. • We vary the representations, example count, training duration, and answerable truncations used for training. We report all seeds and include controls with a similar amount of added data and a base model with the same backbone. The largest representation effects occur in energy coordinates. Energy wording helps some model–task combinations and hurts others. Unadapted models seldom abstain when relevant evidence is missing, and most adaptation gains are limited to the representations and dataset used for training. 2 Related work Optical response prediction and inverse design. Neural models have also been used to predict spectra from nanostructure geometry. Lin et al. [14] transferred a model trained on annular aperture arrays to nanohole arrays to improve prediction with limited target data. Wang et al. [27] used a single network to predict transmission spectra for gold nanodisks, truncated cones, and nanocones. Chen et al. [5] combined transmission prediction for annular aperture arrays with a geometry search for inverse design. These studies learn mappings between structure and optical response. Our study tests decisions made from measured spectra, including whether the answer changes with the representation or when relevant evidence is missing. Equivalent representations and modality gaps. IsoBench gives the same problems as images, text, and formal notation. It finds that multimodal models generally perform better with text [ 8 ] . MaCBench reports a similar result for chemistry and materials data, including a large text advantage in reading X-ray diffraction peak positions [ 2 ] . For some table-reasoning tasks, however, table images match or outperform text formats [ 7 ] . Chart robustness studies keep the data fixed and change scale, colour, or layout [ 17 ] . We examine coordinate changes that also require a physical direction relation, such as longer wavelength and lower photon energy. We include controls where the answer should change and where only the question wording changes. Chart and spectral question answering. ChartQA and ChartQAPro test visual and logical reasoning about charts, with unanswerable questions included in the latter [ 15 , 16 ] . SpecVQA uses expert questions about seven types of scientific spectra [ 24 ] . We use fixed templates and spectra with clear extrema. This makes the comparisons easier to control, but covers a narrower range of questions than practical use. Evidence sufficiency and abstention. Whether evidence is sufficient depends on the question. Retrieval-augmented models often answer despite insufficient context. Fine-tuning on sufficiency labels can increase abstention but also reduce the number of correct answers [ 11 ] . Easily constructed unanswerable examples can teach cues that fail on harder cases [ 1 ] , a form of shortcut learning [ 9 ] . TrustDABench tests refusal when table changes remove necessary evidence, together with invariance under changes that preserve the answer, including unit conversion [ 25 ] . In our answerable control, we delete an irrelevant part of the same spectrum. We then test whether adding these examples to training changes abstention behavior. This use of controlled input changes follows the approach of behavioral testing frameworks such as CheckList [ 21 ] . Decision interfaces and option bias. Multiple-choice models can prefer particular option identifiers [ 28 ] . In typed decision models, option labels can outweigh the definitions supplied by the caller [ 4 ] . Effects of thresholds and option order also vary across systems [ 19 ] , and gains from language-side adaptation may not transfer to other settings [ 29 ] . Other work evaluates scientific semantic decisions through their downstream effects [ 6 ] . Here we test evidence order, coordinate meaning, and the queried range. We also compare one decision model with its base model. 3 Data and paired task construction 3.1 Measured optical data and deterministic labels MOCLIP contains transmission spectra from 10,000 fabricated silicon-on-glass metasurfaces measured under two polarizations [ 23 , 22 ] . Each spectrum has 29 samples from 450 to 730 nm, spaced by 10 nm. The main evaluation uses y y polarization. We define the target dip as the global minimum on this grid and do not fit a sub-grid minimum. We keep interior dips with prominence of at least 0.15. Their transmission must differ by at least 0.05 from the next local dip with prominence of at least 0.05. Band questions also require a 15 nm margin from internal band boundaries. These filters give clear labels and exclude overlapping or unresolved resonances. The second dataset contains 448 peptide-assisted gold-nanoparticle syntheses with measured UV–visible absorbance [ 13 , 12 ] . Wavelengths run from 300–995 nm in 5 nm steps. We use background-subtracted absorbance over 450–800 nm, and a clean-peak filter keeps 131 spectra. Gold peaks are often broad, so the questions ask which side of a threshold contains the maximum. The main threshold is 542.5 nm. We require at least 10 nm separation from it and a 3% peak-absorbance margin over the other side. Comparison questions require a peak shift of at least 25 nm. 3.2 Task instances, variants, and representations An original instance uses one spectrum, a pair, or three candidates. A variant changes one part of its question or evidence, and a request presents one variant in one representation (Table 1 ). MOCLIP has four task types, each with 48 original instances. Extreme asks which band contains the minimum or maximum of the same spectrum. Compare asks which of two dips lies at longer or shorter wavelength, with a minimum separation of 40 nm. We use both evidence orders and both question directions. Specification asks which of three candidates meets a dip-location window and a depth threshold. One wrong candidate is deep enough but outside the window; the other is inside but too shallow. The correct candidate rotates through A, B, and C. Sufficiency pairs a full spectrum with a partial view that removes its true dip. Long-side, short-side, and middle deletions occur equally often. Gold has 20 threshold, 16 compare, and 18 sufficiency instances. Only sufficiency questions offer “cannot be determined” as an option. Table 1: Evaluation sets. Transmission and absorbance are response ratios, so changing the horizontal coordinate does not require a spectral-density Jacobian. Answer keys are fixed, while the displayed ranges and units follow the representation. Rows in eV tables are sorted by increasing energy, which reverses the wavelength order. Compare questions normally ask about longer or shorter wavelength , even when the evidence uses energy coordinates. Additional variants on eV tables and plots ask about lower or higher photon energy . We use the same control for gold threshold questions. The evidence stays fixed, so this comparison tests the extra step of relating wavelength wording to an energy axis. 3.3 Query-relative evidence controls Every truncated view in the main sufficiency tasks is unanswerable. A model can therefore succeed by treating any missing data as a reason to abstain. The scope sets test this possibility. Each question states an interval Q Q , and each spectrum is shown in three forms: full; with samples outside Q Q removed, leaving the answer available; and with the segment inside Q Q containing the relevant extremum removed, labeled “cannot be determined” (Figure 1 ). MOCLIP uses query intervals of 450–685 or 605–730 nm. Gold uses 450–650 or 490–800 nm, with five thresholds from 522.5 to 562.5 nm. Both sets balance long-side, short-side, and middle inside cuts, with 18 MOCLIP and 20 gold instances per type. The MOCLIP scope set uses x x polarization and excludes all structures used in main testing and training. It reuses some pilot structures because the clean-dip filters limit the available pool. Gold scope and main sets share 30 spectra. They are two task constructions from one dataset, not independent replications. Figure 1: One MOCLIP scope instance in wavelength and energy coordinates. Shading marks the queried interval and is added for illustration only; model inputs state the interval in the question. 4 Models and analysis 4.1 Model configurations d3-nano is a 2.21B-parameter multimodal decision model fine-tuned from Qwen3.5-2B. We use checkpoint 80820624a817 (v3.1.0 weights) in bfloat16 and its local probability readout [ 26 ] . d3-nano-pa averages the runtime outputs for forward and reversed option orders. Jev 1.13 is a text-only decision model accessed through OpenRouter [ 3 ] . We test three GPT-6 Luna configurations through OpenRouter, with OpenAI as the only provider. These are the decision route ( openai/gpt-6-luna-decisions ; Luna Decisions ) and the generative route through Chat Completions with a strict JSON schema at reasoning effort none or low ( Luna gen. none , Luna gen. low ). The routes share a model family and provider. We cannot verify that they share checkpoints or inference implementations, so we compare the available configurations. We did not test the native OpenAI /v1/decisions endpoint [ 18 ] . As a reference with the same backbone, we test the Qwen3.5-2B base model [ 20 ] with thinking disabled. After giving lettered options, we restrict its next-token distribution to those letters (Appendix B ). API calls were made on 14–16 and 24 October 2026. Local models ran on RunPod GPUs. 4.2 Metrics and uncertainty We calculate accuracy using the canonical answer keys. Unless stated otherwise, summaries of representation, training, and interface effects use core variants. Energy-worded probes are used only in the wording analysis. We measure each representation effect as its paired accuracy difference from the nm table on the same variants. Most models change little when only the wavelength number format changes. Energy coordinates cause larger differences. Asking directly about photon energy helps some models and hurts others on the same evidence. Jev’s below-chance result on gold eV tables suggests a systematic direction error. To assess this behavior, accuracy and paired representation differences need to be considered together with controls where the correct answer changes. Agreement alone adds little because it largely follows accuracy. Evidence needed for the question. Removing samples outside the query interval leaves the answer available. Removing the relevant extremum does not. Unadapted models often answer both, while some adapted models abstain on both. A high abstention rate on unanswerable deletions alone cannot distinguish these behaviors. Similar problems have been reported in retrieval-augmented question answering [ 11 , 1 ] . In our experiments, answerable truncations remove the shortcut in every training seed. A similar amount of extra original-task data, with or without longer training, does so in only one of six seeds. Limited transfer after adaptation. LoRA improves comparison on table representations included in training. Adding answerable truncations also improves scope behavior on the source dataset, with some transfer to plots. Plot comparison remains weak, performance on an unseen task varies widely, and accuracy on a second optical dataset falls. These gains are limited to the tested templates, representations, and data. They do not establish that the model has learned a general physical rule. 7 Limitations Both datasets cover a narrow range of optical systems, and we select spectra with clear extrema. MOCLIP’s 10 nm sampling does not support claims about narrow resonances. The gold dataset contains repeated nominal recipes across batches, so instance-bootstrap intervals may understate uncertainty for a new experimental campaign. The tasks use fixed multiple-choice templates. Each scope set has two query intervals, and their boundaries are partly associated with answer bands. We vary plot style, noise, and phrasing only through the stated controls. Only sufficiency tasks offer an abstention option. The adaptation study uses one base model, a frozen vision tower, and three seeds per arm. The arms differ in training updates, and two epochs leave ftC seed 1 under-trained. The supplementary and original arms also use different GPUs, so hardware differs in the ftD–ftC comparison. We cannot verify whether service routes share the same internal model, and we did not evaluate the native OpenAI decision endpoint. API models may change over time, and local runs use different GPUs across rounds. The study developed through sequential pilots and controls; all comparisons are exploratory. 8 Conclusion For the tested models, equivalent optical data often lead to different decisions, and removing required evidence seldom leads to abstention. Paired representation tests show errors that average accuracy can hide. Testing both answerable and unanswerable partial spectra also reveals whether a model abstains simply because data are missing. Task-specific adaptation improves the cases covered by training, but transfer to other representations, tasks, and optical data remains limited. Data and code availability. Appendix A Task and execution details MOCLIP band labels are 450–605, 605–645, 645–685 and 685–730 nm, with boundaries between sampled wavelengths. Specification windows are 605–665, 625–685 and 645–705 nm; positive candidates lie at least 15 nm inside their window and location distractors at least 30 nm outside; positive and location-distractor dips are at least 0.05 below the depth threshold and shallow distractors at least 0.10 above it. Main MOCLIP contains 96 extreme, 384 compare (192 energy-worded), 144 specification and 96 sufficiency variants; main gold contains 80 threshold (40 energy-worded), 128 compare (64 energy-worded) and 36 sufficiency variants. Jev therefore receives 2,304 MOCLIP and 664 gold main requests, multimodal models 3,552 and 1,048. Dataset generation seeds are 2 (MOCLIP main), 3 (gold main), 5 and 6 (scope sets), and 10, 11 and 12 for the three training pools. LoRA targets are q_proj , k_proj , v_proj , o_proj , in_proj_qkv , in_proj_z , out_proj , gate_proj , up_proj and down_proj (15,630,336 trainable parameters). Development assignment uses the SHA-256 of the instance identifier modulo 1,000 (values below 100). Sample-matched arms draw 632 requests without replacement using the optimization seed. No model is selected by a test score. Main d3-nano inference ran on an RTX 2000 Ada and the original training rounds on L4 GPUs; the supplementary batch (Appendix E ) ran on an A40. For each model, task and representation the last successful response is used; requests that failed and were retried count once.
