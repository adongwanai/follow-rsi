However, differences in morphology, sensing, and control interfaces make it difficult to learn effectively from their combined demonstrations. We introduce EgoX, an egocentric cross-embodiment learning framework that organizes heterogeneous demonstrations through semantically corresponding observation and action modalities. the average task progress score and success rate (right, bottom). 1 Introduction Large-scale pretraining has driven rapid progress in natural language processing ( Devlin et al., 2019 ; Brown et al., 2020 ) and computer vision ( Radford et al., 2021 ; Dosovitskiy et al., 2021 ) , demonstrating the potential of learning reusable knowledge from diverse data. A corresponding ambition in robotics is to develop generalist models that accumulate manipulation knowledge across tasks and environments and can be adapted to different robot embodiments. We envision a future populated by robots with complementary physical capabilities: tabletop arms, wheeled mobile manipulators, humanoids, and quadrupeds offer different combinations of mobility, reach, and workspace access, while grippers, dexterous hands, and specialized tools support different forms of object interaction ( Pfeifer et al., 2007 ; Baines et al., 2024 ; Kirschner et al., 2025 ) . Their different ways of perceiving and interacting with the world also provide complementary experience for learning. However, collecting data and training models separately for every embodiment, task, and scene is costly and limits the reuse of this experience. How can we draw on experience across these embodiments to develop shared manipulation knowledge while allowing each robot to adapt it to its own body and environment? Several complementary data sources offer a path toward this goal. Cross-embodiment robot learning draws on demonstrations collected across different bodies, sensors, and action spaces ( O’Neill et al., 2024 ; Octo Model Team et al., 2024 ; Kim et al., 2025 ; Doshi et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2024 ; Gemini Robotics Team et al., 2025a ) . Egocentric human data captures diverse physical interactions in real-world settings beyond existing robot deployment environments ( Kareer et al., 2025 ; Niu et al., 2025 ; Qiu et al., 2025 ; Zheng et al., 2026 ) . Simulation enables scalable data collection and has supported policy pretraining, sim-to-real transfer, and co-training with real-world demonstrations ( Deshpande et al., 2026 ; NVIDIA et al., 2025 ; Maddukuri et al., 2025 ; Lin et al., 2026b ) . Together, these sources can broaden the objects, scenes, and interaction strategies available for learning beyond the demonstrations collected for any individual robot. Realizing this potential, however, requires training recipes that can make effective use of heterogeneous experience. Demonstrations from different embodiments encode interactions through distinct morphologies, viewpoints, proprioception, action semantics, and motion dynamics, making it challenging to turn their combined experience into useful manipulation capabilities across robots. Recent work has examined scaling laws and data composition for robot manipulation ( Lin et al., 2025 ; Lin et al., 2026a ; TRI LBM Team et al., 2025 ) . Alongside these questions, we focus on how experience from heterogeneous embodiments should be collected, represented, and used for shared learning. Our central concern is the design of representations and learning interfaces that allow diverse demonstrations to contribute to a common manipulation model while preserving the differences that matter for each embodiment’s sensing and control. We treat humans, real robots, and simulated agents as embodiments on equal footing, each contributing its own observations and actions. Through joint pretraining and adaptation to target embodiments, we investigate how this shared experience can improve downstream manipulation across different robot bodies and end-effectors. Fig. 2 illustrates this perspective through corresponding stages of Kitchen-Bread performed by human, real-robot, and simulated embodiments. We introduce EgoX (Fig. 1 ), an egocentric cross-embodiment learning framework built around a single methodological premise: transfer should occur through semantically corresponding modalities, while sensing and control interfaces remain embodiment-specific. The framework has three components. First, EgoX organizes human, real-robot, and simulated demonstrations in a unified observation-action representation, grouping heterogeneous signals into anatomically or functionally relevant modalities and expressing poses in a unified egocentric frame system.
Second, MXT+, a Modularized Cross-Embodiment Transformer, adopts a fine-grained modular design: within each embodiment, each observation modality has its own tokenizer and each action modality its own expert, while a shared Transformer trunk integrates information across modalities and embodiments.
Third, MXT+ uses embodiment dreaming , an auxiliary objective that predicts future embodiment latents from the current shared representation. This training-only branch encourages the trunk to encode how each embodiment’s visual and proprioceptive state evolves, without requiring pixel reconstruction or deployment-time computation. We evaluate EgoX on five manipulation tasks, from long-horizon loco-manipulation to precise insertion and tool use ( Kitchen-Bread , Kitchen-Cleanup , Sort , Insert , and Scoop ), across six robot embodiments: G1-Dex5 , xArm7-Dex4 , G1-Sim-Dex3 , LocoMan-Dex2 , R1Lite-Gripper , Z1-Tool . Human demonstrations provide an additional pretraining source. Our experiments compare MXT+ variants with baseline policies across all tasks and ablate the pretraining mixture, embodiment dreaming objective, and MXT+’s modular design. Cross-embodiment pretraining raises MXT+’s average task progress from 0.63 to 0.80, yielding the highest average score among the evaluated methods and the best performance on 12 of 13 task–embodiment pairs. The gains from pretraining are particularly pronounced under out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions, where average task progress increases from 0.53 to 0.77. Broader pretraining mixtures transfer better, and embodiment dreaming raises task progress from 0.63 to 0.78 across seven settings while making the trunk’s latent geometry more similar across embodiments.
In summary, our contributions are: 1. A unified, modality-structured representation for egocentric cross-embodiment manipulation. EgoX organizes human, real-robot, and simulated demonstrations into a common observation–action structure based on anatomically or functionally corresponding modalities, while retaining embodiment-specific sensing and control interfaces. 2. MXT+, a modular cross-embodiment Transformer with embodiment dreaming. Within each embodiment, MXT+ assigns dedicated tokenizers and action experts to individual observation and action modalities, connected through a shared Transformer trunk. Embodiment dreaming encourages the trunk to model each embodiment’s transition dynamics through future-latent prediction used only during training. 3. A systematic study of cross-embodiment transfer. Experiments across five tasks and six diverse robot embodiments demonstrate the benefits of cross-embodiment pretraining, particularly under OOD conditions. Ablations show benefits from broader pretraining mixtures, modular interfaces, and embodiment dreaming, with dreaming also producing more similar latent geometry across embodiments. Soft spotlights highlight the active interaction regions. 2 Related Work Cross-Embodiment Learning for Robotic Manipulation. Cross-embodiment robot learning aims to train policies that benefit from data collected on different bodies, sensors, and control interfaces. Large robot datasets and generalist policies demonstrate the value of scaling policy learning across tasks and robot embodiments ( O’Neill et al., 2024 ; Kim et al., 2025 ; Black et al., 2025b ; Black et al., 2025a ; Gemini Robotics Team et al., 2025b ) , and recent work extends such pretraining to include human data ( Luo et al., 2026a ; NVIDIA et al., 2025 ) . Prior methods handle embodiment heterogeneity with shared-trunk policies that use embodiment-specific tokenizers or action heads ( Doshi et al., 2025 ; Niu et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2024 ) , with a single embodiment-conditioned action head ( Li et al., 2026b ) , or with image editing that aligns robot appearance across embodiments ( Chen et al., 2024 ; Lepert et al., 2025 ) .
Existing methods either cover a relatively narrow range of embodiments and end-effectors or preserve heterogeneity by giving each embodiment a separate interface, leaving the extent of modality-level sharing unclear. In contrast, EgoX covers highly heterogeneous embodiments and end-effectors: it is evaluated on humanoid, wheeled bimanual, tabletop-arm, quadrupedal, and simulated platforms, with three- to five-fingered dexterous hands, two-finger and parallel-jaw grippers, and a tool. MXT+ combines embodiment-specific interfaces with modality-level sharing through modular tokenizers and action experts, and adds embodiment dreaming as a training signal through the prediction of future embodiment latents. Robot Learning from Egocentric Data. Egocentric human data offers a scalable source of manipulation experience spanning diverse tasks, objects, and environments. Recent advances in wearable devices, such as XR headsets and smart glasses, have improved the quality and scale of such data ( Chen et al., 2025 ; Hoque et al., 2026 ; Punamiya et al., 2026 ) .
Human videos have been used to learn transferable priors through visual, affordance, and latent-action objectives ( Nair et al., 2023 ; Srirama et al., 2024 ; Ye et al., 2025 ; Bu et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2026 ) . Other methods use human demonstrations directly for policy learning, through domain adaptation ( Punamiya et al., 2025 ) , pretraining or co-training ( Kareer et al., 2025 ; Yuan et al., 2025 ; Yang et al., 2025 ; Zheng et al., 2026 ; Shi et al., 2026 ; Zhu et al., 2026 ; Bi et al., 2026 ; Wei et al., 2026a ) , and transfer without additional target-robot demonstrations ( Xu et al., 2026 ) .
Much of this literature emphasizes transferring human experience to target robot embodiments, while EgoX focuses on collecting and jointly learning from egocentric demonstrations across human and heterogeneous robot embodiments, and investigates how this shared training benefits manipulation across diverse platforms. World Action Models and Embodiment Modeling. World models learn to predict how the world evolves under actions ( Assran et al., 2025 ; Goswami et al., 2025 ; Gao et al., 2026 ) .
World action models couple such prediction with policy learning by jointly predicting actions and future observations, with targets including pixels, pretrained visual features, and 3D motion flow ( Li et al., 2026a ) , embeddings from frozen or separately pretrained encoders ( Zheng et al., 2025 ; Luo et al., 2026b ) , and future tactile signals or tactile latents ( Niu et al., 2026 ; Lou et al., 2026 ) .
These works show that predicting future observations provides a useful training signal for policy learning.
Other methods model the embodiment itself, either by predicting its dynamics or kinematics ( Fu et al., 2025 ; Patel & Song, 2025 ) , or by encoding its structure through body graphs, canonical morphology representations, or embodiment-specific functional components in action generation ( Sferrazza et al., 2025 ; Wei et al., 2026b ; Li et al., 2026b ) .
EgoX combines the ingredients of world action models and embodiment modeling through embodiment dreaming: alongside actions, MXT+ predicts future embodiment latents from egocentric visual features, robot joint states, and end-effector joint states using exponential-moving-average copies of its own modality encoders. alongside a close-up of each end-effector and its end-effector-link keypoints. 3 EgoX: Egocentric Cross-Embodiment Manipulation EgoX makes three key design decisions to enable effective learning across diverse embodiments. First, we organize heterogeneous data into a shared modality structure, with coordinate conventions chosen for each modality (Sec. 3.1 ). To handle diverse sensing and control interfaces with different robots while facilitating positive transfer, EgoX leverages MXT+ (Fig. 3 ), where embodiment-specific tokenizers encode each observation modality for a shared Transformer backbone, and modular action experts decode its outputs into each action modality (Sec. 3.2 ). Finally, the auxiliary objective of embodiment dreaming encourages the Transformer to learn each embodiment’s dynamics (Sec. 3.3 ).
EgoX uses a two-stage training pipeline: pretraining on mixed cross-embodiment data, and fine-tuning on the target embodiment task (Sec. 3.4 ). 3.1 Egocentric Cross-Embodiment Data Representation Although embodiments differ substantially as whole bodies, many of their parts play corresponding roles. We exploit this by decomposing each embodiment into anatomically or functionally corresponding modalities and organizing their observations and actions in a shared structure.

We evaluate policies on 13 task–embodiment pairs spanning five manipulation tasks and six robot embodiments, covering long-horizon loco-manipulation, semantic object selection, precise alignment, tool use, and contact-rich interaction.
In Kitchen-Bread (Sauce Spreading), the robot retrieves sauce containers from a fridge or tabletop, picks up a spreading tool, dips it into the sauce, and spreads the sauce onto bread.
In Kitchen-Cleanup (Tool Organization), the robot picks up tools from a tabletop and places them into a container.
In Sort (Block Sorting), the robot sorts blocks into containers of the corresponding colors.
In Insert (T-shaped Block Insertion), the robot picks up a T-shaped block, aligns it with its base, and inserts it.
In Scoop (Cat Litter Scooping), the robot scoops cat litter from a litter box into a disposal bin, then returns the scoop to the edge of the box.
Figs. 13 – 17 in the appendix show example rollouts of each task across the evaluated embodiments. Embodiments. Our evaluation covers six robot embodiments (five physical robots and one simulated humanoid), spanning diverse morphologies, mobility configurations, end-effectors, and unimanual or bimanual operation. G1-Dex5 is a bimanual Unitree G1 humanoid equipped with five-finger Inspire RH56F1 dexterous hands. xArm7-Dex4 is a tabletop xArm7 equipped with a four-finger LEAP Hand ( Shaw et al., 2023 ) ; G1-Sim-Dex3 is a simulated Unitree G1 equipped with three-finger Dex3-1 dexterous hands. LocoMan-Dex2 ( Lin et al., 2024 ) is a quadruped with two-finger grippers mounted on its front legs, evaluated in unimanual mode. R1Lite-Gripper is a Galaxea R1 Lite wheeled bimanual mobile manipulator with parallel-jaw grippers. Z1-Tool is a tabletop Unitree Z1 arm with a fixed, non-actuated scoop. Egocentric human demonstrations ( Human-Dex5 ) provide an additional embodiment for cross-embodiment pretraining. Together, these platforms span dexterous hands, grippers, and fixed tools across diverse robot morphologies and mobility configurations. Baselines and training settings. We compare MXT+ with ACT ( Zhao et al., 2023 ) and π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} ( Black et al., 2025a ) , and evaluate MXT+ both with and without cross-embodiment pretraining. ACT is trained on demonstrations from the target embodiment and task, while π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} is fine-tuned from its pretrained checkpoint on the same demonstrations (Appendix A.4 ). MXT+ without pretraining is trained from scratch on these target-task demonstrations. MXT+ with pretraining is first trained on a mixture of human, physical-robot, and simulated demonstrations, excluding demonstrations of the target task on the target embodiment, and then fine-tuned on those held-out demonstrations following Section 3.4 . Both MXT+ variants use the same downstream policy architecture and training objective, including embodiment dreaming , to assess the benefit of cross-embodiment pretraining. We additionally ablate the pretraining composition, embodiment dreaming , and modality-level modularity. Evaluation settings and metrics. For each task–embodiment pair, we assess policies under in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions, defined relative to the target-task demonstrations of the evaluated embodiment. ID settings use similar objects, initial configurations, and environmental conditions to those in these demonstrations, while OOD settings introduce held-out variations in object instances, appearances, poses, or scene configurations. Similar conditions may appear in other embodiments’ pretraining demonstrations, allowing us to assess cross-embodiment transfer. For each method, we run 20 trials (13 ID and 7 OOD) per real-world task–embodiment pair and 50 trials (32 ID and 18 OOD) per simulated pair, using the same trial configurations across methods. We report the mean normalized task progress score separately for ID and OOD conditions. Each trial’s score credits intermediate stages and adds a one-point bonus for successful completion, normalized by the task’s maximum score including the bonus. Task-specific evaluation and scoring criteria are detailed in Appendix A.9 ; success rates on the same trials are reported in Appendix A.10 . 4.2 Experimental Evaluation Our evaluation aims to address the following questions:
(1) Does egocentric cross-embodiment pretraining improve downstream manipulation compared to training from scratch on target-embodiment data? (2) How does pretraining composition affect transfer? (3) Does embodiment dreaming improve cross-embodiment learning?
(4) Does modular design enhance cross-embodiment transfer?
(5) How do cross-embodiment pretraining and embodiment dreaming affect latent geometry across embodiments? Figure 5: Manipulation performance across tasks and embodiments. Normalized task progress of ACT, π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} , MXT+ without pretraining, and MXT+ with cross-embodiment pretraining across 13 task–embodiment pairs spanning five tasks and six embodiments (five physical robots and the simulated G1-Sim-Dex3 ). Each panel reports ID and OOD performance. Does egocentric cross-embodiment pretraining outperform training from scratch? Cross-embodiment pretraining raises MXT+’s mean normalized task progress from 0.63 to 0.80 across the 13 task–embodiment pairs (Fig. 5 ), pooling ID and OOD trials within each pair before averaging across pairs. Improvements occur on 11 of 13 pairs under ID evaluation and all 13 pairs under OOD evaluation. The gains are particularly pronounced under OOD conditions, where mean task progress increases from 0.53 to 0.77, with large improvements on G1-Dex5 Insert and Z1-Tool Scoop . Among all evaluated methods, pretrained MXT+ achieves the highest average task progress across the 13 task–embodiment pairs and ranks first on 12 of them. These results suggest that learning from heterogeneous human and robot experience improves downstream manipulation, particularly under conditions absent from the target-task demonstrations of the evaluated embodiment. How does pretraining composition affect transfer? We next study how the composition of the pretraining mixture affects downstream performance. As shown in Fig. 7 , we compare four settings: no pretraining, human-only pretraining, human plus other-robot pretraining, and full cross-embodiment pretraining. Human-only pretraining uses egocentric human demonstrations; human plus other-robot pretraining additionally incorporates demonstrations from robot embodiments other than the target robot; and full cross-embodiment pretraining further includes demonstrations from the target embodiment on non-target tasks. All variants use the same target-embodiment, target-task demonstrations for downstream training. We evaluate this ablation across both real-robot and simulation settings, covering Sort on R1Lite-Gripper , Sort and Insert on G1-Dex5 and xArm7-Dex4 , as well as Kitchen-Bread on simulated G1-Sim-Dex3 . Every pretraining mixture improves average OOD performance over no pretraining, and the average gains grow as the mixture broadens. Human-only pretraining already improves OOD performance on five of six pairs, most strongly on G1-Dex5 Insert , suggesting that egocentric human demonstrations can provide transferable manipulation priors despite the embodiment gap. Adding other robots’ demonstrations raises ID performance on five of six pairs, and adding the target robot’s demonstrations on other tasks improves OOD performance on every pair. Full cross-embodiment pretraining achieves the highest average task progress, improving over no pretraining by 0.12 on ID and 0.23 on OOD. These results show that combining human demonstrations, other robots’ experience, and the target robot’s other tasks improves downstream transfer. Does embodiment dreaming improve cross-embodiment learning? We compare MXT+ with MXT, which removes the embodiment-dreaming components (the embodiment tokenizer, dreaming expert, and EMA teachers) during both pretraining and fine-tuning (Appendix A.3 ). MXT+ improves average task progress from 0.63 to 0.78 across seven task–embodiment settings spanning five embodiments (Fig. 7 ). These settings include Scoop on four embodiments ( G1-Dex5 , xArm7-Dex4 , LocoMan-Dex2 , and Z1-Tool ), Insert on G1-Dex5 , and both Kitchen-Bread and Kitchen-Cleanup on G1-Sim-Dex3 . Task progress improves in all seven settings, with the largest gain on G1-Dex5 Scoop , supporting the benefit of the embodiment-dreaming components for downstream manipulation. Does modular design enhance cross-embodiment transfer? Figure 8: Coarse ablation. Arrows go from w/o to w/ pretraining; violins show each mean’s uncertainty (from its SEM). We compare MXT+ with a coarse variant that uses one tokenizer for all camera views, one for concatenated pose and proprioceptive streams, and one action expert for all action modalities within each embodiment. The image encoders, shared trunk, embodiment dreaming, training objective, and input/output token budgets remain unchanged. Fig. 8 evaluates both variants with and without pretraining on Z1-Tool Scoop , xArm7-Dex4 Scoop , and G1-Sim-Dex3 Kitchen-Bread and Kitchen-Cleanup . These embodiments span progressively richer modality sets, from fixed-tool manipulation to dexterous-hand control and bimanual loco-manipulation with multiple camera views. After pretraining, modular MXT+ matches or exceeds the coarse variant in every ID and OOD setting. Both variants benefit from pretraining on Z1-Tool . However, pretraining reduces the coarse variant’s performance on xArm7-Dex4 Scoop and both simulated kitchen tasks, whereas modular MXT+ improves in most settings and avoids these large drops. These failures also occur on single-camera xArm7-Dex4 Scoop , without additional wrist views. On Kitchen-Cleanup , the pretrained coarse variant achieves zero task progress, while modular MXT+ retains substantial performance. This task starts with locomotion, making its 3D velocity command critical despite its small share of the 51-dimensional action space. Dedicated action experts may help preserve this modality. Separate camera tokenizers may also help when wrist views are introduced during fine-tuning: MXT+ retains its head-camera tokenizer and adds wrist-camera tokenizers, whereas the coarse variant jointly attends to all views. After pretraining, the performance gap between modular and coarse policies is small on Z1-Tool but substantially larger on xArm7-Dex4 and G1-Sim-Dex3 , which involve richer observation and action modalities. Together, these results support modality-level modular design for effective cross-embodiment transfer, particularly for embodiments with diverse observation and action modalities. How do cross-embodiment pretraining and embodiment dreaming affect latent geometry across embodiments? Figure 9: GW change across embodiment pairs. Pretraining : coral, lower triangle; Embodiment dreaming : purple, upper triangle. To assess pretraining, we compare MXT+ with and without cross-embodiment pretraining. To assess the embodiment-dreaming components, we compare MXT+ with MXT, both pretrained and fine-tuned. For each comparison, we compute Gromov–Wasserstein (GW) distances between embodiment-specific distributions of shared-trunk latent representations extracted from Scoop demonstrations. Within each embodiment, we use matched demonstration samples across conditions, encoded by the corresponding checkpoints. For each sampled time step, we extract the right wrist-pose tokens from the shared-trunk output, before the action experts, and mean-pool them into one 512-dimensional latent vector. We use the right wrist pose because it is the most important action modality for Scoop , where the right end-effector is the only active end-effector shared across embodiments. GW compares the internal distance structures of these distributions using median-normalized within-distribution Euclidean distances in the original latent space. Lower GW distances indicate greater geometric similarity; we report percentage changes relative to the baseline in each comparison. To summarize each comparison, we first average GW over all 10 embodiment pairs for the baseline and for the compared model, and then report the percentage change from the baseline average to the compared model’s average, rather than averaging the per-pair changes. Fig. 9 shows that cross-embodiment pretraining lowers GW for 9 of 10 embodiment pairs and reduces mean GW across the pairs by 21.30%. In the embodiment-dreaming comparison, MXT+ yields lower GW than MXT for all 10 pairs, reducing mean GW across the pairs by 38.52%. Both comparisons therefore show greater average geometric similarity among embodiment-specific latent representations of the same manipulation task. 5 Conclusion We presented EgoX, a framework for collecting and learning from egocentric human, physical-robot, and simulated demonstrations through a shared modality structure. Treating these sources as embodiments on equal footing, EgoX pretrains on their combined experience and fine-tunes on each target embodiment and task. Its model, MXT+, connects each embodiment’s modality-specific tokenizers and action experts through a shared Transformer trunk. Embodiment dreaming complements action supervision by predicting latent representations of future visual and proprioceptive observations, encouraging the trunk to capture how each embodiment’s state evolves during interaction. Across 13 task–embodiment pairs spanning five tasks and six robot embodiments, cross-embodiment pretraining improves downstream manipulation, particularly under OOD conditions. Broader pretraining mixtures improve transfer, and embodiment dreaming further improves task performance and yields more similar cross-embodiment latent geometry on Scoop . Modality-level modularity is particularly beneficial for embodiments with diverse observation and action modalities, helping avoid the large performance drops observed when modalities are merged in the coarse variant. These findings support learning shared manipulation knowledge while preserving embodiment-specific sensing and control interfaces. Several limitations merit further investigation. First, the demonstrations used in our cross-embodiment experiments were collected by our team, limiting the available data scale; performance on substantially larger training datasets remains untested. Second, our simulation experiments cover one humanoid embodiment and two kitchen tasks, limiting the diversity of simulated experience. Third, extending EgoX to new embodiments still requires manually specifying modality correspondences and coordinate transforms and integrating embodiment-specific control interfaces. Finally, data availability varies substantially across embodiments, yet pretraining samples an equal-sized mini-batch from each embodiment. Samples from smaller datasets, such as LocoMan-Dex2 , are consequently reused more frequently, which may increase overfitting and negatively affect shared pretraining. Larger and more balanced datasets, broader simulation coverage, automated embodiment integration, and sampling strategies that account for data availability and diversity are important directions for future work. across embodiments. fine-tuning dataset, i.e., the demonstrations of the target task on the target
embodiment, which are excluded from pretraining
(Appendix A.3 ). In-distribution (ID) trials use
settings drawn from the same distribution as the fine-tuning demonstrations, including the same objects and receptacles and the same range of initial object placements. Out-of-distribution (OOD) trials use configurations that are unseen in the fine-tuning dataset, such as
new object shapes, sizes, and colors, a different receptacle, sauce, or plate,
or new initial object placement, while the task goal and the stage scoring remain
unchanged. The ID and OOD configurations of each pair are given after the

simulation. OOD evaluation tests cross-embodiment transfer. OOD configurations are absent from the target task–embodiment fine-tuning data. Some occur in the cross-embodiment pretraining mixture: in the
demonstrations of other embodiments, most notably the human demonstrations,
or in demonstrations of other tasks. A policy trained only on the
target-embodiment data, such as ACT or MXT+ without pretraining, has therefore
never observed these configurations, whereas a pretrained policy may have
observed them while they were handled by a human or another robot. The OOD
evaluation is designed to measure exactly this case: whether experience
with a configuration on other embodiments, especially humans, transfers to a
target robot that has never encountered it . This is the setting that
cross-embodiment pretraining targets, and OOD results should be read as a test
of cross-embodiment transfer rather than of generalization to configurations

We simulate G1-Sim-Dex3 , a Unitree G1 humanoid with three-finger Dex3 hands, in a kitchen environment built in Isaac Sim and Isaac Lab. In Kitchen-Bread , the robot retrieves a sauce can from the refrigerator, carries it to the worktop, and spreads its contents onto bread; the shorter Kitchen-Cleanup task requires picking up the spreader and placing it in a container. Objects interact through rigid-body contact, while sauce transfer is rendered visually without fluid simulation. Demonstrations are collected by teleoperation with PICO controllers, and the egocentric head-camera and wrist-camera images are rendered offline with RTX. Physics runs at 200 ​ Hz 200\,\mathrm{Hz} and the locomotion controller at 50 ​ Hz 50\,\mathrm{Hz} . Randomization. At reset, we randomize the robot and object positions, object sizes, aisle width, and worktop height, keep the spreader supported by the plate, and check each sampled layout for clearance. We also vary the bread shape and the positions of background objects and furniture. During offline rendering, we randomize object and room materials, lighting, camera poses and fields of view, and image properties such as color, noise, blur, and compression. These visual changes leave the recorded motions and action labels unchanged. Each rendered episode uses a fixed visual configuration, with noise varying across frames, and the dataset also retains variants close to the original appearance. Fig. 19 shows the object appearances, including those used only in OOD trials. Evaluation. Each task is evaluated on 50 fixed trials: 32 ID and 18 OOD. OOD trials use object and scene appearances excluded from the demonstrations; Kitchen-Bread OOD trials also include plate–spreader arrangements outside the demonstrated range. Trial configurations were refined through pilot evaluations and then fixed for all methods. Kitchen-Bread and Kitchen-Cleanup trials last 90 and 45 simulated seconds, respectively. Actions are executed at 30 ​ Hz 30\,\mathrm{Hz} . MXT+ and ACT execute 45 actions before each new prediction, while π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} uses RTC (Appendix A.4 ). Kitchen-Bread success requires spreading sauce onto the bread and, at the end of the trial, a released spreader settled on a support. Kitchen-Cleanup success requires grasping the spreader and placing it inside the container, released and supported, at any point during the trial. We score nine prerequisite-dependent stages for Kitchen-Bread and four for Kitchen-Cleanup , plus one point for success, giving maximum scores of 10 and 5; normalizing by these maxima gives the task progress score reported in Sec. 4 .
