frame, keeping object positions comparable. The registration pipelines it relies on are selected
on feature-matching benchmarks in which only the camera moves. Over a busy intersection, vehicles
moving together supply mutually consistent correspondences that can draw the robust homography
estimate toward their motion: consensus capture, which inlier statistics cannot flag. A two-part
benchmark measures it and the object masking meant to prevent it. A static protocol
scores ten pipelines under exactly known homographies on 4K aerial frames, tuned on a
site-disjoint split at a matched resolution and feature budget. A dynamic protocol scores them at an offline and a
real-time budget on hovers selected from the video alone to span the amount and
coherence of moving foreground, plus a stationary control. It measures background alignment, object
position error and the share of a moving group’s displacement an estimate follows, against a
pseudo-ground-truth reference audited with manually annotated ground control points, which also
score every candidate. On static scenes, tuned AKAZE, RootSIFT, SIFT and BRISK lead, retuning the
matcher thresholds raises the mean average accuracy (mAA) by at most 0.0062 0.0062 , and masking costs
less than 0.005 0.005 . Unmasked ORB is captured on 28 28 of 304 304 real-time dynamic pairs
and on 6 6 offline; its real-time captured estimates have a median corner error
of 18.8 px 18.8\text{\,}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{x} yet typical inlier counts and ratios. Masking removes capture for every pipeline and
leaves the leading pipelines unchanged. Code, data and results are released. Keywords: UAV video stabilization , image registration , homography estimation , feature matching , oriented bounding box , traffic monitoring 1 Introduction Reference-frame stabilization is the registration model used when object positions must stay
consistent over a long hover, during which wind keeps shifting and rotating the view: every frame is aligned directly onto one
fixed reference frame, so that error does not accumulate and a position measured in frame k k is
comparable with one measured minutes earlier. Trajectory extraction from high-altitude UAV ( UAV ) video is the application studied here, and traffic monitoring supplies the footage, but
the requirement is shared by any analysis that compares moving objects across time in image or map
coordinates, such as river surface velocimetry ( Ljubičić et al., 2021a ) . The estimate is a homography, and the pipeline that produces it (feature detector,
descriptor, matcher, robust estimator, and, where objects are annotated, a masking strategy that
withholds them from feature detection) is assembled from components each of which has been
benchmarked extensively ( Jin et al., 2021a ; Efe et al., 2021a ; Barath et al., 2023a ) . Those benchmarks evaluate correspondence accuracy on scenes without independently moving content:
planar surfaces, landmark facades, building walls. The only source of disagreement between their
two views is the camera. A hovering drone over a busy intersection presents the opposite case. The
camera barely moves, and dozens of objects in the frame move without it, many of them together: a
platoon released by a green light translates as a unit, and every vehicle in it supplies
correspondences that agree with one another under a single transform, the one describing their
motion. If those correspondences win the consensus, the robust estimator does not fail. It solves
the problem it was posed, returning a homography that is internally consistent and well supported
but describes the vehicles rather than the road ( Fig. 1 ). Figure 1: Consensus capture on real footage. Unmasked ORB at the real-time budget (left) registers

with the stored parameters and seed of the real-time campaign ( Section 7.3 ). We call this failure consensus capture , and it has a consequence for evaluation before it
has one for method design. Inlier count, inlier ratio, and reprojection error measure the
self-consistency of a fit, and a coherently moving foreground is self-consistent by construction.
Where it wins, these quantities are no longer merely weak proxies for accuracy, as they are already
known to be on static scenes ( Jin et al., 2021a ; Barath et al., 2023a ) ; they become
anti-informative, because the moving-object consensus can be the cleaner of the two available
fits. An evaluation that reads them cannot detect capture. What is needed scores the alignment of
the static world directly, makes a moving-object consensus observable, and relates it to how much
of the frame moves and how coherently. To our knowledge no matching or registration benchmark provides
this: none combines
independently moving content with reference geometry ( Section 2 ), so a risk flagged for
airborne video ( Kirchhof & Stilla, 2006a ) has gone unmeasured. Capture matters most where trajectories are used one at a time. Macroscopic quantities such as flow
and density average a transient offset away, but microscopic analyses read individual trajectories
and their derivatives: car following, lane changes ( Kim et al., 2026a ) , per-vehicle noise
emission ( Espadaler-Clapés et al., 2023a ) , and surrogate safety measures such as
time-to-collision, post-encroachment time and the deceleration rate to avoid a
crash ( Abdel-Aty et al., 2023a ; Espadaler-Clapés et al., 2025a ) , which drone trajectory
datasets now provide or are built to support ( Zheng et al., 2024a ; Fonod et al., 2025b ) .
Under reference-frame registration each frame is registered on its own, so nothing ties a captured estimate to those of neighboring frames: its error
follows the group’s motion and is present only while the group holds the consensus. Speeds and
accelerations are differences of positions over fractions of a second, so an error that varies from
frame to frame in this way is amplified in them ( Punzo et al., 2011a ) . The accuracy these
datasets aim for is correspondingly tight: the inD dataset requires a positioning error below 0.1 m 0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{m} ( Bock et al., 2020a ) , under two pixels at the GSD ( GSD ) of the footage studied here
( Section 3 ), whereas the captured estimates of Section 7.3 have a median
corner error of 18.8 px 18.8\text{\,}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{x} , about a meter on the ground, against the reference estimator. Since
the estimates pulled toward the group keep typical inlier statistics, a pipeline captured on a few
frames passes their error, unflagged, to every trajectory in those frames. Several of these datasets are also recorded lower
than the 140 m to 150 m 140\text{\,}\mathrm{m}150\text{\,}\mathrm{m} studied here: inD from up to 100 m 100\text{\,}\mathrm{m} at about 4 cm 4\text{\,}\mathrm{cm} per pixel ( Bock et al., 2020a ) and the intersections of CitySim from 120 m 120\text{\,}\mathrm{m} ( Zheng et al., 2024a ) ; a view that covers less ground can give a vehicle group
of the same size a larger share of the frame and of its features. We provide such an evaluation in two parts. Part I is a static protocol under exactly known
homographies and photometric degradation, on 4K frames from a multi-drone experiment over Songdo,
South Korea: one from each of the 29 29 viewpoints from which its ten drones recorded 20 20 intersections. It establishes what each pipeline can do when nothing moves and which of its
settings matter, extending the campaign of Fonod et al. (2025b) with paired trials,
per-pipeline tuning on a site-disjoint split, and a matched resolution and feature budget. Part II is a dynamic
protocol on nine hovers over busy intersections, each registered onto a reference frame selected
from the video alone to span the amount and coherence of
moving foreground, plus a stationary control that separates the removal of moving objects from the removal
of texture. It runs every pipeline at an offline and a real-time point of Part I’s budget grid.
Real footage has no true homography, so Part II measures background alignment outside the annotated
objects, the position error a trajectory inherits, the direction of the error relative to the
dominant motion, and the share of a moving group’s displacement an estimate follows, which
measures capture where it happens. Its pseudo-ground-truth reference estimator is audited against
manually annotated GCP (image positions of static ground points, not surveyed coordinates),
which also score every candidate directly, so that the
masking comparison does not depend on the reference estimator’s own masking. Masking with HBB and OBB is a controlled axis throughout, since the shape of a mask could
matter in either direction ( Section 5 ). On hovers over Songdo intersections, the measurement finds capture. At the real-time budget,
unmasked ORB is captured in all five cases that present a coherently moving group, the four with
much moving foreground and the stationary control, on 28 28 of the 304 304 pairs that carry
one, though 22 22 of them in a single case. The 17 17 within the first second are pulls toward
the group; 10 10 of the 11 11 beyond it are breakdowns of the estimate, which unmasked ORB also
shows with no group in view. At the offline budget capture is rarer (unmasked ORB, 6 6 pairs in
two cases). Estimates pulled toward a group cannot be told apart by their inlier statistics: their
inlier counts and ratios match those of the same pipeline at the nearest offsets of the same case,
at both budgets. Masking removes capture for every pipeline at both budgets while leaving
the leading pipelines, SIFT, RootSIFT and AKAZE, where they were; for real-time ORB, whose feature
budget it also returns to the ground, the captured pairs can account for at most a quarter of the gain. HBB and OBB do not differ at these densities and orientations, where the two geometries
withhold nearly the same pixels.
Averaged over all nine cases, four of which never present a moving group, the masking benefit is
either not significant or smaller than the repeatability of the GCP ( Section 7.5 ). The conclusions are bounded by the
moving share of these cases; the protocol is not, and it applies unchanged to footage from lower
altitude or over denser traffic, where that share is larger. 1.1 Contributions 1. A benchmark for the complete registration pipeline of reference-based UAV stabilization in
dynamic urban scenes: a static protocol under exactly known homographies
( Section 6 ) and a dynamic protocol on real hovering-drone footage at an offline and a
real-time compute budget ( Section 7 ). It covers the pipelines a deployed
stabilization library exposes, as a measurement of that system rather than a leaderboard
of state-of-the-art matchers ( Section 3.2 ). 2. An evaluation methodology for dynamic scenes, where self-consistency statistics are
unreliable: background-alignment measures that need no ground-truth homography,
object-level errors that bound the position error a trajectory inherits, two measures that
make consensus capture observable (the direction of the error and the share of a moving
group’s displacement an estimate follows), and a GCP audit that measures the error of the
pseudo-ground truth and supplies an independent comparison ( Section 4 ), under a
pre-specified analysis plan ( Section 4.9 ). 3. A case-selection procedure that measures from the video alone how much of the foreground
moves, how coherently, and when, and chooses the cases and their reference frames to span
these conditions, under which capture can arise; its
measurements are validated against published vehicle trajectories
( Section 7.1 ). 4. A measurement of consensus capture and of object-aware masking on real footage: at the
real-time budget unmasked ORB is captured in every case that presents a coherent group, its
pull toward the group is invisible to inlier statistics, and capture disappears under
masking at detection ( Sections 7.3 and 7.5 ). 5. The public release of the benchmark implementation ( Fonod, 2026f ) and of its
data ( Fonod & Geroliminis, 2026b ) (annotated frames, cases, GCP and every
campaign’s results), from which every benchmark number is regenerated by a command. Also
released are hbb2obb ( Fonod, 2026e ) , the SAM-assisted conversion of HBB to OBB that makes OBB masks available at benchmark scale, and Songdo
Vision OBB ( Fonod & Geroliminis, 2026c ) , the OBB version of Songdo
Vision ( Fonod et al., 2026a ) , together with the human-verified oriented reference against
which the conversion is validated ( Sections 5.1 and E ). 2 Related work Local features and matching Correspondence estimation has been surveyed repeatedly as it moved from hand-crafted operators to
learned ones ( Ma et al., 2021a ) . The pipelines
benchmarked here span three generations of that work. Hand-crafted detectors and descriptors, from
SIFT ( Lowe, 2004a ) and its RootSIFT normalization ( Arandjelović & Zisserman, 2012a ) to the
binary ORB ( Rublee et al., 2011a ) and BRISK ( Leutenegger et al., 2011a ) and the nonlinear
scale spaces of KAZE ( Alcantarilla et al., 2012a ) and AKAZE ( Alcantarilla et al., 2013a ) , remain
the default in deployed stabilization pipelines, because they need no training data, no
accelerator, and no assumption about the imagery. Learned detectors and descriptors replace one or
both stages with a network trained for repeatability and matchability, among them
SuperPoint ( DeTone et al., 2018a ) , DISK ( Tyszkiewicz et al., 2020a ) ,
DeDoDe ( Edstedt et al., 2024b ) , Key.Net ( Barroso-Laguna et al., 2019a ) and
XFeat ( Potje et al., 2024a ) ; learned matchers such as LightGlue ( Lindenberger et al., 2023a ) replace the nearest-neighbor search and its ratio test with attention over the two keypoint sets;
and detector-free methods such as LoFTR ( Sun et al., 2021a ) drop keypoints and match dense features
directly. Dense matchers such as RoMa ( Edstedt et al., 2024c ) go further and estimate a warp and
its certainty for every pixel, from which correspondences are sampled.
A stabilization pipeline is chosen as a whole and its components interact (a matcher accepts only
certain descriptors, a detector-free method has no keypoint budget), so the comparison of Section 6.2 spans all three. Two properties of this literature shape the protocol.
Orientation estimation is not universal, so several learned models are effectively upright, which
is why rotation is swept, as Tareen & Saleem (2018a) sweep it for the classical detectors,
which they find unequally invariant to it; and the input resolution of
a learned model is a memory bound rather than a free choice, which is why the compute budget is an
axis of the benchmark. Direct homography regression A parallel line of work regresses the transform from an image
pair ( DeTone et al., 2016a ) . Zhang et al. (2020a) observe that a loss computed uniformly
over the image cannot keep moving or non-planar objects from contributing to it, and learn a
content mask that rejects those regions: the problem measured here, approached by learning what to
ignore instead of by annotating it. These methods lie outside the feature-based family deployed in the trajectory extraction systems of Section 2 . Robust estimation and dominant structure RANSAC ( Fischler & Bolles, 1981a ) and its modern variants ( Barath et al., 2020a ; Barath & Matas, 2018a ) make a homography recoverable from contaminated correspondences. That a coherent structure of
outliers can capture the consensus is known ( Stewart, 1997a ) , and Chum et al. (2005a) detect a dominant plane inside the estimator; Section 4.1 explains why that defense
does not transfer to a coherently moving group. Filters that prune matches before the estimator rest
on a similar vote: Jiang & Jiang (2018a) keep matches whose motion agrees in direction and
length, Zhou et al. (2024a) build local motion consensus into a learned matcher, and the motions
of a coherently moving group agree as well. Scenes with more than one structure Multi-model fitting accepts that a scene may contain several coherent structures and recovers them
jointly, by clustering correspondences ( Toldo & Fusiello, 2008a ; Wong et al., 2011a ) or by fitting
instances progressively inside the robust estimator ( Barath & Matas, 2019a ) .
Vehicles crossing an intersection are such a
second structure, and AdelaideRMF ( Wong et al., 2011a ) does contain independently moving
content. These methods are evaluated by how well correspondences are partitioned among structures,
not by the accuracy of one recovered transform, and AdelaideRMF supplies no reference homography for
the background. Recovering several homographies also leaves open which one describes the ground,
which correspondence counts cannot decide when the moving structure is the larger. Multi-model
fitting is a remedy that changes the estimator; what is missing first is a measurement of the
failure under the estimators actually deployed. Benchmarks for matching and homography estimation Comparative studies of classical detectors and descriptors ( Tareen & Saleem, 2018a ) and
HPatches ( Balntas et al., 2017a ) established the vocabulary this field still uses, and Schonberger et al. (2017a) set the pattern of judging hand-crafted and learned features on a
common downstream task. Three benchmarks set the
methodological standard we adopt. Jin et al. (2021a) argue that local features must be judged by
a downstream task rather than by intermediate proxies, and show that carefully tuned classical
pipelines remain competitive with learned ones, a conclusion Efe et al. (2021a) reach on HPatches
by optimizing a single parameter; Barath et al. (2023a) contribute a large-scale homography
benchmark with an explicit tuning-and-test protocol. Their shared lesson is that a comparison is
only as meaningful as the care taken to place every method on equal footing
( Section 4.7 ). None of them measures registration accuracy on a scene whose content
moves independently of the camera ( Table 1 ), because their reference geometry, SfM ( SfM ) or manually labeled correspondences on static structure, must exclude independently moving
objects to work at all. Registration benchmarks that do model change place it between
acquisitions ( Sun et al., 2025a ) . Pseudo-ground truth has a further cost: Brachmann et al. (2021a) show
that the algorithm generating it can reorder the methods evaluated against it, which is why the
reference estimator of Part II is audited against GCP ( Section 4.8 ). Table 1: Benchmarks for feature matching, homography estimation and stabilization, and the property

in dynamic SLAM ( SLAM ). DynaSLAM ( Bescos et al., 2018a ) and DS-SLAM ( Yu et al., 2018a ) segment
likely-moving classes and withhold those regions from tracking and mapping. Both report that suppression keeps
the estimate usable in populated scenes; what is missing is a measurement. Those systems are
evaluated end to end, by trajectory error on indoor sequences with a handful of walking people or
on driving sequences, which does not say how much of the frame the objects may cover before the consensus shifts to them, which box
geometry is worth its cost, or what the failure looks like without suppression. A hovering drone
over an intersection is the opposite regime: a nearly static camera and dozens of vehicles moving
in coherent streams. Isolating the effect needs the masking strategy varied as a controlled axis
while everything else is held fixed ( Section 7.5 ), and a metric that does not read
the objects themselves ( Section 4.3 ). Video stabilization as a different objective Stabilization also names work whose goal is a pleasant camera path rather than a fixed frame of
reference: smoothing the path under geometric constraints ( Grundmann et al., 2011a ) , over a mesh of
local homographies ( Liu et al., 2013a ) , or by learning the warp ( Yu & Ramamoorthi, 2020a ) . Its
measures (cropping ratio,
distortion, a frequency-domain stability score) follow from a perceptual objective, and none asks
where a pixel actually is. A smoothed path may drift from any particular frame, which is
inadmissible when object positions are compared across a recording, and a mesh of local warps can
absorb independent object motion instead of rejecting it. Moving objects enter this literature as a
threat to the global motion estimate, met by adaptive RANSAC ( Choi et al., 2009a ) , clustered motion
vectors ( Wu et al., 2021a ) or foreground trajectories ( Zhao & Ling, 2019a ) , and scored by the
similarity of the output frames, not against reference geometry. Registration and stabilization of aerial video Registering airborne video to a fixed reference is long established in
photogrammetry ( Kumar et al., 2001a ) . The remote sensing literature on image registration
concentrates on matching across modalities, sensors and acquisition times ( Geng et al., 2025a ) ,
with structural or phase-congruency descriptors built to be insensitive to radiometric
change ( Ye et al., 2017a ; Li et al., 2020a ) and networks trained on large multimodal
datasets ( Wu et al., 2026a ) , so transient content is a nuisance to be removed rather than a
property to be measured. Moving traffic in airborne registration Vehicle measurement from the air rests on this
registration ( Reinartz et al., 2006a ; Karimi et al., 2006a ) , and moving traffic is a recognized
hazard to it. Kirchhof & Stilla (2006a) warn that many objects moving in the same direction, as on
dense roads, can survive RANSAC and bias the homography, and exclude points at detected motion. Karimi & Lindenbergh (2014a) rule out direct SIFT with RANSAC against the first image of a
helicopter sequence, in part because of the moving vehicles, and chain consecutive homographies
instead. Ljubičić et al. (2021a) compare seven stabilization tools on UAV river footage,
where the water moves and the banks do not, by residual motion at static features. None of these
measures capture or what it costs in position. Aerial detection benchmarks and trajectory datasets Aerial object detection has well-established
benchmarks ( Ding et al., 2022a ; Zhu et al., 2022a ) , DOTA made OBB standard for
aerial objects, and UAVid ( Lyu et al., 2020a ) separates moving from static cars in 4K UAV video
for semantic segmentation. They evaluate recognition , not the registration that must precede
any use of object positions when the camera moves. Drone-based trajectory datasets show the dependency most
clearly: pNEUMA ( Barmpounakis & Geroliminis, 2020a ) , inD ( Bock et al., 2020a ) ,
CitySim ( Zheng et al., 2024a ) and Songdo Traffic ( Fonod et al., 2025b ) all extract positions from
hovering footage after a registration step, whose error georeferenced
positions ( Novikov et al., 2024a ) inherit directly, and the intersections they are recorded over are where a
moving-object consensus is most likely. UAV stabilization for traffic monitoring The work closest to this paper is Fonod et al. (2025b) , which extracts georeferenced vehicle
trajectories from hovering-drone recordings of urban intersections and stabilizes each frame onto a
reference frame with an object-masked homography, releasing that stage as stabilo ( Fonod, 2026g ) and its parameter study as stabilo-optimize . The vehicles are masked to keep
correspondences on moving objects out of the fit, the failure this paper names consensus capture,
but the pipeline was selected on static scenes under synthetic warps, so neither that failure nor
what masking does about it was measured on the recordings. That is the gap addressed here: the static campaign is
extended ( Section 6 ) and paired with a dynamic one on the footage the pipeline is deployed
on ( Section 7 ), where the masking axis can be measured rather than assumed. 3 Problem setting Scene and platform assumptions Recordings come from hovering drones at approximately constant altitude
( 140 m to 150 m 140\text{\,}\mathrm{m}150\text{\,}\mathrm{m} ) in BEV ( BEV ) at 4K resolution ( 3840 × 2160 3840\times 2160 ), so inter-frame
motion is small and wind-induced. The GSD ( GSD ) is about 5.5 cm 5.5\text{\,}\mathrm{cm} per pixel: the

Every pixel quantity in this paper converts at that rate.
The stabilization model is a reference-frame homography, which holds the static scene in place
over a hover exactly only where that scene is planar. Above-ground structures (buildings, poles,
vegetation) depart from the plane in proportion to how far the platform translates; a hovering
platform translates little but does drift, so the evaluation excludes buildings and large tree
canopy, the largest such structures, by polygons drawn on each reference frame ( Section 4.3 ) and scores
the rest of the static scene. The deployed regime is a
hovering platform, so the geometric perturbation a pipeline has to recover in practice is a few
pixels of drift and a fraction of a degree of yaw per frame relative to the reference, growing
slowly over a recording. Part I samples a wider regime than that, so that a
pipeline’s operating limits are measured, and Section 6 states where the two regimes differ. 3.1 Images, transforms, and the registration model Let Ω = { 0 , … , W − 1 } × { 0 , … , H − 1 } \Omega=\{0,\dots,W-1\}\times\{0,\dots,H-1\} be the pixel domain of a recording and let F k : Ω → ℝ F^{k}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R} denote its k k -th frame, with F ref F^{\mathrm{ref}} a designated reference

scene and defines what replaces them: the metrics, the treatment of failures, comparability, the
audit of the reference estimator, and the analysis plan. 4.1 Why internal-consistency metrics are insufficient Inlier count, inlier ratio, and reprojection error measure the internal consistency of a fit: how
well the correspondences the estimator retained agree with the model fitted to them. On static
scenes they are already known to be weak proxies for accuracy. Barath et al. (2023a) report that the inlier count depends on image resolution, on the inlier threshold and on the
estimator, so that MAGSAC returns far more inliers than its competitors at comparable pose
accuracy, and in the tabulation of Jin et al. (2021a, Table 13) the method with the highest inlier
ratio, R2D2 at 52.9 % 52.9\text{\,}\mathrm{\%} , is outperformed downstream by RootSIFT at 39.6 % 39.6\text{\,}\mathrm{\%} . The claim here is stronger, and it follows from the scene rather than from the estimator. A
homography needs four correspondences; a group of objects moving together, such as a stream of
vehicles crossing an intersection, supplies hundreds that are mutually consistent under a single
transform, the one describing their motion. Their coverage ranges from a small fraction of a
high-altitude view to a large share of a low-altitude view over congested lanes, but the estimator
weighs their share of the correspondences, not of the area, so even a small coverage can cast many
votes. Outliers that form a coherent structure of their own bias a robust estimate toward a
compromise between the structures or capture it outright ( Stewart, 1997a ) , and a busy
intersection produces that situation persistently. Wherever the moving group wins, every
internal-consistency check is satisfied by the wrong answer: these statistics are not merely
uninformative but anti-informative . They cannot tell whether it has won; Section 7.3 measures that for the cases studied here. The estimator cannot detect this failure on its own. Chum et al. (2005a) detect a scene
dominated by a single plane with a test inside the estimator, because that case is a
degeneracy: the data underconstrain the fundamental matrix, and the deficiency is visible in the
sample. A homography fitted to coherently moving objects is fully determined and over-supported; it
differs from the intended solution only in which part of the scene it describes, which no individual
correspondence encodes. None of the estimators compared here contains a check that could apply:
OpenCV runs DEGENSAC only for the fundamental matrix, and the only tests reaching a homography are
conditions on the minimal sample (no three collinear points, unchanged orientation), which a stream
of vehicles over several lanes satisfies. The information needed, which image content is static, is
external to the estimation problem, so the failure has to be handled by the protocol. These quantities are therefore recorded as diagnostics , to be explained rather than
optimized, and never rank a pipeline. What replaces them is the rest of this section: agreement
with an external geometry where one exists ( Section 4.2 ), direct measurement of
background alignment where one does not ( Section 4.3 ), and two measures that make
a moving-object consensus observable, the direction of the error ( Section 4.4 ) and the
share of a moving group’s displacement an estimate follows ( Section 4.5 ). 4.2 Geometric metrics Corner transfer error Let 𝒞 = { 𝐜 1 , … , 𝐜 4 } \mathcal{C}=\{\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{4}\} be the frame corners, 𝐜 1 = ( 0 , 0 ) \mathbf{c}_{1}=(0,0) , 𝐜 2 = ( W , 0 ) \mathbf{c}_{2}=(W,0) , 𝐜 3 = ( W , H ) \mathbf{c}_{3}=(W,H) , 𝐜 4 = ( 0 , H ) \mathbf{c}_{4}=(0,H) . estimator returns a degenerate transform. Implementations commonly substitute the identity, the
stabilization library evaluated here among them ( Fonod, 2026g ) , which keeps a campaign running but
hides the failure and biases every metric, because the identity is a plausible answer for a
nearly stationary drone. We therefore admit ⊥ \bot explicitly in Eq. 4 and detect
it from what the estimator returns: an absent inlier count, which every fallback leaves, or a
missing or non-finite matrix. An estimate exactly equal to the identity is not a failure, since
between near-identical frames the estimator returns exactly the identity with thousands of
inliers, and that answer is correct. A finite estimate that drives the third homogeneous
coordinate of Eq. 1 to zero at a corner yields non-finite coordinates, which Eqs. 8 and 9 score as a miss; clamping that division, as some implementations do, would
score a collapsing estimate as merely inaccurate. The reference-free measures need a different treatment, because an undefined value drops out of a
mean: a pipeline that failed on a third of its pairs would otherwise report its background
alignment over the two thirds it solved. A failed pair is therefore scored with the identity warp,
the transform the pipeline actually delivered, and so is a pair whose evaluation region is too
small to be read; declining to answer costs exactly what delivering the identity costs. A configuration that exhausts the benchmark machine’s memory is not a failure. It is
reported as not available and its partial measurements are discarded, because a sparser scene needs less memory and is also easier to
register, so the subset that fit would flatter it. Counting it as a failure would make a score depend
on the machine’s memory. 4.7 Placing pipelines on equal footing A benchmark that compares ten pipelines at one shared set of matcher settings measures the

markings between them. OBB keep that texture. Conversely, what moves with a vehicle is
not confined to its body: its cast shadow, its motion blur and the reflections off its windows
travel with it, lie partly outside a tight OBB , and supply correspondences that agree with
its motion, and the extra area of an HBB covers part of them. Which effect dominates, if
either, is an empirical question, so the box geometry is a controlled axis of both parts, and the
margin sweep of Section 7.5 compares the two geometries at equal suppressed area, so

by the conversion of Appendix E for the oriented ones. Neither model runs inside the

Every hover is read at points 30 s 30\text{\,}\mathrm{s} apart, clear of its first and last seconds and with
the full 3600 3600 frames after them, and each point is a candidate reference frame; a hover
supplies at most one case. Each candidate is read the way the benchmark reads it: as pairs of the
candidate reference with one later frame in each of the five shortest offset bins ( 1 1 , 2 2 , 5 5 , 11 11 and 22 22 frames, the bins’ geometric centers), since every benchmark
pair shares the reference and a moving group moves as one only briefly. In each pair the platform’s
motion is removed by an affine enhanced correlation coefficient fit to the
background ( Evangelidis & Psarakis, 2008a ) , with each frame’s objects filled with the background mean,
and every object annotated in the reference frame is sought among the later frame’s detections by
appearance: its patch, resampled along its OBB , is compared by ZNCC , and only a mutual best
match between boxes of similar size is accepted. A reference object is then found where it was,
found displaced (moved by more than a tenth of the median object width), or not found, and the
largest set of displaced objects moving together is the largest structure a single wrong homography
could fit, both as defined for the capture measure ( Section 4.5 ). Averaged over the
offsets, the displaced share of the frame is how many votes the moving foreground can cast, and the
share of it moving as one structure, the coherence , is how many it can cast for one model;
opposing or crossing streams have low coherence however aligned their boxes are. Distances are
measured in object widths, so the procedure does not depend on ground resolution, and nothing is
tracked: six annotated frames per candidate suffice. The design is a 2 × 2 2\times 2 split at the

occurs without masking: for ORB at both budgets, in real time in every case that presents a
coherently moving group, and by a pixel or two, at the limit of what the GCP resolve, for BRISK, SIFT and RootSIFT at the real-time budget. Under masking it occurs nowhere. This
bears on the pipeline with which Fonod et al. (2025b) extracted the
Songdo Traffic trajectories ( Fonod et al., 2025c ) , ORB at the offline budget under HBB masks: unmasked, it is captured on 6 6 pairs, masked on none, which supports masking at
detection for trajectory extraction. Three properties make this a measurement rather than an anecdote. The
instruments resolve it: a complete capture misaligns the fit where the moving group lies by a
sizable fraction of an object width, far above the audited error of the reference and the
repeatability of the GCP , and a partial capture still raises the capture
fraction and the motion alignment. The cases were chosen to contain it: selected from the video alone,
at reference points spread over every hover of a recording day, they span the amount and
coherence of moving foreground that pool offers. And the tests were fixed before the outcome: the
primary and mechanism contrasts were specified in advance and are reported whatever their result. Capture
itself is counted descriptively, at a threshold above what the identity transform reaches, with the
counts at stricter thresholds beside it. Of the two descriptors the cases were
selected on, the moving share orders where capture occurs and coherence does not: the case with
most captures lies in a mixed cell, because the survey averages coherence over its offsets while a
capture needs one coherent group at one offset ( Section 7.3 ). What the cases cannot say is how the effect scales with the moving share. Their moving foreground
covers 0.17 0.17 % to 1.94 1.94 % of the frame outside the control ( Table 5 ), and
unmasked ORB keeps on average 949 949 inliers per pair offline and 513 513 in real time in the
high-moving coherent cell (released files offline_masking_by_scenario.csv and realtime_masking_by_scenario.csv ). The estimator weighs a structure by its share of the
correspondences, not of the area ( Section 4.1 ), so the feature budget and the
detector’s preference for the high-contrast content of vehicles ( Fig. 3 ) matter as
much as the area the vehicles cover, and a low-altitude view over congested lanes, where moving
vehicles can fill a large share of the frame, is a different regime. The protocol carries over
unchanged; the conclusion need not. 8.2 Joint reading of the two protocols Part I ranks pipelines against exact truth on static scenes and shows which of their settings
matter there: the compute budget, by a wide margin, then the estimator’s threshold for estimators
that commit to one, while the matcher thresholds have little effect at the operating point and
masking costs almost nothing. Part II asks whether that ranking survives footage in which part of
the frame moves. Capture predicts a specific pattern: a pipeline falls without masking, recovers
under masking, and has its error along the motion while unmasked, with a capture fraction near one on the
pairs that carry a moving group. The data show that pattern for ORB at the real-time budget: its
unmasked estimates follow the group with a capture fraction up to 0.96 0.96 within the survey’s
offsets ( Fig. 1 ), and its error along the motion rises; the alignment rises little,
since the captures are a few percent of the pairs. Its fall from 0.6785 0.6785 masked to 0.5835 0.5835 unmasked is mostly not on the captured pairs, which bound their share of it at 0.023 0.023 ,
and the rest is of the size of the budget effect, which masking has on static scenes as well
( Section 7.5 ). For the other pipelines the static ranking carries over: SIFT, RootSIFT and AKAZE
lead Part II in both regimes, masked or not. The static masking cost is the control that makes
that reading possible: in Part I the annotated objects satisfy the same homography as the
background, so suppressing them cannot improve the consensus, only change which correspondences the
budget keeps. 8.3 Practical guidance No single pipeline is called best. The leading tie set on mAA is AKAZE, RootSIFT, SIFT and BRISK
under the main Part I configuration and grows to six pipelines at deployment-scale perturbation;
beyond 30 ° 30\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree} of rotation the upright learned models drop out ( Fig. 2 ).
In Part II the tie set against the reference is SIFT, RootSIFT and AKAZE under every masking mode
in both regimes; against the GCP every configuration ties at the median in both,
and at the 90 90 th percentile all but ORB in real time ( Fig. 11 ). Offline,
masking offers little a practitioner could act on: every configuration lies within the repeatability
of the GCP , and the masking benefits are tenths of a pixel or less. In real time
it makes ORB usable, at a cost on static scenes of less than 0.005 0.005 mAA. For a real-time pipeline the choice is open: masked ORB
registers in 12 12 to 13 ms 13\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{s} at a median error of 1.17 1.17 to 1.19 px 1.19\text{\,}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{x} against the GCP , and SIFT, RootSIFT and AKAZE, masked or not, in 18 18 to 22 ms 22\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{s} at 0.51 0.51 to 0.57 px 0.57\text{\,}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{x} ; all fit one frame interval at the median, and for the latter three masking changes
background alignment by less than 0.001 0.001 in either direction, which no contrast resolves
( Tables 9 , 11 and 13 ). Compute excludes some pipelines on
its own: LoFTR cannot run at Part I’s full resolution on the benchmark GPU, and four of the ten
(BRISK, LoFTR, and DeDoDe and KeyNet with LightGlue) need more than one frame interval at the
real-time point. HBB and OBB do not differ measurably for registration at
these densities and orientations, in the masking contrasts or in the margin sweep; a road grid oblique to
the image, larger vehicles at a lower altitude or long turning queues would give the geometry more
room to matter, and the protocol measures it there unchanged. OBB remain useful elsewhere, in the
motion axis of Section 4.4 and in object-level errors that respect an object’s extent.
SIFT and the learned detectors apply the mask after selecting their keypoints in the library
evaluated here ( Section 3.2 ), so for them masking can only remove keypoints; selecting
after the mask is a change to the library, which the protocol would measure unchanged. 8.4 Limitations Coverage of the cases The cases come from one recording day at one altitude band, and their moving foreground at the
reference covers at most 1.94 1.94 % of the frame, so what is found about capture holds for that
range. Each case is registered onto one reference frame, chosen to span the conditions capture
depends on; how often capture occurs over all the frames a deployed pipeline might register onto is
a different quantity, which the design does not estimate. Part II has nine independent units at seven intersections: a
masking effect is detectable only if it has the same sign in all of them, or in eight with the
dissenting case the smallest, and the 2 × 2 2\times 2 with two cases per cell is descriptive. Taking
the intersection as the unit leaves every claim unchanged, but the per-pipeline benefits that meet
the rule fall short of it, since seven units cannot pass Holm’s adjustment over ten pipelines, and
ORB’s real-time coverage correlation loses its interval above zero. Cases
with a larger moving share, from lower altitudes or congested approaches, and
more sites and sessions are the most informative extension; the selection procedure is automated
so that they can be added without revisiting the design. Resolution of the ground control points The GCP are repeatable to 1.00 px 1.00\text{\,}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{x} at the 95 95 th percentile. They
separate a captured estimate from a correct one and give a masking contrast independent of the
reference, but they cannot rank pipelines whose errors differ by less than a pixel, and the
reference-relative columns, which can, are limited by the audited residual. With eight audited
frames per case, the GCP see only the few captured estimates that fall on one
( Section 7.3 ); the capture fraction, read against the reference on every pair, is
what counts them. The reference estimator
is a member of the family under test, run with OBB masking; its absolute error is measured
by the audit, but not its covariance with a candidate’s, which is why the masking comparison is read
from the GCP and the reference-free measures. Budget, resolution and scene Part II’s two regimes change the resolution and the feature budget together; Part I separates the
two on static scenes ( Fig. 4 ), and the mask-margin sweep was run in the offline
regime only. Part I’s 29 29 scenes come from one session on one day, so the split prevents
tuning on the evaluation scenes but does not make the session representative of other cities,
altitudes or lighting. The registration
model is a single homography: buildings, raised structures and large tree canopy are excluded from
the reference-free measures, but poles, signs and isolated street trees are not, and their parallax
grows with the platform’s drift, which within a hover is small and is the same for every candidate.
Footage from a translating platform, or with tall structure filling much of the frame, needs a
different registration model. Annotations and object classes The OBB come from a segmentation-based conversion ( Appendix E ); its
fallback share is zero on this benchmark’s image sets, and where it is not, a comparison between the two box geometries is diluted by it. Most
of the benchmark is indifferent to what the objects are, but two parts assume vehicles: the motion
axis is recovered from the long axes of OBB , which holds for vehicles and vessels and not
for people, and the coherence descriptor assumes a population that moves as streams. Both degrade
to an undefined axis and an unfilled cell rather than to a wrong number. 9 Conclusion Coherently moving objects can capture the geometric consensus of a robust estimator, and the
statistics that describe a fit’s self-consistency would then describe the wrong fit. This paper
turns that possibility into a measurement for the complete registration pipeline used in
reference-frame stabilization of hovering-drone video. A static protocol under exact ground truth
establishes what each pipeline can do. A dynamic protocol on nine intersection cases, each
registered onto a reference frame selected from the video alone, measures what the same pipelines
do when part of the frame moves, at an offline and a real-time budget, with a ground-control-point
audit that measures the error of the pseudo-ground-truth reference and scores every candidate
independently of it. On static scenes, four tuned classical pipelines (AKAZE, RootSIFT, SIFT, BRISK) lead; the compute
budget decides accuracy far more than the matcher thresholds, which MAGSAC++ makes nearly
irrelevant, and masking costs every pipeline less than 0.005 0.005 mAA. On the dynamic cases
unmasked ORB is captured in real time in every case that presents a coherently moving group, on 28 28 of 304 304 real-time pairs and 6 6 offline, and the estimates pulled toward a group
have the inlier statistics of the same pipeline at the nearest offsets. Object masking
removes capture for every pipeline at both budgets and restores ORB in real time, though the captured
pairs can account for at most a quarter of that gain; it leaves the leading pipelines (SIFT, RootSIFT, AKAZE)
unchanged, and HBB and OBB do not differ at these densities and orientations,
where the two withhold nearly the same pixels. How capture scales with the moving share, from lower altitudes or over congested approaches, is the
question the benchmark is built to answer next. The protocol needs only
annotated boxes for the objects and a dozen or so GCP on each of a
handful of frames per case. The benchmark implementation, the data every reported number is
computed from, the OBB dataset and the human-verified reference that validates its conversion
are released so that the comparison can be extended to other pipelines, other platforms and other
classes of moving object without changing the protocol. CRediT authorship contribution statement Robert Fonod : Writing – review & editing, Writing – original draft, Visualization,
