However, visual realism does not ensure physical or causal consistency: objects may disappear or multiply, and contact may fail to produce a plausible response [ 3 , 4 , 29 , 33 ] . Other common failures include unintended slow-motion effects and changes in speed or direction that are inconsistent with gravity, inertia, or contact forces [ 18 ] . Such errors disrupt the temporal and causal relationships within a scene, so a video can appear convincing frame by frame while depicting a physically implausible sequence of events. Improving physical behavior requires training videos that systematically cover how events unfold under different conditions. Such data should capture physical interactions across diverse objects and scenes [ 54 ] . Collecting these videos through real-world experiments is difficult to scale while maintaining controlled conditions and reliable annotations [ 16 ] . Consider a glass falling and shattering on impact: varying its release height, initial velocity, or surroundings requires repeated setup, while unintended changes between trials make the effect of any one factor difficult to isolate. This collection burden also makes it difficult to obtain targeted fine-tuning data for physical behaviors that a model handles poorly, since suitable examples require deliberate coverage of the relevant conditions and reliable annotations. The challenge is therefore to obtain diverse physical videos together with dependable signals for identifying and correcting errors. A long-term direction is recursive self-improvement (RSI), in which video generation models produce and learn from their own training data with minimal human intervention. Recent work such as KBAlign [ 46 ] and SEAL [ 55 ] adapts language models using self-generated training data, while Absolute Zero [ 51 ] learns to propose and solve tasks through verifiable execution feedback. For video generation, such a loop would first autonomously identify weaknesses in the model’s physical behavior and generate candidate videos targeting them, then assess and refine the candidates and select suitable data for fine-tuning. The updated model would then generate the next round of candidates, whose quality would again be assessed. However, current video models still produce physical errors, and general-purpose multimodal evaluators can struggle to judge their physical validity reliably [ 4 ] . Generating more videos therefore does not by itself ensure that the model receives better training data. A key requirement is to identify what is physically wrong, guide its correction, and turn the resulting videos into dependable learning signals. PhysLoop addresses this need through a unified harness that guides LLMs to generate video data in a sandbox, applies physical constraints through an internal simulator, and iteratively refines the data through physical assessment and correction. LLMs already possess physical knowledge that supports reasoning about physical processes, but they do not apply it consistently across tasks [ 37 ] . Realizing that knowledge in a video requires spatial relationships, physical conditions, and motion to remain consistent throughout an event; choices that appear plausible individually may still produce an incorrect outcome when combined [ 26 ] . Our insight is to guide and constrain the application of this existing knowledge within an executable physical environment. The simulator inside PhysLoop supplies physical constraints and observable outcomes, allowing the harness to check whether the generated behavior satisfies the intended physical requirements. Feedback from these outcomes guides the LLM in correcting how its knowledge is applied, while repeated assessment determines which videos are suitable to retain. The accepted videos serve as physical supervision from which video generation models can learn, providing a path to distilling the LLM’s knowledge into their generated behavior. This shared principle supports controllable training-data construction and the assessment and repair of physical errors in generated videos. We introduce PhysLoop, a unified harness that guides LLMs to turn existing physical knowledge into video data under simulator constraints, integrating physical assessment and iterative correction to support video-model self-improvement. • Datasets and generation tools. We release 100K videos covering common physical phenomena in diverse, realistic indoor and outdoor scenes, alongside the PhysLoop-Gen and PhysLoop-Repair evaluation datasets and generation tools. • Evaluation across generation and repair. We evaluate controllable generation, localized repair, and physical behavior on PhysLoop-Gen, PhysLoop-Repair, and the external benchmark [ 38 ] , respectively, using human judgments and automatic metrics. 2 Related Work Self-improvement through generated data. Learning from self-generated data offers a route to model adaptation with less human annotation [ 42 , 45 ] . Two relevant directions are constructing training supervision from supplied knowledge or examples and autonomously generating new learning tasks [ 20 ] . For adaptation to supplied inputs, self-verification and feedback from downstream performance offer complementary ways to assess and improve the generated supervision [ 46 , 55 ] . For autonomous task generation, an executable environment can validate proposed tasks and solutions, allowing task proposal and solving to be optimized together [ 51 ] . For video models, VLM feedback guides offline fine-tuning to improve object interactions [ 12 ] , while embodied video planning supports further training on successful trajectories collected from the environment [ 40 ] . These approaches connect generated content to model updates through feedback on its validity or learning utility. Physical supervision for video generation. Controlled physical data connect known scene conditions with observed behavior, providing structured supervision for learning physical dynamics. Task-oriented datasets cover stability and support, temporal and causal reasoning, and physical prediction across object interactions [ 17 , 43 , 5 ] . A complementary direction varies latent physical properties to test whether models infer dynamics from observed motion and interactions [ 8 , 41 ] . Reusable simulation platforms provide annotated scenes and multimodal observations, supporting data construction beyond individual tasks [ 16 , 13 ] . A unified physical representation allows captured 3D Gaussian assets, meshes, and fluids to interact within the same simulation [ 28 ] . Integrated suites combine physical domains and visual learning tasks [ 54 ] , while simulation-based supervision and feedback from vision-language models support learning physical controls for video generation [ 34 ] . LLM-guided simulation and physical video generation. Work on language and vision-language agents spans interactive simulator access, programmatic scene construction, and coordinated generation of dynamic physical worlds [ 39 , 23 , 44 , 47 ] . Visual feedback and physical constraints help refine language-generated scene layouts while avoiding interpenetration and unstable support [ 27 ] . For video generation, simulated trajectories supply motion guidance [ 11 ] , while agentic planning uses tool execution and verification to revise generation decisions [ 10 ] . Stage-specific verification of reconstructed scenes and simulated events supports targeted program repair before video synthesis [ 26 ] . When simulated motion guides visual refinement, the final video still needs to preserve the dynamics verified in simulation. Physical assessment and video correction. Video assessment separates perceptual quality from physical behavior. Perceptual protocols evaluate appearance and temporal coherence [ 21 ] , while complementary benchmarks assess physical commonsense, rule adherence, and consistency with real-world principles [ 3 , 4 , 29 , 52 ] . Reference-based tests compare generated motion with recorded experiments or simulated references [ 33 , 38 , 54 ] , and temporally localized diagnoses identify when physical failures occur and explain them [ 48 ] . At inference, self-refining sampling revises denoising predictions without external verifiers [ 22 ] , while VLM feedback supports iterative refinement and selection of video plans for embodied tasks [ 40 ] . Motion controllers instead guide generation through user-specified trajectories or optical flow [ 50 , 6 ] . For existing videos, diagnosis-guided localized refinement corrects text–video misalignment while preserving faithful regions [ 25 ] . Inpainting fills missing regions [ 53 , 49 ] , and correspondence-based editing maintains consistency across frames [ 14 ] . These methods provide different forms of control, but visual coherence or adherence to a prescribed motion alone does not establish physical correctness. PhysLoop connects diagnosis to simulation-grounded correction and evaluates both the resolution of the target physical error and the preservation of unaffected content. 3 PhysLoop Figure 2 : Dataset overview: (a) multi-views, depth, segmentation, and states; (b) controlled variations; (c) representative diverse cases. 3.1 Workflow PhysLoop takes a text request, optionally accompanied by image or video references, and produces a generated or repaired video together with its task specification and execution record. A code agent uses harness tools to specify the task, acquire assets, and construct an executable simulator scene. The harness includes a task manager for converting user requests into task specifications, an asset-management and scene-generation module, a simulator execution module, a verifier, and a video refiner, together with supporting tools. Simulation verification precedes appearance refinement, and final-video verification follows it. The stages of data construction and video repair have different task constraints and output purposes. Figure 1 illustrates the harness components and the workflow shared by data construction and video repair. 3.2 Task Specification and Scene Construction The agent expands the request into a task specification that records the target event, construction assumptions, and conditions used for verification. Text specifies the intended behavior, while images and videos supply visual references. For a falling-glass request, the agent must choose a release height and an impact surface when these are unspecified. These assumptions are recorded alongside the expected outcome so that construction and verification refer to the same task. The agent retrieves assets that meet the visual and interaction requirements, using external search or Meshy [ 30 ] to obtain or generate 3D assets when suitable objects are unavailable. Before scene construction, the harness checks asset scale and collision geometry against the target interaction. Visual meshes and physical representations are handled separately, so detailed appearance can be paired with an appropriate collision proxy. For example, a glass can retain a detailed rendering mesh while a separate physical representation determines its contact with the ground. The agent writes a scene program that combines retrieved assets and procedural geometry to specify object placement and relationships. For a falling glass, the program sets the release position and impact surface, while the physics solver computes the subsequent motion. The program can place the event in different settings and compose structures that are not predefined in the asset catalog. Before full simulation, the harness renders a low-resolution preview frame from each planned camera view to inspect asset placement and visibility of the interaction region. If an object is misplaced or an interaction is occluded, the agent adjusts the layout or camera and repeats the preview before full simulation and video rendering. 3.3 Physics Execution and Video Refinement Unreal Engine (UE) [ 9 ] is the primary simulator integrated into the harness, while Taichi [ 19 ] and Genesis [ 2 ] provide additional support. Given the initial conditions and permitted actions specified by the agent, the physics solver numerically computes the resulting motion according to the physical model of the scene. Simulator adapters are conversion interfaces that translate scene configurations into each simulator’s inputs and transfer the resulting motion to UE with aligned coordinates and timing. Multiple camera views share the same simulation timeline. Alongside RGB, the harness captures depth and instance segmentation and records object states and backend-provided contact or event signals. Each frame is linked to its simulation time and camera configuration, allowing verification to relate visible behavior to physical states. After simulation checks pass, appearance refinement adapts the rendered scene to the requested visual appearance. The video refiner takes the verified simulation video as a motion reference, together with the task description and visual references that specify the desired appearance. 3.4 Verification and Revision Successful execution does not establish that the requested event occurred. The verifier compares simulation states and visual observations with the predefined event requirements and physical conditions. For a collision task, it checks whether contact occurred and whether the subsequent motion satisfies the specification. Conservation checks account for external forces, dissipation, and numerical error. The final-video check evaluates whether visible behavior preserves the target event and its physical relationships after refinement. Refinement may alter motion that passed simulation checks, so the verifier reassesses the refined result against the same event requirements. If either simulation or final-video verification fails, the verifier returns a failure description with supporting simulation or visual evidence to the agent. Using this feedback and the recorded history, the agent revises the configuration for the responsible stage. A missed collision may require changes to the scene or initial conditions, while a refinement error may require different refiner inputs. The task objective and verification criteria remain fixed throughout revision. Execution ends when all required checks pass or the attempt or resource budget is exhausted; unresolved failures remain in the run record. The specification, configurations, observations, and revision history remain linked in a persistent run record. Users can participate mainly during verification by reviewing candidate results, with their judgments recorded together with automated checks and agent revisions. 3.5 Application: Training-Data Construction The agent exposes task-specific parameters in the generated scene configuration, such as release height and initial velocity for a falling glass. The agent or user varies selected parameters while holding the remaining conditions fixed, producing different instances of the same event. Scene and camera settings can also be edited, and multiple views can be rendered from the same simulation. For data construction, the task description and visual references specify the appearance to be produced. Videos that pass final verification are paired with their task descriptions as training examples for video generation models. Each sample retains its generating configuration for inspection, subsequent expansion, and regeneration. Downstream training can use these video–text pairs to update the video generation model. Raw simulation renders retain their synchronized depth, segmentation, and object-state annotations. Because refinement may alter geometry or object positions, these annotations are not treated as pixel-aligned supervision for refined videos. Frames before and after the selected segment constrain how the corrected event must connect to the source and which content must be preserved. This diagnosis and the source constraints define the task specification used by the shared scene-construction and simulation stages. During refinement, the source video supplies the appearance reference for the reconstructed objects and environment. Repair replaces only the selected interval [ a , b ) [a,b) of a source video V V with T T frames. The corrected segment V ^ a : b \hat{V}_{a:b} matches the source resolution and frame rate and contains exactly b − a b-a frames. The output is assembled as V ′ = V 0 : a ∥ V ^ a : b ∥ V b : T , V^{\prime}=V_{0:a}\;\|\;\hat{V}_{a:b}\;\|\;V_{b:T}, (1) where ∥ \| denotes concatenation. Blending remains within the replacement interval, leaving source frames outside it unchanged. The replacement is evaluated within the complete video because a plausible isolated segment may still conflict with the surrounding event. The verifier checks target-error correction, consistency with preceding and following frames, and preservation of unaffected content. If refinement changes the intended physical behavior or the segment fails to connect to the source, the resulting evidence guides further revision under the same stopping conditions. 4 PhysLoop Dataset Our data resources comprise the PhysLoop-100K training set and two evaluation sets, PhysLoop-Gen and PhysLoop-Repair. PhysLoop-100K provides video–text pairs and simulation annotations, while the evaluation sets assess generation and physical-error repair, respectively. 4.1 PhysLoop-100K PhysLoop-100K covers broad physical categories, including kinematics, dynamics, rigid-body dynamics, magnetism, fluid mechanics, flexible and elastic materials, and fragmentation and fracture. For each case, the system automatically varies relevant physical parameters, such as mass and initial velocity, together with assets, scenes, lighting, and camera views. The dataset includes all valid combinations of the parameter settings defined for each case, allowing users to select configurations of interest. PhysLoop-100K contains 100K accepted videos from 2.5K distinct physical events. Each event contains an average of 40 videos, captured from 3-5 perspectives and covering 2-4 key parameters that can be configured to multiple levels. Alongside refined video–text pairs and their generating configurations, the dataset provides the original simulation videos and associated annotations. Depth maps, instance segmentation maps, and object states are provided as additional information and are not pixel-aligned with the refined videos. The split protocol groups each source event with its camera views and parameter variants. Coverage statistics, parameter ranges, and split details are provided in the supplementary material. 4.2 PhysLoop-Gen PhysLoop-Gen addresses generation requests specifying a target physical event. Each instance contains a text request, with image references provided for some cases. The requests cover rigid-body, cloth, and fluid phenomena, ranging from simple bouncing or sliding to coupled interactions and multistage mechanical chains. Task categories and difficulty are defined from input properties, including interaction count, event ordering, and observable conditions, rather than from a particular model’s performance. PhysLoop-Gen contains 100 generation tasks. The tasks support comparisons among video generation approaches, including agentic frameworks such as PhysLoop and direct video generation models. Task descriptions and representative examples are provided in the supplementary material. Figure 3 : Qualitative comparison of PhysLoop and baseline methods. 4.3 PhysLoop-Repair PhysLoop-Repair addresses existing videos with identifiable physical or event-level errors. Each instance contains a source video with a physical error, an optional repair request, and annotations describing the error and its temporal interval. Annotations locate the error and specify conditions for a successful correction, such as maintaining a falling object’s presence and motion consistent with the event conditions. The primary evaluation protocol supplies only the source video and request; error and interval annotations remain evaluation-only. Source videos are generated by different video models, including Seedance series, MiniMax H3, and Gemini Omni 1.1 Flash, yielding 50 repair tasks. Inclusion requires an observable error, a specified correction, and source content to preserve. Evaluation checks target-error removal, connection to surrounding events, and preservation of unrelated content. 5 Evaluation 5.1 Models and Experimental Settings We evaluate video generation on PhysLoop-Gen, video repair on PhysLoop-Repair, and how well generated video continuations match recorded physical events on Physics-IQ Verified. The default configuration uses GPT-6 Astra [ 35 ] as the agent backbone and Seedance 2.5 [ 7 ] for video refinement. With the refiner fixed, we compare the default backbone against Claude Opus 5 [ 1 ] and Kimi K3 [ 32 ] . With the backbone fixed, we compare the default refiner against MiniMax H3 [ 31 ] , Gemini Omni 1.1 Flash [ 15 ] , and a no-refiner configuration. Results for additional models are provided in the supplementary material. Across the three benchmarks, both PhysLoop and standalone baselines allow up to five repeat calls to the video generation model per task and return one final result. The supplementary material provides model versions and settings, prompts, output examples, costs, and runtimes. 5.2 Metrics Human Evaluation. Human and model-based quality metrics are restricted to PhysLoop-Gen. Human raters score physical plausibility, visual realism, and causal consistency on five-point scales. Overall win rate reflects pairwise preferences for physical and visual quality, with ties counting as half a win. Each video is assigned to multiple raters, with method identities hidden and presentation order randomized. Automatic Evaluation. VideoPhy’s VideoCon-Physics evaluator [ 3 ] provides Auto-PC for physical commonsense and Auto-SA for adherence to the shared task description. VBench [ 21 ] measures imaging quality (IQ) and aesthetic quality (AQ). For each metric, we average scores over cases valid across compared configurations. Task-Specific Metrics. Task-specific performance is the primary criterion, while human ratings provide the main assessment of generated-video quality. Compared with standalone Seedance 2.5, PhysLoop scores slightly lower on SA and AQ, lower on PC, and higher on IQ; the disagreement on physical plausibility may partly reflect limitations of automatic evaluation. Following prior work, we treat automatic metrics only as supporting references: they may favor visual styles common to video generators, while physical errors unfolding over time can be difficult to detect from sparsely sampled frames [ 4 , 54 ] . 5.3 Result Analysis Overall Comparison. Table 1 summarizes results on PhysLoop-Gen, PhysLoop-Repair, and Physics-IQ Verified for standalone video generators and PhysLoop configurations. On PhysLoop-Gen, PhysLoop achieves an 85% task-success rate, compared with 73% for standalone Seedance 2.5. It also receives higher human ratings for physical plausibility (4.06 vs. 3.80) and causal consistency (4.12 vs. 3.80), although its visual realism rating is lower than that of standalone Seedance 2.5 (3.72 vs. 4.29). The gap in perceived realism is most apparent in material appearance and backgrounds. On PhysLoop-Repair, PhysLoop achieves an 86.00% success rate, compared with 19.33% for the strongest standalone baseline, Seedance 2.5. All three standalone generators remain below 20%, showing that they struggle to satisfy the repair task’s combined requirements. A successful repair must correct the physical error while preserving the required source content and connecting coherently to surrounding events. PhysLoop expresses these requirements in an explicit reconstruction task and checks both the simulated event and the refined video against them. These results support the shared workflow of scene construction, simulation, and verification for correcting existing videos, extending its utility beyond controllable generation. With GPT-6 Astra fixed as the agent backbone, the no-refiner configuration already achieves 80.00% generation success, indicating that scene construction and simulation can realize many of the requested events. Adding Seedance 2.5 refinement raises generation success to 85.00% and repair success from 60.67% to 86.00%. Human-rated visual realism increases from 2.59 to 3.72, accompanied by slightly higher ratings for physical plausibility and causal consistency. In this configuration, refinement improves appearance alongside task completion. Results also vary across refiners: H3 and Gemini Omni yield lower success rates on both tasks under the same backbone. This variation motivates evaluating refiners within the complete workflow, where the final video must satisfy both the requested appearance and the physical requirements of the task. On Physics-IQ Verified, the default configuration scores 52.06, compared with 48.07 for standalone Seedance 2.5. With Seedance 2.5 fixed as the refiner, Claude Opus 5 achieves the highest score in our comparison (53.75), despite lower success on PhysLoop-Gen and PhysLoop-Repair. The differing rankings suggest that task completion and agreement with an observed physical continuation capture distinct aspects of performance. A plausible event may still differ from the specific dynamics of the reference video. Refiner choice also matters: with GPT-6 Astra fixed, H3 and Gemini Omni score 39.10 and 12.08, respectively, both below the default Seedance 2.5 configuration. Evaluation Interpretation. Task-specific performance is the primary criterion, while human ratings provide the main assessment of generated-video quality. The full PhysLoop configuration achieves the highest scores across all human-evaluation dimensions, although its automatic scores are slightly lower than those of standalone video generators. Following prior work, we treat automatic metrics only as supporting references: they may favor visual styles common to video generators, while physical errors unfolding over time can be difficult to detect from sparsely sampled frames [ 4 , 54 ] . 5.4 Case Analysis Figure 3 shows why plausible local motion is insufficient for judging task completion. In the domino example, H3 depicts sequential toppling but leaves the vase upright, so the intended interaction chain remains incomplete. Standalone Seedance 2.5 replaces the requested S-shaped layout with a straight chain, violating the task’s spatial specification. In the billiard example, Gemini Omni introduces extra balls and Seedance 2.5 adds an extra cue ball, altering the object set required by the task. These failures concern the event outcome and scene consistency, which visual realism alone cannot establish. The UE-only and refined PhysLoop outputs both depict the rack breaking apart, while differing in material appearance and lighting. This comparison illustrates how event realization and appearance refinement serve complementary purposes in the pipeline. 6 Limitations PhysLoop depends on the coverage of its simulators, assets, and verification conditions. Reconstructing a video requires assumptions about unobserved geometry, materials, and initial states, so a valid simulation may not recover the original physical process. Appearance refinement can introduce new errors, and local replacement may be insufficient when a mistake has already changed later events. These limitations constrain which videos can be accepted as learning signals and may require human review for ambiguous cases. Extending PhysLoop to a complete RSI training loop requires connecting weakness discovery, candidate generation, verification and repair, data selection, and model updates across successive rounds. Each updated video model should influence which failures are targeted and which candidates are generated next. Future work must establish whether this process yields sustained gains on held-out events while preserving video quality and data diversity. Independent evaluation, tracking the effects of incorrect quality judgments, and controlling simulation and training costs will be necessary to support reliable self-improvement with decreasing human intervention. 7 Conclusion We introduced PhysLoop, a unified harness that guides LLMs to realize physical knowledge as videos under simulator constraints. Its shared workflow supports controllable training-data construction and video repair through failure identification, correction, and verification. PhysLoop provides a quality-control component toward recursive self-improvement, enabling video models to generate, assess, and improve learning data with limited human intervention. Appendix A Data Resources and Construction A.1 Training-Data Construction We construct training data by expanding physical event families into multiple scene configurations. For each family, we vary physical parameters such as mass, initial velocity, and material properties, together with object assets, backgrounds, lighting, and camera views. Each configuration undergoes simulation, verification, appearance refinement, and a final-video check before the output is paired with a text description. The training pair is linked to its generating configuration and native simulation records, including the rendered video, depth maps, instance segmentation, and object states. A.2 PhysLoop-Gen PhysLoop-Gen contains 100 tasks: 68 rigid-body tasks, 12 cloth tasks, 9 fluid tasks, and 11 image-conditioned tasks. The image-conditioned group is defined by its input modality and can involve rigid-body or other interactions. Each task contains a text request, optional image references, and success conditions that name the required objects, key interactions, event order, and final outcome. Task difficulty is defined by the input: the number of interacting objects, required contacts, constraints, and event stages. Multistage tasks are checked stage by stage. In the image-conditioned paddle-wheel task (Fig. 4 ), a falling ball turns a wheel, whose blade pushes a second ball off its support and down a ramp into a basket. A.3 PhysLoop-Repair Each repair task contains a source video, an optional repair request, and evaluation-only annotations of the error, its interval, and the conditions for a successful correction. Error types include motion or contact failures, changes in object count or identity, missing support, wrong event order, and inconsistent material response. Repairs follow the interval replacement defined in the main paper. A localized repair replaces the interval [ a , b ) [a,b) with exactly b − a b-a frames at the source resolution and frame rate, keeps blending inside the interval, and is checked for unchanged decoded frames outside it. When the error has already altered later events, the agent reconstructs the whole clip, which corresponds to a = 0 a=0 and b = T b=T . Each task records which scope was used. A repair succeeds when the target error is removed, the result connects to the surrounding events, required source content is preserved, and no new severe error appears. Evaluation always inspects the complete output video. Appendix B Harness Execution Details B.1 Verification Checks Simulation-stage verification has three layers. Task assertions. The task specification declares machine-checkable assertions over the native simulation record: existence, count, or order of contacts between named objects; thresholds on an object’s state value or state change; trajectory integrity; and completeness of the required media and state artifacts. Contact evidence combines simulator hit events, when available, with oriented-box proximity tests using a 4 cm tolerance. Proximity-based contacts are geometric inferences, not native solver contact impulses. For the paddle-wheel task, the assertions require contact between the driving ball and a wheel blade, contact between another blade and the second ball, a displacement of the second ball of at least 0.45 m, and complete trajectories. Technical quality gate. Independently of the task, the gate checks media validity (codec, frame count, frame rate), that each camera moves only as declared, that the initial scene is free of unintended contacts, that compiled object placements match the simulator’s pre-simulation state, and, when a depth reference exists, that native depth agrees with the declared reference support surface (mean absolute error ≤ \leq 1 cm, 95th percentile ≤ \leq 2 cm). Joint frames must coincide within 2 mm and 0.1 ∘ 0.1^{\circ} at the initial pose. Semantic review. A reviewer checks the evidence bundle against the task conditions. The bundle contains event-selected keyframes from each camera, the contact records, sampled trajectories and constraint states, and the quality-gate results. After refinement, the final-video check applies the same task conditions to the refined video, because refinement can alter motion that passed simulation. B.2 Budgets, Stopping, and Human Involvement Each task runs under a controller budget that limits scene-specification revisions, simulator launches, technical retries, and wall-clock time. Configured limits and consumed usage are recorded for each run. A run stops when all checks pass or a limit is reached; unresolved failures stay in the run record. Both PhysLoop and the standalone baselines permit at most five video-generation calls per task. Users can review candidates during verification. Each human judgment, the change it requested, and the resulting revision are stored in the run record, so automated and human-requested revisions can be counted separately.
