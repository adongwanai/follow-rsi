From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive
Computation
Minh-Anh Nguyen1
1Nanyang Technological University, Singapore
Abstract
Transferring LLMs into System One decision
models typically replaces the language mod-
eling head with a decision head while retain-
ing the full backbone, making every decision
pay for all layers. Yet it remains unclear how
much depth such decisions require. We find
that decision information is concentrated in
intermediate layers, with early layers build-
ing essential features and the final six layers
removable without loss, while independently
trained smaller models largely mimic the full
model rather than developing specialized deci-
sion capabilities. These findings suggest that ef-
ficiency requires not simply smaller backbones,
but shared computation across different depths.
Building on this, we propose NESTONE, which
jointly trains a full model with prefix sub-
models that share its final layers and decision
head through self-distillation. A lightweight
gate reads the hidden state at each exit to deter-
mine whether additional computation is needed,
allowing one model to operate at multiple lay-
ers. Across seven decision benchmarks, our ap-
proach matches or outperforms independently
trained models at every depth, while achieving
better accuracy-efficiency trade-offs through
adaptive computation.

flagging unsafe prompts, and recognizing intents,
often under tight latency constraints (TypeSafe AI,
2026). System One decision models address this
need by fine-tuning an LLM to predict a distribu-
tion over candidate options at a single position,

This raises a fundamental question: how much
computational depth does a System One decision
actually require? Fine-tuning is necessary to equip
an LLM with decision capabilities that its base
model lacks (Section 3), but whether all inherited
layers are necessary remains unclear. A natural
way to reduce inference cost is to truncate the
backbone, trading computational depth for poten-
tially lower accuracy. As shown in the left panel
of Figure 1, this trade-off is highly non-uniform:
reducing the model from 32 to 24 layers largely
preserves performance, whereas further truncation
causes substantial degradation, with accuracy drop-

ping from 84.3% to 72.8% and 61.5% at 16 and 12
layers, respectively. This highlights the limitation
of fixed-depth inference, which imposes the same
computational budget on all inputs. Yet, not every
decision requires the same amount of computation.
This motivates adaptive depth allocation (Figure 1,
right), which allows computation to stop once suf-
ficient depth is reached, reducing inference cost
while preserving accuracy.
We find that decision computation is concen-
trated in intermediate layers, while independently
trained smaller models largely replicate the full
model rather than develop specialized decision
capabilities (Section 3). First, decisions emerge
within a narrow band of middle layers: integrated-
gradient attribution places 50% of the decision by
layer 20 and 80% by layer 23. Consistently, ablat-
ing layers 24–29 leaves accuracy unchanged, while
ablating layers 15–23 eliminates the decision mar-
gin (Section 3.1). Second, independently trained
smaller models behave more like truncated full
models than independent specialists: 89–92% of
their correct decisions are already made by the full
model, and their distinct wins exceed full-model
seed disagreement by only 2–3 points, mostly on
low-confidence decisions (Section 3.2). These find-
ings show that training smaller models or truncat-
ing the backbone alone is insufficient. Instead, deci-
sion models should learn to make reliable decisions
at multiple depths, adapting computation to input
difficulty.
Based on these findings, we propose NESTONE,
a framework that transforms an LLM into a multi-
depth System One decision model.
NESTONE
jointly trains the full model and nested prefix
sub-models with shared final layers and decision
head, combining decision supervision and self-
distillation from the full model. This enables a
single training run to produce multiple exits within
one checkpoint. At each exit, a lightweight gate
reads the hidden state to determine whether fur-
ther computation is needed before producing the
decision. Our contributions are threefold: (i) We
show that decision information is concentrated in
intermediate layers and that independently trained
smaller models largely reproduce, rather than spe-
cialize in, the full model’s decisions; (ii) We
propose NESTONE, a nested multi-depth train-
ing framework that combines decision supervision,
self-distillation, and a hidden-state stopping gate
for transfering from LLMs to System One Mod-
els efficiency; (iii) We demonstrate that NESTONE
matches or outperforms independently trained mod-
els at every depth while reducing inference cost
through adaptive early exiting, using a single train-
ing run to obtain multiple models.

Background and Related Work
System One Decision Models.
System One mod-
els take a state, a typed question, and a set of
options, and return a distribution over the op-
tions in a single forward pass without generat-
ing text (TypeSafe AI, 2026).
Following Jev,
a growing number of such models are built by
adapting a pretrained LLM. Open recipes read
the option-symbol logits of an instruction-tuned
LLM at the last position, with no training, or
add LoRA adapters and a decision head initial-
ized from the language-model head (Geuter et al.,
2026). Intern-Decision (InternLM Team, 2026)
fully fine-tunes Qwen3.5 (Qwen Team, 2026) at
0.8B, 2B, and 4B parameters, Microsoft-Decision-
1 is post-trained from Qwen3.5-9B (Microsoft,
2026), CLM trains a decision model contrastively
from Qwen3-8B (Kwok et al., 2026), and CS1
trains a 4B model on 12 million clinical deci-
sions (Zhang et al., 2026). Other work changes
how options are read out. Jeva encodes the request
and each option separately so that the decision is
permutation-equivariant, can abstain, and can reuse
option states across requests (Li and Wang, 2026);
Prism-1 scores each option in its own attention
block so that neither option order nor added rivals
change the result (Teng et al., 2026); and Consider-
then-Choose removes order dependence at infer-
ence without training (Huang et al., 2026).
Evaluating System One Models.
A second line
of work asks where System One models can re-
place LLMs. System1-Arena compares ten Sys-
tem One models with open and closed LLMs
through a shared decision interface and finds that
the fast models suffice for many single-step deci-
sions but fall behind on long-horizon tasks (Chen
et al., 2026). Other studies evaluate System One
models as decision points in agent harnesses (Li,
2026), and test their transfer to low-resource lan-
guages (Fadli et al., 2026). These studies treat each
model as a black box and measure what it outputs.
Layer Pruning.
The deeper layers of LLMs are
known to be partly redundant. Removing a block of
late layers costs little on question answering (Gro-
mov et al., 2024; Men et al., 2024), and merg-

ing or pruning layers followed by distillation re-
covers most of the original accuracy at a smaller
size (Yang et al., 2024; Muralidharan et al., 2024;
Xia et al., 2024). These methods produce one
smaller model with one fixed depth, and they select
layers by similarity or importance scores computed
for generation rather than by where a decision is
computed.
Existing work on System One models focuses
on how to train an LLM into a decision model,
whether through readout design, training data, or
architecture, and on how the resulting models be-
have. Each of these models keeps the full depth of
its backbone, and cost is reduced only by choos-
ing a smaller backbone. To our knowledge, no
prior work asks where inside the LLM the deci-
sion is computed, or what a smaller decision model
learns relative to the full one. We address both
questions first (Section 3). The answers then guide
an efficient way to turn one LLM into a System
One model with several depths: nested prefix train-
ing with self-distillation, and a gate that reads the
hidden state before the readout is computed (Sec-
tion 4).
3
Understanding Depth in System One
Models
We investigate what enables an LLM to become
a System One decision model by comparing the
model before and after decision fine-tuning, along
with smaller variants obtained by progressively re-
moving layers from the fine-tuned model. Specifi-
cally, we ask two questions: (i) Where in the net-
work are decisions computed (Section 3.1)? (ii)
What do smaller models learn compared to the full
model (Section 3.2)?
Setting. We study Qwen3.5-4B (Qwen Team,
2026), whose 32 layers we index 0–31, and Intern-
Decision-4B (InternLM Team, 2026), a released de-
cision model built on the same backbone. To estab-
lish a controlled reference, we re-train Qwen3.5-4B
using the same training recipe as Intern-Decision-
4B. We refer to this re-trained model as full32,
which serves as the starting point and reference for
all our models. Specifically, full32 is fully fine-
tuned with cross-entropy on the option symbols
(AdamW, learning rate 2×10−6 with cosine decay,
2 epochs, about 131K tokens per step) on 44,941
decisions drawn from public training splits (Zhang
et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2023;
Ji et al., 2023; Casanueva et al., 2020; FitzGer-
ald et al., 2023; Clark et al., 2019) and dedupli-
cated against all test states. It reaches 84.3 ± 0.2%
over three seeds, compared with 90.0% for Intern-
Decision-4B, whose larger training set covers the
J-Hard and Typed benchmarks better. We con-
sider two layer-cutting strategies: a middle cut,
Mk, which retains layers 0, . . . , k−3 and the fi-
nal two layers (30–31), and a tail cut, Tk, which
retains only the first k layers (0, . . . , k−1).
3.1
The Decision Is Written at Mid Depth
When Does the Decision Become Readable?
We examine when the decision-related informa-
tion emerges among layers and when it becomes
accessible to the model’s own readout. At each
layer, we extract the residual state at the readout
position and evaluate it using two complementary
methods. The logit lens (Nostalgebraist, 2020) ap-
plies the model’s final normalization and output
head to the intermediate state, measuring whether
the model can already produce the correct answer
at that depth. In contrast, a linear probe (Alain and
Bengio, 2016), trained to predict the gold option
from the same state, measures how much decision-
relevant information is linearly recoverable, regard-
less of whether the model’s own readout can access
it. Thus, the gap between the probe and logit-lens
accuracies indicates information that has been en-
coded in the residual stream but is not yet usable
by the model’s output head.
Figure 2(a,b) shows a consistent pattern across
the base model and Intern-Decision-4B. At layer
15, the probe substantially outperforms the logit
lens (76.1% vs. 26.2% for the base model; 77.9%
vs. 38.3% for Intern-Decision-4B), indicating that
decision information is already encoded but not
yet accessible to the model’s readout. This gap
closes over layers 19–23: for Intern-Decision-4B,
the probe reaches 90.1% at layer 20, matching its
final accuracy (90.0%), while the logit lens rises
from 64.4% at layer 19 to 89.4% at layer 23. The
two readouts then differ by only 0.1 points (vs. 2.8
points for the base model). Neither improves be-
yond layer 23, suggesting that layers 24–31 add no
linearly recoverable decision information. Fine-
tuning raises the probe plateau by about seven
points (89.5% vs. 83.2% at layer 23) without shift-
ing the depth at which decisions become readable.

The two interventions reveal distinct layer roles.
In Intern-Decision-4B, removing layers 24–29
without retraining leaves accuracy unchanged,
while removing layers 24–31 costs only 0.6 points.
Similarly, full32 retains 84.4% accuracy under T24,
compared with 84.2% uncut; cutting into the mid-
dle band is substantially more damaging, with T22
losing 11.6 points. Mean ablation confirms this
pattern: jointly ablating layers 24–29 slightly im-
proves margin and accuracy, whereas ablating lay-
ers 15–23 eliminates the entire margin and reduces
accuracy by 57.8 points. Early layers, however,
remain essential despite their small direct margin
contributions: ablating layer 0 costs 46.6 points
and layer 6 costs 7.8, reflecting their role in con-
structing features used by later layers. Consistent
with this, a 16-layer model trained only on layers
8–21 and 30–31, the layers most affected by fine-
tuning, achieves just 35.7%, compared with 73.8%
for the M16 prefix model.
Takeaway. A decision-tuned LLM writes its
decision in a band of middle layers (15–23) on top
of feature-building early layers, and its last body
layers carry no decision.

Methodology
Section 3 shows that intermediate layers already
encode sufficient information to identify the cor-
rect option, while smaller decision models are best
viewed as prefixes of the full model that should
learn to imitate it. Building on these findings, NE-
STONE trains nested prefix sub-models within the
full model using self-distillation to facilitate infor-
mation sharing across depths (Sections 4.1 and 4.2).
A lightweight gate then selects the exit depth for
each decision based on question difficulty (Sec-
tion 4.3).

τ, runs the coda, and returns arg max pe; other-
wise, it continues from the saved states. The final
exit always answers.
Answering at exit e costs e + 3 layers: the pre-
fix plus the coda. Because the gate reads the hid-
den state before the coda, evaluating it requires
only a dot product and a sigmoid operation. A sin-
gle threshold τ is shared across exits and selected
on held-out data as the most aggressive value for
which macro accuracy remains within δ points of
always exiting at the final intermediate depth.

One Checkpoint versus Separate Models
One Checkpoint Matches or Outperforms Sepa-

5.2
Choosing Depth per Decision
The Hidden State Predicts Whether an Exit Is
Correct.
We first measure how well each signal
separates correct from wrong answers at an exit,
using AUROC on the test halves. The state gate
reaches 0.86, 0.89, and 0.88 at exits 9, 13, and 21,
against 0.85, 0.86, and 0.84 for the calibrated top
probability, and 0.86, 0.86, and 0.84 for the confi-
dence gate that combines top probability, margin,
and entropy. The hidden state thus carries more
information about correctness than the exit’s own
confidence, especially at exit 13 and 21, and it is
available before the coda is computed.
Stopping Rules.
We compare the state gate with
three rules based on the exit distribution pe: Max-
prob, which uses the calibrated top probability;
confidence gate, a logistic regression on the top
probability, top-two margin, and entropy; and State
+ confidence, which combines these features with
the hidden state ze. All rules use the same train-
ing labels, class-balanced weights, and fitting data.
They evaluate exits 9, 13, and 21 in order, stop-
ping at the first score above τ or answering at exit
21 otherwise; τ is selected as in Section 4.3. The
key difference is inference cost: distribution-based
rules must run the coda to obtain pe, adding two
layers at each checked exit, whereas the state gate
reads ze before the coda and runs it only once, at
the selected exit. Fixed exits 13 and 21 serve as
references.
The State Gate Gives the Best Trade-off.
Ta-
ble 4 compares the gating rules at two accuracy
thresholds. The state gate achieves the lowest av-
erage depth at both δ = 0.25 and δ = 2, using
21.6 and 20.2 layers, respectively, compared with
24 layers for the fixed exit. On hard benchmarks, it
uses 23.9–24.0 layers, whereas confidence-based
rules incur two additional layers per readout, result-
ing in an average depth of 26.6–27.9 layers. On the
four largest benchmarks, which account for 96%
of decisions, the state gate preserves the fixed-exit
accuracy, with changes of only +0.08 and +0.03
points at the two thresholds. The other rules also
remain within 0.13 points of the fixed exit, but
achieve savings differently: confidence-based rules
save computation on easy decisions while spending
more on hard ones, whereas the state gate reduces
depth on easy decisions (to 20.2 and 17.5 layers)
without incurring additional computation on hard
decisions. Randomly mixing exits 13 and 21 to
match the same average depth instead reduces ac-
curacy by 3.0 and 4.7 points.
The Accuracy Drop Is Concentrated on Small
Benchmarks.
The state gate’s macro-accuracy
drop (−1.56 and −1.68 points) comes almost en-
tirely from the three smallest benchmarks, which
contain only 430 of 12,351 decisions but account
for three of seven macro-averaged benchmarks. At
δ = 0.25, it misses seven additional decisions on
these benchmarks but loses none on larger ones.
Compute savings depend on traffic mix: average
depth ranges from 24 layers on hard-only traffic to
20.2 on easy-only traffic (22.1 for an even mix),
and to 17.5 layers at δ = 2 (20.7 for an even
mix; Figure 1, right). Since the selected thresh-
old varies across training splits (0.80 vs. 0.92), it
should be calibrated on held-out deployment-like
traffic. Adding the full model as a final exit further
improves the trade-off: at δ = 2, the gate matches
its accuracy on the four largest benchmarks (−0.01
points) while using 23.9 layers on average, 19.8 on
easy decisions, and 31.3 on hard ones.

6
Conclusion
We studied adaptive computation for System One
decision-making in LLMs. Our analysis reveals
that decisions primarily emerge in middle layers,
with later layers contributing little additional in-
formation, and that smaller independently trained
models largely replicate the full model’s decisions.
Motivated by these findings, NESTONE jointly
trains nested prefix sub-models via self-distillation
and employs a hidden-state gate for adaptive early
exiting. Across three seeds, a single checkpoint
matches or outperforms independently trained mod-
els at all exit depths, improving 12-layer accuracy
by 2.9 points and approaching the 32-layer model
within 0.6 points at 24 layers. Adaptive exiting
further reduces computation by 16–27% on easy
decisions without sacrificing accuracy, while re-
taining fixed-depth exits for harder cases. These
results highlight the benefits of nested training for
aligning shallow exits with full-model decisions.
Future work will investigate end-to-end latency,
gate calibration under real-world traffic, and scal-
ing to larger models and finer-grained exits.
