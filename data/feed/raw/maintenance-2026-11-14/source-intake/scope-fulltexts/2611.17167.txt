Weiming Lu Jun Xiao Yongliang Shen Abstract Decision models answer a question with a typed output, such as a choice among options with a probability for each, so one model can choose an agent’s actions, evaluate actions already taken, or score every action to teach a policy with reinforcement learning. Prior work has studied them mainly as actors, judging their usefulness by how well they act. We ask whether a decision model that acts poorly can still teach well. We compare three decision models and three general LLMs as actors and judges on ALFWorld, and use JEV as a teacher on ALFWorld and WebShop. As actors, all six models are pulled away from a subgoal that must come first by an easier one. As judges that see the whole trajectory, the same models credit the steps toward that subgoal, and asking whether each action helped, rather than whether the task will succeed, separates useful from useless actions. As a teacher, JEV raises a Qwen2.5-1.5B policy over GRPO from 72.8% to 82.9% success on ALFWorld and from 56.8% to 71.4% on WebShop. These results suggest that a poor actor can still teach well, because judging an action after the episode ends reveals the consequences that acting must anticipate. Code is available at https://github.com/ZJU-REAL/decision-agents . 1 1 footnotetext: The first three authors have equal contributions. † Corresponding author. 1 Introduction Decision models are pretrained models that answer a question about a context with a typed output, such as a choice among given options with a probability for each, instead of generated text, and they do so quickly and cheaply ( Almeida, 2026 ; OpenAI, 2026 ; Perplexity AI, 2026 ) . An LLM agent can therefore query the same decision model in two ways: to choose its next action, or to evaluate an action that has already been taken. Its evaluations can in turn teach a policy during reinforcement learning, by scoring each action of every rollout. This use matters because outcome rewards give every action in a trajectory the same credit ( Harutyunyan et al., 2019 ; Shao et al., 2024 ) , while step-level judgments are otherwise costly, requiring labels for each environment ( Lightman et al., 2024 ) or an LLM judge queried at every action ( Tan et al., 2026 ; Zhu et al., 2026a ) . Yet decision models have been evaluated mainly as actors ( Zhu et al., 2026b ; Ling et al., 2026 ) , and in that role they often fail: they are drawn to commands that mention task objects, skip prerequisite subgoals, and complete subgoals in the wrong order ( Yang et al., 2026 ; Li et al., 2026 ; Ling et al., 2026 ) . These failures do not settle how useful a decision model is when it evaluates rather than acts. On the same ALFWorld evaluation tasks, JEV completes 42.9% of the tasks when it chooses the actions itself. When it instead scores every action of a 1.5B policy during training, after seeing the whole trajectory, the policy completes 82.9% of the tasks if JEV is asked whether each action helped achieve the goal, compared with 72.8% for training with outcome rewards alone. Asked instead whether the task will succeed after each action, JEV yields a policy that completes only 69.3%. The same model can thus act poorly yet teach well, but only when it is asked the right question. This paper asks why a decision model that acts poorly can still teach well, and which queries make this possible . We answer these questions by querying the same decision models in each way on the same tasks (Figure 1 ). As an Actor , a model chooses each action before seeing its effect. As a Judge , it evaluates actions in recorded trajectories, and we vary two choices: what it sees, either the trajectory up to the action ( Prefix ) or the whole trajectory ( Hindsight ), and what it is asked, either whether the task will succeed after the action ( Value ) or whether the action helped achieve the goal ( Progress ). As a Teacher , it applies the same queries to the actions of a policy being trained, and its scores, centered within each rollout group, are added to the outcome advantage of GRPO ( Shao et al., 2024 ) . Each analysis changes one factor, such as the goal, the question, or the context, and holds the rest fixed. We evaluate three decision models and three general LLMs given the same questions, two of them the base LLMs of decision models, on ALFWorld ( Shridhar et al., 2021 ) and WebShop ( Yao et al., 2022 ) , where some subgoals must be completed in order. Acting fails when an easier subgoal draws the model away from the required one. All six models succeed far less often when one subgoal must be completed before another, such as heating a mug before placing it on a table; JEV, for example, completes 70.8% of tasks with two independent subgoals but only 14.7% of tasks with ordered ones. The models do not lack knowledge of the required step. In states where a single action would complete the easier subgoal, removing that subgoal from the goal moves most of each model’s probability to the required step; for JEV, the probability on the required state change rises from 14.6% to 77.6%. A reminder about the dependency changes success by only − 2.8 % -2.8\% to + 5.2 % +5.2\% . To act correctly, the model must anticipate that the easier action leaves the task incomplete before any consequence is visible, whereas a judge evaluates the action after its consequences have occurred. Whether a judge avoids this failure depends on what it sees and what it is asked. Seeing the whole trajectory helps Judges recognize contributions that the same models overlook as Actors: these contributions can be unclear from the prefix alone but become apparent from what follows. Seeing the consequences is not enough, however. With the whole trajectory visible, asking whether each action helped separates actions that complete a subgoal from actions that change nothing, with JEV scoring the former 0.36 higher within a trajectory, whereas asking whether the task will succeed gives a gap of only 0.05, because every action in the trajectory then has the same answer, the outcome. All six models show both effects, and the two decision models with a disclosed base LLM judge more accurately than that LLM given the same question, at 28% to 43% lower cost per score. The same choices determine how much a decision model teaches. The question and context that judge best also teach best. The ALFWorld result above holds on WebShop, where JEV’s Progress scores with the whole trajectory raise success from 56.8% with outcome rewards alone to 71.4%, against 68.3% with the Value question, and the 1.5B policy taught this way outperforms a 7B policy trained with outcome rewards alone on both environments. With only the prefix visible, the two questions perform similarly. The gains are largest on the ALFWorld tasks with ordered subgoals, where the policy completes 86.8%, 6.9% to 16.2% more than with the other questions and contexts, although JEV completes only 14.7% of these tasks as an actor. Taken together, these results suggest that a decision model that acts poorly can still teach well, because judging an action after the episode ends reveals the consequences that acting must anticipate . This benefit requires asking about each action rather than about the outcome, and it is largest for the actions the model handles worst as an actor, those whose value depends on what follows. Two implications follow for using decision models, and possibly other frozen judges, in agent training. First, a model’s suitability as a supervisor should be assessed by how well it judges recorded trajectories, not only by how well it acts. Second, step-level supervision can be obtained cheaply by querying a frozen decision model after each episode, without step labels or a learned critic, for 22 to 30 USD of queries per training run in our experiments. We evaluate six models that answer questions in this form, grouped by how the decision interface is obtained (Table 1 ). Dedicated decision models. These models are released by their providers specifically as decision models and are queried through their own decision APIs. JEV ( ) ( TypeSafe AI, 2026 ) is the earliest public decision model; it is closed, and neither its base LLM nor its adaptation procedure is disclosed. Decider v1.2 ( ) ( Perplexity AI, 2026 ) has open weights and is fully fine-tuned from Qwen3.8-27B ( Qwen Team, 2026 ) . GPT Decisions ( ) ( OpenAI, 2026 ) is closed and built on GPT-6 Luna, with an undisclosed adaptation procedure. General LLMs as decision models. A general LLM can answer the same questions when queried in a suitable way, with its weights and architecture unchanged. We apply two common methods to the base LLMs of Decider v1.2 and GPT Decisions. Prompting ( ) instructs the model to output its choice and probabilities in JSON, which we then parse. Constrained readout ( ) uses the first-token probabilities returned for valid answer labels. For multiple-choice questions, we select the highest-scoring returned option label and normalize the probabilities over the returned option labels. For yes/no questions, we normalize over Yes and No to obtain the score. Applying both methods to Qwen3.8-27B gives Qwen prompted ( ) and Qwen constrained ( ), and applying prompting to GPT-6 Luna gives GPT Luna ( ). Reasoning is disabled for all three. JEV has no such counterpart because its base LLM is undisclosed. Comparing each dedicated decision model with its own corresponding LLM queried in these ways shows what the dedicated model adds. For Decider v1.2, the adaptation is fine-tuning; for GPT Decisions, the adaptation procedure has not been disclosed. Appendix B gives the model identifiers and interface details. 2.2 Benchmarks We use two text-based environments in which a task contains several requirements, some of which must be satisfied in a fixed order. ALFWorld. ALFWorld ( Shridhar et al., 2021 ) is a household environment in which an agent completes goals such as “put a clean sponge on the cart” by issuing text commands such as go to , take , and clean . Its six task types fall into three groups according to how their requirements relate. Pick and Look involve a single object. Clean , Heat , and Cool are tasks with dependencies: satisfying the placement requirement depends on first completing the required change to the object’s state. Pick2 also has two requirements, one for each object, but they can be satisfied in either order. Comparing Pick2 with Clean , Heat , and Cool lets us examine how models handle requirements with an ordering dependency (Section 3.1 ). WebShop. WebShop ( Yao et al., 2022 ) is a shopping environment in which an agent searches a product catalogue, opens product pages, selects options such as size and color, and purchases an item. Each instruction specifies the product attributes, options, and a price limit. The required options must be selected before purchase. The final reward measures how well the purchased item matches the instruction, and a task succeeds only when the reward is full. Appendix A gives the training and evaluation splits, inference seeds, and action budgets. 3 Understanding Decision Models on Agentic Tasks We study each decision model in three roles that differ in what the model is asked and how its answers are used (see Figure 1 for an overview and Appendix B for the prompt templates). Roles of Decision Models • Actor. At each step, the decision model answers a multiple-choice question about which action to take next. It receives the goal, the interaction history, and the current observation, then selects one of the valid actions and assigns a probability to each option. The selected action is executed in the environment (Section 3.1 ). • Judge. The decision model scores the actions in trajectories recorded from other policies, as in LLM-as-a-judge ( Zheng et al., 2023 ) . For each executed action, it answers a yes/no question about that action, such as whether it helped achieve the goal, and returns a score in [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] (Section 3.2 ). • Teacher. The decision model scores actions as a Judge does, but on trajectories from the policy being trained. The scores are combined with outcome rewards in GRPO ( Shao et al., 2024 ) as step-level supervision. The decision model stays fixed during training and is not used at test time (Section 3.3 ). In Clean , Heat , and Cool tasks, the actor must clean, heat, or cool an object and place it at a destination; we refer to them as tasks with dependency . Tasks with dependency and Pick2 tasks both have two subgoals. In “put a heated mug on the side table,” the mug must be heated and on the side table. In “put two pencils in the drawer,” both pencils must be in the drawer. The pencils can be placed in either order, but the mug must be heated before placement. Higher success on Pick2 suggests it is ordering that makes these tasks difficult. Failed trajectories show how this order is broken. Suppose the actor holds the mug at the side table. It can place the mug immediately in one action. Alternatively, it can go to the microwave, heat it, and return before placing it. In many failures, the actor places the unheated mug directly, leaving the task incomplete.
We next ask whether the easier subgoal draws actors away from the subgoal that must be met first but takes steps to accomplish (Section 3.1.1 ), and whether reminding them of the dependency helps (Section 3.1.2 ). Figure 3: (a) Action probabilities with (left) and without (right) the placement subgoal. (b) Success rates with the original prompt, a dependency hint, and a hint plus heated-mug example. 3.1.1 Does the Easier Subgoal Distract Actors? We collect states in which the actor could satisfy the easier subgoal with a single action, while the subgoal that must come first is still unmet. In ALFWorld, the actor stands at the destination holding an object that still needs to be cleaned, heated, or cooled. This yields 116 states from 66 tasks, and every actor is queried at every state. At each state, we query the actor under two goals: the full goal and the same goal without the easier subgoal (e.g., “put a heated mug on the side table” versus “heat a mug”). The history, observation, candidate actions, and remaining budget are identical in both queries. We group the probabilities assigned to candidate actions into three categories: actions toward the subgoal that must come first, the action that satisfies the easier subgoal, and other actions. Figure 3 a shows the results. With the full goal, JEV ( ) assigns 63.8% of its probability to the easier subgoal and only 14.6% to the subgoal that must come first. Without the easier subgoal, these become 2.5% and 77.6%. All six actors shift in the same direction. The actors can therefore identify the required step when it is the only subgoal, but adding an easier subgoal to the goal draws probability away from it. 3.1.2 Can a Hint Prevent This Error? We next test whether a hint helps actors avoid this error. We compare three prompts on the same 116 states with the full goal: the original question, the question with “Consider possible dependencies among task requirements” appended, and the hint followed by an ordering example. The example starts with a mug neither heated nor on the table, and explains that it must be heated before being placed on the table. Each continuation uses the same history, environment state, candidate actions, and remaining budget, with its prompt applied at every step. Figure 3 b compares success rates across the three conditions. The hint alone changes success by − 2.8 % -2.8\% to + 5.2 % +5.2\% across the six actors. Adding the example raises success from 41.7% to 58.3% for Qwen constrained ( ), from 46.6% to 58.6% for Qwen prompted ( ), and from 20.8% to 32.1% for GPT Decisions ( ). JEV ( ) improves more modestly, from 19.0% to 24.1%, while GPT Luna ( ) changes little and Decider v1.2 ( ) declines from 89.7% to 85.3%. An ordering example thus helps some actors, but gains vary across models and substantial failures remain. 3.2 Decision Models as Judges Finding 2 As Judges, the same models can recognize contributions they overlook as Actors. This depends on what they see and what they are asked. Seeing the whole trajectory reveals how earlier actions contribute to later goal completion. Asking whether each action helped achieve the goal then distinguishes their contributions more clearly than asking whether the task will succeed, which gives similar scores to actions within a trajectory. Figure 4: Prefix (left) and Hindsight (right) scores on the same states in Section 3.1.1 . Section 3.1 shows that Actors struggle when subgoals must be completed in a particular order. However, this leaves open how the same models perform as Judges when they encounter such tasks. We reuse the new trajectories generated under the original prompt in Section 3.1.2 . For each successful continuation, we evaluate the first action taken from the corresponding diagnostic state and ask whether this action helped achieve the goal. We compare two choices of context given to the six Judges: the trajectory up to the action ( Prefix ) or the whole trajectory ( Hindsight ), with outcome labels masked. Prefix therefore shows how the models judge an action at the same decision point studied in Section 3.1.1 . The model, full goal, target action, and question remain the same. Figure 4 shows higher mean contribution scores with Hindsight . JEV’s ( ) mean score rises from 0.41 to 0.59. All six models show the same pattern. It seems that when observing full trajectories, decision models can assign higher scores to such actions. But it also raises several questions. Full trajectory may substantially suggest the outcome of the task. Could this lead models to give every action in a trajectory an evenly high score (Section 3.2.1 )? What additional information do models gain from seeing the full trajectory (Section 3.2.2 )? 3.2.1 Can Judges Distinguish Different Action Contributions? As Actors, decision models choose actions to maximize the chance of completing the task. When using the same models as Judges, we can retain this focus on task success and ask whether the task will succeed after the action ( Value ), or ask whether the action helped achieve the goal ( Progress ).
We compare these two questions using the same Hindsight context to test whether shifting from predicting task success, as in the Actor role, to evaluating action contribution changes how Judges score individual actions. Figure 5: JEV ( ) as a Judge with Hindsight context on ALFWorld. (a) Mean action scores. (b) Milestone Recall@ R R , grouped by the number of milestone actions R R in a trajectory. (c) Within-trajectory score gaps relative to no-op actions. To address this problem, we group actions into three categories. A milestone action achieves a subgoal ( Wang et al., 2026b ) . A no-op action leaves the environment and observation unchanged. Actions of neither type are grouped as other actions. Appendix C.1 describes the labeling procedure. We compare the two questions on 462 ALFWorld trajectories, each containing at least one milestone and one no-op action. Figure 5 a shows JEV’s ( ) mean scores. Under Value, milestone, other, and no-op actions receive similar scores (0.632, 0.604, and 0.577). Under Progress, the scores spread to 0.659, 0.440, and 0.298. All six Judges show a larger gap between milestone and no-op actions under Progress (Appendix E.2 ). We next check how many milestone actions a Judge places at the top of each trajectory. For a trajectory with R R milestone actions, we take its R R highest-scored actions and measure the fraction that are milestones (Recall@ R R ). Figure 5 b groups trajectories by R R , from simpler tasks with two milestone actions to more complex ones with eight. Progress finds more milestones than Value at every R R , by 8.0% to 16.0%. Within the same trajectory, Progress also scores milestone actions 0.36 higher than no-op actions, whereas Value scores them only 0.05 higher (Figure 5 c). Judges do not inherently assign similar scores to actions in the same trajectory. When the question retains the Actor’s focus on task success, scores differ little across action types. Asking about each action’s contribution produces clearer distinctions, suggesting that the question should be adapted when decision models are used to judge individual actions. 3.2.2 What Do Judges Gain from Observing Full Trajectories? This example connects the Actor’s difficulty with subgoal dependencies to the Judge’s use of Hindsight: observing the full trajectory helps JEV recognize the dependency between heating the mug and the preceding steps needed to accomplish it, which is not apparent in its judgments from immediate feedback alone. 3.3 Decision Models as Teachers Finding 3 The judgments that best distinguish action contributions also provide the most effective supervision for policy learning. Scoring whether each action helped achieve the goal after seeing the whole trajectory produces the strongest policies, with the largest gains on tasks that the Actor struggles to complete. The trained policies outperform the decision model that teaches them, especially on tasks it rarely completes as an Actor. Sections 3.1 and 3.2 show that decision models struggle to act on tasks with dependent subgoals, yet can avoid this limitation and can judge action contributions differently when given an appropriate question and the whole trajectory. We next test whether these judgments provide useful supervision for policy learning. We use decision models as Teachers to score actions in trajectories generated by the policy being trained, and combine these scores with outcome rewards as step-level supervision. The decision model stays fixed during training and is not queried at test time. Training with Teacher scores. We add the Teacher’s scores to GRPO ( Shao et al., 2024 ) so that actions in the same trajectory can receive different training signals. On the same ALFWorld evaluation tasks, JEV ( ) completes 42.9% as an Actor, whereas the 1.5B policy trained with its scores completes 82.9%, including tasks with dependencies, on which JEV performs worst as an Actor (Figure 2 ). The Teacher thus does not need to solve a task itself to provide a useful training signal for it. This gap reflects the difference between the two roles. On tasks with dependent subgoals, an easier subgoal draws Actors away from the actions needed to complete the subgoal that ought to be met first (Section 3.1 ). Yet these actions can receive greater credit when the same models judge their contribution after seeing the whole trajectory: the later steps reveal how they support the subgoal that needs to be met first (Section 3.2 ). We use these judgments as Teacher scores, giving the policy additional supervision about which actions contribute to the goal. The resulting improvement on tasks with dependency connects the three roles: a decision model that struggles to choose the required actions can still recognize their contribution when observing full trajectories and provide useful supervision for learning to act. 4 Takeaways for Using Decision Models in Agents Beyond the above findings, our analyses suggest several lessons for using decision models in agents. • Actors know the required step but are drawn to the easier one. Actors choose the step that must come first when it is the only subgoal in the goal, and general reminders do not prevent the error when an easier subgoal is present. When a decision model must act, restructuring the goal, for example into ordered subgoals, is more promising than merely adding instructions to the prompt (Section 3.1 ). • The question sets the granularity of the signal. A question about the eventual outcome gives similar scores to all actions in a trajectory and largely repeats the outcome reward, while a question about each action’s contribution separates actions within a trajectory (Sections 3.2.1 and 3.3.1 ). • Hindsight judgments target the actor’s own failure. Seeing the whole trajectory helps Judges recognize the contributions of actions toward the subgoal that needs to be met first, which Actors may overlook in favor of an easier subgoal. Decision models are therefore best used to score actions after an episode ends, as in RL training, and are less suited to filtering actions online, where only the prefix is available (Sections 3.2.2 and 3.3.2 ). • Judges can be screened before training. The Judge measures on recorded trajectories identified the question and context that trained the best policy, at a small fraction of the cost of an RL run. Compare candidate judges and questions this way first, and choose by judging quality, not acting ability (Sections 3.2 and 3.3.2 ). • Step-level supervision becomes affordable. Decision models judge more accurately and at lower cost than prompted general LLMs, and the Teacher is not needed at test time. Scoring every action during RL training is therefore practical, and can let a small policy outperform a larger one trained with outcome rewards alone (Section 3.3.1 and Appendix E.5 ). 5 Related Work Decision models. JEV ( Almeida, 2026 ) takes a question and a context and returns a choice among options or a score, providing a single interface for both selecting and evaluating actions. Similar models include GPT Decisions ( OpenAI, 2026 ) and Decider v1.2 ( Perplexity AI, 2026 ) . Prior work mainly uses decision models to act. Yang et al. (2026) study JEV’s action selection, finding failures caused by surface shortcuts and improvements from explicit subgoals and simulated action outcomes. JevAgentHarness ( Zhu et al., 2026b ) trains decision models with correct-option labels and JEV’s option probabilities, and lets them share online control with an LLM. We instead study decision models in three roles, focusing as Actors on goals whose subgoals must be met in order, and show that their performance as Actors does not reflect their value as Judges and Teachers. LLM judges and process supervision. LLM judges ( Zheng et al., 2023 ; Yan et al., 2026 ) and AI feedback ( Bai et al., 2022 ; Zhang et al., 2024 ) provide evaluation without task-specific training, but they are mostly used to compare final responses, not to score every step of a long trajectory. Process reward models score intermediate steps and improve over outcome supervision in mathematical reasoning ( Lightman et al., 2024 ; He et al., 2024 ) , but they require step-level labels for each domain. Our Judge analysis shows that decision models can score individual actions without such labels, and that the question asked and the context shown determine how useful the scores are. Credit assignment for LLM agents. LLM agents act through long interactions with their environment ( Yao et al., 2023 ; Shridhar et al., 2021 ; Zhou et al., 2024 ) and are commonly trained with outcome rewards using group-based policy optimization such as GRPO ( Shao et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2026a ) , where every step of a trajectory receives the same advantage. Recent work derives finer credit from the structure of the rollouts ( Feng et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2026b ) ; GiGPO, for example, compares actions taken from the same state across trajectories. Hindsight credit assignment ( Harutyunyan et al., 2019 ) uses what happens after an action to judge its contribution. We obtain step-level credit from a frozen decision model and find it most useful when the model, as in hindsight credit assignment, sees the rest of the trajectory. Because the Teacher does not need to solve the task itself, our setting also relates to weak-to-strong generalization ( Burns et al., 2024 ) , where a strong model trained on labels from a weaker one can surpass it. 6 Conclusion We presented a systematic study of decision models for long-horizon agents in three roles: Actor, Judge, and Teacher. As Actors, decision models often satisfy an easier subgoal before the one that needs to be met first, and a general reminder in the prompt does not reliably prevent this. As Judges, the same models can recognize the contributions of actions they overlook when acting, provided they are asked whether each action helped achieve the goal and shown the whole trajectory. As Teachers, judgments made this way improve policy training on both ALFWorld and WebShop, including on tasks that the Teacher rarely completes as an Actor. Together, these results show that a decision model’s ability to act does not fully reflect its value for agent training, which depends on the question it is asked and the context it is given. These judgments provide step-level supervision during training without requiring additional Teacher calls at test time. 7 Limitations and Future Work Building on our findings, we identify several directions for future work: • Longer and more varied tasks. All experiments use two text-based environments with episodes of at most 30 actions. Software engineering, tool use, and assistant tasks involve longer trajectories and more varied dependencies. Whether asking about each action’s contribution with the full trajectory visible remains effective in these settings is an important open question. • Online judging. Our judges score actions after an episode ends. When a judge is used online, for example to filter or rerank actions, only the prefix is available. Methods that delay or revise scores as later steps arrive could extend hindsight credit to such settings. • Training decision models as judges. JEV and GPT Decisions are closed models with undisclosed adaptation procedures, so we cannot tell which training choices make a decision model a good judge. Open-weight decision models such as Decider v1.2 make it possible to train directly for judging the contribution of individual actions, the setting in which decision models are most useful as teachers. Prompted Actor System Prompt You select the next action for an agent. Answer the question using only the supplied context and available options. Assign a probability to every option, expressing your estimate that it is the best next action under the supplied question.     }
}} Use the supplied question key and option IDs exactly. Include every option ID exactly once in probabilities. Each probability must be between 0 and 1, and their sum must equal 1. Do not include additional fields, explanations, or Markdown fences. Constrained. We map candidate IDs to single-token labels such as A , B , and C , preserving the corresponding commands. Constrained Actor System Prompt Answer the supplied question using only the supplied context and available options. Each option ID is an opaque, case-sensitive label. Return exactly the ID of the best option as a single token. Do not include spaces, explanations, punctuation, JSON, or Markdown. For example, “put a heated mug on the side table” becomes “heat a mug”. Dependency hint and ordering example. In the dependency-hint experiment (Section 3.1.2 ), all three conditions retain the full goal and use their assigned prompt at every decision. Original uses the Actor Question. Hint appends the following sentence: “Consider possible dependencies among task requirements”. Hint + example appends the following passage after this hint. Dependency Example For example, the task is to place a heated mug on a table. Initially, the mug has not been heated and is not on the table. Heating the mug and placing it on the table are two task requirements, and they have a dependency: to finish the task, you need to first heat the mug, then place the heated mug on the table. Target-Action Instructions Evaluate only the action at trajectory[{target_step}]. Trajectory indices start at 0. For Value , we append the remaining action budget and the following question. The budget counts the target action itself. Value Question Steps remaining before max_steps: {remaining_steps} (including the current action). Question: What is the probability that the task will be completed within the remaining step budget if this action is executed first and the same agent policy then continues? Estimate the success probability conditioned on this action, not the probability of choosing the action. The remaining step budget includes the current action. For Progress , we append the following question. Progress Question Question: Did the target action make a useful contribution toward completing the goal? Later failure does not erase earlier useful progress. Later success does not make an unnecessary detour useful. Appendix C Analysis Protocols C.1 Action-Type Annotation We identify milestone and no-op actions from recorded environment states and action transitions. A milestone action achieves a subgoal, a definition we adopt directly from Wang et al. [2026b] . No-op actions require an unchanged environment state together with repeated observation–action behavior or feedback indicating an ineffective command. An unchanged environment state indicates that the action has not altered the environment. Repeated observations indicate that the action has not provided new information about the environment. In the question comparison, actions outside these two categories are grouped as Other. C.2 Judge scoring costs For a fair comparison of scoring costs, we query all six Judges through the OpenRouter API. For each Judge, we average the charges reported by OpenRouter for the successful requests that produced the action scores and multiply the result by 1,000 to obtain the cost per 1,000 action scores. Using a Judge as a Teacher requires scoring many actions during policy training. We therefore examine the cost and ranking quality of the Hindsight + Progress judgments used in Section 3.3 . We compare all six Judges on the ALFWorld trajectories from Section 3.2.1 . For each trajectory with R R milestone actions, we measure the fraction of milestones missing from its R R highest-scored actions. Likewise, for each trajectory with R R no-op actions, we measure the fraction of no-ops missing from its R R lowest-scored actions. Figure 10 plots these miss rates against the cost per 1,000 action scores. Decider v1.2 ( ) misses fewer milestone actions than Qwen prompted ( ) (36.5% vs. 49.6%) and fewer no-op actions (57.1% vs. 80.8%), while costing about 43% less per score. GPT Decisions ( ) likewise misses fewer milestone actions than GPT Luna ( ) (50.2% vs. 54.1%) and fewer no-op actions (61.1% vs. 78.4%), while costing about 28% less per score. Although Qwen constrained ( ) has the lowest no-op miss rate (51.3%), it costs about 1.5 times as much per score as Decider v1.2 and has a higher milestone miss rate (41.8% vs. 36.5%). In both paired comparisons, the decision model yields lower miss rates at lower scoring cost than its prompted counterpart, supporting its use for step-level supervision during training.
