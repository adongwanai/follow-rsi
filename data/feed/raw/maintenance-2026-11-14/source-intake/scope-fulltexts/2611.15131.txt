a full-duplex world model that continuously perceives audiovisual inputs while responding to users in real time.
The RWM treats intelligence and physical reality as equally fundamental to what constitutes a world. Existing world models largely focus on simulating the latter, leaving reasoning and decision-making as external controls rather than intrinsic parts of the world itself.
In RWM , we identify two equally fundamental capabilities: Intent , which provides the cognition underlying world interactions, and Realization , which generates coherent audiovisual behavior.
To realize this formulation, Duo v1 introduces three key technical designs: (1) Leveraging autoregressive modeling for continuous interaction reasoning and participation decisions, rather than restricting it to instruction understanding for diffusion models.
(2) Constructing chunk-specific conditions and synchronize tokens across Intent and Realization on a unified timeline. Module-specific attention masks enforce causal dependencies both within and across chunks, preventing access to future inputs.
(3) Mitigating distribution shift in both modules through dual rollout distillation, training on autoregressively generated Intent tokens and synthesized audiovisual histories from Realization.
Both modules continuously perceive real-time audio, video, and text, enabling full-duplex interaction with spontaneous conversations and interruptions.
Beyond conversation, users can share real objects, reshape virtual scenes, guide assistants, or play audiovisual games without mouse or keyboard controls.
The release of Duo v1 makes our first step, with the next version coming soon. We look forward to broader exploration of this emerging field. Contents 1 Introduction World models have made rapid progress in simulating the physical side of a world ( Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018 , Hafner et al., 2025 , Brooks et al., 2024 ) : given past observations and control signals, they predict how scenes evolve ( Bruce et al., 2024 , Valevski et al., 2025 , Hu et al., 2023 , NVIDIA, 2025 ) , with increasingly realistic dynamics, appearance, and long-range consistency ( Google DeepMind, 2024 , Parker-Holder and Fruchter, 2025 , Robbyant Team, 2026 , Gao et al., 2026 , Chen et al., 2026 , Huang et al., 2026a ) .
In most of these works, however, intelligence resides outside the world.
A user steers the generated world through inputs with limited degrees of freedom, such as keyboard and mouse commands, gamepad actions, or text prompts, but nothing within the world perceives the full state of the user in real time or decides how to respond.
Such worlds can be accurate, yet they remain lifeless: there is a world to explore, but no one to talk to. We argue that intelligence and physical reality are equally fundamental to a world. Building on this view, we introduce the Reciprocal World Model ( RWM ).
The RWM generates a world inhabited by living presences and connects it to the real world through full-duplex, real-time interaction. Each side continuously perceives audio and video from the other and can respond, interrupt, or remain silent while the other is still acting.
This reciprocal interaction requires two complementary capabilities: Intent , which provides the cognition underlying world interactions, and Realization , which generates coherent audiovisual behavior. These functional roles can be fulfilled by a unified multimodal model or by a modular system combining a multimodal language model with a diffusion model. In this report, we present Duo v1 , a modular instantiation of the RWM . Interaction proceeds in chunks of 17 video frames, approximately 0.71 s at 24 fps. Three technical designs coordinate Intent and Realization within a single streaming system. Autoregressive intent for interaction. In most audio-visual video generative model ( MiniMax, 2026 ) , a language model serves as an instruction encoder for the diffusion model. In Duo v1 , the intent module instead reasons over the ongoing interaction autoregressively and makes participation decisions: at every chunk, it emits a <speak> action followed by text or a <wait> action, conditioned on the user input so far and its own earlier language. Its hidden states, rather than its decoded text alone, carry each decision to the realization module ( Sec. 3 ). Action-aware rebalancing prevents the intent module from collapsing into perpetual waiting, since most chunks contain no new assistant speech, and history perturbations teach it to recover from imperfect earlier responses ( Sec. 4 ). Causal chunk conditions on a unified timeline. User speech, user video, intent tokens, and assistant media are synchronized on one timeline, and each chunk is given exactly the conditions that are available at its decision boundary. Module-specific attention masks enforce causality within and across chunks. In the intent module, temporal patches are completed inside each chunk, so no future frame leaks into a decision through the visual frontend. In the realization module, noisy media tokens of a chunk can attend to the reference, intent features, and clean history, but not to their own clean targets or to any later chunk ( Sec. 3 ). Dual rollout distillation. Before distillation, the realization module is trained on intent features of recorded assistant text and on recorded media history, whereas in a live exchange both are produced by the system itself.
Dual rollout brings inference-time conditioning into distillation: within each training window, the frozen Intent Module autoregressively generates the final assistant turn, and the Realization Module conditions on the resulting intent features and its own generated audiovisual history.
Distribution matching distillation on these rollouts yields a four-step streaming generator trained under conditions that match live interaction. We develop several data construction pipelines and a streaming runtime to support Duo v1 .
The training data combine scripted synthetic video calls and recorded conversations, with audio, video, and text aligned on a shared timeline for causal interaction training ( Sec. 5 ).
The streaming pipeline coordinates intent inference, audiovisual generation, and incremental decoding while continuing to capture user inputs ( Sec. 6 ).
Speech and vision provide a rich, direct channel between the two worlds, conveying information that keyboard and mouse commands often fail to capture, including tone, timing, expression, and visual details of real objects, actions, and scenes.
This richer interface creates opportunities for a wide range of shared activities shaped jointly by real and generated participants. 2 Related Work World models and persistent interaction. World Models and Dreamer learn predictive representations for control ( Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018 , Hafner et al., 2025 ) , while Genie learns action-controllable environments from video ( Bruce et al., 2024 ) . LingBot-World studies a data engine and staged causal adaptation for continuing visual worlds ( Robbyant Team, 2026 ) . These works connect learned dynamics to an agent’s actions and subsequent observations. Full-duplex and multimodal participation. Moshi represents concurrent user and assistant speech streams ( Défossez et al., 2024 ) . Qwen-Omni separates multimodal understanding from streaming speech generation through connected Thinker and Talker components ( Xu et al., 2025a , Xu et al., 2025b ) . Wan-Streamer extends the streaming setting to audio–visual foundation models and their execution schedule ( Huang et al., 2026b ) . ELLSA jointly models full-duplex speech, gesture, and expression, including non-verbal behavior while listening ( Wang et al., 2026 ) . These works establish participation timing and visible communicative behavior as closely related modeling problems. Joint and streaming audiovisual generation. Ovi, MOVA, and LTX develop synchronized audio–visual generation through coupled modality streams ( Low et al., 2025 , SII-OpenMOSS Team, 2026 , HaCohen et al., 2026 ) . MiniMax-H3 is a reference-conditioned audio–visual generator whose weights can initialize related models ( MiniMax, 2026 ) . Audio-driven animation methods such as EMO and OmniHuman realize visible behavior from supplied speech ( Tian et al., 2024 , Lin et al., 2025 ) . LiveTalk combines a distilled video performer with streaming audio from Qwen-Omni ( Chern et al., 2025 ) . These approaches connect speech with visible articulation and motion, with different choices of conditioning and generation order. Streaming joint diffusion further requires audio and video to remain aligned as their generated histories grow. OmniForcing addresses this conversion through asymmetric block-causal alignment, audio-sink stabilization, and joint self-generated rollouts ( Su et al., 2026 ) . OmniLiveAvatar combines progressive autoregressive distillation, synchronized audio-video memory, and rolling prompt planning ( Zhu et al., 2026b ) . Causal adaptation and few-step training. Diffusion Forcing assigns different noise levels across a sequence, and CausVid distills a bidirectional generator into a causal few-step student ( Chen et al., 2024 , Yin et al., 2025 ) . Self Forcing uses self-generated history during training to reduce the context mismatch with autoregressive inference ( Huang et al., 2025 ) . Work on causal-compatible initialization further aligns teacher and student during the transition to few-step generation ( Zhu et al., 2026a , Zhao et al., 2026 ) . Consistency models and distribution matching provide complementary tools for reducing sampling steps ( Song et al., 2023 , Yin et al., 2024b ) , with later distribution-matching work incorporating multistep alignment and adversarial training ( Yin et al., 2024a ) . 3 Architecture Duo v1 couples a language-based Intent Module with a causal audiovisual Realization Module ( Fig. 2 ). Hidden states produced by the Intent Module serve as the interface between the two modules, providing semantic guidance for the joint generation of video and stereo audio for the assistant. The two modules process complementary inputs and maintain separate histories, integrating response planning and audiovisual synthesis within a unified architecture. Their interaction is reciprocal: Intent determines how the system responds to the evolving environment, while Realization expresses that intent through audiovisual behavior, which in turn becomes part of the environment for subsequent interaction. Given the context, it generates the text output y k y_{k} and provides hidden states h k h_{k} . Causal input assembly. Inputs enter C k ϕ C^{\phi}_{k} according to when they become available, without requiring all modalities to share a sampling rate. ASR recovers user text u t u_{t} from user audio u a u_{a} , and forced alignment assigns each text token a span on the source audio timeline. A token is admitted only after the end of its aligned span. Video frames and audio samples are mapped to the same half-open chunk intervals using source timestamps. Assistant text targets are assigned to chunks according to speech onset, while the initial chunk uses a fixed <wait> action ( Fig. 3 ). We preserve fine-grained alignment before constructing chunk views, avoiding early rounding of speech boundaries. Each input context therefore contains only information available at the corresponding decision boundary, and different chunk configurations can be derived from the same aligned record. The ASR and alignment tools, together with their quality checks, are described in Sec. 5.3 . Causal visual encoding. Causality must also hold before attention is applied. Temporal patch construction can combine multiple source frames, so an attention mask cannot remove future information already mixed into a visual token. We therefore form each temporal group entirely within one input chunk. For a native 17-frame chunk with source frames numbered 0 , … , 16 0,\ldots,16 , the final pair is ( 16 , 16 ) (16,16) : the last frame is repeated instead of paired with frame 17 17 from the next chunk. Each group becomes available at the latest timestamp among its source frames. This preserves the pretrained visual input format while preventing temporal grouping from crossing chunk boundaries. Participation and language outputs. The text output y k y_{k} contains either <speak> followed by a text body or <wait> without new text. Both actions are generated through the standard token prediction path. The explicit action separates the decision to begin speaking from the words of the response, since appropriate content can still be delivered at the wrong time. A <wait> output can coexist with ongoing playback and media continuation. Participation supervision, target balancing, and history augmentation are described in Sec. 4.1 . Only user inputs available before chunk k k are included. Positional embedding. We use 3D-RoPE to represent temporal and spatial relationships among tokens. The text-conditioning embeddings use only the temporal coordinate, with both spatial coordinates set to zero. Audio tokens use the two extreme spatial position IDs to distinguish the left and right channels. Temporal coordinates for audio and video are aligned with actual playback times. At 40 latent vectors per second per channel, each audio latent corresponds to 25 milliseconds. The beginning of the user input audio defines the common temporal origin. Each audio token receives a temporal position equal to the offset of its segment start from this origin, divided by 25 milliseconds. Video temporal positions use the corresponding playback times with the same origin and temporal unit. This accounts for the nonuniform temporal mapping from 17 source frames to five video latent frames. 4 Training Strategy Training proceeds in three stages. Stage 1 trains the Intent Module for causal participation and text prediction through supervised fine-tuning. Stage 2 trains the Realization Module for causal joint audiovisual generation, conditioned on recorded media history and intent features extracted from recorded assistant text by the frozen Intent Module ( Fig. 4 ). Stage 3 distills four-step generation through distribution matching, with dual rollout using predicted text and generated audiovisual history to match inference-time conditioning. 4.1 Stage 1: Intent Training We adapt the multimodal Intent Module with causal supervised fine-tuning on chunk-aligned conversations. At the start of chunk k k , the model predicts the assistant output text y k y_{k} from the global instruction d d , the user video u v u_{v} and ASR text u t u_{t} available through chunk k − 1 k-1 , and earlier assistant language y < k y_{<k} . The decision context excludes assistant video. In the training data, user tokens become available at the ends of their aligned spans, while assistant text tokens are assigned to the chunk containing their speech onset. The <speak> action introduces new text, including continuations of an earlier turn. The <wait> action adds no new text and can coexist with ongoing playback or non-verbal behavior. The initial assistant output y 0 y_{0} is a fixed <wait> used as context and excluded from the loss. Action-aware rebalancing. Most chunks introduce no new assistant text, so unweighted training can favor waiting. We assign supervision categories offline from recorded assistant events and from whether the user is speaking at the chunk start. This speaking status distinguishes idle waits from waits during user speech and ordinary openings from interruption openings. The assistant annotation distinguishes openings from continuations, and an annotated question in the preceding user turn provides a separate opening tag. We use these labels to subsample targets and weight the loss, with separate action and content weights and adjustments for opening categories. Subsampled targets remain in the conversation history. The model receives only the causal context described above. Validation retains the full set of eligible targets and reports separate token-level metrics for actions, speech, and waits. Training under imperfect assistant history. To expose the model to imperfect histories, we truncate or repeat text in selected continuation chunks, mask their losses, and retain supervision on later valid targets. For missed-opening recovery, we replace selected ordinary openings with <wait> in the history and mask their losses. In the next chunk of the same assistant turn, only <speak> contributes to the loss. Preserving visual and linguistic grounding. The training mixture contains examples whose correct answer depends on the user video as well as examples solvable from text alone. We identify visually grounded assistant turns through data inspection and upweight their content-token loss, while retaining the same causal visual input path. Prompt augmentation varies the format, ordering, and completeness of the static assistant/scene description without inventing new facts. Together with source-grouped train/validation splits, these choices discourage the Intent Module from relying on a fixed prompt template or on text-only shortcuts. Optional emotion labels are kept as separately weighted attachments and are admitted only at their annotated causal boundary. We use low-rank adaptation to fine-tune the Intent Module. After this stage, the frozen Intent Module supplies hidden states from its forward passes to condition media training. 4.2 Stage 2: Realization Training Stage 2 trains the Realization Module for causal joint audiovisual generation while keeping the Intent Module frozen. Training follows the conditional structure in Eq. ( 2 ) , learning to generate audio and video latents from the current intent representation and available interaction context. The sequence layout and attention mask encode the dependencies among modalities within each chunk and restrict access to preceding interaction history across chunks. First-frame consistency. We compare the first frame with the conditioning image, requiring an SSIM of at least 0.90 and a perceptual-hash Hamming distance of at most 4. Conditioning uses the character image initially, then the preceding segment’s processed final frame. Unexpected audio. We compare the generated audio with the source audio using loudness normalization, waveform residuals, and VAD, rejecting segments with unexplained audio activity lasting at least 0.40 s. Camera push-in. We track face scale at 4 FPS, using the first valid detection as the baseline. Rejection thresholds for sustained and abrupt enlargement are a smoothed scale of 1.30 times baseline for 0.75 s and a 1.22-fold jump within 0.50 s, respectively. Persistent large-area visual changes. We sample frames at 4 FPS and measure LAB differences, changed-pixel proportions, and color/brightness histogram distances. Using these metrics, we reject abrupt changes that remain above thresholds relative to the pre-change frame, or cumulative drift from the initial frame exceeding both appearance and histogram thresholds. 5.3.2 Live-Stream Data Processing Collection and initial screening. We collect archived live streams and deduplicate source records. Sampled frames are used to check the presence of real participants and visual quality, retaining only suitable material. Annotation and filtering. We produce transcripts with word- or character-level timestamps by Qwen3-ASR-1.7B and Qwen3-ForcedAligner-0.6B ( Shi et al., 2026 ) . And we also use Silero VAD ( Silero Team, 2024 ) to identify speech and silence intervals and Qwen2.5-Omni-7B ( Xu et al., 2025a ) to review audio types. Speaker diarization and speaker-count checks combine FSMN-VAD ( Gao et al., 2023 ) for speech detection, Paraformer ( Gao et al., 2022 ) for transcription, and CAM++ ( Wang et al., 2023 ) for speaker embeddings followed by clustering. These checks exclude windows containing singing, whispered or breathy speech, or multiple speakers, producing timestamped candidate material for dialogue construction. Dialogue construction and video preparation. To obtain the content and timing needed for user-side video synthesis, we match audience comments extracted from real chat logs to the presenter’s responses. When such matching is not possible, we will generate counterpart utterances and schedule their interjections based on the presenter’s original speech and available silence. After reviewing conversational coherence, completeness, and audio-visual correspondence, we extract continuous assistant-side video segments and crop them around the presenter to remove on-screen comments and watermarks. We then synthesize the corresponding user-side videos and pair the two views on a shared timeline. This dependency introduces additional waiting at chunk boundaries during streaming playback. We therefore adapt the decoding procedure to process available video latents incrementally. After joint generation of a response chunk, its video latents are decoded one at a time. Each latent produces four video frames, followed by temporal alignment to match the original playback schedule and preserve the 17-frame chunk duration. This procedure allows playback to begin from the available chunk without waiting for additional latents from the next chunk. At 24 frames per second, the shortest playback interval corresponds to the first latent in each group, which represents one video frame after temporal alignment. This interval is 1 / 24 ​ s ≈ 41.7 ​ ms 1/24\,\mathrm{s}\approx 41.7\,\mathrm{ms} . The measured per-latent decoding time is approximately 30 ​ ms 30\,\mathrm{ms} , below this interval. The decoder can therefore keep pace with playback provided that generated latents are available on schedule. 6.3 Inference Acceleration The runtime coordinates memory management, stage scheduling, and GPU placement to sustain continuous audiovisual generation. Each input chunk covers 17 / 24 ​ s 17/24\,\mathrm{s} of captured audio and video. Sustained playback requires the pipeline to produce output chunks at least as quickly as they are consumed, while keeping memory usage bounded over long interactions. As shown in Fig. 11 , the system runs on a single server with eight GPUs. For each response chunk, Intent Module inference precedes joint audiovisual latent generation by the DiT. The remaining VAE decoding steps for one chunk overlap with Intent Module inference for the following chunk, reducing the interval between successive outputs. Unified KV Management A shared KV cache manager tracks cache ownership and device placement, with sliding-window eviction for the Intent Module and periodic history resets for the Realization Module. For each chunk, four denoising forwards read committed historical KV without committing intermediate noisy states. An additional clean forward processes the generated latents z k a z_{k}^{a} and z k v z_{k}^{v} and writes their KV states for subsequent chunks. At reset boundaries, the manager discards historical media KV before denoising while retaining reference and shared conditioning. The clean forward then establishes a new history, as illustrated by chunk k + 1 k+1 in Fig. 11 . The reset interval controls retained context, memory use, and context-dependent latency. After a restart, the manager reconstructs the active media cache by replaying completed latents through clean forwards in their original order, following the same reset boundaries and retention policy. Each reply is judged the way an end user would experience it, at normal playback speed with sound, against six binary criteria: content responsiveness, language adherence, speech intelligibility, visual identity stability, audio-visual synchronization, and audio-visual emotional consistency. Every criterion has written failure conditions, and no partial credit is given. Results Of 180 checks (30 cases × 6 criteria), 168 passed (93.3%). The clearest strength is generation fidelity: the model passed all 90 checks on language adherence, speech intelligibility, and visual identity stability. Every reply came in the expected language as complete, intelligible speech, and no case showed a visible identity swap, facial or body malformation, or unexplained change in appearance. Cross-modal coherence is nearly as strong. Vocal emotion was consistent with facial expression and body language in 96.7% of cases, and lip movement stayed aligned with speech in 90.0%. Where headroom remains Content responsiveness, which asks whether a reply substantively addresses what the user has just said and shown, reached 73.3% (22/30). It is the most demanding criterion in the protocol, since a duplex model must settle on a reply while speech and video are still streaming in. The misses fall mainly into two patterns. The first is the long tail of conversation, such as jokes and odd remarks, where a fitting reply requires reading intent beyond the literal words, and doing so in real time. The second is intent that only becomes clear when speech and video are read together. In one case, a user sitting indoors said they had just unlocked a shared bike when it started to rain. Given the indoor setting, this reads as a complaint, yet the model treated it as a request for advice and suggested holding off on riding. Both patterns come down to inferring what the user means from everything they say and show, at conversational speed. This is one of the hardest problems in duplex interaction and the central focus of our next iteration, which builds on a foundation that already passed every language, speech, and identity check. 7.2 Showcase Fig. 12 organizes selected examples: plan revision, visual guidance, collaborative design, social support, and interactive customization.
