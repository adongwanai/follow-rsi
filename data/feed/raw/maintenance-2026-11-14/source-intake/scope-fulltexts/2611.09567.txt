Affiliation: Sorbonne Université, CNRS, ISIR, Paris, France Affiliation: Delft University of Technology, Faculty of Aerospace Engineering, Delft, The Netherlands Armand Kassaï Koupaï 1 1 footnotemark: 1 Affiliation: Sorbonne Université, CNRS, ISIR, Paris, France Paola Cinnella Patrick Gallinari Affiliation: Sorbonne Université, CNRS, ISIR, Paris, France Affiliation: Sorbonne Université, CNRS, Institut Jean Le Rond d’Alembert, Paris, France Affiliation: Criteo AI Lab, Paris, France Abstract Machine-learning surrogates are increasingly used to accelerate computational fluid dynamics, yet progress is limited by the lack of benchmarks capturing realistic, time-dependent turbulent flows. We introduce TurtLES , a 14 TB dataset of 3D turbulent cylinder wakes in the shear-layer transition regime , computed via high-fidelity implicit large-eddy simulations. The dataset contains 400 trajectories of 400 time steps each, with 3–11 million points per frame, spanning variations in geometry, Reynolds number, and angle of attack. Unlike existing datasets, TurtLES combines (i) physically realistic turbulence with an active energy cascade driven by vortex stretching , (ii) dense irregular meshes, and (iii) long-horizon temporal dynamics. We benchmark state-of-the-art neural operators on autoregressive rollouts and identify key failure modes, including temporal instability, loss of small-scale energy, and challenges in learning on large irregular domains. TurtLES provides a new benchmark for evaluating surrogate models on 3D turbulent wakes at industrially relevant mesh resolution. Project page : turtlesprojectml-alt.github.io Figure 1: TurtLES visualized through Q-criterion isosurfaces and its dataset parameters. 1 Introduction The simulation of physical phenomena is central to industrial workflows in design ( Allen et al., 2022 ) , forecasting ( Richardson, 1922 ) , control ( Brunton and Kutz, 2022 ) , and decision-making ( Lam et al., 2023 ) . These phenomena are governed by partial differential equations describing the spatio-temporal evolution of physical fields. Because such systems are rarely analytically tractable, they are solved numerically, often at substantial computational cost ( Ames, 1992 ; Quarteroni and Valli, 2008 ) . Yet downstream decisions rarely require the full resolution produced by high-fidelity solvers ( Bonnet et al., 2022 ; Yang, 2015 ) . This mismatch has motivated surrogate models: low-cost approximations that reproduce task-relevant physical behavior while avoiding repeated full-scale simulation. Deep learning has emerged as a powerful framework for surrogate modeling ( Long et al., 2018 ; de Bézenac et al., 2018 ) , with rapid progress in operator learning ( Li et al., 2021 ; Lu et al., 2021 ; Serrano et al., 2023 ; Wu et al., 2024 ; Alkin et al., 2024 ) , neural simulation ( Brandstetter et al., 2022 ) , and generative emulation ( Serrano et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2026 ; Kassaï Koupaï et al., 2025 ) . Its adoption in realistic simulation settings, however, remains limited by a circular dependency: accurate surrogates require large, high-quality datasets, but such datasets must typically be generated by the same numerical solvers or physical experiments that surrogates are meant to replace. Recent datasets have begun to address this bottleneck, but each leaves a critical axis uncovered. The Well ( Ohana et al., 2024 ) provides approximately 15 TB of dynamical trajectories across sixteen physical settings on regular grids, with most three-dimensional cases capped at 128 3 128^{3} resolution. DrivAerNet ( Elrefaie et al., 2024 ) reaches industrial-scale three-dimensional geometries with tens of millions of degrees of freedom on irregular meshes, but only at steady state. EAGLE ( Janny et al., 2023 ) captures dynamic flows on irregular geometries, yet its 𝒪 ⁡ ( 10 3 ) \mathcal{O}(10^{3}) points per frame are too coarse to resolve realistic three-dimensional flow structures. RealPDEBench ( Hu et al., 2026 ) pairs real-world PIV measurements with matched simulations, but its fluid measurements are two-dimensional laser-sheet observations with only in-plane velocity components. It is therefore valuable for simulation–experiment alignment, but does not directly expose the three-dimensional velocity fields and multiscale structures needed to assess turbulent flow surrogates. More broadly, many machine learning benchmarks conflate temporal complexity with turbulence. A flow may be unsteady, chaotic, or visually complex without containing the mechanisms that define three-dimensional turbulence. Several datasets described as “dynamic fluid” benchmarks fall into this category: they consist of unsteady laminar flows, chaotic two-dimensional systems, or three-dimensional dynamics dominated by buoyancy, radiative forcing, magnetic effects, or other mechanisms rather than by an inertial cascade ( Pfaff et al., 2021 ; Takamoto et al., 2022 ) . In three dimensions, turbulence is sustained by vortex stretching, represented by the ( 𝝎 ⋅ ∇ ) 𝐮 (\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u} term in the vorticity equation, which transfers kinetic energy from large eddies to progressively smaller scales until viscous dissipation closes the energy budget ( Pope, 2000 ) . In two dimensions, this mechanism is absent; in other forced systems, it may be secondary. A surrogate trained where vortex stretching is absent or marginal is therefore not learning three-dimensional turbulence, but rather interpolation of unsteady or chaotic fields without the cascade physics that define turbulent flow. Standard error metrics often hide this distinction, which becomes visible only through energy spectra, fluctuation statistics, and regime-transition behavior. At the same time, the machine learning community has made substantial progress on the ingredients required for realistic surrogate modeling: long-horizon stability on regular grids ( McCabe et al., 2024 ; Lippe et al., 2023 ) , generalization ( Huang et al., 2022 ; Kassaï Koupaï et al., 2024 ; Subramanian et al., 2023 ; Takamoto et al., 2023 ) , neural operators for dense unstructured static fields ( Li et al., 2023b ; Wen et al., 2025 ; Luo et al., 2025 ) , and generative emulators for chaotic dynamics ( Zhou et al., 2025 ; Pedersen et al., 2025 ) . What remains missing is a benchmark that stresses these capabilities jointly: dynamic three-dimensional trajectories, dense irregular discretizations, long temporal horizons, and physically turbulent flow driven by vortex stretching. To fill this gap, we introduce TurtLES , a 14 TB dataset of high-fidelity simulations of three-dimensional cylinder wakes in the shear-layer transition regime. The dataset contains 400 trajectories of 400 time steps each, with 3–11 million points per frame sampled on irregular point clouds. Across trajectories, we vary the cylinder cross-section, Reynolds number, and angle of attack, producing geometry-dependent wakes that span subcritical to transitional regimes. In this range, shear-layer transition generates a turbulent near wake, so the dataset contains an active three-dimensional cascade in the simulated fields themselves. TurtLES makes three contributions to surrogate modeling for PDEs. First, it provides dynamic three-dimensional flow data on dense irregular discretizations, reaching millions of points per frame rather than relying on regular grids or coarse point clouds. Second, it enables long-horizon evaluation over hundreds of autoregressive time steps in chaotic, geometry-dependent wakes. Third, it supports physically grounded assessment of turbulent surrogates through high-frequency quantities such as fluctuation statistics, near-wake spectra, and intermittent shedding-mode behavior. By combining industrially relevant mesh resolution , long temporal trajectories, irregular geometry, and physically turbulent flow, TurtLES targets a regime that existing benchmarks do not jointly cover. It exposes whether learned surrogates can remain stable, resolve multiscale turbulent content, and generalize across geometry-dependent flow regimes, rather than only minimizing standard field-wise errors. 2 Related Work Operator Learning Operator learning provides a way to train neural networks to approximate PDE solution maps directly between function spaces. Early neural operators such as DeepONet and FNO showed that these models can act as mesh-independent surrogates for PDEs ( Lu et al., 2021 ; Li et al., 2021 ) . Since then, the field has moved toward models that are more accurate on complex geometries, better at resolving multiscale and high-frequency features, and scalable to very large meshes. Recent work has explored several directions. Multiresolution and wavelet-based operators improve the representation of fine-scale structures ( Gupta et al., 2021 ) , while latent and geometry-aware formulations extend neural operators to irregular domains ( Tran et al., 2023 ; Li et al., 2023b ) . Attention-based models such as OFormer, GNOT and Transolver further improve operator learning on point clouds and arbitrary domains by using graph or transformer encoders to handle irregular inputs and complex PDE dynamics ( Li et al., 2023a ; Hao et al., 2023 ; Wu et al., 2024 ) . However, full attention remains expensive for high-resolution fields. This has motivated more efficient neural operator surrogates. Perceiver-style and latent-token models such as Aroma and UPT compress large inputs into compact latent representations, reducing the cost of learning on dense meshes ( Serrano et al., 2024 ; Alkin et al., 2024 ) . Hagnberger et al. (2025) ; Kassaï Koupaï et al. (2026) proposed continuous convolution for learning hierarchical neural operators that can operate on very dense grids, allowing million point trajectory generation. Wen et al. (2025) uses geometry-aware multiscale graph/attention encoding to scale neural PDE surrogates to arbitrary large meshes, including industrial 3D computational fluid dynamics (CFD) domains. Datasets for Machine Learning Applications A key bottleneck in training neural operators for fluid dynamics is the limited availability of high-quality datasets. Generating such data requires solving the Navier-Stokes equations at high spatial and temporal resolution, which comes with substantial computational and storage costs. As a result, most existing datasets are restricted in either fidelity, dimensionality, or physical complexity. A substantial body of work ( Bonnet et al., 2022 ; Sung et al., 2025 ; Elrefaie et al., 2024 ; Kanchi et al., 2025 ; Paischer et al., 2025 ) has focused on performing steady-state flow simulations through the Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) equations, where all turbulence fluctuations are modeled rather than computed directly. While these approaches significantly reduce computational complexity, RANS-based simulations suffer from well-documented limitations in capturing complex phenomena such as sharp pressure gradients, laminar-to-turbulent transition and flow separation ( Wilcox, 1998 ) . Furthermore, by definition, steady-state solutions neglect temporal dynamics, preventing operators from learning transient flow phenomena. Therefore, several benchmarks based on unsteady flows have been introduced. A common cost-reducing strategy is to restrict simulations to two-dimensional domains ( Huang et al., 2019 ; Viquerat and Hachem, 2020 ; Jakob et al., 2021 ; Pfaff et al., 2021 ; Han et al., 2022 ; Xu et al., 2023 ; Janny et al., 2023 ; Tali et al., 2025 ; Luo et al., 2024 ; Dapelo and Bridgeman, 2025 ) , at the expense of losing relevant three-dimensional flow phenomena. Alternatively, higher-fidelity transient datasets generated using large-eddy simulation (LES) or direct numerical simulation (DNS) have been explored in well-known (multi-)physics benchmarks ( Hassan et al., 2023 ; Chung et al., 2023 ; Koehler et al., 2024 ; Takamoto et al., 2022 ; Ohana et al., 2024 ; Mao et al., 2026 ) . There, they are typically limited to regular grids and idealized configurations (e.g., homogeneous isotropic turbulence, no objects within the domain, periodic boundary conditions). These flow problems do not introduce geometric complexities or capture the anisotropic nature of real-world turbulent flows. It should be noted that scale-resolving simulations for machine learning applications have been conducted by Ashton et al. (2024b) ; Ashton et al. (2024a) on the Ahmed and Windsor car bodies, respectively. However, due to storage constraints, only time-averaged volume data was made publicly available. To the best of our knowledge, there is no dataset that combines three-dimensional, time-resolved high-fidelity CFD simulations with systematic variations in complex geometries while fully resolving a broad range of turbulent scales. The present study aims to address this gap by performing implicit large eddy simulations on irregular grids for the canonical cylinder flow case across a range of inflow and geometric configurations. 3 Dataset Presentation The dataset captures a wide range of fluid–object interactions and wake dynamics through systematic variations in geometric parameters and inflow conditions , as illustrated in Figure 1 and Appendix K . To accurately resolve the resulting flow features and their temporal evolution, costly high-fidelity numerical simulations are performed. Generating a large number of such trajectories requires a robust and fully automated data generation pipeline. The remainder of this section describes the components of this pipeline, including the governing fluid flow model, the definition of the parameter sampling space, the automated simulation setup, and the subsequent data processing. Fluid flow simulation The fluid dynamics are governed by the Navier–Stokes equations, a system of equations that expresses the conservation of mass, momentum, and energy for a compressible fluid. Given a set of inflow and boundary conditions, characterized by the Reynolds number R ​ e Re , the Mach number M ​ a Ma and Prandtl number P ​ r Pr , these equations describe how a fluid evolves over space and time. In this study, we focus our attention on the evolution of the pressure p p , density ρ \rho and velocity components u u , v v , w w . At sufficiently high Reynolds numbers, the Navier-Stokes equations give rise to turbulence, whose multi-scale, chaotic dynamics make direct numerical solution computationally prohibitive. We solve these equations using an implicit Large Eddy Simulation (iLES) approach, in which the most energetic turbulent scales are computed directly, while the truncation error of the numerical scheme acts as a model for the behavior of the smaller, unresolved scales. In this context, the Flux Reconstruction (FR) scheme ( Huynh, 2007 ) has proven to be effective and is implemented using PyFR ( Witherden et al., 2014 ) , an open-source, high-order accurate GPU-based solver. The reader is referred to Appendix D for quantity definitions, and to Appendix E for a detailed description of the compressible Navier-Stokes equations and the solution method used in this study. Sampling space The dataset is constructed within a four-dimensional parameter space. Variations in geometry are defined by the super-ellipse (or Lamé curve), i.e. all points ( x , y ) (x,y) satisfying: | x a | n + | y b | n = 1 , \left|\frac{x}{a}\right|^{n}+\left|\frac{y}{b}\right|^{n}=1, (1) where a a and b b represent half the horizontal and vertical dimensions of the object, respectively, as illustrated in Figure 2 a . The aspect ratio is defined as A ​ R = a / b AR=a/b . To reduce the number of variable parameters, b = 1 / 2 b=1/2 is fixed for all cases , giving a constant object thickness D = 2 ​ b = 1 D=2b=1 . The semi-axis a a is sampled log-uniformly over the range [ 1 / 6 , 3 / 2 ] [1/6,3/2] , which results in aspect ratios spanning from 1/3 to 3, with an equal proportion of cases having A ​ R < 1 AR<1 and A ​ R > 1 AR>1 . The exponent n n is selected from the discrete set { 1 , 2 , 5 , ∞ } \{1,2,5,\infty\} , corresponding to diamond, elliptical, rounded rectangle and rectangular shapes, respectively. The inflow conditions are characterized by Reynolds numbers R ​ e ∈ [ 1000 , 3900 ] Re\in[1000,3900] , where the length scale used in the definition of R ​ e Re is the object thickness D D . Within this range, the wake transitions to turbulence while the boundary layer remains laminar, consistent with observations from circular cylinder flow studies ( Lienhard, 1966 ) . The inflow angle of attack α \alpha is the counter-clockwise angle between the x x -axis and the object’s centerline. It is varied between 0 0 and 45 45 degrees, introducing unique flow separation behavior for each configuration. Limiting the range to 45 ∘ 45^{\circ} prevents generating geometrically redundant cases. Both R ​ e Re and α \alpha are varied uniformly and restricted to integer values for simplicity. To obtain representative samples of the parameter space, we employ a Sobol’ sequence ( Sobol’, 1967 ) , which is a quasi-random low-discrepancy sequence designed to minimize clustering and gaps in high-dimensional sampling. Unlike related methods such as Latin Hypercube Sampling, it supports sequential sampling, making it well suited for dataset generation where the total number of cases need not be specified in advance. A four-dimensional unit hypercube is sampled N s = 400 N_{s}=400 times and subsequently transformed to the previously defined (log-)uniform or discrete parameter ranges. The resulting space is visualized in Figure 2 b together with histograms of the different parameters in Figure 2 c–f . Trajectories are partitioned into fixed training, validation and test sets of 320/40/40 cases. The split is released with the dataset and described in Appendix I . Simulation setup For each case, the super-elliptical object is placed inside the 3D computational domain in Figure 3 a at an angle α \alpha . The flow is prescribed through boundary conditions placed on the walls ( A – G ). A is the inlet, B – D are the outlet and E is the object surface. Walls F and G represent the side-walls of the domain. They are assigned periodic boundary conditions, which means flow leaving F enters back through G . An automated routine is implemented to generate the computational mesh using Gmsh ( Geuzaine and Remacle, 2009 ) . In Figure 3 b , we show the resulting mesh in the xy-plane for the domain presented in Figure 3 a . This two-dimensional mesh is then extended into three dimensions by a 30-layer structured extrusion in the span-wise direction. The flux reconstruction method places solution nodes inside these elements. A single element contains 75-125 points, yielding 2.9M to 10.5M unique solution points per case within the stored region (see Data processing below) . Additional information on the domain definition and meshing procedure are given in Appendix F . The numerical framework is validated against four benchmark cases: a circular cylinder ( a = 0.5 a=0.5 , n = 2 n=2 ) at R ​ e = 1000 Re=1000 and R ​ e = 3900 Re=3900 , a square cylinder ( a = 0.5 a=0.5 , n = ∞ n=\infty ) at R ​ e = 2000 Re=2000 and an elongated rectangle ( a = 2.5 a=2.5 , n = ∞ n=\infty ) at R ​ e = 3000 Re=3000 . Data processing Processing steps are applied to limit the total data size while ensuring the solutions remain physically representative. Data collection starts only after the initial transient has been washed away according to the numerical convergence statistic proposed by Boudet et al. (2015) . Subsequently, 400 three-dimensional snapshots are saved of the three velocity components, pressure and density: { u , v , w , p , ρ } \{u,v,w,p,\rho\} at fixed time intervals Δ ​ t s \Delta t_{s} . A snapshot is restricted to all solution nodes inside the rectangular box given by x / D ∈ [ − 3 , 10 ] x/D\in[-3,10] , y / D ∈ [ − 3.5 , 3.5 ] y/D\in[-3.5,3.5] and z / D ∈ [ 0 , 2 ​ π ] z/D\in[0,2\pi] . The sampling interval Δ ​ t s \Delta t_{s} exceeds the solver time-step to manage storage constraints but is chosen such that the inertial range of the turbulence spectrum is retained for all cases. Ultimately, one trajectory covers a time-period in which most characteristic frequencies are repeated multiple times. For instance, four vortex shedding cycles are captured during 400 ​ Δ ​ t s 400\Delta t_{s} in the circular cylinder case ( a = 0.5 , n = 2 a=0.5,n=2 ). A case directory contains one set of node coordinates, as the grid remains constant throughout the entire trajectory, accompanied with the corresponding solutions for all 400 time-steps. Each case directory additionally stores power spectra of the streamwise ( u u ) and cross-stream ( v v ) velocity components at three centerline ( y / D = 0 y/D=0 ) probes located behind each object, together with the script used to compute them, providing per-case evidence of the turbulent cascade and a physically grounded target for surrogate evaluation. A solution comprises the three-dimensional pressure ( p p ), density ( ρ \rho ), and velocity ( u u , v v , w w ) fields. 4 Benchmarking Setup Motivation and benchmark. To better demonstrate the value of this dataset, we evaluate the performance of current neural operators on these data. The machine learning task considered here is temporal prediction of the fields p p , ρ \rho , u u , v v , and w w . The dataset is designed to expose limitations of existing neural operators for dynamic forecasting : handling dense meshes remains computationally expensive, stability over long rollout horizons is often poor, and high-frequency features are frequently underrepresented or smoothed out. Since the dataset is parametric, it also tests generalization across a rich distribution of physical configurations. We evaluate several recent state-of-the-art neural operator architectures from the literature: UPT ( Alkin et al., 2024 ) , GINO ( Li et al., 2023b ) , Transolver++ ( Luo et al., 2025 ) , and the continuous layer proposed in CALM-PDE ( Hagnberger et al., 2025 ) , combined with the scaling improvements introduced in ECHO for larger meshes ( Kassaï Koupaï et al., 2026 ) . These baselines are intended as an initial benchmark, illustrating the level of performance attainable with well-established models under a shared, fixed training budget. Architectures, hyperparameters and model-specific adjustments are documented in Appendix L . Training setup. The models are trained on all 320 training trajectories ( Appendix I ) to predict the temporal residual between time t t and time t + 1 t+1 from a single input frame: NO ⁡ ( u t , R ​ e ) + u t ↦ u t + 1 , \mathrm{NO}(u_{t},Re)+u_{t}\mapsto u_{t+1}, where the Reynolds number R ​ e Re is also provided as an input. Learning the residual is generally easier for neural networks than directly predicting the full next state. The training loss is a standard mean squared error, computed on the predicted residual. Since the meshes are very large, a subset of points is sampled during training, and each model predicts the corresponding points at the next time step. All models are trained for 100,000 100{,}000 steps with a batch size of 2 2 , for approximately 20 20 hours on a single H100 GPU. We use the AdamW optimizer with a weight decay of 10 − 3 10^{-3} and a learning rate of 10 − 4 10^{-4} . All models are trained with a linear warmup over the first 5,000 5{,}000 steps, followed by a cosine learning-rate scheduler. Inference and evaluation setup. At inference time, each model rolls out the full dynamics using only the initial condition and the Reynolds number. Each trajectory contains 400 400 time steps, while the number of mesh nodes varies across trajectories; we therefore use a batch size of 1 1 and predict all nodes in each frame by iterating over subsets of points, since the full mesh cannot be processed in a single forward pass. Encoder–decoder baselines such as UPT and CALM-PDE encode the input mesh once and decode query points in chunks, whereas Transolver ++ processes the mesh through a tile partition; all models use the same per-frame timing protocol, differing only in kernel-launch granularity. 5 Results We evaluate the models along three complementary axes: rollout accuracy, spectral fidelity, and latency. These criteria are designed to separate errors that are often conflated in field-wise metrics. A model may achieve low pointwise error while dissipating small-scale turbulent content, or may perform well over short horizons while drifting during long autoregressive rollouts. Pointwise errors do not fully reveal whether a surrogate preserves the multi-scale structure of the turbulent wake. We therefore compare spanwise energy spectra between predicted and reference trajectories. In Figure 5 , spectra are averaged over test trajectories and rollout times, and the spectral error is shown as a function of wavenumber. A model that tracks large coherent structures but dissipates small eddies is expected to match low wavenumbers while underestimating high-wavenumber energy. We also report a persistence baseline, defined by the one-step rule u ^ t + 1 = u t \hat{u}_{t+1}=u_{t} ; under autoregressive rollout, this corresponds to repeating the initial state. Because this baseline preserves a real turbulent snapshot, it can match the time-averaged energy spectrum reasonably well despite not predicting the temporal evolution of the wake. This highlights that spectral agreement alone is insufficient and should be interpreted together with field-level and long-horizon rollout errors. Among the learned models, CALM-PDE most closely matches the reference spectrum across the resolved range, with near-zero log-power discrepancy over low and intermediate wavenumbers. UPT follows the correct spectral decay but shows larger high-wavenumber errors, indicating reduced fidelity at small scales. In contrast, Transolver++ strongly overpredicts spectral energy, especially at intermediate and high wavenumbers, suggesting spurious small-scale content during rollout. Latency. Finally, we compare the full-mesh inference cost of the neural surrogates in the last column of Table 2 , together with the cost of generating one trajectory with the solver . All evaluated methods remain costly at this spatial resolution, requiring hundreds to thousands of seconds to predict a full trajectory. UPT is the fastest baseline, followed by CALM-PDE and Transolver++, while GINO is substantially slower than the other methods , which also constrains its accuracy: it is the only baseline that did not converge within the shared training budget. In GPU-seconds, every surrogate provides a net speedup over the solver, ranging from ∼ 3 × {\sim}3\times for GINO to ∼ 16 × {\sim}16\times for UPT. This margin is modest compared with the speedups commonly reported for neural surrogates on smaller problems. These results show that, even when surrogate models improve rollout accuracy, scaling inference to dense irregular meshes with millions of points remains a major computational bottleneck. Together, these evaluations expose complementary failure modes of neural PDE surrogates on TurtLES : short-horizon prediction error, long-horizon instability, loss of high-frequency turbulent energy, and insufficient computational acceleration on dense irregular meshes. Limitations. TurtLES is restricted to one physical regime: turbulent cylinder wakes in the shear-layer transition range. Whether the failure modes observed here transfer to other geometries or Reynolds regimes remains an open question. The reference trajectories are themselves an approximation: computed via implicit-LES, so every surrogate error reported here is measured against this solver rather than against a DNS or experimental ground truth. To keep the dataset tractable, snapshots are subsampled in time; although the sampling interval is chosen to preserve the inertial range, some high-frequency information is inevitably discarded. The resulting short sampling window also rules out converged averaged statistics such as Reynolds stresses, which would require substantially longer trajectories than storage permits, limiting the physical diagnostics available for evaluation. Finally, the four baselines surveyed here are not an exhaustive account of how neural operators perform on this class of flows; building that account is the role we intend TurtLES to serve. 6 Conclusion We introduced TurtLES , a large-scale benchmark for learning neural PDE surrogates in realistic three-dimensional turbulent flow regimes. The dataset contains 400 high-fidelity iLES trajectories of cylinder wakes across variations in geometry, Reynolds number, and angle of attack, with millions of irregularly distributed solution points per frame and hundreds of time steps per trajectory. By combining dense unstructured spatial discretizations, long temporal horizons, and physically turbulent wake dynamics, TurtLES targets a regime that is not jointly covered by existing CFD benchmarks. Our benchmark experiments show that current neural operators face major challenges in this setting. While several models achieve reasonable short-horizon accuracy, long autoregressive rollouts reveal substantial differences in stability. Spectral diagnostics show that pointwise errors alone are insufficient: models may appear accurate while misrepresenting turbulent energy at fine scales. TurtLES is intended to support progress toward neural surrogates that are not only accurate on average, but also stable over long horizons, faithful to multiscale turbulent structure, and efficient on meshes of industrially relevant size . Future work may extend the benchmark to additional geometries, higher Reynolds-number regimes, richer boundary conditions, and uncertainty-aware or generative surrogate models. We hope that TurtLES will provide a useful testbed for developing and evaluating next-generation neural operators for high-resolution computational fluid dynamics. Appendix A Broader impact TurtLES provides high-fidelity three-dimensional turbulent flow trajectories on dense irregular meshes, and is intended to serve as a benchmark for the development of neural surrogate models in computational fluid dynamics. We anticipate the following uses: 1. evaluating geometry-aware operator-learning models on dynamic, physically turbulent three-dimensional flow rather than on regular-grid or two-dimensional benchmarks; 2. studying the long-horizon stability and multiscale spectral fidelity of learned PDE surrogates through the evaluation protocol introduced in Section 5 ; 3. informing the design of new architectures for irregular meshes with millions of degrees of freedom, where memory and inference cost become first-order constraints; 4. extending point-cloud and graph-based benchmarks toward industrially relevant fluid-dynamics regimes; 5. supporting the development of surrogate solvers that accelerate early-stage design exploration in aerospace and automotive engineering; 6. studying the generalization behavior of neural operators across geometry, Reynolds number, and angle of attack in a controlled parametric setting. We do not anticipate direct negative societal impact: the dataset is synthetic and contains no personally identifiable, geographic, or sensitive information. As with any data-driven surrogate, downstream practitioners should validate predictions before relying on them for safety-critical engineering decisions; surrogate models trained on TurtLES are not substitutes for verified high-fidelity simulation in such contexts. An analytical solution to this system of equations only exists under simplifying assumptions, and thus numerical methods are used to approximate solutions for complex flow problems. The domain is then discretized into a grid on which flow variables are iteratively solved. To capture the entire turbulence spectrum of a fluid, Direct Numerical Simulation (DNS) employs a grid fine enough to resolve the smallest physical structures. The computational overhead is often intractable for industrially relevant applications, as the cost of fully resolving all turbulent scales in 3D Navier-Stokes simulations grows with R ​ e 3 Re^{3} , where R ​ e Re denotes the Reynolds number. Instead, Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) based simulations decompose an instantaneous fluid quantity into its time-averaged and fluctuating component, whereby the effects of the latter are represented by a turbulence model rather than computed directly. While this allows a significant reduction in computational cost, these methods exhibit limited accuracy for complex flow phenomena (e.g. laminar-to-turbulent transition, separation, three-dimensional effects) ( Wilcox, 1998 ) . Large Eddy Simulation (LES) provides a high-fidelity alternative for simulating turbulent flows at reasonable cost. It is based on the separation between the energy-containing large scales of turbulence and the smaller high-frequency scales through a filter operation ( Pope, 2000 ) : u i ​ ( x , t ) = u ^ i ​ ( x , t ) + u i ′′ ​ ( x , t ) . u_{i}(x,t)=\hat{u}_{i}(x,t)+u_{i}^{\prime\prime}(x,t). (8) The filtered flow variables ( u ^ i \hat{u}_{i} ) are also referred to as the resolved variables, as they are directly solved for in the LES framework. The residual of the filter operation, u i ′′ u_{i}^{\prime\prime} , represents the unresolved turbulent motions. Numerous filtering strategies exist, but often the computational mesh itself acts as a filter where the local grid size, Δ g ​ r ​ i ​ d \Delta_{grid} , dictates the cut-off between represented turbulent scales and sub-grid scales (SGS). The scale separation of Equation 8 is physically motivated by each component’s distinct behavior. The resolved scales are highly energetic, anisotropic, and strongly influenced by the flow geometry, which requires their direct computation. In contrast, the unresolved scales are more isotropic, carry less energy and exhibit a more universal behavior, which allows for unified approaches to modeling their effects. Because these smaller scales are not resolved, LES utilizes significantly coarser meshes than DNS, leading to reduced computational complexity. In explicit LES, an explicit model is used to replicate the effects of SGS turbulence, which entails providing the correct energy drain from the resolved flow to the smaller unresolved scales. This approach assumes that numerical errors associated with the discretization scheme are small. In practice, this is often not the case, as even high-order schemes exhibit truncation errors of similar magnitude to SGS model contributions ( Ghosal, 1996 ) . Both truncation errors and SGS model terms are typically most significant at the smallest resolved scales. Due to the strong non-linear nature of Navier-Stokes, their combined effect can be difficult to anticipate. One way to address this challenge is to apply an explicit filter at a scale larger than the grid spacing ( Δ f ​ i ​ l ​ t ​ e ​ r > Δ g ​ r ​ i ​ d \Delta_{filter}>\Delta_{grid} ), effectively shifting the SGS model’s activity away from the high-frequency truncation errors. However, this significantly increases computational cost as much finer grids are required to maintain the same level of resolved turbulence. Alternatively, by recognizing that some numerical schemes exhibit errors of similar dissipative nature as the effect of the sub-grid scales, implicit LES (iLES) abandons the use of an explicit SGS model and lets the truncation error serve as the energy drain from the resolved flow. The present study adopts this approach by employing the Flux Reconstruction (FR) or Correction Procedure via Reconstruction (CPR) scheme ( Huynh, 2007 ) . CPR functions well in the context of iLES as the scheme is inherently dissipative at high wave numbers ( Vincent et al., 2011 ) . To ensure the solution trajectories are unaffected by this startup phase, a numerical convergence criterion is applied following Boudet et al. (2015) . Measurement probes are placed in regions where the flow exhibits low frequency dynamics. In the present study, they are located along the centerline ( y / D = 0 y/D=0 ) at streamwise distances 4 ​ D 4D , 6.5 ​ D 6.5D and 9.5 ​ D 9.5D behind the object, denoted by P 4 P_{4} , P 6.5 P_{6.5} and P 9.5 P_{9.5} , respectively. These positions correspond to the wake region, which provides a consistent reference across all flow cases, whereas other regions of interest, such as the shear layer, change with geometry. Each probe measures a time-dependent signal, ϕ \phi . At time t t , the signal is cut into four equal segments. The final three segments are then compared using the following criterion: χ ⁡ ( ϕ ) = max ⁡ [ max i , j = 2 , 4 ⁡ ( | ϕ ¯ ( i ) − ϕ ¯ ( j ) ϕ ¯ ( 4 ) | ) , max i , j = 2 , 4 ⁡ ( | ϕ ′ ( i ) − ϕ ′ ( j ) ϕ ′ ( 4 ) | ) ] . \chi(\phi)=\max\left[\max_{i,j=2,4}\left(\left|\frac{\overline{\phi}^{(i)}-\overline{\phi}^{(j)}}{\overline{{\phi}}^{(4)}}\right|\right),\max_{i,j=2,4}\left(\left|\frac{{\phi^{\prime}}^{(i)}-{\phi^{\prime}}^{(j)}}{{\phi^{\prime}}^{(4)}}\right|\right)\right]. (9) The superscripts indicate the temporal segment the data originates from, while ϕ ¯ \overline{\phi} and ϕ ′ \phi^{\prime} represent the spanwise-averaged mean and standard deviation of the signal, respectively. Once χ \chi settles around a certain percentage, the flow’s statistics have stabilized over the three most recent temporal quarters, implying the numerical transient was evacuated after the first quarter. Nevertheless, data sampling starts only after the fourth quarter to provide an additional margin of confidence. The convergence criterion method is initially evaluated after four flow-through times (FTTs) to ensure enough samples are collected for a reliable assessment. If the signal variations have not yet reached a steady level, the simulation is extended for one FTT at a time until they have. For illustration, Figure 17 displays the metric for the u u and v v velocity components for the [ R ​ e , α , a , n ] = [ 1796 , 11 , 0.424 , 1 ] [Re,\alpha,a,n]=[1796,11,0.424,1] case. Note that for the vertical velocity, only the standard deviation is considered, as its mean approaches zero, which would cause the criterion to diverge. For v v , χ \chi is computed using standard deviation only. Stabilization indicates the evacuation of numerical transients and the onset of statistically steady flow. Once the numerical transient has been evacuated, the solution fields are sampled at a non-dimensional time-step of Δ ​ t s = 0.04732 \Delta t_{s}=0.04732 . While the solver requires much smaller temporal steps to maintain numerical stability and accuracy, saving the full resolution is computationally prohibitive due to storage requirements. Sub-sampling the simulation at a coarser time-step inevitably results in a loss of information. To quantify the extent of this loss, the energy spectrum is examined. Figure 12 and Figure 13 display the energy spectra for the circular cylinder flow case at R ​ e = 3900 Re=3900 , evaluated at ( x / D , y / D ) = [ ( 0.71 , 0.66 ) , ( 3.0 , 0.0 ) ] (x/D,y/D)=[(0.71,0.66),(3.0,0.0)] , computed using the original u u -velocity signal and the sub-sampled solution at Δ ​ t s = 0.04732 \Delta t_{s}=0.04732 . The spectra match closely across most of the frequencies. In particular, all major characteristic frequencies are accurately recovered. Beyond the inertial subrange, however, the sub-sampled signal is unable to represent the highest frequencies due to its coarser temporal resolution. This is deemed acceptable as this portion of the spectrum is primarily influenced by numerical dissipation rather than physical processes and contains relatively low energy. Note that the sub-sampling acts in time only: each stored snapshot retains the full spatial resolution, so the small-scale spatial content scored by the spectral metric of Section 5 is preserved in full. Per-case wake-probe spectra. In addition to the convergence probes P 4 P_{4} , P 6.5 P_{6.5} and P 9.5 P_{9.5} described above, three further centerline probes, denoted P 2.5 ′ P^{\prime}_{2.5} , P 3.5 ′ P^{\prime}_{3.5} and P 4.5 ′ P^{\prime}_{4.5} , are placed 2.5 ​ D 2.5D , 3.5 ​ D 3.5D and 4.5 ​ D 4.5D behind the trailing edge of each object ( y / D = 0 y/D=0 ). A velocity power spectrum of u u and v v is computed at each probe for every one of the 400 cases from the 400 saved snapshots of the sub-sampled trajectory ( Δ ​ t s \Delta t_{s} ). The resulting spectra are released with each case in the public dataset, together with the script used to compute them. They provide per-case, physically grounded evidence of the turbulent energy cascade as well as an evaluation target for surrogate models beyond the aggregate metrics of Section 5 . Appendix I Dataset splits TurtLES is split at the trajectory level into 320 training, 40 validation and 40 test trajectories (80/10/10) such that no trajectory is shared between subsets. Each shape family n ∈ { 1 , 2 , 5 , ∞ } n\in\{1,2,5,\infty\} contains 100 trajectories and is split exactly 80/10/10. Within each family, the assignment of trajectories to subsets is optimized so that the distribution of ( R ​ e , α , log ⁡ a ) (Re,\alpha,\log a) in every subset matches that of the full dataset. This prevents a held-out set from concentrating in a corner of the parameter space, which at these subset sizes (40 trajectories) is otherwise likely. Appendix J mini-TurtLES : standardized reduced-size subsets The full dataset is 14 TB, which is prohibitive for many research groups. We therefore define mini-TurtLES , a collection of standardized subsets that reduce the dataset along two independent axes: geometry and time. Fixing the subset definitions ensures that experiments performed in different laboratories use identical data, facilitating reproducibility and fair comparison across published results. Some storage floor is unavoidable: resolving three-dimensional turbulence requires dense meshes and a sampling interval fine enough to retain the inertial subrange, and reducing either removes the physical content that distinguishes TurtLES from existing benchmarks. Geometry tiers (G4–G1). These tiers reduce the number of obstacle geometry families while retaining the full range of Reynolds numbers ( R ​ e Re ), angles of attack ( α \alpha ), and the geometric semi-axis parameter ( a a ). G4 contains all four families n ∈ { 1 , 2 , 5 , ∞ } n\in\{1,2,5,\infty\} ; G3 removes the n = 5 n=5 family; G2 further removes n = 1 n=1 ; and G1 retains only the elliptical family ( n = 2 n=2 ). Each tier therefore covers the same physical parameter space with progressively fewer geometries. The ordering follows increasing simplicity while preserving representative flow behavior: n = 5 n=5 is removed first because it exhibits the least distinctive dynamics, whereas n = 2 n=2 is retained in every tier because it is the best-studied and most widely used benchmark geometry. Temporal tiers (T100–T400). These tiers retain only the first 100, 200, 300, or 400 snapshots of each trajectory. The geometry and temporal tiers are independent and can be combined freely (e.g. G2-T200 or G1-T400). Table 6 reports the approximate download size of each subset. Appendix L Baseline implementations This section documents the architectures, hyperparameters, and training recipes used for the four neural-operator baselines reported in Section 5 . We first describe the components shared across baselines, then provide a per-model architectural summary. A consolidated view of the hyperparameters appears in Table 7 . Shared training and inference setup. All baselines are trained on the same temporal task: predict the residual Δ ​ u t + 1 = u t + 1 − u t \Delta u_{t+1}=u_{t+1}-u_{t} from a single context frame u t u_{t} , conditioned on the Reynolds number R ​ e Re . Training uses a standard mean-squared-error loss on the residual. We sample one window per gradient step from a uniform-random subset of 524,288 524{,}288 mesh nodes per trajectory, repeated for 100,000 100{,}000 gradient steps with an effective batch size of 2 2 on a single H100 GPU (80 GB). All four baselines are trained on the 320 training trajectories and evaluated on the 40 test trajectories of the split defined in Appendix I . The optimizer is AdamW with learning rate 10 − 4 10^{-4} , minimum learning rate 10 − 7 10^{-7} , weight decay 10 − 3 10^{-3} , β 1 = 0.9 \beta_{1}=0.9 , β 2 = 0.95 \beta_{2}=0.95 , and gradient-norm clipping at 1.0 1.0 . The schedule is a linear warmup over the first 5,000 5{,}000 steps followed by a cosine decay. PDE-parameter conditioning uses only R ​ e Re ; geometry parameters ( α , a , n ) (\alpha,a,n) are not exposed to the model and are absorbed into the spatial coordinates and field statistics. For all models except GINO we enable bfloat16 mixed precision and torch.compile . GINO’s FNO backbone relies on complex-valued spectral convolutions that interact poorly with bfloat16 autocast and produce graph-breaking warnings under torch.compile via the neuraloperator / tltorch factorized-tensor indexing path. We therefore train GINO in fp32 without torch.compile , with a per-GPU batch size of one and gradient accumulation of two to keep the effective batch size at two. Activation checkpointing is enabled for all transformer backbones. Apart from the precision and gradient-accumulation adjustments required by GINO, all four baselines follow an identical protocol: the same residual objective, the same node subsample per gradient step, and the same optimizer, schedule, step count, effective batch size and single-GPU budget. No baseline received additional hyperparameter tuning. At inference time we use a batch size of one and a context window of one frame. The full mesh ( 2.9 2.9 – 10.5 10.5 million nodes per trajectory) is processed either by the model’s encoder–decoder split (UPT, GINO, CALM-PDE) or by tile partition (Transolver++) with a chunk size of 524,288 524{,}288 output points. Each test trajectory is unrolled for 399 399 autoregressive steps starting from the first ground-truth frame. UPT ( Alkin et al., 2024 ) . UPT is a perceiver-style transformer for unstructured meshes. The mesh is first pooled to K = 16,384 K=16{,}384 supernodes via a runtime k k -nearest-neighbor gather ( k = 32 k=32 ) and a small message-passing MLP. Two encoder transformer blocks then operate on the supernode tokens. A perceiver cross-attention compresses the supernodes to L = 4,096 L=4{,}096 learned latent tokens, on top of which we run four self-attention blocks. Two more transformer blocks act on the decoder side before a perceiver cross-attention queries the original mesh coordinates with sincos positional embeddings (max angular frequency 100 100 ) and produces the per-node residual. The hidden dimension is 384 384 with 6 6 attention heads, an MLP expansion ratio of 4 4 , no dropout, and DiT-style adaLN-zero conditioning on R ​ e Re . The trained model has 61.96 61.96 M parameters. GINO ( Li et al., 2023b ) . GINO consists of a graph-neural-operator (GNO) encoder that maps the irregular mesh onto a regular latent grid, an FNO operating on that grid, and a GNO decoder that queries the predicted residual at the original mesh nodes. The latent grid has resolution 12 3 12^{3} in [ 0 , 1 ] 3 [0,1]^{3} , with input coordinates min–max normalized to that cube. Both GNOs use a linear transform with neighborhood radius 0.033 0.033 (about one cell at this resolution). The FNO has four spectral layers, 6 × 6 × 6 6{\times}6{\times}6 Fourier modes, 64 64 hidden channels, and lifting/projection MLPs with channel ratios of 2 2 and 4 4 respectively. PDE-parameter conditioning is realized through an AdaIN layer ( 4 4 conditioning features). We use the official neuraloperator implementation with Open3D-accelerated GNO kernels ( Kossaifi et al., 2026 ) . The trained model has 3.10 3.10 M parameters. This size reflects the shared training budget rather than a tuned choice. GINO is by some margin the slowest baseline per gradient step, because the complex-valued spectral convolutions of its FNO backbone preclude bfloat16 autocast and torch.compile and therefore run in fp32 with gradient accumulation. Within the shared budget of 100,000 100{,}000 steps ( ∼ \sim 20 h on a single H100) the model had not converged even at 3.10 3.10 M parameters, and larger configurations were less accurate for the same wall-clock time. The reported configuration is therefore the best achievable under the shared budget. Transolver++ ( Luo et al., 2025 ) . Transolver++ is a monolithic transformer that aggregates N N spatial tokens into a small set of learned physics-aware slices via Gumbel-softmax routing, applies self-attention on the slice tokens, then scatters back to the mesh. We use 8 8 transformer blocks with hidden dimension 512 512 , 8 8 attention heads, MLP ratio 4 4 , 128 128 slices per head, GELU activation, and no dropout. PDE-parameter conditioning is an additive bias on the encoder output. Because Transolver++ is monolithic, with input and output cardinality equal by construction, partitioning the output necessarily partitions the input as well; there is no latent bottleneck at which the two can be split. Its inference path therefore uses a tile partition rather than an encoder–decoder split: disjoint chunks of the mesh are processed independently, which bounds memory but removes cross-chunk context. This is a consequence of the architecture at this mesh size. The trained model has 21.79 21.79 M parameters. Transolver++ was developed for steady-state prediction rather than autoregressive dynamics. We include it because it represents the current state of the art for PDE surrogates on million-point geometries, which is one of the defining constraints of this dataset. CALM-PDE ( Hagnberger et al., 2025 ; Kassaï Koupaï et al., 2026 ) . We adopt the continuous-convolution encoder/decoder of CALM-PDE combined with the scaling improvements introduced in ECHO; the implementation is registered as CALM-PDE in our codebase.
