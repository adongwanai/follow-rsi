Large language models (LLMs) have been increasingly composed into multi-agent workflows to accomplish complex and multi-step tasks. As the open-weight models continue to close the performance gap with the proprietary ones, model parameters are becoming a less significant source of competitive advantage in the artificial intelligence market. Yet how these models are efficiently scheduled onto distributed infrastructure such as the cloud, with respect to overall latency, monetary cost and resource utilization, remains largely unaddressed in the literature despite being an increasingly critical problem. Existing agentic workflow frameworks mostly focus on output quality and task success rates over popular benchmarks, rarely accounting for execution dynamics of LLM-based agents such as inference latency, computation power of heterogeneous resources, cloud-specific billing period and resource provisioning overheads. Therefore, this paper formulates multi-agent scheduling in cloud computing as a multi-objective optimization problem that jointly minimizes makespan and cost while maximizing resource utilization. Building on this formulation, we propose MO-SAIC ( M ulti- O bjective S cheduling of Multi- A gent Workflows i n C loud Computing), a scheduling framework comprising four multi-objective schedulers with Non-Dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II), Weighted-Sum Genetic Algorithm (W-GA), a multi-objective adaptation of Heterogeneous Earliest Finish Time (MO-HEFT) and a random baseline (RAND). We evaluate the schedulers from the workflow scheduling, multi-objective optimization and system perspectives to measure their overall performance, quality of their Pareto-optimal fronts and impacts of workflow topology, cloud resource composition and scalability parameters. We report the experimental results with their statistical significance via the Wilcoxon test. Lastly, we discuss the limitations of MO-SAIC as well as future research directions. Keywords: Large Language Models, Multi-Agent Workflows, Workflow Scheduling, Multi-Objective Optimization, Cloud Computing, Evolutionary Algorithms 1. Introduction Consider an analogy from the history of cryptography. The field spent its early years as a state secret where advantage depended on which government could keep the cryptographic techniques hidden the longest through the mid-twentieth century. The decades since have reversed this trend, especially with the emergence of public-key cryptography. After that shift, security came to rest not on hiding the techniques but on protecting comparatively small secret keys. This transition follows the famous Kerckhoffs’s principle ( Kerckhoffs, 1883 ) , stating that a cryptosystem should not rely on the secrecy of the algorithm. We believe that large language models and agentic systems are currently heading toward an analogous transition. As open-weight models continue to close the performance gap with proprietary ones and model architectures are increasingly shared, model parameters are becoming a less significant source of competitive advantage. What increasingly differentiates is becoming not which models are used but how they are used at scale with respect to the efficient management of overall latency, cost and resource utilization. Rather than the model layer of artificial intelligence, the operational layer will be the harder challenge for large organizations, making the scheduling of agentic workflows on distributed infrastructures an increasingly critical research problem. LLMs have rapidly progressed from static text generators to autonomous agents with advanced capabilities of planning, reasoning, invoking external tools and acting on their own. Further techniques for LLMs including tool-augmented generation ( Schick et al., 2023 ) and interleaving of reasoning and acting ( Yao et al., 2022 ) have enabled these agents to actively query APIs, execute code and retrieve information to complete complex tasks. They often chain together many such steps instead of acting on a single request in isolation. This conceptual shift in LLMs has also been documented across a rapidly growing body of survey literature ( Wang et al., 2024 ) . However, all these autonomous actions consume real computational resources in the background, incurring latency and cost. As agents are integrated into production systems at increasing scale, their total operational footprint has become a significant concern to address in its own right. A single LLM-based agent is rarely sufficient for complex and multi-step tasks, which has led to composing multiple specialized agents into multi-agent workflows. An increasing number of works in the literature address the automation and optimization of this multi-agent workflow composition. For instance, AFlow ( Zhang et al., 2025b ) employs a Monte Carlo tree search technique to iteratively sample, evaluate and refine different workflows. EvoFlow ( Zhang et al., 2025a ) instead evolves a diverse population of workflows based on an evolutionary algorithm with niching solution selection. However, once workflow topology has been fixed, no prior work has sufficiently addressed how its agents should be effectively assigned to and executed on physical cloud resources. In addition, while scheduling a single-agent system is relatively straightforward, the same is not true for multi-agent systems since such systems allow distinct agents to run on entirely separate resources. This gap motivates the problem of multi-agent workflow scheduling in distributed systems ( Ismayilov, 2026b ) . Scheduling traditional workflows across a pool of resources is not a new problem in the literature. Multi-objective evolutionary algorithms, most notably NSGA-II ( Deb et al., 2002 ) , have provided a technique to search for Pareto-optimal trade-offs when two or more objectives (e.g., makespan and cost) cannot be simultaneously minimized. In this respect, NSGA-II has been applied extensively to workflow scheduling in cloud computing ( Durillo et al., 2012 ; Ismayilov and Topcuoglu, 2020 ) for the optimization of several objectives including makespan, cost, utilization, energy consumption, reliability and degree of imbalance. However, existing scheduling techniques are primarily designed for traditional workflows (e.g., Montage in astronomy ( Deelman et al., 2015 ) ), not for agentic workflows where they do not consider the inherent characteristics of agents (e.g., inference time, tool calling time, output token size). Additionally, agents increasingly require heterogeneous CPU and GPU instances rather than a CPU-only pool. Figure 1 illustrates the distinction between the single-objective and multi-objective scheduling where the single-objective scheduler commits to a single solution purely via makespan without evaluating potential trade-offs even when it is extremely costly. The multi-objective scheduler instead offers multiple solutions that minimize latency, minimize cost or maximize resource utilization. This clearly shows that multi-agent workflow scheduling is inherently a multi-objective problem. Therefore, we position our work precisely at this intersection. Figure 1 . Contributions This paper makes the following contributions to the literature: • A formal model of multi-agent workflow scheduling in cloud computing. We formulate multi-agent workflow scheduling in cloud computing as a multi-objective optimization problem by considering the minimization of workflow makespan and monetary cost and the maximization of resource utilization. This problem abstracts four critical models: (i) a LLM-based agent model with inference latency, model-specific throughput, tool latency and output token size; (ii) a multi-agent workflow model through canonical agentic reasoning patterns including chain-of-thought, self-consistency, tree-of-thought, debate and self-refine; (iii) a heterogeneous cloud resource model by unifying CPU and GPU computation capacity; and (iv) a workflow simulation model to execute workflows on resources by considering cloud-specific issues such as hourly-billing and resource (de)provisioning overheads. • MO-SAIC – a multi-objective scheduling framework. We propose a novel multi-objective multi-agent workflow scheduling framework for cloud computing, MO-SAIC, with the total of four schedulers: (i) a non-dominated sorting genetic algorithm scheduler (NSGA-II) as Pareto metaheuristic; (ii) a weighted-sum genetic algorithm scheduler (W-GA) as a scalarized metaheuristic; (iii) a multi-objective HEFT scheduler (MO-HEFT) as a classical list heuristic; and (iv) a random baseline scheduler (RAND) with no intelligence. • A controllable synthetic workflow generator. We employ a parametric and synthetic multi-agent workflow generator ( Ismayilov, 2026a ) to generate workflows with a diverse combination of canonical agentic reasoning patterns with given workflow size, depth and width. This generator allows controlled empirical study of how workflow topology affects scheduling decisions and quality. • Extensive experimental study. We extensively evaluate these four schedulers under various test scenarios to measure: (i) their overall performance relative to a random baseline scheduler; (ii) quality of their Pareto-optimal fronts; (iii) effects of workflow topology with changing size, depth and width; and (iv) effects of cloud resources with resource size and CPU/GPU pool composition. In our experiments, we use several performance measures from three distinct perspectives: (i) optimization objectives, resource load imbalance, distinct resource count for the workflow scheduling perspective; (ii) number of non-dominated solutions, Schott’s spacing, hypervolume and visual Pareto-optimal fronts for the multi-objective perspective and (iii) scheduling runtime for the system perspective. The statistical significance is shown via Wilcoxon rank-sum test across independent runs on distinct workflows. • A further discussion of limitations and future directions. We also discuss the current assumptions and limitations of MO-SAIC against the practical requirements for production deployments and the potential research directions to extend this research. 1.2. Related Work This section reviews the relevant works from the literature in three main perspectives including LLM-based agentic workflows, multi-objective workflow scheduling in cloud computing and agent scheduling for LLM serving. 2.1. LLM-Based Agentic Workflows Many works in the literature have built LLM-based agentic workflows by composing multiple agents with heterogeneous roles and models. Wu et al. ( Wu et al., 2023 ) present AutoGen, a general framework where multiple agents with different roles and tools communicate with one another to accomplish a common task collaboratively. Hong et al. ( Hong et al., 2024 ) propose MetaGPT which employs standardized operating procedures from human software-engineering workflows for role-specialized agents. This enables agents to have human-like expertise and verify intermediate results to reduce error. Li et al. ( Li et al., 2023 ) design Camel to study how two agents autonomously and complementarily can cooperate toward a common goal with role-playing. Park et al. ( Park et al., 2023 ) populate an interactive sandbox environment with 25 generative agents that plan, act and reflect over time to produce emergent social behavior (e.g., planning their days, sharing news). For architectural characterization, construction techniques and application domains, Wang et al. ( Wang et al., 2024 ) present broader perspectives. The multi-agent workflows all these frameworks orchestrate are mostly composed from a small set of canonical reasoning patterns. For instance, Wei et al. ( Wei et al., 2022 ) show that eliciting intermediate reasoning states via chain-of-thought substantially improves LLM performance on complex tasks. Wang et al. ( Wang et al., 2022 ) extend the concept by sampling multiple independent reasoning paths and aggregating into a single agent via self-consistency. Yao et al. ( Yao et al., 2023 ) address explicit search over a tree of intermediate reasoning states rather than a linear reasoning. Du et al. ( Du et al., 2023 ) show that two agents debating and critiquing the answers of the other over several rounds improves factuality and reasoning. Madaan et al. ( Madaan et al., 2023 ) show that only a single agent iteratively critiquing its own response improves the response quality over several rounds. Finally, Yao et al. ( Yao et al., 2022 ) propose ReAct, which interleaves reasoning traces with tool actions so that agents dynamically revise their reasoning based on their observations. 2.2. Multi-Objective Workflow Scheduling in Cloud Computing Multi-objective optimization has been extensively applied to workflow scheduling in cloud computing, most commonly targeting the trade-off between makespan and monetary cost. Durillo et al. ( Durillo et al., 2012 ) adapt the classical HEFT heuristic ( Topcuoglu et al., 1999 ) into MO-HEFT which is a Pareto-based list scheduling algorithm to maintain a set of partial scheduling solutions and measure its performance against synthetic and real-world workflows. Zhu et al. ( Zhu et al., 2015 ) instead propose a NSGA-II-based evolutionary multi-objective metaheuristic for the joint optimization of the makespan and cost. Li et al. ( Li et al., 2021 ) later improve the search performance of NSGA-II ( Deb et al., 2002 ) for workflow scheduling through a scoring criterion and a dynamic hierarchical population. Ismayilov et al. ( Ismayilov and Topcuoglu, 2018 ; Ismayilov and Topcuoglu, 2020 ) extend multi-objective workflow scheduling to a dynamic setting so that it reacts better to environmental changes such as resource failures by incorporating a neural network into NSGA-II. They consider makespan, cost, utilization, energy, reliability and degree of imbalance as objectives. To minimize energy consumption together with makespan and cost, Yassa et al. ( Yassa et al., 2013 ) combine a hybrid particle swarm optimization with the dynamic voltage and frequency scaling technique. This enables the underlying processors to operate in different voltage supply levels by changing their clock frequencies. Later, Verma et al. ( Verma and Kaushal, 2017 ) hybridize particle swarm optimization with a budget and deadline-constrained HEFT especially for scientific workflows. Mohammadzadeh et al. ( Mohammadzadeh and Masdari, 2023 ) propose another hybrid algorithm based on the seagull and grasshopper optimization algorithms to jointly optimize makespan, cost, energy and throughput, now with resources from multiple cloud providers. Abazari et al. ( Abazari et al., 2019 ) broaden the set of objectives further to include the minimization of security risk in the cloud and the response time. 2.3. Model and Agent Scheduling for LLM Serving There exists a smaller and more recent group of works for scheduling of LLM executions across LLM engines. Luo et al. ( Luo et al., 2025 ) propose Autellix which enriches schedulers with program-level statistics and minimizes end-to-end latency. Lin et al. ( Lin et al., 2024 ) consider the data dependencies between consecutive LLM calls through semantic variables so that they can be co-scheduled. Jiang et al. ( Jiang et al., 2023 ) formally define the scheduling problem of LLM serving over a set of heterogeneous GPU resources as a constrained optimization problem. Griggs et al. ( Griggs et al., 2024 ) assign LLM calls to GPU resources with distinct prices and capabilities to reduce cost under a certain latency target. Ong et al. ( Ong et al., 2025 ) focus on routing to two classes of models as strong models generating high-quality responses at a higher cost and weak models generating lower-quality responses but at a reduced cost. They present a training framework that dynamically select between these models during inference. Finally, the recent work ( Peng et al., 2026 ) proposes HexAGenT as a workflow and heterogeneity-aware scheduling algorithm for the online workflow scheduling problem. Unlike these works, our focus is offline scheduling that assigns each agent to a cloud resource and executes the model on that instance while optimizing makespan, cost and utilization jointly. 3. Multi-Objective Multi-Agent Workflow Scheduling Formulating the multi-agent workflow scheduling problem requires the characterization of (i) the individual LLM-based agents, (ii) the agentic workflows as the composition of individual agents with a certain topology, (iii) the cloud computing as the infrastructure provider to run the workflows and (iv) the simulator to simulate the execution of the workflows in the cloud under certain conditions and constraints. Therefore, this section develops these models to culminate in a formal multi-objective optimization of the problem to be addressed. Multi-Agent Workflow Model A multi-agent workflow is a directed acyclic graph (DAG) G = ( 𝒜 , E ) G=(\mathcal{A},E) , where 𝒜 \mathcal{A} is the finite set of agents and E ⊆ 𝒜 × 𝒜 E\subseteq\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A} is a set of directed edges. Each edge ( α , α ′ ) ∈ E (\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})\in E indicates that agent α ′ \alpha^{\prime} depends on the output of agent α \alpha and hence, α \alpha must complete before α ′ \alpha^{\prime} can start execution. For an agent α ∈ 𝒜 \alpha\in\mathcal{A} , we write pred ⁡ ( α ) = { α ′ ∈ 𝒜 : ( α ′ , α ) ∈ E } \mathrm{pred}(\alpha)=\{\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}:(\alpha^{\prime},\alpha)\in E\} and succ ⁡ ( α ) = { α ′ ∈ 𝒜 : ( α , α ′ ) ∈ E } \mathrm{succ}(\alpha)=\{\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}:(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})\in E\} to define predecessors and successors, respectively. The workflows we generate are not arbitrary DAGs. Instead, they are composed of several canonical agentic reasoning patterns. A single-agent (SA) pattern is the simplest topology consisting of a single agent α 0 \alpha_{0} , serving as the atomic block for more complex pattern compositions. A chain-of-thought (CoT) pattern is a sequential chain of k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 agents α 0 , … , α k − 1 \alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{k-1} with ( α j , α j + 1 ) ∈ E (\alpha_{j},\alpha_{j+1})\in E for j ∈ { 0 , … , k − 2 } j\in\{0,\ldots,k-2\} . In CoT, each agent refines the output of the previous agent. A self-consistency (SC) pattern is k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 agents α 0 , … , α k − 1 \alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{k-1} that execute in parallel and all merge into a single agent α k \alpha_{k} , with ( α j , α k ) ∈ E (\alpha_{j},\alpha_{k})\in E for j ∈ { 0 , … , k − 1 } j\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\} . A tree-of-thought (ToT) pattern introduces parallel exploration, with a single agent α 0 \alpha_{0} fanning out to k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 parallel agents, with ( α 0 , α j ) ∈ E (\alpha_{0},\alpha_{j})\in E for j ∈ { 1 , … , k } j\in\{1,\ldots,k\} . A debate (Db) pattern models adversarial multi-turn reasoning between two agents back-and-forth over r ≥ 2 r\geq 2 rounds. To achieve acyclicity, we unroll it into 2 ​ r 2r distinct agent instances as α A ( 1 ) , α B ( 1 ) , … , α A ( r ) , α B ( r ) \alpha_{A}^{(1)},\alpha_{B}^{(1)},\ldots,\alpha_{A}^{(r)},\alpha_{B}^{(r)} in sequence. Finally, a self-refine (SR) pattern is a chain of k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 agents that revises its own output over r ≥ 2 r\geq 2 rounds, which is similarly unrolled into r r copies of the k k -agent chain. Figure 2 illustrates all these six patterns. A complete workflow is assembled by drawing multiple pattern instances and connecting them into a single weakly connected DAG where the last agent of the previous pattern is linked to the first agent of the next pattern and additional edges are added between agents at random. The order in which the patterns are sequenced follows a first-order Markov chain with pattern-to-pattern transition probabilities. Therefore, the workflow is not said to be merely a disjoint union of isolated patterns. Each workflow is characterized by properties: size N = | 𝒜 | N=|\mathcal{A}| as the total number of agents; depth d ⁡ ( G ) d(G) as the length of the longest path; and width w ⁡ ( G ) w(G) as the largest number of agents at the same depth level. Heterogeneous Cloud Model A cloud resource is a virtualized and on-demand computation instance to which an agent can be assigned for execution. Each resource r ∈ ℛ r\in\mathcal{R} is characterized by a tuple r = ( v r , g r , B r , p r ) r=(v_{r},g_{r},B_{r},p_{r}) , where v r ∈ ℤ ≥ 0 v_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} is the vCPU count, g r ∈ ℤ ≥ 0 g_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} is the vGPU count, B r ∈ ℝ + B_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{+} is the network bandwidth (Gbps) and p r ∈ ℝ + p_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{+} is the hourly on-demand price (USD/hour). For the total compute power of a resource, we unify CPU and GPU capacity into a single scalar via a weighted sum as: cp ⁡ ( r ) = w v ⋅ v r + w g ⋅ g r \mathrm{cp}(r)\;=\;w_{v}\cdot v_{r}\;+\;w_{g}\cdot g_{r} where w v , w g ∈ ℝ + w_{v},w_{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{+} are the weights for the relative contribution of a vCPU and a vGPU to inference. For instance, a CPU-only resource ( g r = 0 g_{r}=0 ) with w v = 1 w_{v}=1 reduces cp ⁡ ( r ) \mathrm{cp}(r) to only its vCPU count exactly. From the available resources in the cloud, we compose three environments as CPU-only ℛ cpu \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{cpu}} with g r = 0 g_{r}=0 for all r r , GPU-accelerated ℛ gpu \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{gpu}} with g r > 0 g_{r}>0 for r r and CPU+GPU including all resources. When two dependent agents run on different resources, the output of the first agent must be transferred to the second over the network, subject to the available bandwidth. The bottleneck bandwidth between any two resources r i , r j ∈ ℛ r_{i},r_{j}\in\mathcal{R} is computed as: B ⁡ ( r i , r j ) = min ⁡ ( B r i , B r j ) B(r_{i},r_{j})\;=\;\min(B_{r_{i}},B_{r_{j}}) where B ⁡ ( r i , r j ) B(r_{i},r_{j}) is the bandwidth between r i r_{i} and r j r_{j} . No network transfer occurs when two agents are hosted exactly on the same resource. In this work, we consider | ℛ cpu | = 30 |\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{cpu}}|=30 AWS EC2 CPU-only instances (see Table 5 ) and | ℛ gpu | = 30 |\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{gpu}}|=30 AWS EC2 GPU-accelerated instances (see Table 6 ). This resource catalog serves solely to parameterize v r v_{r} , g r g_{r} , B r B_{r} and p r p_{r} with concrete values for our simulation and our cloud model itself remains independent of this particular set of resources. Notably, our unified computation model treats CPU and GPU capacity as interchangeable, which is very straightforward. This might result in assigning large models to CPU-only resources. Although such assignments are impractical in real executions, they are admissible under our model which does not intend to capture real executions. For the sake of simplicity, no workflow-driven or cloud-driven uncertainty (e.g., fluctuation in inference latency, network congestion) is considered in this work. 3.3.1. CPU-Only Environment ℛ ⊆ ℛ cpu \mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{cpu}} This environment takes into account only the resources with no active GPU g r = 0 g_{r}=0 . This deliberate limitation on resource heterogeneity naturally results in certain scheduling impacts. First, unlike the GPU-only environment, there is no hardware-driven improvement on latency and the schedulers cannot buy more speed for the critical-path agents and must instead rely more heavily on parallelizing agents across many resources. Second, per-hour prices of CPU-only resources are cheaper when compared to the resources with GPU, resulting in lower total resource costs at the end of the optimization. In addition, CPU-only resources span a finer-grained range of vCPU counts and prices, which gives the schedulers a more fine-grained makespan–cost trade-off. In Table 5 , the specifications of the CPU-only instance families (M1, M3, M4, M5, M5D) considered are listed, spanning vCPU counts from 1 to 96, network bandwidth from 10 to 25 Gbps and hourly pricing from $0.044 to $5.424. 3.3.2. GPU-Accelerated Environment ℛ ⊆ ℛ gpu \mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{gpu}} Every resource in this environment is GPU-accelerated g r > 0 g_{r}>0 , offering more computational power per unit than a CPU-only environment. Here, agents complete their inference faster, compressing makespan toward the lower values. However, this compression is not free since GPU-accelerated resources incur considerably higher per-hour costs in return. Therefore, this environment challenges the schedulers to still find a meaningful cost–utilization trade-off once the makespan objective is largely satisfied by hardware alone. In Table 6 , the specifications of the GPU-accelerated instance families (G4dn, G5, G6, G6e, G7, G7e, P4d, P4de, P5 and P5en) are listed, spanning several NVIDIA GPU generations, from the T4 through the H200. 3.3.3. CPU+GPU Environment ℛ ⊆ ℛ cpu ∪ ℛ gpu \mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{cpu}}\cup\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{gpu}} This environment considers both CPU-only and GPU-accelerated resources at the same time for agent assignment. This provides a broader scheduling since organizations generally provision elastic cloud capacity in practice and relax the schedulers to make workload-specific decisions about which agent runs on which resource. In return, this hardens the overall optimization since the schedulers now have to map an agent on a cheaper but moderate-throughput CPU resource or a more expensive but high-throughput GPU resource or their hybrid composition. For instance, an agent on the critical path might benefit more from a GPU to reduce latency for the agents immediately waiting on it. Consequently, achievable Pareto-fronts for this environment are likely to be more diverse, spanning the full range from all-CPU to all-GPU scheduling solutions and every combination in between. 3.4. Multi-Agent Workflow Scheduling as Multi-Objective Optimization A multi-objective optimization problem (MOP) is a problem with two or more objectives where at least two objectives are in conflict. Without loss of generality, a MOP over a decision space X X can be formally defined in the following way: min x ∈ X \displaystyle\min_{x\in X} F ⁡ ( x ) = ( f 1 ​ ( x ) , … , f k ​ ( x ) ) T , \displaystyle\quad F(x)\;=\;\bigl(f_{1}(x),\ldots,f_{k}(x)\bigr)^{T}, s.t. g i ( x ) ≤ 0 , i = 1 , … , p , \displaystyle\quad g_{i}(x)\leq 0,\ i=1,\ldots,p, h j ( x ) = 0 , j = 1 , … , q , \displaystyle\quad h_{j}(x)=0,\ j=1,\ldots,q, where F : X → ℝ k F:X\to\mathbb{R}^{k} , k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 , maps each solution x x in decision space into a point in objective space ℝ k \mathbb{R}^{k} under the inequality and equality constraint functions g i g_{i} and h j h_{j} . Because of the conflicting objectives (i.e., improving an objective comes at the expense of another), there is typically no single x ∗ ∈ X x^{*}\in X that simultaneously optimizes every f i f_{i} . Therefore, a MOP represents such trade-offs via the concept of Pareto-domination. For a minimization problem, a solution x x Pareto-dominates x ′ x^{\prime} ( x ≺ x ′ x\prec x^{\prime} ) if f i ​ ( x ) ≤ f i ​ ( x ′ ) f_{i}(x)\leq f_{i}(x^{\prime}) for every i ∈ { 1 , … , k } i\in\{1,\ldots,k\} and f i ​ ( x ) < f i ​ ( x ′ ) f_{i}(x)<f_{i}(x^{\prime}) for at least one i i . In other words, x x is at least as good as x ′ x^{\prime} on every objective and strictly better on at least one. A solution x ∗ ∈ X x^{*}\in X is Pareto-optimal if no solution in X X dominates it, ∄ x ∈ X : x ≺ x ∗ \nexists\,x\in X:x\prec x^{*} where the set of every such solution is the Pareto-optimal set (POS) in decision space and its projection under F F in objective space is the Pareto-optimal front (POF). 3.5.1. Decision Variables The decision space for our agentic workflow scheduling problem is the set of all the resource assignment solutions as: X = { σ : 𝒜 → ℛ } X\;=\;\{\,\sigma:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{R}\,\} where the function maps every agent in the workflow to exactly one resource in the cloud. The schedulers search over the entire decision space to seek such a feasible assignment solution σ \sigma . For the sake of simplicity, we assume that each resource can execute at most one agent at a time, without considering the resource-level parallelism at the moment. 3.5.2. Objective Functions Every σ ∈ X \sigma\in X is evaluated by simulating its execution (see Algorithm 1 ) and collecting the resulting makespan, cost and utilization values. This lets us state the multi-agent workflow scheduling problem as the multi-objective optimization problem: min σ ∈ X F ⁡ ( σ ) = ( M ⁡ ( σ ) , C ⁡ ( σ ) , − U ⁡ ( σ ) ) T : X → ℝ 3 \min_{\sigma\in X}\quad F(\sigma)=(M(\sigma),C(\sigma),-U(\sigma))^{T}:X\to\mathbb{R}^{3} where the makespan objective M ⁡ ( σ ) M(\sigma) is the completion time of the last agent to finish: M ⁡ ( σ ) = max α ∈ 𝒜 ⁡ FT ⁡ [ α ] M(\sigma)\;=\;\max_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{FT}[\alpha] FT ⁡ [ α ] \mathrm{FT}[\alpha] is the finish time of agent task α \alpha . The cost objective is the total monetary cost across the billing periods of all resources: C ⁡ ( σ ) = ∑ r ∈ ℛ ∑ ( t s , t e ) ∈ billing ⁡ [ r ] ⌈ t e − t s 3600 ⌉ ⋅ p r C(\sigma)\;=\;\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\sum_{(t_{s},t_{e})\in\mathrm{billing}[r]}\Bigl\lceil\frac{t_{e}-t_{s}}{3600}\Bigr\rceil\cdot p_{r} where each billing period is rounded up to the nearest full hour and charged at its hourly on-demand price p r p_{r} . Note that the inner sum counts the number of cycles the resource r r is provisioned and de-provisioned. The utilization objective refers to the average resource busy time as: U ( σ ) = 1 | R ⁡ ( σ ) | ∑ r ∈ R ⁡ ( σ ) ∑ α : σ ⁡ ( α ) = r ET [ α ] ∑ ( t s , t e ) ∈ billing ⁡ [ r ] ( t e − t s ) U(\sigma)\;=\;\frac{1}{|R(\sigma)|}\sum_{r\in R(\sigma)}\frac{\sum_{\alpha\,:\,\sigma(\alpha)=r}\mathrm{ET}[\alpha]}{\sum_{(t_{s},t_{e})\in\mathrm{billing}[r]}(t_{e}-t_{s})} where ET ⁡ [ α ] \mathrm{ET}[\alpha] is the execution time of agent α \alpha and R ⁡ ( σ ) R(\sigma) is the set of resources to which σ \sigma assigns at least one agent. In this regard, M ⁡ ( σ ) M(\sigma) and C ⁡ ( σ ) C(\sigma) are in conflict since faster resources incur higher on-demand prices. U ⁡ ( σ ) U(\sigma) is also in conflict with M ⁡ ( σ ) M(\sigma) since maximizing busy times favors using fewer and heavily-loaded resources, which lowers parallelism. 3.5.3. Optimization Constraints The feasible region of a MOP is limited through the inequality and equality constraints g i ​ ( x ) ≤ 0 g_{i}(x)\leq 0 and h j ​ ( x ) = 0 h_{j}(x)=0 . Our agentic workflow scheduling problem involves four such constraints. The precedence constraint asks that no agent begin execution before all its predecessor agents have finished and all data has fully arrived: ST ⁡ [ α ] ≥ FT ⁡ [ α ′ ] + o ~ ​ ( α ′ ) / B ⁡ ( σ ⁡ ( α ′ ) , σ ⁡ ( α ) ) , ∀ ( α ′ , α ) ∈ E \mathrm{ST}[\alpha]\;\geq\;\mathrm{FT}[\alpha^{\prime}]+\tilde{o}(\alpha^{\prime})/B(\sigma(\alpha^{\prime}),\sigma(\alpha)),\qquad\forall\,(\alpha^{\prime},\alpha)\in E The resource serialization constraint requires that no resource execute two agents concurrently. For any two distinct agents α , α ′ ∈ 𝒜 \alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A} sharing a resource, σ ⁡ ( α ) = σ ⁡ ( α ′ ) = r \sigma(\alpha)=\sigma(\alpha^{\prime})=r , their execution intervals should not overlap: ST ⁡ [ α ] ≥ FT ⁡ [ α ′ ] or ST ⁡ [ α ′ ] ≥ FT ⁡ [ α ] \mathrm{ST}[\alpha]\geq\mathrm{FT}[\alpha^{\prime}]\qquad\text{or}\qquad\mathrm{ST}[\alpha^{\prime}]\geq\mathrm{FT}[\alpha] The assignment completeness constraint requires that every agent be assigned to exactly one resource: ∀ α ∈ 𝒜 , ∃ ! r ∈ ℛ : σ ( α ) = r \forall\,\alpha\in\mathcal{A},\ \exists!\,r\in\mathcal{R}:\sigma(\alpha)=r and finally the non-negativity constraint requires that no agent start before the workflow itself begins: ST ⁡ [ α ] ≥ 0 , ∀ α ∈ 𝒜 \mathrm{ST}[\alpha]\geq 0,\quad\forall\alpha\in\mathcal{A} The schedulers in MO-SAIC are designed to satisfy all these optimization constraints by construction, so no explicit constraint check or repair mechanism is necessary during solution construction. 3.5.4. Pareto-Optimality A scheduling solution σ \sigma is said to weakly dominate σ ′ \sigma^{\prime} ( σ ⪯ σ ′ \sigma\preceq\sigma^{\prime} ) if: M ⁡ ( σ ) \displaystyle M(\sigma) ≤ M ⁡ ( σ ′ ) \displaystyle\leq M(\sigma^{\prime}) C ⁡ ( σ ) \displaystyle C(\sigma) ≤ C ⁡ ( σ ′ ) \displaystyle\leq C(\sigma^{\prime}) U ⁡ ( σ ) \displaystyle U(\sigma) ≥ U ⁡ ( σ ′ ) \displaystyle\geq U(\sigma^{\prime}) and σ \sigma Pareto-dominates σ ′ \sigma^{\prime} ( σ ≺ σ ′ \sigma\prec\sigma^{\prime} ) if additionally at least one of these three holds strictly. A schedule σ ∗ ∈ X \sigma^{*}\in X is Pareto-optimal if no σ ∈ X \sigma\in X dominates it where the resulting POS is: X ∗ = { σ ∗ ∈ X : ∄ σ ∈ X , σ ≺ σ ∗ } X^{*}\;=\;\{\,\sigma^{*}\in X:\nexists\,\sigma\in X,\ \sigma\prec\sigma^{*}\,\} and its projection F ⁡ ( X ∗ ) F(X^{*}) in objective space is the POF. However, the decision space of the problem we define is far too large to search exhaustively in the complexity level of | ℛ | | 𝒜 | |\mathcal{R}|^{|\mathcal{A}|} . MO-SAIC Framework This section presents MO-SAIC, our multi-objective multi-agent workflow scheduling framework. Every scheduler in MO-SAIC searches over the same decision space X X and is evaluated through the same workflow simulation environment against the same three objectives of makespan, cost and utilization. The schedulers differ only in how they explore X X without any control over how their solutions are evaluated, so their results are comparable. 4.1. Framework Architecture MO-SAIC is composed of five components: (i) workflow generator, generating individual agentic workflows with given size, depth and width parameters; (ii) cloud, providing the heterogeneous CPU and GPU resource pool to which the agents can be assigned; (iii) schedulers, searching the resulting decision space for the optimal scheduling solutions; (iv) simulator, executing candidate scheduling solutions to reveal the objective vector; and (v) evaluator, ranking the population of scheduling solutions by non-dominated sorting and crowding distance to reveal the Pareto-optimal front. For the fair comparison of the schedulers, we keep all other components identical across the framework. As a pipeline, the workflow generator first generates a single workflow instance. Secondly, the schedulers take this instance and the available cloud resources to generate a population of scheduling solutions. Thirdly, regardless of which scheduler produced them, the simulator evaluates all solutions in the same way. Lastly, evaluator ranks the solutions to feed back to the schedulers until the maximum generation Γ max \Gamma_{\max} is reached and later to select the Pareto-optimal solutions. 4.2. NSGA-II Scheduler NSGA-II ( Deb et al., 2002 ) is an elitist multi-objective genetic algorithm that evolves a population of solutions across generations by using: (i) non-dominated sorting to rank solutions via Pareto-domination into several ranks; and (ii) crowding distance to preserve diversity in the same ranks. In this subsection, we customize NSGA-II to work with the solution representation and optimization objectives of our multi-objective agentic workflow scheduling problem. Refer to Algorithm 2 for the complete algorithm. 4.2.1. Solution Representation Each solution chromosome σ ∈ X \sigma\in X is represented as a string of decision variables with the length of the number of available agents in workflow | 𝒜 | |\mathcal{A}| . Each gene in a chromosome encodes the resource to which the agent on that position is assigned. During the execution of that agent (see Section 3.4 ), each gene is decoded to assign the agent to its corresponding resource. 4.2.2. Population Initialization The initial population of NSGA-II 𝒫 0 = { σ 0 , … , σ λ − 1 } \mathcal{P}_{0}=\{\sigma_{0},\ldots,\sigma_{\lambda-1}\} is drawn by assigning every agent to an independent and uniformly random resource as: σ i ( α ) ∼ Uniform ( ℛ ) , ∀ α ∈ 𝒜 , i = 0 , … , λ − 1 \sigma_{i}(\alpha)\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(\mathcal{R}),\qquad\forall\,\alpha\in\mathcal{A},\ i=0,\ldots,{\lambda-1} where λ \lambda is the number of solutions in the population. All the schedulers in this framework are randomly initialized, which implies they all start from statistically indistinguishable initial populations. Therefore, every improvement reported in the experimental study can be directly attributed to the search space exploration capabilities of the schedulers themselves. 4.2.3. Fitness Evaluation Every chromosome is evaluated at every generation with respect to the makespan, cost and utilization objectives of the simulation in cloud. The resulting objective vector F ⁡ ( σ ) = ( M ⁡ ( σ ) , C ⁡ ( σ ) , − U ⁡ ( σ ) ) F(\sigma)=(M(\sigma),C(\sigma),-U(\sigma)) is preserved alongside the solution for the rest of its lifetime across the generations. Refer to Section 3.5 for the calculations of these objectives. 4.2.4. Non-Dominated Sorting Given the population 𝒫 \mathcal{P} with 2 ​ λ 2\lambda individuals during environmental selection, the non-dominated sorting partitions it into successive fronts F 0 ​ ( 𝒫 ) , F 1 ​ ( 𝒫 ) , … F_{0}(\mathcal{P}),F_{1}(\mathcal{P}),\ldots by using Pareto-domination to select the best λ \lambda solutions. The next front F 1 ​ ( 𝒫 ) F_{1}(\mathcal{P}) has every remaining solution that is not dominated by any other solution where it goes until the entire population is exhausted. 4.2.5. Crowding Distance Within a single front F k ​ ( 𝒫 ) F_{k}(\mathcal{P}) , the crowding distance cd ⁡ ( σ ) \mathrm{cd}(\sigma) calculates how isolated σ \sigma is from its neighbors in the same front to push evolution to sparsely-explored regions. Parent Selection Parents are drawn via tournament selection where a certain number of chromosomes are first randomly sampled from the population and the best chromosome with respect to the rank and crowding distance wins the tournament. Every child needs two independently drawn parents from two independent tournaments. Pressure through rank and crowding distance naturally pushes parent selection toward a better Pareto-optimal front with less-crowded regions. 4.2.7. Crossover and Mutation Once two parents σ a , σ b \sigma_{a},\sigma_{b} are selected, the two-point crossover is applied by partitioning each chromosome into three contiguous segments. Then, the child inherits the outer two segments from σ a \sigma_{a} and the middle segment from σ b \sigma_{b} . Every gene of the resulting child is then independently mutated with the pre-specified mutation rate (see Table 2 ), which reassigns that agent to a new uniformly random resource in ℛ \mathcal{R} . 4.2.8. Weighted-Sum Genetic Algorithm Scheduler W-GA ( Murata et al., 1995 ) , shares the same solution representation, population initialization, crossover and mutation operators with NSGA-II. The difference from NSGA-II is that W-GA replaces the non-dominated sorting and crowding distance operators with a single scalar fitness score operator with respect to a dynamic weight vector of w ⁡ ( σ ) = ( w M , w C , w U ) w(\sigma)=(w_{M},w_{C},w_{U}) . At every generation, every individual σ \sigma is assigned its own independently drawn random weight vector w ⁡ ( σ ) w(\sigma) with w M + w C + w U = 1 w_{M}+w_{C}+w_{U}=1 . In other words, no two individuals and no two generations are scored against the same weights, which pushes the population toward different regions of search space for more front diversity. The fitness score function is defined in the following way: w M ⋅ M ⁡ ( σ ) − M min M max − M min + w C ⋅ C ⁡ ( σ ) − C min C max − C min + w U ⋅ ( 1 − U ⁡ ( σ ) − U min U max − U min ) w_{M}\cdot\frac{M(\sigma)-M_{\min}}{M_{\max}-M_{\min}}\;+\;w_{C}\cdot\frac{C(\sigma)-C_{\min}}{C_{\max}-C_{\min}}\;+\;w_{U}\cdot\left(1\;-\;\frac{U(\sigma)-U_{\min}}{U_{\max}-U_{\min}}\right) where M min , M max M_{\min},M_{\max} are the smallest and largest makespan in a generation; and C min , C max C_{\min},C_{\max} and U min , U max U_{\min},U_{\max} are defined analogously for cost and utilization, respectively. To maintain elitism across generations, two individuals with the best fitness scores are moved unmodified into the next generation while the remainder of the next population is filled through the tournament selection, crossover and mutation operators, as in NSGA-II. 4.4. Experimental Setup This section describes the experimental setup and methodology to compare the four schedulers in Section 4 . We present (i) the synthetic benchmark workflow generation for evaluation; (ii) the performance measures to inspect scheduling quality from the workflow scheduling, multi-objective and system perspectives; and (iii) the Wilcoxon rank-sum statistical significance test. 5.1. Benchmark Workflows Every workflow used in our experiments is generated by the parametric and synthetic workflow generator ( Ismayilov, 2026a ) so that the workflow structure can be varied systematically and independently. Given a target size N N , depth d ⁡ ( G ) d(G) and width w ⁡ ( G ) w(G) satisfying the feasibility inequality in Section 3.2 , the generator constructs a layered DAG with exactly d ⁡ ( G ) + 1 d(G)+1 depth levels with the widest level of w ⁡ ( G ) w(G) agents and wires every agent in an interior level to at least one predecessor in the level above and at least one successor in the level below (see Figure 5 ). In-degree and out-degree of each agent are drawn uniformly from [ 1 , 5 ] [1,5] . Each agent is additionally injected with up to five tool invocations. Every tool θ ∈ Θ ⁡ ( α ) \theta\in\Theta(\alpha) an agent invokes can be an internal tool with per-invocation latency ℓ θ ∈ [ 0.05 , 2 ] \ell_{\theta}\in[0.05,2] seconds or an external tool with ℓ θ ∈ [ 0.5 , 10 ] \ell_{\theta}\in[0.5,10] seconds, each invoked n θ ∈ [ 1 , 5 ] n_{\theta}\in[1,5] times. This topology construction lets three parameters N N , d ⁡ ( G ) d(G) and w ⁡ ( G ) w(G) be set directly and independently. The generator also supports composing a workflow directly from the six canonical reasoning patterns of single-agent, chain-of-thought, self-consistency, tree-of-thought, debate and self-refine, ordered by the pattern-transition Markov chain ( Ismayilov, 2026a ) to generate more semantically realistic agentic workflows (see Figure 6 for exact probabilities). Unless otherwise stated, experiments use the workflow size N = 50 N=50 , depth d ⁡ ( G ) = 15 d(G)=15 and width w ⁡ ( G ) = 8 w(G)=8 . Statistical Analysis All experiments are conducted on a single machine of MacBook Air with Apple M2 chip, 8 GB memory and 8 CPU cores. We run every scheduler independently 20 20 times with freshly generated workflow instance and random seed for every run. Once a workflow is generated, all schedulers are evaluated on this workflow. We collect the performance metrics per run and report their results with their mean and standard deviations across these 20 runs. With independent measures, our goal is to eliminate the impact of randomness available in evolutionary computation and catch the typical behaviors of schedulers across distinct workflow instances, rather than a single specific draw. The RAND scheduler is just used as the baseline algorithm. The percentage improvements of the other schedulers with respect to RAND are reported where the positive percentage means better than the baseline and vice versa. We perform the statistical significance test via the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and report significance at the conventional p < 0.05 p<0.05 threshold. Further Discussions MO-SAIC makes a number of design and implementation assumptions to keep the problem tractable. In this section, we discuss such further issues as well as potential future research directions: (1) Synthetic workflows rather than realistic agentic traces. The workflows in this paper are generated through our synthetic and parametric workflow generator ( Ismayilov, 2026a ) by compiling several agentic reasoning patterns under a Markovian transition matrix. Admittedly, this generator enables us to vary workflow size, depth and width independently and study their impacts in isolation. However, it might not fully reflect how real-world organizations compose their workflows in practice. In the future, we aim to collect realistic agentic workflow traces and re-evaluate our schedulers against them. (2) A simulated but not measured execution environment. Agent and cloud characteristics including inference latency, tool latency, provisioning and deprovisioning delays and network bandwidth are modeled in this work, by using static AWS pricing and LLM catalogs and Gaussian noise, rather than real-world measurements taken from cloud providers. However, real environment execution might show variability from network congestion, cold-start delays and interference, which can affect makespan. Deploying and simulating the MO-SAIC schedulers on an actual cloud environment is a promising direction. (3) A simplified computation power unification for CPUs and GPUs. Our computation power model combines vCPU and vGPU counts through a single fixed-weighted sum cp ⁡ ( r ) = w v ​ v r + w g ​ g r \mathrm{cp}(r)=w_{v}v_{r}+w_{g}g_{r} where it treats every vGPU unit as equally powerful regardless of GPU generation. However, in reality, a T4 and an H200 differ for the same workload. A natural next step is to replace this linear abstraction with per-generation throughput multipliers for more realistic workflow simulations. (4) Abstracting away hardware compatibility. MO-SAIC permits any agent to be assigned to any cloud resource, so a scheduler might place a large model onto a CPU-only instance whose memory could not actually hold the model weights. However, in practice, model weights impose minimum memory footprint that only certain resources can satisfy. Some models might require multiple GPUs together, rather than a single resource. Consequently, some of the solutions our schedulers generate as optimal might not be deployed on real infrastructure. Therefore, incorporating per-model memory requirements into the assignment constraints might be a critical future direction for a production-ready MO-SAIC. (5) A static and offline scheduling formulation. This paper considers the multi-objective optimization problem as time-invariant over fixed workflows and fixed cloud resources. However, real deployments might face changes over time, such as software and hardware failures. In addition, agents might fail and retry in tool calling. The schedulers in this paper are not capable of reacting to these scenarios effectively. Therefore, extending our work to a dynamic multi-objective problem would be beneficial for more adaptive multi-objective scheduling. (6) Set of optimization objectives. MO-SAIC optimizes makespan, cost and utilization objectives by remaining entirely agnostic to the correctness of model responses. However, scheduling depending on smaller models might degrade the quality of that agent contribution even if it reduces cost and latency. This trade-off is not addressed where introducing task success probability or response quality as a further objective might be taken into consideration. In addition, energy consumption might be also studied in the future together with the existing objectives. 8. Conclusion In this paper, we formulate LLM-based multi-agent workflow scheduling in cloud computing as a multi-objective optimization problem, jointly considering the minimization of makespan and cost and the maximization of resource utilization. For the given problem, we propose MO-SAIC, a multi-objective scheduling framework with a total of four schedulers. To evaluate the schedulers in MO-SAIC, we employ a parametric and synthetic multi-agent workflow generator to systematically vary workflow topology with respect to size, depth and width. We measure performance under the extensive test scenarios as: (i) their overall performance improvements relative to RAND; (ii) quality of their fronts; (iii) impacts of workflow topology; and (iv) impacts of cloud resources. The performance metrics we use span three main perspectives as: (i) objective functions, resource load imbalance, distinct resource count for the workflow scheduling perspective; (ii) number of non-dominated solutions, Schott’s spacing, hypervolume and visual Pareto-optimal fronts for the multi-objective perspective; and (iii) scheduling runtime for the system perspective. Lastly, we identify the current limitations of MO-SAIC and the potential future research directions. We expect that, rather than the model layer, the operational layer of artificial intelligence will be increasingly important in the future as the open-weight models close the performance gap with the proprietary ones, making the multi-objective multi-agent workflow scheduling a critical problem to address for large organizations.
