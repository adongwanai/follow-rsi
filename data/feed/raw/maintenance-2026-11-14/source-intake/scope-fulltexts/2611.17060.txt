E Acknowledgments License: CC BY 4.0 arXiv:2611.17060v1 [cs.CV] 12 Nov 2026 Ropedia Xperience 2 Ropedia Research Team Abstract Large foundation models such as GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 have recently been shown to control robots directly from visual observations.
They nevertheless remain brittle on long-horizon, contact-rich tasks in real-world environments.
Further progress depends on two complementary resources: demonstrations of human behavior that supervise how tasks are performed, and interactive environments in which policies can be rolled out, evaluated, and improved in closed loop.
Language models have advanced along well-characterized scaling laws; the corresponding question for physical AI is how capability scales with environments and behaviors, which first requires identifying which data to scale and how to produce and reuse such data continuously.
To address this question, we introduce Ropedia Xperience 2 , comprising 1,589 released metric-scale 3D scans and a collection of 4,230 egocentric recordings of 74 tasks (131 hours).
Data are captured with a handheld LiDAR scanner and a head-mounted multi-camera recorder, without robots or motion-capture infrastructure; a processing pipeline reconstructs meshes and 3D Gaussian splatting models, estimates camera trajectories and hand and body motion, generates hierarchical language annotations, and supports registration of recordings to scene geometry.
The reconstructions serve as reference for benchmarks on depth and geometry estimation, 3D object detection, and spatial reasoning.
We further show that each capture can be repurposed for robot learning: scenes are converted into physically interactive simulation environments, human motion is retargeted to whole-body robot trajectories, and additional robot behaviors are obtained through virtual teleoperation and automated long-horizon trajectory generation.
We propose this joint capture of environments and behaviors as a gold standard for physical AI data collection, laying a data foundation for establishing scaling laws over environments and behaviors. The scene count refers to released scans; location, recording, task, and duration counts describe the broader collection inventory. 1 Introduction Large foundation models such as GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 have recently been shown to control robots directly from visual observations [ 63 , 50 ] .
On the 42-task RoboDojo benchmark [ 8 ] , GPT-6 Astra attains a reported average success rate of 22.48%, surpassing the 40 public policies in that comparison [ 63 ] , and it reaches 92% on RxR [ 23 ] instruction following and 82% on HM3D [ 45 ] object search [ 16 ] .
These models nevertheless remain far from practical deployment: an evaluation across eight robot configurations reports no success in 11 plug-insertion trials across three platforms and 9 successes in 23 navigation-and-search trials for GPT-6 Astra, while tracking progress across many subtasks remains difficult [ 49 ] . Enabling large foundation models to control robots reliably over long-horizon, complex tasks in real environments requires two complementary resources: (i) behavior demonstrations from which the models can learn, and (ii) environments in which they can execute tasks and accumulate experience.
Language models have advanced along well-characterized scaling laws relating performance to data, model size, and compute [ 18 ] ; the corresponding question for physical AI is how capability scales with environments and behaviors.
Answering it first requires identifying which environment and behavior data to scale, and how such data can be produced and reused continuously. Bars are qualitative five-step ratings, not measurements; the measured and demonstrated notes refer to results from Section 5.2 , and the reference row quotes DROID [ 21 ] . To address this question, we introduce Ropedia Xperience 2 , a dataset of real environments and behaviors for physical AI.
Ropedia Xperience 2 combines LiDAR scans of real scenes with egocentric recordings of human activity; our registration pipeline can place the corresponding scene geometry, video, camera trajectories, and hand and body motion in a common metric frame.
Registered recordings can support human-to-robot motion retargeting, while scanned scenes can be converted into interactive simulation environments; registration checks and per-scene quality control provide tools for auditing, filtering, and reusing the data.
With Ropedia Xperience 2, we aim to establish a gold standard for next-generation physical AI data collection and to lay the data foundation for scaling laws of physical AI over environments and behaviors. Ropedia Xperience 2 is collected with a two-stage protocol that requires a single operator and no robot or motion-capture infrastructure on site.
Each scene is first scanned once with a handheld 3D scanner, and its assigned tasks are then recorded with the HOMIE head-mounted recorder, so that one scan provides the spatial context for multiple recordings.
The released scan component contains 1,589 scenes.
The broader collection inventory spans 596 locations and 4,230 task recordings totaling 131 hours.
An automated pipeline produces scene meshes and 3D Gaussian representations, alongside camera trajectories, depth, hand and body motion, and hierarchical language captions.
Ropedia Xperience 2 extends Xperience-10M [ 47 ] , which provides about 10 million egocentric interaction clips totaling 10,000 hours, with metric scene scans and a pipeline for registering egocentric recordings to them. To assess the fidelity of the data, we use the scan reconstructions as reference geometry in three benchmarks: depth and geometry estimation, 3D object detection, and spatial reasoning.
These benchmarks evaluate existing models across diverse real-world scenes. Each capture is further reused to scale both environments and behaviors for robot learning (Figure 2 ).
Here, environments are real spaces with their layouts and object relations, and behaviors comprise the recorded human task executions as well as the robot motions and interactions derived from them.
We demonstrate four routes: • Real-to-sim conversion turns a single scan of a real-world environment into an interactive digital twin that preserves the original layout and appearance, incorporates realistic physical properties, and supports part-level object interaction, yielding reusable environments for policy and control learning. • Human-robot retargeting converts the recorded human behaviors, including long-horizon activities, into robot demonstrations from the estimated body and hand motion, without on-site robot deployment. • Virtual teleoperation lets operators collect robot demonstrations in the converted simulation scenes, without physical robots and with automatic scene resets. • Long-horizon trajectory generation rolls out robots on new multi-step tasks in the converted simulation scenes, chaining navigation and manipulation across subtasks from varied start states and object configurations; more trajectories require only additional compute. Real-to-sim conversion scales environments, while retargeting, virtual teleoperation, and trajectory generation scale robot behaviors.
Capture thus scales with operators rather than robots, and the captured data can be expanded into robot data without further on-site collection.

Rooms, tasks, viewpoints, and device views are illustrative. Our goal is to capture natural human activity together with the 3D structure of the environment in which it occurs, providing spatial context for understanding how people interact with their surroundings.
Scene collections such as ScanNet++ and DL3DV-10K emphasize multi-view observations of real environments [ 61 , 30 ] , while Ego4D and Nymeria capture egocentric activity and motion [ 17 , 32 ] .
Holi-World organizes human-egocentric experience into grounded learning resources [ 27 ] , and SpatialBench evaluates spatial foundation models across scene domains and input densities [ 42 ] .
UMI further demonstrates portable human-demonstration capture without requiring a robot at the collection site [ 10 ] . We collect Ropedia Xperience 2 in real, in-use environments, including homes, restaurants, stores, offices, and outdoor sites.
Collection is organized by scene: each scene (Level 1) is assigned a set of tasks (Level 2) before capture (Figure 3 a). Capturing environmental geometry and natural activity requires different sensing configurations.
Scene scanning benefits from freely chosen viewpoints to observe high, low, and partially occluded regions, while activity recording requires both hands to remain free and the cameras to follow the operator’s viewpoint.
We therefore use two complementary devices (Table 1 ).
The 3D scanner is a handheld LiDAR device equipped with two fisheye and two wide-angle cameras, allowing the operator to scan the scene from varied viewpoints.
HOMIE is a head-mounted egocentric recorder with four global-shutter fisheye cameras, two facing forward and two facing backward.
The forward pair captures the hands and manipulated objects, while the global shutters avoid rolling-shutter distortion during rapid head motion.
HOMIE also records inertial measurements and four-channel audio. For each scene, the operator first performs a 3D scan and then wears HOMIE to complete the assigned tasks one by one (Figure 3 ).
The protocol is designed to associate task recordings with a 3D scan of the same scene, linking activity to its physical environment; Section 3.3 describes how they can be spatially registered.

It covers scene preparation, 3D scanning, and HOMIE task recording; the latter two are shown in Figure 3 b and c. Scene preparation. The scene must remain static from the start of the 3D scan until task recording begins.
Before capture, we therefore minimize appearance changes and transient content: screens are turned off or set to a static image, lights are turned on and kept constant, bystanders and pets leave the scene, and windows and other transparent surfaces are covered where possible. 3D scanning. Scanning aims for complete coverage with redundant views.
The operator walks slowly through the scene, traversing each route in both directions, scans occluded and detailed regions at close range, and sweeps the device vertically to add high and low viewpoints. HOMIE task recording. Task recording aims to capture each activity completely and naturally.
Before each recording, the operator checks that the device is secure and that no lens is obstructed.
Recording begins before the task starts and ends after it is completed, so that each recording contains the entire activity. 3 Processing and Annotation For each scene, the 3D scan provides a static, metric 3D reconstruction (Section 3.1 ).
The HOMIE processing pipeline can turn task recordings into synchronized video, a device trajectory, and hand, body, and activity annotations (Section 3.2 ).
The pipeline can register a recording’s device trajectory and estimated hand and body motion to the reconstruction of the corresponding scene (Section 3.3 ).

These assets share one coordinate frame, which we call the scene frame ; because the geometry is measured by LiDAR, the scene frame is metric.
The posed images and the mesh serve as the reference for registering the task recordings of the scene (Section 3.3 ), and the 3DGS model renders the scene from arbitrary viewpoints, such as the recorded HOMIE camera poses (Figure 5 ). 3.2 HOMIE  Processing We use the same automated pipeline to process HOMIE recordings. Synchronization and calibration. The four cameras are hardware-synchronized, and all sensor samples are timestamped by the device clock.
We place the four videos on a common 30 fps frame index and link the IMU and audio samples to it by timestamp.
Each device is calibrated before capture, and every recording stores the intrinsics of the four fisheye cameras and their extrinsics relative to the IMU (Appendix B.1 ). Device trajectory and depth. The two forward-facing views are rectified into a pinhole stereo pair, on which SLAM, depth, and hand estimation operate.
SLAM estimates a 6-DoF device pose for every frame and a sparse landmark map in a gravity-aligned metric frame whose origin is the head position at the first frame.
A stereo module additionally estimates metric depth with per-pixel confidence. Hand pose. Hands are represented with MANO [ 46 ] and estimated with our in-house annotation pipeline.
The pipeline fits MANO to hand keypoints recovered from the calibrated stereo views, using cross-view reprojection consistency to refine the estimates.
The output is 21 joints per hand in the stereo-left camera frame.
When a hand is outside the view or occluded, no estimate is produced for that frame. Body motion estimation. We estimate the wearer’s full-body motion with EgoAllo [ 62 ] , using the SLAM-derived head trajectory as conditioning and our hand estimates as guidance.
The motion is represented with SMPL-H [ 46 ] , with body shape held constant within each recording.
The output comprises 52 body and finger joints at 30 Hz in the recording’s SLAM frame.
The estimated motion is constrained by the observed head and hands, while unobserved body parts, particularly the legs, are inferred using the learned motion prior. Hierarchical activity annotations. We use a large multimodal model to generate hierarchical activity annotations from the recordings.
The annotations describe the recorded activity at three temporal scales.
For the full recording, they summarize the main task and provide an overall activity description.
Within the recording, they identify sub-tasks and actions with associated time intervals.
For each sampled frame, they describe visible objects and their states, the state of each hand, and ongoing hand–object interactions.
All annotations are associated with the video timeline.
The temporally localized actions are distinct from the task-level action categories in Section 2.1 . Registration can then transform the device trajectory and estimated hand and body poses into the scene frame (Section 3.3 ), while the activity annotations remain associated with their corresponding timestamps. 3.3 Scan–HOMIE  Registration The scan reconstruction and each HOMIE recording are expressed in separate coordinate frames.
To place human activity in its spatial context, we register the recording’s device trajectory and estimated hand and body motion to the scene frame.
As illustrated in Figure 5 , this brings the egocentric observations, estimated motion, and scene geometry into a shared spatial reference.
Connecting egocentric activity to scene geometry is also central to EPIC Fields, which augments activity videos with reconstructed camera information [ 53 ] . We localize stereo-left frames in the scan using SIFT [ 31 ] matching, RANSAC [ 15 ] , and PnP against scan image keypoints lifted onto the mesh, then fit the recording-to-scene transform by robust Umeyama alignment [ 54 ] of the corresponding camera centers.
The transform allows a small scale correction when supported by the trajectory and validation; otherwise, it is rigid.
Held-out frames, IMU gravity consistency, and an ICP [ 4 ] check of stereo depth against the mesh are used to assess the alignment and assign PASS, WEAK, or FAIL labels (Appendix B.3 ).
A single transform cannot correct local SLAM drift within a recording. 4 Dataset Statistics and Quality 4.1 Composition and Scale Table 3 distinguishes the released scan component from the broader collection inventory.

MimicGen studies expanding demonstration datasets by adapting human demonstrations to new contexts [ 33 ] . Input: an object-level simulator scene from Real-to-Sim, a robot model, and a task given as a sequence of subtasks. Output: robot trajectories that chain navigation and manipulation through every subtask, from varied initial robot and object poses. Checks: per-subtask and full-task success from the simulator’s object states, task rules on the objects (nothing tipped or dropped, appliances and doors left as each subtask requires), trajectory duration, and generation yield and time per valid trajectory.
We generate trajectories for one eight-subtask task in a converted home environment: a wheeled dual-arm robot picks up a mug from the kitchen counter, opens the microwave, puts the mug inside, closes the door to heat it, takes it out, closes the microwave again, pushes the closed bedroom door open, and sets the mug on the bedside table (Figure 11 ).
Each subtask is a policy program that acts through a whole-body controller and observes objects only through perception, not through the simulator state; the eight run in one continuous rollout without resets.
From 30 held-out start states, each with its own robot and mug pose, all 30 rollouts complete all eight subtasks without breaking a task rule.
A trajectory lasts 3,869–4,255 control steps at 30 Hz (2.2 min on average) and takes 10–16 min of wall-clock time on one simulation worker, so further trajectories cost only compute. 6 Limitations The released scan component is dominated by home interiors and includes Reuse scans with partial coverage or other reconstruction defects.
Release grades incorporate policy overrides and resolution of conflicting QC labels; they should not be interpreted as a uniform visual or geometric validation of all retained scans.
Separate scans may depict the same physical space, and the collection-site inventory is not a verified site census of the released subset. The recording count and duration describe the collection inventory.
Hand and body motion are estimated rather than motion-capture ground truth, unobserved body parts are inferred, and activity captions are model-generated.
A single recording-to-scene transform cannot correct local SLAM drift. 7 Conclusion Ropedia Xperience 2 extends Xperience-10M’s focus on human behavior with metric scans of real environments and a pipeline for placing egocentric recordings and estimated motion in a shared scene frame.
Its two-stage collection protocol captures environments and behaviors without requiring robots on site.
The resulting scene representations support three benchmarks for evaluating existing models, while real-to-sim conversion, human-robot retargeting, virtual teleoperation, and long-horizon trajectory generation demonstrate how captured scenes and recordings can be reused to produce simulation environments and robot trajectories.
This reuse allows a real capture to support both the study of observed human activity and the generation of further robot experience.
We propose joint capture, spatial registration, and reuse of environments and behaviors as a gold standard for physical AI data collection.
These resources provide a data foundation for studying scaling laws that relate physical AI capability to the scale and diversity of environments and behaviors. References [1] M. Ahn, A. Brohan, N. Appendix B Processing and Annotation Details B.1 Synchronization, Calibration, and Coordinate Frames The four HOMIE cameras are hardware-synchronized to within 50 µ ​ s 50\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s} .
Video, audio, and IMU samples are timestamped in nanoseconds on the device clock, and every frame-indexed output of the pipeline shares one 30 fps frame index; the IMU (200 Hz) is linked to this index through keyframe indices.
Each device is calibrated before capture, with a reprojection error below 0.5 px on the 1920 × 1088 1920\times 1088 camera images, and every recording stores the calibration of the device that recorded it: the intrinsics of the four fisheye cameras, the transform from the device body (IMU) frame to the first camera, and the chain of transforms between consecutive cameras.
The two forward-facing fisheye cameras are rectified into a pinhole stereo pair of 1920 × 1088 1920\times 1088 pixels, of which a 1452 × 824 1452\times 824 crop without black borders is used; stereo depth is stored at 480 × 272 480\times 272 . Transforms use homogeneous column vectors, and 𝐓 A ​ B \mathbf{T}_{AB} maps a point from frame B B to frame A A .
The SLAM world frame of a recording is gravity-aligned with + z +z up, in meters, with its origin at the head position of the first frame; device poses map the body (IMU) frame to this world frame.
Hand joints are expressed in the rectified stereo-left camera frame (OpenCV convention), and body joints in the SLAM world frame.
After registration (Appendix B.3 ), the scene frame of the 3D scan replaces the SLAM world frame for all three. B.2 Pose and Language Annotation Hands. For each hand and frame, our in-house annotation pipeline provides 21 3D joints, the wrist pose, the finger-joint rotations, and the MANO shape coefficients, all in the rectified stereo-left camera frame.
Frames in which a hand is not estimated are marked as missing. Body. The body estimate comprises 52 SMPL-H joints covering the body and fingers, the local joint rotations, the root and head poses in the SLAM world frame, shape coefficients that are constant over the recording, and soft contact scores.
Whenever a hand is observed, the wrist and finger joints of the body agree with its MANO estimate. Language. We use a large multimodal model to generate hierarchical language annotations for each recording.
These annotations summarize the overall task, identify temporally localized sub-tasks and actions, and describe objects, hand states, and hand–object interactions. B.3 Scene–Activity Registration Inputs. Registration needs the scan images with their COLMAP camera model and the mesh, and from HOMIE the SLAM trajectory, the rectified stereo-left video with its intrinsics, and the calibration.
Scans exported only as 3DGS or point clouds cannot be registered.
The recording–scan pairs come from the collection metadata (Section 2.2 ), and the initialization below also verifies that both show the same place. Global initialization. We select well-textured stereo-left frames spread over the recording and match each against the scan images with SIFT features, scoring candidate images by the inliers of a fundamental matrix estimated with RANSAC.
Matched scan keypoints are lifted to 3D by intersecting their camera rays with the mesh, and PnP gives the pose of the HOMIE camera in the scene frame.
Initialization succeeds when several frames yield a consistent transform, and the initial rotation is the rotation average of their PnP poses. Localization and fitting. About 200 query frames, evenly spaced along the recording, are localized by PnP against scan images retrieved near the pose predicted by the current transform, and localization is repeated once with the fitted transform. 𝐒 \mathbf{S} is fit by robust Umeyama alignment between the SLAM camera centers and the PnP camera centers, preferring frames with many PnP inliers.
The fit uses even frames, and odd frames give the held-out error.
The scale of 𝐒 \mathbf{S} absorbs small discrepancies between the two metric reconstructions.
A similarity transform is chosen only when the trajectory spans at least 2.5 m and its held-out error is clearly lower than that of a rigid transform; otherwise 𝐒 \mathbf{S} is rigid.
As a drift indicator, the two halves of the recording are also fit separately and compared. Independent checks and labels. We compare the gravity direction from the HOMIE IMU with the floor normal of the scan mesh, measure the height of the SMPL-H toe joints above the floor, and compare the stereo depth with the scan: its scale relative to the SLAM landmarks, the fraction of depth points that lie on the mesh and their residual, and the displacement of an ICP refinement started from 𝐒 \mathbf{S} .
A registration is labeled FAIL if initialization finds no consistent transform, fewer than eight frames are localized, the held-out error has a 90th percentile above 30 cm, or ICP moves the registration by more than 30 cm; the last case indicates that the images agree but the geometry does not, as in an identical-looking neighboring unit.
Otherwise it is labeled PASS when the held-out error has a median of at most 5 cm and a 90th percentile of at most 12 cm, the gravity deviation is at most 2 ∘ 2^{\circ} , and ICP moves the registration by at most 10 cm and 2 ∘ 2^{\circ} , and WEAK when any of these criteria is missed.
The toe height is reported but does not affect the label. Limitations. Recordings performed in place, with a trajectory span below about 2 m, do not constrain scale and are registered rigidly.
Localization succeeds less often for frames that look down at the hands or a tabletop, because these views differ strongly from the scanner’s viewpoints.
A single transform cannot correct local SLAM drift, which affects long recordings; per-segment correction is left to future work. B.4 Scene QC and Release Decisions The QC checklist has five checks: (1) preparation, whether the scene was prepared as required by the protocol; (2) door state; (3) full room, whether the whole room is covered; (4) gaps and corners; and (5) occluded regions and views.
QC notes identify defects such as moving people, door motion, invalid traversal, double imaging, failed reconstruction, and partial coverage.
Release grades combine the recorded QC result with the release policy below; the checklist is not a claim that every retained scan passes every check. The approved release retains only completed uploads assigned Perfect, Usable, or Reuse, counting each work once.
Previous Delete decisions in Living room, Multi-room, Office, Meeting room, Store, Restaurant, Outdoor corridor, and Urban street are reassigned to Reuse.
Completed scans without an explicit QC result are retained as Usable only in Living room, Multi-room, Outdoor corridor, Urban street, and Parking lot.
This rule accounts for 7 scans in the QC registry and 84 additional scans documented in the release supplement.
For conflicting recorded grades, the release takes the best in the order Perfect, Usable, Reuse, Delete; 33 retained scans use this rule.
Delete decisions remaining after these rules are outside the release.
