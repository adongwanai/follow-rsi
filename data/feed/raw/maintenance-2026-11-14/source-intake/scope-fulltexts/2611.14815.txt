This requires plausible motion of surrounding vehicles as well as precise ego-trajectory control. Learning vehicle motion from perspective video alone is challenging because motion is entangled with appearance and vehicles can be occluded. Prior work therefore conditions video generation on layouts that specify vehicle motion, providing control over surrounding vehicles but requiring their future trajectories as inputs. We introduce BirdDreams, an autoregressive driving world model that jointly generates perspective video and a schematic bird’s-eye-view (BEV) video, making scene layout part of the predicted future. The BEV, rendered from scene annotations for training, depicts roads and vehicles as simple shapes at a fixed metric scale, providing a top-down view of their positions and motion with reduced occlusion between vehicles. Perspective video, by contrast, retains scene details that the BEV omits, such as traffic signals and object appearance. Joint attention between two video diffusion transformers, one per stream, lets each stream inform the other during generation. The predicted BEV also provides a geometric interface for ego control: we translate and rotate its latest frame along an input ego trajectory and use the resulting frames as a soft condition that shows how the scene would appear to move if only the ego vehicle moved. At inference, BirdDreams generates both videos from a single camera image without requiring an HD map or vehicle-state inputs. On PhysicalAI-AV and nuScenes, BirdDreams follows input ego trajectories more faithfully than prior driving world models and generates more plausible ego motion without ego-action conditioning. Schematic BEV targets supervise vehicle motion during training, while the predicted BEV provides a geometric interface for ego-trajectory control. 1 Introduction Simulation allows autonomous driving systems to be tested in rare, dangerous scenarios that are difficult to reproduce safely on real roads. Driving world models learn from real driving videos to synthesize realistic future observations conditioned on ego actions [ 11 , 34 ] . From the same initial observation, they can simulate how a scene unfolds under different ego maneuvers. Such simulation requires the model to predict plausible responses of surrounding vehicles to the ego maneuver while generating ego motion that follows the input trajectory. At an intersection, for example, an ego vehicle proceeding instead of yielding may cause a crossing vehicle to slow down or change course. Recent driving world models such as OmniDreams [ 4 ] condition video generation on external scene layouts that specify road geometry and surrounding-vehicle motion. Replaying these layouts from driving logs fixes surrounding vehicles to their recorded trajectories, so they cannot react to changes in the ego maneuver. A separate traffic model can generate new trajectories, but leaves interaction prediction outside the video world model [ 49 ] . Without external scene-layout inputs, the world model must learn surrounding-vehicle motion from perspective video. This is difficult because motion is entangled with texture and lighting, occlusion often hides vehicles, and the same pixel displacement can correspond to different speeds depending on a vehicle’s distance from the camera. In our OmniDreams baseline trained without surrounding-vehicle trajectories as inputs, vehicles can disappear or make implausible maneuvers at complex intersections ( Fig. 6 ). We introduce BirdDreams, an autoregressive driving world model that jointly generates video from the ego vehicle’s perspective and schematic bird’s-eye-view (BEV) video of the same scene. Scene layout thus becomes part of the generated future rather than an external input ( Fig. 1 ). The schematic BEV depicts road elements and vehicles as simple shapes with semantic colors. This simplified prediction task is intended to help the model focus on learning scene dynamics rather than reproducing detailed appearance. In addition, the BEV’s fixed metric scale makes a given pixel displacement correspond to the same distance anywhere in the view, unlike in perspective video. Its top-down view also reduces occlusion between vehicles. At a complex intersection, for example, crossing vehicles that overlap in the perspective view can appear separately in the BEV, making their paths relative to the road easier to follow. Photorealistic perspective video, by contrast, contains visual information that the schematic representation omits, such as road-surface details and distant objects beyond the BEV’s range. Joint prediction combines the BEV’s representation of vehicle motion with this visual information from perspective video. To let each stream shape the other during generation, BirdDreams couples two video diffusion-transformer branches, one for perspective video and one for schematic BEV video, through joint attention throughout denoising. The perspective branch can thus use vehicle motion from the BEV, and the BEV branch can use visual features from the perspective video. We train both branches to predict synchronized perspective and BEV videos, with the BEV rendered from scene annotations. At inference, the model generates both videos from a single image, without requiring an HD map or vehicle-state inputs. Accurately following an ego trajectory specified in meters also remains challenging for video world models. Even with trajectory conditioning, subtle steering maneuvers may not be faithfully reproduced in the generated ego motion. We use the predicted BEV as an action interface for video generation. In an ego-centered, orthographic BEV at a fixed metric scale, a change in ego position shifts the scene by a uniform number of pixels, and a change in heading rotates it about the ego vehicle. Our BEV-aware action conditioning uses these transformations to reproject the latest predicted BEV frame into the ego coordinate frame at each pose along the input trajectory. The resulting frames show how the scene would appear to move if the surroundings stayed fixed. They serve as a soft condition for the BEV branch, and the perspective branch attends to the conditioned BEV features through joint attention. The model predicts how surrounding vehicles move and interact in both videos. Without ego-action conditioning, ego motion is also predicted as part of the rollout. We evaluate BirdDreams on PhysicalAI-AV [ 27 ] and nuScenes [ 6 ] . Compared with open driving world models and OmniDreams-based baselines that generate perspective video alone, it precisely follows input ego trajectories specified in meters and, without them, generates more plausible ego motion. When the ego vehicle departs from the logged trajectory, surrounding vehicles in its generated videos can respond to the new maneuver, as when a following vehicle slows down after the ego vehicle cuts into its lane. Human evaluation further demonstrates that BirdDreams generates more realistic surrounding-vehicle motion and behavior than an OmniDreams baseline under the same ego maneuvers. 2 Related Work Video world models. GenAD’s action-conditioned extension [ 48 ] and Vista [ 11 ] instead generate future video under ego actions without future surrounding-vehicle layouts.

Concurrent work CausalDrive [ 47 ] focuses on how surrounding vehicles respond to counterfactual ego actions in generated video.

In driving, BEVWorld [ 52 ] forecasts learned scene-level BEV latents and decodes sensor observations, while GenieDrive [ 50 ] predicts future occupancy before generating video. Joint appearance–structure prediction takes several forms: GEM [ 16 ] jointly denoises RGB and depth, UniFuture [ 22 ] exchanges features between video denoising and depth prediction, and OmniNWM [ 21 ] jointly generates panoramic RGB, semantics, and depth, then lifts these outputs into 3D occupancy.
MMPhysVideo [ 24 ] jointly generates RGB and camera-aligned perceptual cues before distilling an RGB-only model.
We share this motivation for joint structural supervision, but predict an orthographic schematic BEV video rather than camera-aligned perceptual cues.

The BEV makes road-relative vehicle motion explicit at a fixed metric scale without perspective foreshortening, while perspective video retains scene details omitted by the schematic representation.
Simple shapes remove texture and lighting variation; explicit headings and metric spacing expose relative motion and lane relationships, while the top-down view reduces occlusion.
This simpler target is intended to ease learning vehicle dynamics, while joint attention incorporates RGB cues such as traffic-light states.

We supervise these dynamics through two complementary videos of the same scene. Photorealistic perspective video preserves the scene’s appearance and visual cues such as traffic-light states. An ego-centered, orthographic schematic BEV video makes road geometry, vehicle positions, and headings explicit at a fixed metric scale. By removing texture and lighting variation from the prediction target, the schematic BEV is intended to focus learning on road-relative vehicle motion. We jointly predict both videos so that the perspective branch can use the BEV’s explicit representation of scene dynamics, with the aim of generating more plausible vehicle behavior. We render the BEV training videos from the map and 3D object annotations provided with the driving datasets ( § 3.3 ). We build on the pretrained causal video diffusion transformer of vanilla OmniDreams [ 4 ] . The BEV branch, in turn, can use scene details from perspective video that are absent from its schematic representation. At every transformer block, joint attention lets each branch’s queries attend to keys and values from both streams. The branches retain separate attention projections, feed-forward layers, and output heads to specialize in their respective representations. The perspective branch inherits the pretrained OmniDreams backbone, and the BEV branch is initialized with a copy of its weights. We use the pretrained video VAE from Cosmos-Predict2.5 [ 1 ] , keeping its weights frozen. It encodes each stream into latents, which are partitioned into chunks of three latent frames. Attention is bidirectional within each chunk and causal across chunks, allowing the current perspective and BEV predictions to exchange information while drawing on earlier scene history. 3.2.2 Diffusion Forcing with Per-Stream Noise Sampling Following Diffusion Forcing (DF) [ 7 ] , we train on chunks with independently sampled noise levels. We also sample the perspective and BEV noise levels independently within each chunk. Training includes pairs in which either stream is relatively clean while the other is noisy. Relatively clean BEV latents can provide road geometry and vehicle positions to the perspective branch, while relatively clean perspective latents can provide visual features to the BEV branch. When both streams are noisy, the two branches exchange information through joint attention to denoise the perspective and BEV latents together. The BEV’s fixed metric scale and orthographic projection let us translate and rotate a previously predicted frame according to the input ego trajectory. This reprojection accounts for ego-induced apparent motion while treating the reference scene as stationary. We use the reprojected BEV as a soft condition and train the model to predict the actual future scene, including surrounding-vehicle motion and newly visible content. We keep the latents of provided observations fixed and denoise the remaining latents from Gaussian noise, using the same trained model across input configurations. When a full video is provided in one stream, we generate the synchronized video in the other. An RGB video can therefore condition BEV video generation, while a BEV video can specify road geometry and vehicle motion for perspective video generation. Conditioning can also be restricted to the first frame of either stream. Given an initial RGB frame I 0 I_{0} , we generate I 1 : T I_{1:T} and B 0 : T B_{0:T} , including the initial BEV frame. Conversely, given an initial BEV frame B 0 B_{0} , we generate I 0 : T I_{0:T} and B 1 : T B_{1:T} , including the initial perspective frame. An initial BEV layout can thus condition both the scene’s appearance and its subsequent evolution. Each training example pairs a recorded front-camera RGB video with a schematic BEV video rendered from annotations of the same scene. We construct these pairs using a subset of RDS-HQ-1M [ 4 ] comprising approximately 670 hours of driving and the official nuScenes training split [ 6 ] . The nuScenes training set contains 700 scenes totaling approximately 3.9 hours of paired video. The RDS-HQ-1M subset provides road-layout annotations, tracked object boxes, ego poses, and camera calibration. We construct its road geometry from lanes, road boundaries, crosswalks, and wait lines. For nuScenes, we use its HD vector maps and annotated 3D box tracks. Track interpolation and filtering. For each RGB frame, we render a BEV frame using object positions and orientations at the time the RGB frame was captured. Box annotations may be less frequent than RGB frames. We therefore use the provided track IDs to identify annotations of the same object and interpolate its position and orientation between the earlier and later annotations. We also use these object tracks to interpolate vehicle positions and orientations during temporary occlusions and render the interpolated boxes in the BEV video. Before rendering, we remove duplicate or excessively overlapping vehicle boxes. Schematic BEV rendering. We render the road geometry and object boxes in an orthographic overhead view, with the ego vehicle at the center and its forward direction pointing upward. Road elements are drawn as polylines and polygons, and objects as oriented cuboids. Each BEV frame covers a square of approximately 69.3 ​ m 69.3\,\mathrm{m} per side in both datasets, so the same pixel displacement represents the same physical distance across scenes. This pixel-to-meter mapping is also used for ego-action reprojection in Eq. ( 4 ). Colors distinguish road elements and object categories: road boundaries are magenta, crosswalks purple, and pedestrians yellow. Vehicle cuboids are shaded red at the front and blue at the rear, making their headings visible. This representation preserves object positions and headings relative to the road without image texture or perspective foreshortening. The BEV video provides an explicit training target for vehicle motion relative to the road, complementing the appearance and motion supervision in the paired RGB video. Additional experiments examine how independent noise sampling affects generation quality, explore ego-trajectory prediction through post-training, and demonstrate scenario editing in BEV. 4.1 Experimental Setup Implementation details. BirdDreams is trained from a checkpoint of an OmniDreams variant without external scene-layout conditioning (HD maps and surrounding-vehicle states), on a subset of RDS-HQ-1M [ 4 ] comprising approximately 670 hours of driving. Perspective frames ( 848 × 480 848\times 480 ) and schematic BEV frames ( 480 × 480 480\times 480 ) are encoded by a causal video VAE and modeled by a ∼ 4 {\sim}4 B dual-branch Mixture-of-Transformers diffusion transformer with block-causal attention.
Training proceeds in two stages: the model is first trained with Diffusion Forcing [ 7 ] , and then further trained with Resampling Forcing [ 14 ] to reduce error accumulation over autoregressive rollouts. We train on 64 H100 GPUs with a total batch size of 64, using AdamW with a learning rate of 3 × 10 − 5 3\times 10^{-5} , weight decay of 10 − 3 10^{-3} , and a linear learning-rate schedule with 1,000 warm-up steps. Benchmarks. We evaluate on two benchmarks. PhysicalAI-AV is a subset we construct from PhysicalAI-Autonomous-Vehicles [ 27 ] , a public driving video dataset released by NVIDIA, by randomly sampling 200 clips from its evaluation split. nuScenes [ 6 ] is a public driving video dataset that enables comparison in a widely used setting; for this comparison, we further post-train our model on the nuScenes train split and evaluate on all 150 scenes in its official validation split. Baselines. We compare against open driving world models: Vista [ 11 ] , UniFuture [ 22 ] , GEM [ 16 ] , and DrivingDojo [ 42 ] , evaluating each with and without ego-action conditioning. Without ego-action conditioning, we additionally compare against OmniDreams (Vanilla), a variant of the OmniDreams series without external scene-layout conditioning (HD maps and surrounding-vehicle states), post-trained on the same dataset as BirdDreams. With ego-action conditioning, Vista, UniFuture, and GEM receive ego waypoints, while DrivingDojo receives per-frame ego displacements. The OmniDreams baseline for this setting is OmniDreams (Plücker), which conditions on ego motion through Plücker ray embeddings. Evaluation settings and metrics. Each benchmark is evaluated in two settings: without ego-action conditioning , where the model rolls out from the conditioning frame(s) alone, and with ego-action conditioning , where the ground-truth ego trajectory is additionally provided as the action condition. Our metrics largely follow the DrivingGen protocol [ 53 ] and cover two axes. For perspective video quality, we report the Fréchet Video Distance (FVD) [ 38 ] , the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) [ 18 ] , and Image Quality (reference-free perceptual quality via CLIP-IQA+ [ 40 ] ). For ego-trajectory quality, we report the Fréchet Trajectory Distance (FTD), computed between ego-trajectory embeddings from a pretrained motion-forecasting encoder [ 35 ] . To compare ego motion with models that generate only perspective video, we reconstruct ego trajectories from generated and corresponding real perspective videos using Depth Anything 3 [ 23 ] . We measure agreement with the trajectories from real videos using average displacement error (ADE) and dynamic time warping (DTW). ADE averages the Euclidean distance between their 2D positions at corresponding timestamps. DTW computes the minimum sum of pairwise Euclidean distances over monotonic temporal alignments. We find this alignment by dynamic programming, allowing one-to-many position matches to accommodate differences in traversal speed [ 53 ] . With ego-action conditioning, we evaluate one generated sample per scene. Without ego-action conditioning, where many futures are plausible, we generate three samples per scene with different random seeds and take the minimum ADE and DTW independently for each scene, denoted minADE and minDTW. 4.2 Video Generation and Ego-Trajectory Control We compare BirdDreams with existing open driving world models on PhysicalAI-AV and nuScenes, without ego-action conditioning ( Tab. 1 ) and with ego-action conditioning ( Tab. 2 ). Without ego-action conditioning, every model receives only the conditioning frames, so the metrics jointly assess the visual fidelity of the generated perspective video and the plausibility of the generated ego motion. With ego-action conditioning, the ground-truth ego trajectory is additionally provided, and ADE and DTW quantify how faithfully each model executes it. Results. Without ego-action conditioning, BirdDreams achieves the lowest minADE, minDTW, FTD, FVD, and FID among the evaluated methods on both benchmarks ( Tab. 1 ). It reduces minADE relative to OmniDreams (Vanilla) by 21% on PhysicalAI-AV and 37% on nuScenes, indicating closer agreement with logged ego behavior among the sampled futures. For frame-level Image Quality, BirdDreams ranks second to OmniDreams (Vanilla) on PhysicalAI-AV and DrivingDojo on nuScenes. With ego-action conditioning, BirdDreams follows the input ego trajectory more accurately in the generated perspective videos ( Tab. 2 ), reducing ADE by 35% on PhysicalAI-AV and 36% on nuScenes relative to OmniDreams (Plücker). Trajectory improvements in both settings are accompanied by lower FVD and FID. For per-frame Image Quality, BirdDreams ranks first on PhysicalAI-AV and second on nuScenes. These benchmarks use logged ego trajectories as action conditions; we next examine surrounding-vehicle behavior when the ego departs from those trajectories. 4.3 Reactive Behavior of Surrounding Vehicles Counterfactual scenarios. A world model useful for closed-loop simulation must generate videos in which surrounding vehicles react to the ego vehicle, rather than replaying logged motion. We probe this with counterfactual rollouts: starting from the same conditioning frames, we provide ego actions that differ from the ground-truth log and observe how the vehicles visible in the first frame behave. Figure 5 shows selected vehicle interactions with and without ego-action conditioning. Without ego-action conditioning, the ego and a crossing vehicle stop. With ego-action conditioning, a crossing vehicle stops as the ego proceeds, and a following vehicle slows after the ego cuts into its lane. In extreme scenarios that leave no room to react, the rollout ends in a collision between the ego and another vehicle, which can be a plausible outcome under such maneuvers. Comparison with baselines. To compare surrounding-vehicle behavior with and without vehicle-motion conditioning, we consider two OmniDreams-based baselines. The original OmniDreams conditions on surrounding-vehicle motion replayed from driving logs, so the depicted vehicles follow recorded motion under counterfactual ego actions. To focus the evaluation on the behavior of non-ego agents, we compare both models under the same static road geometry. We train OmniDreams (HDMap) with surrounding vehicles removed from its perspective layout conditions, retaining static road geometry. For BirdDreams (HDMap), we add the same road geometry as a BEV condition, since the standard model may generate roads that differ from the map in areas not visible in the initial image. Conditioned on the surrounding road, terrain, and ego actions, OmniDreams (HDMap) must predict surrounding-vehicle behavior through perspective video alone. As shown in Fig. 6 , its rollouts exhibit failures including spatially inconsistent motion and unnatural driving. The red car appears to switch sides relative to oncoming traffic, while vehicles in the intersection example proceed along conflicting paths without a coherent interaction. In these examples, BirdDreams generates more plausible vehicle motion under the same conditions. Figure 7: Diverse futures from the same initial observation and ego action. Since the realism of reactive behavior is difficult to capture with automatic metrics, we conduct a pairwise human evaluation. We generate paired rollouts of BirdDreams (HDMap) and OmniDreams (HDMap) from the same conditioning frames, counterfactual ego actions, and static road geometry, and show each of 25 participants 15 pairs randomly sampled from a pool of 119 pairs in randomized order, yielding N = 375 N=375 pairwise judgments. For each pair, participants answer in which video the surrounding vehicles move and behave more realistically (Video A, Video B, or about the same), with the instruction that a dangerous situation or a crash is a realistic outcome when the ego maneuver leaves no time to avoid it. When a participant prefers one video, they additionally flag everything wrong with the other one, choosing from impossible motion (teleporting, sliding, passing through objects, or sudden appearance/disappearance), unnatural driving (driving against traffic or wrong spacing), no appropriate reaction to the ego or other vehicles (e.g., no braking or yielding when another car cuts in), and other. Surrounding vehicles can respond differently to the same ego maneuver, so a world model should be able to simulate multiple possible futures from a given initial scene. We examine this capability by varying the random seed in BirdDreams (HDMap) while fixing the initial RGB observation, counterfactual ego trajectory, and static road geometry. The sampled rollouts in Fig. 7 exhibit distinct sequences of vehicle interactions, illustrating how BirdDreams can simulate different ways the same scene may unfold under a fixed ego action. 4.4 Model Analysis and Extensions 4.4.1 Per-Stream Noise Sampling Independent noise sampling exposes the model to cases in which one stream is cleaner than the other, allowing each branch to learn denoising with different levels of information from the complementary stream ( § 3.2.2 ). We compare it with a DF variant that shares one noise level across the perspective and BEV streams within each chunk. Both variants sample noise levels independently across temporal chunks. Figure 10 reports FVD and FTD for the DF variant on RDS-HQ-1M validation clips. Independent sampling yields lower FVD and FTD than shared sampling. 4.4.2 World-Action Model Post-Training We conduct a preliminary experiment to explore extending BirdDreams to a world-action model (WAM) for ego-trajectory prediction. We add an ego-action prediction head to its perspective backbone and apply the same post-training procedure to OmniDreams (Vanilla) to examine whether joint perspective–BEV video modeling benefits ego-trajectory prediction. Both models are post-trained for 22,000 iterations on our RDS-HQ-1M subset, fine-tuning the perspective branch and action head. BirdDreams retains BEV supervision with its BEV branch frozen. We evaluate on 100 held-out clips from this subset. Both models use perspective video as their only visual observation, together with 1.6 seconds of ego-motion history. We therefore feed pure Gaussian noise ( σ = 1 \sigma=1 ) to the BEV branch. The branch participates in joint attention, and its input is not iteratively denoised during action prediction. We report best-of-six minADE over a three-second horizon, averaged across clips. As a separate diagnostic, we additionally condition the model on ground-truth BEV across all input video frames at a low noise level (GT BEV). This diagnostic uses privileged observations beyond the perspective video. As shown in Fig. 10 , BirdDreams achieves 0.295 m minADE, compared with 0.318 m for OmniDreams (Vanilla), a numerical improvement of 7.3%. The GT BEV diagnostic reduces the error to 0.274 m. The schematic top view makes it easy to customize driving scenarios by adding vehicles and adjusting their positions and headings. BirdDreams then jointly generates perspective and BEV videos from this initial layout, synthesizing scene appearance and subsequent vehicle motion without requiring a corresponding perspective image. 4.4.3 Scenario Editing in BEV Flexible conditioning ( § 3.2.5 ) lets users customize driving scenarios by editing an initial BEV frame. Starting from an existing driving log, users can add vehicles or adjust their positions and headings in our editor, as illustrated in Fig. 11 . These edits are made directly in the projected top view, avoiding manipulation of 3D bounding boxes. Given the edited BEV, BirdDreams jointly generates perspective and BEV videos, including the first perspective frame, so users need not provide a perspective image of the modified scene. In the example shown in Fig. 11 , the edited BEV places a vehicle ahead of the ego vehicle at an intersection. From this initial layout, BirdDreams generates a rollout in which the vehicle turns right onto the intersecting road and the ego vehicle follows. 5 Conclusion We have presented BirdDreams, an autoregressive driving world model that jointly generates perspective and schematic BEV videos. Our model learns road-relative vehicle motion from BEV supervision and uses the predicted BEV to condition generation on an ego trajectory. On PhysicalAI-AV and nuScenes, BirdDreams achieves lower ego-trajectory errors than prior methods with and without ego-action conditioning. Qualitative counterfactual rollouts show surrounding vehicles reacting to changed ego maneuvers, such as a following vehicle slowing after an ego cut-in. In a human evaluation where both models received the same road geometry, participants also preferred its surrounding-vehicle behavior to that of a perspective-only baseline. These results suggest that predicting scene geometry alongside appearance can improve ego-trajectory control and the realism of surrounding-vehicle responses in driving world models.
