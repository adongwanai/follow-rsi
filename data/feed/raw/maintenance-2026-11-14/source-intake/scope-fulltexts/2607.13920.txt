][List of Schemas] DeepStress : Stress-Testing Deep Search Agents Ismaël Rousseau Affiliation: Orange Research, Lannion, France Affiliation: Aix Marseille Univ, CNRS, LIS, Marseille, France Email: first.last@orange.com Géraldine Damnati Affiliation: Orange Research, Lannion, France Email: first.last@lis-lab.fr Frédéric Béchet Affiliation: Aix Marseille Univ, CNRS, LIS, Marseille, France Abstract While search agents demonstrate impressive capabilities in multi-step question answering, their robustness to poor-quality evidence remains under-explored. Such evidence is rare in benchmark corpora, and resulting failures are hard to reproduce. We propose DeepStress , a dynamic stress-testing framework that subjects agents to degraded evidence at controlled rates. It replaces the retrieval module with a synthetic environment where documents are generated dynamically for each query, under independent control of their trustworthiness, relevance, and factuality. To characterize behavior under stress, we go beyond binary accuracy with a five-way classification of final-answer outcomes and two new reliability-aware metrics, RAS and CoPRAS, which credit cautious behavior and account for computational cost. Evaluating 12 systems on questions from HotpotQA and BrowseComp-Plus , we find that several open-weight agents answer confidently when sources contain obvious credibility red flags, and that many HotpotQA questions are answered from parametric knowledge even when all returned documents are factually wrong. 1 Introduction In Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), a model first retrieves passages relevant to the user query, then conditions its answer on the retrieved evidence. Search agents extend this paradigm by making retrieval iterative: they interleave reasoning steps with calls to external search tools, gathering evidence before producing a final answer. Their performance depends not only on the language model used for synthesis, but also on how evidence is retrieved, how reliable it is, and how the agent adapts its search and reasoning strategy to the evidence it receives. However, standard search-agent evaluations often reduce performance to final-answer accuracy, hiding qualitatively different failure modes such as poor retrieval, misleading evidence, or ineffective search behavior. Recent work, such as BrowseComp-Plus Chen et al. (2025) , decouples retriever and model performance by utilizing a curated corpus where questions are paired with supportive and hard-negative documents. However, robustness failures are typically observed post-hoc , and the low-signal conditions are difficult to reproduce. Our study focuses on how search agents behave when the quality of retrieved evidence is explicitly controlled: we analyze fine-grained final-answer outcomes and complement them with exploratory analyses of search and reasoning behavior.
Inspired by stress-testing methodologies, we introduce DeepStress , a simulation environment designed to evaluate the robustness of deep search agents. Much like simulated environments used for autonomous vehicles, where extreme
conditions are generated to test resilience, DeepStress places search agents in challenging scenarios where search results can be severely degraded. It intercepts the agent’s search calls and replaces the retrieval module with a synthetic environment, allowing us to precisely manipulate the properties of the returned documents
along three
axes: trustworthiness , relevance , and factuality . DeepStress is not a replacement for existing benchmarks but acts as a controlled laboratory for stress testing of search-agent behavior with the following contributions: 1. We introduce DeepStress , a controlled simulation stress test environment that evaluates search agents dynamically on
synthetic documents modified along three axes: trustworthiness , relevance , and factuality . 2. We evaluate several agents across multiple experimental conditions and document substantial behavior differences when facing degraded documents and propose a novel score called RAS ( Reliability-Aware Score ) that we evaluate against computational costs. 2 Related Work Search agents. Early work such as ReAct Yao et al. (2022) and IRCoT Trivedi et al. (2023) showed that off-the-shelf LLMs can be prompted to interleave reasoning with external actions or retrieval, enabling multi-step information seeking without task-specific training. More recent work trains search behavior directly. Search-R1 Jin et al. (2025) , for instance, uses reinforcement learning (RL) on multi-hop question-answering (QA) tasks, with rewards derived from final-answer correctness, to encourage models to issue useful search queries during reasoning. ZeroSearch Sun et al. (2025) further removes the dependence on a live search engine during training by using a fine-tuned language model to generate documents that mimic search-engine outputs.
A parallel line of work targets deep-research agents , which produce longer, citation-rich answers over multiple rounds of retrieval and synthesis. DR-Tulu Shao et al. (2026) , for example, uses evolving rubrics as RL rewards to improve long-form research answers.
These works focus on improving search-agent capabilities under a given retrieval process. In contrast, our goal is to evaluate how search agents behave under a controlled retrieval environment. Search benchmarks. Search agents are commonly evaluated on their ability to gather relevant evidence through iterative reasoning and search. Multi-hop QA datasets require models to locate and combine evidence about multiple entities before answering. HotpotQA Yang et al. (2018) introduced a diverse set of questions over Wikipedia entries, together with supporting sentence-level facts required for reasoning over the question. More recent benchmarks such as BrowseComp Wei et al. (2025) introduce harder questions answerable through web browsing, where the answer is short and easily verifiable but difficult to find, requiring persistent search over entangled constraints. BrowseComp-Plus Chen et al. (2025) further improves experimental control by using a fixed corpus with human-verified supporting documents and mined challenging negatives, allowing us to disentangle the retriever effect from that of the LLM.
Our work follows this line of controlled evaluation, but isolates a different factor: how agents react to changes in the quality of retrieved evidence. Adversarial environments. Recent work evaluates agents by perturbing the external observations they receive.
AgentNoiseBench Wang et al. (2026) directly replaces or corrupts tool
outputs, while SafeSearch Dong et al. (2026) and
SearchGEO Chen et al. (2026) expose search agents to controlled unreliable or
manipulated search results. WARP Zhang et al. (2026) poisons existing user-generated web pages, exploiting their repeated retrieval by
deep-research agents to steer their reports. Unlike these attack-oriented evaluations, DeepStress uses controlled semantic perturbations to study how search agents respond to changes in evidence trustworthiness, relevance, and factuality. Beyond final-answer accuracy. Search agents can fail in many different ways:
an agent may give a wrong answer, express uncertainty, fail to produce a final answer, or exhaust its tool-call budget. Recent work on agent evaluation has argued for more detailed failure analysis, including taxonomies of reasoning, planning, tool-use, and system-execution errors Deshpande et al. (2025) .
Beyond final outcomes, the trajectory itself is also informative, as highlighted by TRACE Kim et al. (2026) .
Prior work has studied search trajectories through query reformulation and evidence reuse Ning et al. (2026) , and has shown that additional search calls can be helpful, wasteful, or harmful depending on retrieval conditions Shi et al. (2025) ; Xie et al. (2026) . Our work combines these two views: we report fine-grained final-answer outcomes and analyze whether agents detect and verbalize degradations in retrieved documents. Robustness to knowledge conflict. By manipulating the quality of retrieved documents, our setting creates controlled cases where agents must reason from evidence that may be unreliable or conflicting, connecting our work to prior studies on knowledge conflict. DRAGged into Conflicts Cattan et al. (2025) introduces a taxonomy of conflict situations in RAG, together with the expected model behavior for each category.
TRACK Feng et al. (2026) studies how LLMs propagate newly provided facts through multi-step reasoning when these facts conflict with the model’s initial parametric knowledge. We differ from these studies by evaluating active search agents rather than static RAG or in-context reasoning pipelines. Instead of only asking whether a model can resolve a conflict once the evidence is given, we study how agents behave while searching under controlled degradations of the retrieved documents. 3 Methodology 3.1 Simulation environment The core of the DeepStress methodology is to replace the standard search tool with a simulated environment. Documents are dynamically generated for each agent query rather than precomputed, since different agents, or the same agent under different conditions, may follow different search trajectories for the same question. This ensures that each simulated search result is tailored to the agent’s actual query while preserving the controlled reliability attributes imposed by the scenario. Returning one controlled document per call differs from traditional retrieval settings, but is appropriate for our laboratory experiment, as it provides finer control over the evidence shown to the agent and isolates agent behavior from retrieval variability. Let { D 1 , D 2 , … , D n } \{D_{1},D_{2},\ldots,D_{n}\} be the set of dimensions under study. A scenario γ \gamma specifies a probability for each dimension, chosen by the experimenter: 𝝅 γ = ( π γ ​ ( D 1 ) , … , π γ ​ ( D n ) ) \boldsymbol{\pi}_{\gamma}=\bigl(\pi_{\gamma}(D_{1}),\ldots,\pi_{\gamma}(D_{n})\bigr) (1) For each simulated search call, we sample one binary label per dimension from the scenario probabilities. These labels define the target properties of the returned document. In the experiments reported in this paper, they are translated into dimension-specific prompt instructions, although the framework itself is not tied to prompt-based generation. For example, if D k D_{k} corresponds to factuality and π γ ​ ( D k ) = 80 % \pi_{\gamma}(D_{k})=80\% , then the document is sampled as factually correct with probability 80% and factually incorrect with probability 20%.
In our implementation 1 1 1 The code for this flexible environment is publicly available and the repository link will be provided after peer-review. , this label determines whether the dynamic prompt includes supporting evidence or factual errors, both for extreme values π γ ​ ( D k ) = 0 % \pi_{\gamma}(D_{k})=0\% or 100 % 100\% or more balanced situations. 3.2 Simulating document reliability In this paper, we focus on a controlled experimental setup where we manipulate three source attributes: Trustworthiness (T): whether the source appears credible based on its style, structure and URL, regardless of its factuality; Relevance (R): whether the source meaningfully addresses the model-generated query rather than covering unrelated content; Factuality (F): whether the claims contained in the source are factually correct. We generate documents with an LLM, using the sampled values of T T , R R , and F F to control the output. For each search call, the simulator generates one synthetic document with a title and URL; its length is chosen to approximate a Wikipedia paragraph. The agent’s search query is passed to the LLM as the query the document should address, while the sampled labels are converted into dimension-specific prompt instructions. Full prompt construction, generation details as well as document examples are provided in Appendix D.1 . Factually correct documents ( F = 1 F=1 ), are grounded in dataset-provided evidence: gold Wikipedia paragraphs for HotpotQA and human-verified evidence documents for BrowseComp-Plus . This reduces accidental errors on details such as dates or locations. Factually incorrect documents ( F = 0 F=0 ) are generated with instructions to introduce severe factual errors. Trustworthy documents (T=1), are generated with a plausible reliable URL and page title. For low-trust results (T=0), we sample a domain from the OpenSources list 2 2 2 https://github.com/OpenSourcesGroup/opensources of misleading and outright fake websites, generate a plausible page title for that domain, and include specific instructions in the content generation prompt. Irrelevant documents (R=0), are generated by replacing the generation target with an unrelated question sampled from the dataset and use its associated supporting context. To mimic a realistic partial web search, the query passed to the generator is a document title sampled from that unrelated support context.
This produces a coherent and grounded document that is useful for another information need, but irrelevant to the original question asked to the agent. 3.3 Sanity check Because our framework relies on synthetic documents generated under controlled conditions, we conduct a human evaluation to verify that the intended labels are reflected in the resulting documents.
We manually annotate a random sample of 50 documents with three annotators 3 3 3 Examples are provided in Appendix A.3 and details of the protocol and full results in Appendix A.1 . Agreement with the intended labels is high for trustworthiness (96.4%), with strong inter-annotator agreement. Agreement remains strong but is lower for factuality (85.5%), suggesting that this attribute is more difficult to assess consistently for human annotators: a qualitative feedback further suggests that the generated factual errors can be easy to recognize with prior knowledge of the topic in some cases, while remaining plausible to non-specialists for other queries.
We did not ask annotators to check for relevance as this dimension is accurate by construction by selecting irrelevant documents from human-verified evidence from the datasets.
Although limited in size, this manual sanity check validates our generation methods, showing that documents generally reflect their intended attributes. 3.4 Metrics In order to verify how agents react under degraded conditions, we use several metrics: Final-answer outcomes. Let 𝒟 \mathcal{D} be a set of evaluated runs. Each run i ∈ 𝒟 i\in\mathcal{D} is associated with a final answer a i a_{i} and a document-quality condition γ i \gamma_{i} .
We analyze final answers through five outcomes, going beyond a binary correct/incorrect evaluation: • Correct ( C i ) (C_{i}) : the final answer is factually equivalent to the ground truth. • Incorrect ( I i ) (I_{i}) : the model gives a confident but factually wrong answer. • Abstention ( A i ) (A_{i}) : the model states that it doesn’t know or have enough information. • Exceed tool budget ( B i ) (B_{i}) : the agent reaches its tool-call limit before answering. • Missing answer tag ( M i ) (M_{i}) : the run terminates without a closing answer tag ( e.g. formatting error or premature <eos> token) Runs that exceed the tool budget or miss the answer tag are assigned directly to their corresponding category. For runs that contain a final answer, we use GPT-4.1 as an LLM-as-a-judge to classify the answer as C i C_{i} , I i I_{i} or A i A_{i} . In our setting, every question is answerable in principle, but the simulator can make the available evidence unreliable or uninformative. As with any aggregate metric, RAS and CoPRAS reflect a particular scoring choice; we discuss alternative choices and weightings in Appendix B . 4 Experimental Protocol 4.1 Datasets We draw questions from two QA datasets that differ in both question difficulty and exposure to LLM pretraining. HotpotQA questions typically require a few reasoning hops over Wikipedia passages and are answerable from a small set of supporting paragraphs. BrowseComp-Plus , derived from BrowseComp , instead contains queries constructed by inversion from a target entity under multiple constraints; answering them requires iterative search and aggregation across many documents, and state-of-the-art retrievers achieve low recall on its corpus of adversarially mined distractors. The two datasets are also complementary in their dependence on supporting documents. HotpotQA answers often involve general knowledge that models may store in their parameters, and the corpus itself has likely been seen during pretraining. By controlling the factuality of the supporting documents, we can therefore study how a model handles conflicts between its parametric knowledge and the dynamic knowledge returned by search. BrowseComp-Plus questions, in contrast, cannot be answered without supporting documents, as the corpus is too recent to be known by current LLMs. With no such conflict, we can study instead how contradictory supporting documents affect the reasoning strategy of a search agent. 4.2 Evaluated systems We have selected systems to cover a diverse set of search-agent designs: closed-weight and open-weight models, different model sizes, and different training procedures such as supervised fine-tuning and RL under different reward objectives.
A system denotes a model together with its harness, i.e. , the orchestration code and prompts that determine how context is assembled, how tools are invoked, and how model outputs are parsed. To keep the comparison controlled, simulated documents are formatted according to the conventions expected by each harness 5 5 5 Additional implementation details, including model versions and references, tool-use templates, and training procedures, are provided in Appendix D.2 . .
In total, twelve systems are evaluated. GPT systems ( gpt-5-nano and gpt-5-mini ), each run with low , medium , and high reasoning effort, provide six closed-weight tool-calling baselines. SpiqaDR 6 6 6 URL will be provided after peer review. is a 3B Qwen2.5-based search agent trained with RL
for scientific literature search; a detailed description is given in Appendix D.3 . Search-R1 Jin et al. (2025) is a 7B Qwen2.5-based search agent trained with RL to interleave reasoning with multi-turn search. AutoRefine Shi et al. (2026) is a 3B Qwen2.5-based retrieval-augmented reasoning model that alternates search with explicit evidence-refinement steps before answering. ASearcher Gao et al. (2026) is an 8B open deep-research model trained with RL and evolving rubrics to produce long-form, citation-grounded answers.

Figure 1 summarizes final-answer outcomes by system and degraded document-quality dimension, one at a time, aggregated over both datasets. The top histograms correspond to the fully reliable setting and are repeated for reference. When degrading trustworthiness (Figure 1a ),
several open-weight search agents, including SpiqaDR , Search-R1 , AutoRefine , and ASearcher , show little or no change.
For example, Search-R1 remains between 78% and 80% correct across all trustworthiness levels, with no clear change in its abstention rate. These models rely heavily on the information provided in their context, even when sources have clear surface cues of low credibility. By contrast, DR-Tulu becomes less reliable as trustworthiness decreases: its correct-answer rate drops from 59.8% to 40.9%, while the rate of no-answer increases from 10.8% to 34.6%. This suggests that lower-quality documents can make this system more brittle with respect to following a specified format. GPT-based systems show a different pattern: when trustworthiness is degraded, they more often avoid giving a final answer, either by abstaining or by exhausting the tool-call budget. For instance, GPT-5-mini (low) increases its abstention rate from 14.0% to 22.5%, while GPT-5-mini (high) increases its tool-budget exhaustion rate from 32.0% to 62.7%. Overall, even though the low-trust documents contain very clear, sometimes almost caricatural low-credibility signals, all systems still give non-abstaining final answers in many runs at 0% trustworthiness. When degrading relevance (Figure 1b ), the effect is stronger: the correct-answer rate drops sharply for all systems. Systems also fail in different ways. Search-R1 mostly fails by producing factually incorrect answers, with its incorrect-answer rate increasing from 19.5% to 79.2%. SpiqaDR shows a mixed failure pattern, with more incorrect answers (from 30.5% to 52.2%) and more abstentions (from 8.5% to 38.0%). GPT-based models mostly fail by either abstaining or exhausting tool-call budget: low-effort models tend to abstain, while high-effort models tend to keep searching until they reach the budget limit. DR-Tulu fails to produce a valid answer tag as documents become less relevant, whereas DR-Tulu without reasoning mostly outputs factually incorrect answers. This suggests that DR-Tulu ’s reasoning trace may help prevent some incorrect final answers, but at the cost of more format failures. AutoRefine also shows a specific brittleness to relevance degradation: its rate of missing answer tags increases from 18.2% to 61.9%, a much sharper increase than what we observe for the other degradation axes. Finally, factuality degradation (Figure 1c ) increases the trends seen for relevance degradation. SpiqaDR abstains much less at 0% factuality than at 0% relevance (15.2% versus 38.0%), and DR-Tulu reaches 59.1% incorrect answers at 0% factuality, compared with 33.8% at 0% relevance. This suggests that irrelevant documents are easier to reject than relevant but false documents. These differences in behavior suggest that model robustness is heavily influenced by training methods. Specifically, narrow RL-based rewards tend to produce brittle models, whereas the scale and diversity of GPT training enable more robust generalization. Aggregate metrics. Table 14 reports aggregate metrics (correctness, RAS, and cost-normalized variants TPC and CoPRAS) at 0%, 50%, and 100% document quality. Each value is averaged across three settings, where one dimension is varied while the others remain at 100%.
Degrading document quality increases the average cost from 10.9k to 20.5k. This is consistent with Xie et al. (2026) , who also observe that noisier search conditions lead agents to issue more searches, although the effect is highly system-dependent: the cost of GPT-5-nano (medium) increases by a factor of 3.5 between 100% and 0% document quality, whereas AutoRefine shows almost no change. Correctness and RAS give different views of behavior under degradation. At 0% quality, average correctness drops to 0.30 while average RAS stays at 0.61.
This gap shows that not all incorrect outcomes are equal, some agents avoid unsupported answers when evidence is unreliable. For instance, GPT-5-nano (medium) maintains a stable RAS despite a drop in correctness (0.83 to 0.41). Conversely, Search-R1 drops in both correctness (0.80 to 0.35) and RAS (0.80 to 0.37), receiving little reliability credit. Thus, RAS differentiates between robust non-answering and overconfident failure in low-quality settings. Cost-normalized metrics show that TPC increases sharply from 18.1k to 75.7k as quality drops to 0%, as it ignores reliability and rewards only correctness. CoPRAS increases less severely (to 36.8k) by incorporating RAS. This shifts the ranking at 0% quality: AutoRefine is favored by TPC for its low cost, while GPT-5-nano (low) and GPT-5-mini (low) are favored by CoPRAS for combining low cost with robustness. Ultimately, TPC rewards cheap correctness, while CoPRAS rewards cost-efficient reliability. Importantly, incorrect documents are not constructed to agree with each other, whereas factual documents are grounded in the same evidence. As a result, an agent that performs multiple searches can in principle identify the stable signal across documents and discount inconsistent false claims. This setting is especially informative on BrowseComp-Plus , where agents cannot rely on parametric knowledge. Correct answers in this condition suggest that the agent is able to aggregate evidence across multiple tool calls, even if some may yield contradictory information. 6 Conclusion We have introduced DeepStress , a controlled simulation framework for stress-testing Deep Search agents under degraded evidence.
Our experiments reveal substantial differences in agents’ robustness, uncertainty expression, and search adaptation, leading to three main conclusions. First, source reliability should be explicitly considered in the design of search agents, so that they can assess retrieved evidence and adapt their behavior accordingly. Second, robustness to conflicts between parametric knowledge and retrieved evidence should be systematically evaluated: producing correct answers despite consistently incorrect retrieved evidence may indicate robustness, but also benchmark contamination.
Third, aggregate measures such as the proposed RAS metrics provide a useful operational characterization of system performance by capturing robustness across different levels of evidence degradation. Finally, although our framework relies on synthetic interventions, the conditions it models reflect genuine deployment risks, as agents operating on the open Web can encounter erroneous or deliberately manipulated content Zhang et al. (2026) .
Given these findings, we recommend integrating controlled stress testing into the evaluation framework for Deep Search systems. Two directions follow from this work.
First, by examining query formulation and reasoning steps, we move toward a process-based evaluation that complements outcome-level results with an analysis of agent search behavior and evidence integration.
Second, DeepStress could also support the training of more robust agents. The brittleness of systems trained with narrow outcome-based rewards motivates reliability-aware training objectives, for which the controlled document labels and RAS could provide a useful starting point. Limitations Our simulator is designed as a controlled environment for studying how document quality affects agent behavior. Each search call returns one generated document. This lets us control the evidence seen by the agent, but makes the search setting simpler than real search. In particular, we do not model cases where a single search call returns several documents with conflicting, incomplete, or irrelevant content. We study simple search agents that use a single search loop and one type of search tool. This helps isolate the effect of document quality and keeps the results easy to interpret. However, our findings mainly apply to this type of agent. They may not directly apply to more complex systems that use summarization, reranking, query reformulation, or sub-agents. We vary the three reliability dimensions independently and use LLM-generated documents. This gives us precise control over each dimension, but we do not study documents where several reliability issues appear at the same time. Synthetic documents may also be cleaner, less noisy, and less diverse than real search results. Our control experiments and human evaluation suggest that the setup is still useful for studying agent behavior, but these documents are not a perfect substitute for real web content. Finally, our document generation pipeline requires each question to have supporting evidence. We use this evidence to ground factually correct documents, such as Wikipedia paragraphs from HotpotQA or reference documents from BrowseComp-Plus, and reduce hallucinations from the generator. Benchmarks without such evidence can still be used, but their factually correct documents may contain generator errors. This would make the factuality axis noisier and the conclusions less reliable. Ethical concerns This work is mainly dedicated to evaluation and diagnosis, and is intended to assess the behavior of models with respect to unreliable documents that could be retrieved by search engines. We believe that the subject of the study itself has a significant impact towards trustworthy and explainable Artificial Intelligence. We are aware that for the sake of evaluation we generate unreliable synthetic documents, including untrustworthy and non-factual documents, but these documents are generated dynamically for the sake of the experiment and are not meant to be distributed. When presenting samples of such documents, we will systematically add a warning notice mentioning that they contain unreliable content. We are however also aware that the methodology could be misused with the objective of generating such documents for illicit purposes. We will include a disclaimer on the code repository discouraging such misuse.
Regarding the human annotation process for evaluating the "quality" of synthetic documents (quality meaning here that they correspond to the purpose they were designed for), the annotators were internal colleagues that contributed on a voluntary basis, and there was no specific compensation. Appendix B Aggregate metrics discussion In Section 3.4 , we describe the Tokens Per Correctness (TPC) metric from Xie et al. (2026) and introduce our reliability-aware metrics: Reliability-Aware Score (RAS) and Cost per RAS (CoPRAS). These aggregate metrics depend on several design choices, including how correctness and cost are defined, and how the maximum tool-call budget is set. In this section, we discuss the motivation for these choices and illustrate their impact empirically. B.1 Design choices Cost. To make our results comparable with prior work, we reuse the cost definition from Xie et al. (2026) , which combines generated tokens, input tokens, and the number of search calls. Following their setup, generated tokens have weight 1 1 , input tokens have weight 0.25 0.25 , and each search call has weight 500 500 . These values are motivated by the relative cost of input and output tokens, and by the estimated cost of a web-search API call expressed in equivalent output tokens.
This choice is reasonable for web-search agents, but it is not universal. In particular, systems that search over a local document collection, or use a cheaper internal retriever rather than a public search API, would require different weights. We therefore treat this cost function as a useful reference point rather than an absolute measure of computational cost. Scoring. Aggregate scores are useful because they allow us to rank systems directly. However, they also compress several different outcomes into a single number. This choice is not neutral: it depends on what we want to evaluate. From a user perspective, it may be enough that the system avoids giving a wrong answer. From a system-design perspective, we may want to distinguish more carefully between a model that says “I don’t know”, a model that keeps searching until the tool budget is exhausted, a model that fails to produce a valid answer tag, and a model that gives an incorrect answer.
RAS defines one possible scoring choice. It gives full credit to correct answers, no credit to incorrect or malformed answers, and partial credit to abstention or tool-budget exhaustion when reliable evidence is unlikely. This reflects the idea that, under degraded document conditions, not answering can be better than giving an unsupported answer. However, this is not the only possible design. For example, one could give less credit to tool-budget exhaustion than to explicit abstention, such as 0.5 − ρ γ i 0.5-\rho_{\gamma_{i}} instead of 1 − ρ γ i 1-\rho_{\gamma_{i}} , because clearly stating uncertainty may be preferable to searching until the budget is reached. This weighting is necessarily a design choice. CoPRAS partly captures this difference through cost, since long searches are more expensive than early abstention.
We therefore treat RAS and CoPRAS as summary metrics, not as replacements for the detailed outcome distributions. They are useful for comparing systems from a reliability-oriented perspective, but other scoring choices could emphasize different behaviors. B.2 Impact of tool-call budget For the main experiments, we set the tool-call budget to 100. This choice directly affects our aggregate metrics. A lower budget penalizes systems that usually need many searches before answering, since they are more likely to stop before producing a final answer. It also affects cost-based metrics, because the number of tool calls is part of the cost definition. Changing the budget can therefore change both metric values and system rankings. We analyze this effect by recomputing the metrics under different tool-call budgets. Figure 4 shows how final-answer outcomes change as the budget increases. Figure 5 shows the corresponding evolution of Correctness , RAS , TPC , and CoPRAS . Overall, correctness increases with the budget, but the rate of improvement differs across systems. Models with narrow tool-call distributions reach their final performance quickly, while models with more spread-out distributions, such as GPT-5-nano (high) and GPT-5-mini (high) , improve more gradually. As a result, correctness-based rankings become reasonably stable only after around 50 tool calls, although high-reasoning models continue to benefit from larger budgets. RAS is more stable with respect to the tool-call budget. Unlike correctness, it does not always increase with additional turns, because abstention and budget exhaustion can receive partial credit under unreliable document conditions. Several systems therefore plateau after only a small number of tool calls. The main exceptions are DR-Tulu and ASearcher , which often terminate without producing a valid final answer tag; increasing the budget can expose more of these incomplete interactions and penalize them in RAS. Overall, the relative stability of RAS suggests that smaller budgets, around 10 tool calls, may already be sufficient to obtain informative RAS-based rankings, while substantially reducing evaluation cost. TPC and CoPRAS show broadly similar trends as the budget increases, since both normalize cost by a performance score. The main difference appears at very small budgets: some systems have near-zero correctness in this regime, which makes TPC extremely large or infinite. CoPRAS is less sensitive to this issue when systems receive reliability-aware credit through RAS, but it still increases when additional tool calls raise the cost without improving the score. We track three query-level strategies that reflect increasingly constrained or source-aware search behavior: • Exact-Match Operator : the query contains quoted text, indicating that the agent searches for an exact entity name, phrase, or clue span. • Specify Source : the query targets a particular source, domain, or source family, for example using site: operators or naming resources such as Wikipedia, IMDb, news outlets, archives, or domain-specific databases. • Authority Cues : the query explicitly seeks authoritative evidence, for example by using terms such as “official” or “reliable source”, or by targeting institutional domains such as government or university websites. Figure 9 shows how often these strategies appear as document quality varies. We observe substantial differences across systems and degraded dimensions. First, systems differ strongly in their use of exact-match operators, even when document quality is 100%. Some agents almost never use quoted queries, such as SpiqaDR . This is consistent with its training setup, where search is based on dense-vector retrieval rather than keyword search operators. By contrast, ASearcher and gpt-5-mini -based agents use exact-match queries much more frequently. gpt-5-nano behaves differently: it uses exact-match operators in only about 30% of queries under perfect document conditions, but increases this rate to about 50% when relevance is degraded to 0%. This suggests that some agents respond to irrelevant results by making their queries more constrained. Second, trustworthiness degradation leads some agents to issue more source-aware queries. GPT-based systems and DR-Tulu more often specify a specific source or domain when trustworthiness is low, suggesting that they try to redirect search toward more credible sources. We observe a similar, though weaker, pattern for authority cues: these systems slightly increase the use of terms such as “official” or “reliable source” under low-trust conditions. Interestingly, degrading relevance has the opposite effect for the same systems, reducing the use of source-specific queries. This suggests that agents adapt differently depending on the perceived failure mode: low trustworthiness encourages source verification, whereas low relevance encourages query reformulation around the target content. Overall, query-content features reveal process-level differences that are not visible from final answers alone. Some agents react to degraded documents by making queries more constrained or more source-aware, while others show little adaptation. These results are exploratory, but they suggest that query reformulation is an important component of robust search-agent behavior. Across systems, we aim to stay as close as possible to the original inference logic, prompts, and action format released by the authors. D.3 Training details about SpiqaDR SpiqaDR is a search-agent fine-tuned from Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct for deep-research-style question answering over scientific literature, using agentic reinforcement learning with verl . The checkpoint is released in the HuggingFace catalog 15 15 15 The link will be shared after peer review. for reproducibility. SPIQA-derived training data. The training data is derived from the SPIQA Pramanick et al. (2024) collection of machine-learning papers, from which the model takes its name. We start from the raw paper texts and enrich them with Semantic Scholar metadata using arXiv identifiers, including titles, authors, venues, years, abstracts, citations, and references. Our clustering procedure is inspired by the citation-graph community detection approach used in MDA-QA ( Huang et al., 2025 ) , but we adapt the graph construction and filtering criteria to our setting. We build an undirected graph where papers are nodes, and edges connect papers that cite or reference each other within our corpus. We then apply SLPA community detection with 50 iterations and a filtering threshold of 0.1, and keep only communities containing 2 to 10 papers. This removes isolated papers, which cannot support multi-document questions, and overly large communities, which can exceed the context budget used for generation. For each selected community, we generate a long-form QA example with GPT-4.1 . The prompt provides the full text of all papers in the community and asks the model to identify facts specific to each document before generating an overarching question that requires information from all documents. The output contains the question, a detailed answer with sources, and document-level annotations describing each paper’s contribution. The final dataset contains 1,000 examples, split into 800 training, 100 validation, and 100 test examples. Search environment. During training, the model interacts with a search tool over the SPIQA paper collection. The tool takes a natural-language query and returns the most similar paper chunk from a vector database, with t ​ o ​ p ​ _ ​ k = 1 top\_k=1 . The returned passage is inserted into the conversation using a fixed format containing the query and the passage. When the model emits a valid tool call, generation is interrupted, the tool result is appended to the context, and generation resumes. Tool responses are masked from the policy loss, so the model is optimized only on its own generated tokens. Reward function. The reward combines three signals: retrieval, answer quality, and format compliance. The retrieval component rewards the model for finding reference papers associated with the question. The answer-quality component is computed with METEOR between the generated final report and the reference answer. Finally, the format component enforces the expected agent protocol: the model must use valid tool calls and produce a final answer after the tool responses. The final reward is the product of these components: R = R retrieval ⋅ R meteor ⋅ R format . R=R_{\mathrm{retrieval}}\cdot R_{\mathrm{meteor}}\cdot R_{\mathrm{format}}. This product form makes the reward strict: the model must retrieve relevant papers, produce an answer close to the reference, and follow the expected tool-use format. In practice, this was useful because optimizing only answer similarity did not reliably induce search behavior, while optimizing only retrieval did not produce useful final reports. Optimization setup. We train the model with Reinforce++ using verl . The base model is Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct , with a context length of up to 24k tokens. Training is run for 60 epochs on a single 80GB A100/H100 GPU. During development, we also explored alternative reward variants, including rubric-based answer-quality rewards with an LLM judge. The SpiqaDR checkpoint used in this paper, however, is trained with the retrieval, METEOR, and format components described above.
