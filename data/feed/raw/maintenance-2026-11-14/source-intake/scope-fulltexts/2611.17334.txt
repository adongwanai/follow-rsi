Most models use a pretrained vision encoder, while some recent recipes train the encoder from scratch with the language model or remove it entirely.
Vision encoders bundle together three design choices:
a visual prior from upstream pretraining, dedicated vision capacity ,
and pre-fusion placement of that capacity
(i.e. , before the multimodal backbone).
Because published models also differ in data, architecture, and compute, comparing them cannot isolate the effect of each choice.
We disentangle these choices via a controlled framework where we pretrain all models from scratch on common data and compare them on visual question answering and language benchmarks at matched lifetime compute .
We find that the visual prior is not necessary; an encoder trained jointly from scratch is on par with a pretrained one, without a separate training stage.
What matters instead is dedicated vision capacity: an encoder-free model trails on visual understanding even when its backbone is enlarged by the encoder’s parameter count.
Yet that capacity need not precede multimodal fusion, as vision-only modules added in-fusion to early backbone layers perform comparably to a pre-fusion encoder and can lower time to first token.
These results also hold across backbone sizes, when encoder pretraining is treated as free, and when starting from a pretrained language model.
Overall, our findings favor dedicated vision capacity trained jointly with the backbone and placed early within it. † † date: November 12, 2026 † † Blog: https://unbundling.github.io Figure 1: A controlled study of multimodal vision encoding. Multimodal training has progressed from freezing this encoder ( Alayrac et al., 2022 ; Li et al., 2023b ; Liu et al., 2023 ; Muse Glimmer Team, 2026 ) to updating it ( Beyer et al., 2024 ; Bai et al., 2025a ) , but encoder pretraining has remained a separate stage.
Recent recipes bring vision and language closer by discarding parts of the pretrained encoder setup: Kimi K3 trains a vision encoder from scratch jointly with the multimodal backbone ( Kimi Team, 2026b ) , and encoder-free models directly process pixel patches, forcing visual encoding to be learned entirely within the backbone ( Adept AI, 2023 ; Diao et al., 2024 ; Liu et al., 2026 ; Gemma Team, 2026 ; Thinking Machines
Lab, 2026a ) .
Each recipe implies a different answer to what the vision encoder contributes, if anything at all.
We ask which of its inherited design choices benefit multimodal models trained entirely from scratch. The predominant multimodal vision encoding recipes bundle together three design choices:
(i) a visual prior from upstream pretraining,
(ii) dedicated vision capacity (i.e. , parameters that only vision tokens use),
and (iii) pre-fusion placement of that capacity in a separate encoder that operates before vision and text tokens interact in the backbone—rather than operating in-fusion , within the shared backbone.
A pretrained encoder keeps all three, a from-scratch encoder gives up only the prior, and an encoder-free model gives up all three. Existing comparisons cannot isolate these choices, for two reasons.
First, they entangle the choices with each other and the rest of the recipe.
Published models differ in data, training stages, compute, and architecture.
While controlled studies hold multimodal training data and compute fixed, their comparisons among pretrained encoders ( McKinzie et al., 2024 ; Tong et al., 2024a ; Fan et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2025 ) vary which visual prior an encoder imports rather than whether it needs one in the first place.
Studies of encoder-based vs. encoder-free models either change all three choices ( Tong et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2026 ) or change capacity and placement together ( Shukor et al., 2025 ) .
Second, comparisons with pretrained encoders control only multimodal training, treating encoder pretraining as free even though it adds substantial compute and data of its own (e.g. , SigLIP 2 trains on 40B image–text samples; Tschannen et al., 2025 ), and several studies attribute the encoder’s advantage to its pretraining ( Lei et al., 2025 ; Diao et al., 2025 ) . In this work, we conduct a controlled study that unbundles the design choices of multimodal vision encoding one at a time ( fig. 1 ):
(i) does the vision encoder need separate pretraining? (ii) does vision encoding need dedicated capacity? and (iii) does that capacity need to precede multimodal fusion? We train every model entirely from scratch, language backbone included, on a common pool of data with one encoder architecture, one dense backbone architecture, and one optimization recipe.
We evaluate models on downstream visual question-answering (VQA) and language benchmarks at backbone sizes from 0.6B to 6.4B.
Because recipes spend compute in different stages, we compare them at matched lifetime compute : the total compute of all training stages, including any pretraining of the encoder or language model. Our experiments answer these questions and test whether the answers hold beyond lifetime compute: 1. Separate pretraining is optional (§ 3 ).
At matched lifetime compute, a from-scratch encoder is on par with encoders pretrained on the same data.
Even if we ignore upstream compute (§ 6.1 ), more encoder pretraining improves recognition but not VQA. 2. Dedicated vision capacity is key (§ 4 ).
An encoder-free model trails an encoder-based model on visual understanding, even when its backbone is enlarged by the encoder’s parameter count.
The encoder’s advantage thus lies in capacity dedicated to vision rather than in total parameter count. 3. Vision capacity can move in-fusion (§ 5 ).
Adding vision-only capacity to the backbone’s earliest layers is comparable to adding a from-scratch pre-fusion encoder.
Scaling laws show in-fusion tracking the encoder, whereas encoder-free trails it at every size (§ 5.4 ). 4. The findings hold beyond lifetime compute (§ 6 ).
Starting from a pretrained 1.7B language model, in-fusion stays comparable to the from-scratch encoder while encoder-free falls further behind.
In-fusion can also lower time to first token, since no separate encoder must run before the backbone. Together, these findings show that multimodal models benefit from dedicated vision capacity, which however needs neither separate pretraining nor pre-fusion placement. \FloatBarrier 2 A controlled protocol for comparing multimodal recipes We propose a protocol that varies the three design choices a vision encoder bundles, while holding fixed the data sources, optimization settings, compute, and backbone architecture. This isolates their effects when comparing current multimodal model recipes.
Conclusions about these design choices cannot be drawn from comparing published models, because they differ in several factors at once—e.g. , they build on different pretrained language models and vision encoders that each vary in training data and compute.
We therefore use no external pretrained models: we train every component from scratch, with common data sources and training setups, and compare recipes at matched lifetime compute , the total compute of all training stages. 2.1 Recipes and the fusion boundary Table 1: Unbundling the encoder’s design choices. Backbone. Our backbones use an architecture modeled after Qwen3 ( Yang et al., 2025 ) : a dense, decoder-only, pre-norm Transformer ( Vaswani et al., 2017 ) with RMSNorm ( Zhang and Sennrich, 2019 ) , RoPE ( Su et al., 2024 ) , SwiGLU feed-forward layers ( Shazeer, 2020 ) , and grouped-query attention ( Ainslie et al., 2023 ) with query–key normalization ( Dehghani et al., 2023 ) ( section A.1 ).
Our main comparisons ( sections 3 , 4 and 5 ) use the Qwen3 1.7B and 4B configurations with dedicated vision capacity fixed at 0.4B parameters, and our scaling study ( section 5.4 ) uses a 0.6B–6.4B ladder that grows vision capacity in proportion to the backbone.
Training uses autoregressive cross-entropy on text tokens; vision tokens precede the text they condition and have no loss.
Each image’s vision tokens attend to each other bidirectionally and text tokens attend causally to all earlier tokens, including vision tokens ( section A.4 ). Fusion boundary. We describe how image patches reach the backbone relative to the multimodal fusion boundary , the first layer in which vision and text tokens can interact: vision-only computation before it is pre-fusion and computation inside the shared backbone is in-fusion ( figs. 1 and 1 ).
Scratch encoders ( ) process image patches pre-fusion with a randomly initialized 27-layer, 0.4B-parameter Vision Transformer ( Dosovitskiy et al., 2021 ; Alabdulmohsin et al., 2023 ) modeled after MoonViT-V2 (Kimi K3’s encoder; Kimi Team, 2026b ), whose output is linearly projected into the backbone.
Pretrained encoders ( ) share this architecture, but we first pretrain them ourselves on our common data ( section 2.2 ) rather than taking external checkpoints.
Encoder-free models ( ) map patches directly into the backbone with a linear patch embedding ( Adept AI, 2023 ; Gemma Team, 2026 ) , followed by RMSNorm and a learned scalar gate ( Qi et al., 2026 ; Li et al., 2026 ) .
In-fusion models ( ) instead add vision-only modules inside the backbone ( section 5 ).
Every recipe trains on 224 × 224 224\times 224 images, read as 256 vision tokens using 14 × 14 14\times 14 patches.
Instruction tuning for evaluation raises the resolution to 448 × 448 448\times 448 (1,024 vision tokens), as in PaliGemma’s short high-resolution stage ( Beyer et al., 2024 ) , since OCR and chart reading needs fine detail ( Tong et al., 2024a ; Beyer, 2024 ) . 2.2 Common data Text-only sequences come from Nemotron-CC v2/v2.1 ( NVIDIA, 2025b ; NVIDIA, 2025a ) , and image–text sequences use MetaCLIP 1.2 pairs (English subset) ( Xu et al., 2024 ) .
Encoder pretraining uses these pairs with their original alt-text, as in each objective’s original setup ( section 3 ; e.g. , CapPa, Tschannen et al., 2023 ), while multimodal training uses captions regenerated by Qwen2.5-VL-32B ( Bai et al., 2025b ) and shared by every recipe.
Multimodal training mixes text-only and image–text sequences 70:30, so recipes with a pretrained encoder spend more lifetime compute on image–text data.
This matches the language share Han et al. (2026) found optimal and gave the best visual understanding in our sweep at a small cost to text ( section A.4 ). 2.3 Lifetime compute Multimodal models often build on separately pretrained components whose training can even exceed the multimodal stage.
For example, SigLIP 2, a commonly used encoder, is trained on 40B image–text samples ( Tschannen et al., 2025 ) , which translates to 10 trillion image tokens, while language models are typically also trained on trillions of text tokens ( Touvron et al., 2023 ; Yang et al., 2025 ) .
Counting only multimodal training therefore favors recipes that spend more compute in these earlier stages.
To make such comparisons fair, we propose lifetime compute , the total training compute of every stage that produces the final model, C lifetime = ∑ s ∈ 𝒮 C s , C_{\mathrm{lifetime}}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}C_{s}, (1) where 𝒮 \mathcal{S} is the set of training stages and C s C_{s} is the training compute of stage s s .
Here, 𝒮 \mathcal{S} contains multimodal training, any text-only training before it and, for pretrained encoders, encoder pretraining, but not instruction tuning.
We report compute in FLOPs; because recipes differ in cost per token, matched compute means that they process different numbers of tokens. Section A.5 describes the compute accounting and converts each budget into token-equivalents, the number of tokens a text-only model of the same size would process with the same compute.
Lifetime compute models a project that allocates one budget across every training stage, which lets us separate the contribution of each design choice.
In practice, pretrained encoders are often available for free, so we also compare encoders with their pretraining compute not counted ( section 6.1 ). 2.4 Training schedule To compare recipes at many training lengths from one run ( Zhai et al., 2022 ) , we use a warmup–stable–decay schedule ( Hu et al., 2024 ; Wen et al., 2025 ; Hägele et al., 2024 ) , where the learning rate stays constant after warmup and any checkpoint of that run can be decayed to 5% of its peak over the final 10% of training ( section A.3 ).
Our main comparisons span compute budgets from 6.5 × 10 20 6.5\times 10^{20} to 2.9 × 10 22 2.9\times 10^{22} FLOPs, or 56B to 1.11T token-equivalents ( table 12 ).
We index budgets by tokens per parameter (TPP): token-equivalents divided by the backbone’s non-embedding parameters, i.e. , all parameters except the tied token embedding (1.4B at 1.7B and 3.6B at 4B; backbones are named by their total parameters).
Budgets reach 788 TPP at 1.7B and 306 TPP at 4B.
All recipes share one peak learning rate per backbone size, scaled with the inverse square root of its total parameter count ( 3 × 10 − 4 3\times 10^{-4} at 1.7B and 2 × 10 − 4 2\times 10^{-4} at 4B).
At 1.7B, the encoder’s lead over encoder-free and parity with in-fusion hold from half to twice that learning rate ( section F.2 ). 2.5 Evaluation We evaluate recipes on text and visual understanding benchmarks and report the following metrics. Avg. Sections 4 and 5.4 also compare held-out caption loss on images never seen in training ( section B.4 ). Appendix B details the benchmarks, and appendix I gives every benchmark score. 3 Does the vision encoder need separate pretraining? The first design choice a vision encoder bundles is a visual prior imported through separate pretraining, which recent models ( Kimi Team, 2026b ) forgo by training the encoder from scratch with the language model.
Under a fixed lifetime compute budget, encoder pretraining directly competes with multimodal training for FLOPs.
Whether the prior is needed also shapes the remainder of our study, because encoder-free architectures cannot easily import one.
We test whether the prior helps by comparing encoders pretrained with different objectives against the same encoder trained from scratch with the backbone. Setup. We pretrain a separate encoder with each of four objectives and the same compute: contrastive image–text alignment (MetaCLIP; Xu et al., 2024 ), self-supervised learning (WebSSL; Fan et al., 2025 ), and captioning (Cap and CapPa; Tschannen et al., 2023 ).
Pretrained encoders are updated during multimodal training and compared with an encoder trained from scratch at matched lifetime compute; we also test a frozen MetaCLIP encoder.
MetaCLIP, an instance of widely used CLIP-style pretraining ( Radford et al., 2021 ; Zhai et al., 2023 ; Tschannen et al., 2025 ) , serves as the pretrained reference in the remaining experiments. Section D.1 gives per-category results. 4 Does vision encoding need dedicated capacity? The from-scratch encoder of section 3 forgoes the imported prior but keeps the second design choice: dedicated vision capacity. Encoder-free models drop this capacity entirely and project image patches directly into the shared backbone ( Adept AI, 2023 ; Diao et al., 2024 ; Liu et al., 2026 ; Gemma Team, 2026 ; Thinking Machines
Lab, 2026a ) .
Dropping the encoder, however, also removes a substantial number of parameters and changes how raw patches enter the backbone, so a gap between the two recipes could reflect fewer total parameters, a weak patch embedding, or missing dedicated capacity.
In this section, we test whether visual understanding needs this dedicated capacity, leaving the study of its precise placement to section 5 . Setup. We compare the from-scratch encoder ( section 3 ) with the encoder-free recipe ( section 2.1 ), which maps each patch into the backbone with a linear patch embedding followed by RMSNorm and a learned gate.
As in every recipe, vision tokens attend to one another bidirectionally in the backbone ( section 2.1 ).
Both recipes share common backbones, data, and lifetime compute budgets, and we compare them at 1.7B and 4B. Encoder-free trails encoder on visual understanding. Figure 3 shows that Avg. VQA clearly suffers from a missing encoder, across scales and budgets, while Avg. Text stays comparable.
This also holds when scaling the encoder’s size ( section 5.4 ).
This result differs from Shukor et al. (2025) , who find that encoder-free models match or slightly outperform encoder-based ones, mainly at smaller sizes.
We identify two choices in their setup that work against the encoder ( section D.4 ).
First, their encoder’s learning rate may have been too small.
Second, they fit their scaling laws to an average of text, interleaved, and caption losses, which drowns out the signal from vision.
In-distribution held-out caption loss instead shows the encoder-free deficit and tracks Avg. VQA across recipes, sizes, and budgets ( fig. 18 ), whereas their averaged loss hides it. Matching total parameters does not overcome encoder-free’s deficit. We next widen or deepen the encoder-free backbone by 0.4B parameters to match the vision parameters added by the encoder ( table 2 ; 4B in table 19 ).
None of the variants comes close to closing the gap on Avg. VQA, though all improve Avg. Text, since text tokens also use the added parameters.
The remaining gap in visual understanding is largest on OCR and chart benchmarks.
The same parameters are thus more useful as a vision encoder.

Adding backbone capacity with the same encoder parameter budget does not close the gap. 5 Does dedicated capacity need to precede multimodal fusion? Section 4 shows that visual understanding benefits from dedicated capacity.
We now ask whether that capacity must precede multimodal fusion.
The encoder’s parameters are both dedicated , used only by vision tokens, and pre-fusion , processing vision tokens before they interact with text.
We test whether pre-fusion placement is needed by moving the encoder’s parameter budget in-fusion, into vision-only modules inside the shared backbone ( section 5.1 ). Section 5.2 studies where this capacity should sit, which tokens should use it, and which modules it adds. (b) MoT uses entirely separate modules for text and vision, so the modality streams interact only through attention. 5.1 Moving the encoder’s capacity inside the backbone In-fusion vision capacity adds vision-only modules to selected backbone layers.
In each of these layers, the attention projections and the feed-forward network (FFN) keep their shared weights and add vision-only counterparts, whose outputs add to the shared outputs after each module ( fig. 4 ).
Each vision-only module thus acts as an additive expert routed by modality: vision tokens use it alongside the shared weights.
Unlike mixture-of-transformers ( Liang et al., 2025 , MoT;) , which gives text and vision tokens entirely separate parameters, in-fusion still passes every token through the shared parameters. Section 6.2 compares the two when starting from pretrained language models. We use two variants: “in-fusion”, our default, adds vision FFN branches of smaller hidden width and vision-only counterparts of all four attention projections, while “in-fusion (FFN-only)” adds only vision FFN branches.

Placement of capacity within fusion. We study placement with the FFN-only variant, because FFN branches can be narrowed to fit any placement.
Full-rank vision-only attention projections cannot, because adding them to every layer would take 88% of the 0.4B budget at 1.7B and exceed it at 4B.
With the budget fixed, we compare four placements ( table 3 ): a narrow branch in every layer, or eight wider branches spread evenly across depth, in the last eight layers, or in the first eight layers.
The first three variants do no better than the encoder-free model on Avg. VQA, whereas placing the modules in the first eight layers matches the scratch encoder.
Early placement also helps most on OCR and chart questions, which depend on fine visual detail, whereas late placement does not.
Dedicated in-fusion capacity is therefore most useful where the backbone first processes vision tokens, similar to an encoder.
We hypothesize that vision tokens benefit most from vision-only processing while they are still close to raw patch features.
Only early placement concentrates the budget there, whereas the every-layer and spread placements dilute it across the backbone.

Widening the shared FFNs barely improves over the encoder-free model.

The all-token variants slightly improve on Avg. Text, because text also uses the added parameters.

We test whether the comparisons of sections 3 , 4 and 5 hold in this setting.

In-fusion, MoT layers 1–8, and the from-scratch encoder perform comparably, and all lead encoder-free.
The full MoT model falls behind despite 3.5 times as many vision-only parameters—adding vision-only copies of the later layers to MoT layers 1–8 hurts, consistent with late placement not helping in section 5.2 .
A free MetaCLIP encoder, which matches the from-scratch encoder when the backbone is also trained from scratch ( section 6.1 ), trails it here, mostly on OCR, chart, and vision-centric questions.
Tuning each encoder’s learning rate does not change these conclusions ( section H.1 ).

Most multimodal models connect a separately pretrained vision encoder ( Radford et al., 2021 ; Tschannen et al., 2025 ; Bolya et al., 2025 ) , kept frozen ( Liu et al., 2023 ; Li et al., 2023b ; Muse Glimmer Team, 2026 ) or updated ( Beyer et al., 2024 ; Steiner et al., 2024 ; Bai et al., 2025a ) , to a pretrained language model.
More recent models train the encoder from scratch with the language model ( Kimi Team, 2026b ; Tian et al., 2025 ) , embed image patches without an encoder ( Adept AI, 2023 ; Diao et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2026 ; Gemma Team, 2026 ; Thinking Machines
Lab, 2026a ) , or quantize images with a separately trained tokenizer ( Chameleon Team, 2024 ; Wang et al., 2026 ) .
Inkling was released as “encoder-free”, yet its successor, Inkling 2 Small, uses a 292M-parameter vision encoder ( Thinking Machines
Lab, 2026a ; Thinking Machines
Lab, 2026b ) .
Because these models also differ in data, pretrained components, and compute, comparing them directly does not isolate a single design choice.
We therefore train every stage of each recipe class from scratch on common data sources and compare the recipes at matched lifetime compute ( section 2 ). Controlled studies. Prior controlled studies of encoder pretraining typically start from a pretrained language model, count only multimodal training, and compare encoder pretraining objectives and data ( Karamcheti et al., 2024 ; McKinzie et al., 2024 ; Tong et al., 2024a ; Laurençon et al., 2024 ; Fan et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2025 ) rather than studying whether separate pretraining is needed at all.
The few works that compare pretrained and from-scratch encoders vary in their findings, possibly because they control neither the encoder’s pretraining data nor lifetime compute ( Tian et al., 2025 ; Kimi Team, 2026b ) .
Studies raise a similar question for language pretraining, finding that adding vision before text-only pretraining ends, rather than after it, improves visual understanding ( Keh et al., 2025 ; Han et al., 2026 ; Kimi Team, 2026a ) , as in our delayed-vision experiments ( table 11 ).
Encoder pretraining also confounds encoder vs. encoder-free comparisons, which typically pit a pretrained encoder against an encoder-free model ( Beyer et al., 2024 ; Diao et al., 2024 ; Lei et al., 2025 ; Diao et al., 2025 ; Tong et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2026 ; Chen et al., 2026 ) .
Such comparisons remove separate pretraining, dedicated capacity, and pre-fusion placement at once, and often attribute the encoder’s advantage to pretraining data they do not control. Shukor et al. (2025) , who train both designs from scratch, find that encoder-free models match or slightly outperform an encoder.
In contrast, we find that the encoder leads on VQA and held-out caption loss, which we trace to their setup ( section D.4 ). Dedicated vision capacity. Testing whether dedicated capacity must precede fusion requires moving the encoder’s capacity inside the backbone, which the encoder vs. encoder-free comparisons cannot accomplish.
Prior works on fusion depth come closer, varying where modalities begin to interact ( Nagrani et al., 2021 ; Hao et al., 2026 ) or where encoder features enter the backbone ( Meng et al., 2024 ) , but none moves a fixed amount of vision-only capacity into layers that vision and text share.
Other multimodal architectures add vision-specific parameters to the backbone, but not in a form that fits this comparison.
Modality-expert designs duplicate the backbone’s weights for vision tokens, typically in every layer, and route vision tokens through these copies instead of the shared weights ( Bao et al., 2022 ; Lin et al., 2024 ; Liang et al., 2025 ) .
Their vision-only capacity is thus set by the backbone’s size and spread over its depth rather than held at an encoder’s budget early in the model, and most also build on a pretrained language model ( Wang et al., 2024 ; Luo et al., 2025b ; Tian et al., 2025 ; Diao et al., 2025 ; Shi et al., 2025 ) .
Adapter architectures fine-tune a pretrained model through small parallel or low-rank modules shared by all tokens ( He et al., 2022 ; Chen et al., 2022 ; Hu et al., 2022 ) , and their multimodal variants keep an encoder alongside the adapters ( Dong et al., 2024 ; Shapourian et al., 2026 ) or distill from one ( Wang et al., 2025 ) .
The encoders in these works still supply pre-fusion capacity or a pretrained prior, the two factors our comparison aims to remove.
We instead give vision tokens their own attention projections and feed-forward branches in selected backbone layers.
Their outputs add to the shared ones and together match an encoder’s parameter budget, so only the placement of this capacity changes ( section 5 ).
Placed in the first eight layers, in-fusion capacity is comparable to a pre-fusion encoder on VQA, so dedicated vision capacity helps whether it sits pre-fusion or in-fusion. 8 Discussion With multimodal models revisiting the vision encoder, we asked which of its design choices are still beneficial.
We proposed unbundling the encoder into three choices: a visual prior from separate pretraining, dedicated vision capacity, and pre-fusion placement of that capacity.
We tested each by training every recipe from scratch on common data, with one architecture and optimization setup, and comparing recipes on text and visual understanding benchmarks at matched lifetime compute. Takeaways from unbundling. Of the three choices an encoder bundles, only dedicated vision capacity maintains its advantage.
Separate pretraining is unnecessary: an encoder trained jointly with the backbone is on par with pretrained ones, and longer pretraining improves recognition but not VQA, even when it is free ( sections 3 and 6.1 ).
Dedicated capacity matters: with the encoder’s parameters added to their shared backbone, encoder-free models gain on Avg. Text but still trail the encoder on Avg. VQA ( section 4 ), most on OCR and chart questions ( table 2 ).
This capacity belongs early, but it need not precede the backbone: vision-only modules in the first backbone layers are comparable to a pre-fusion encoder ( section 5 ).
For recipes that drop the encoder, this suggests reallocating its parameters as vision-only capacity in the earliest layers rather than as shared parameters. Limitations and future work. To keep comparisons controlled, we pretrain encoders on the same images as multimodal training, so our results do not rule out a benefit from released encoders trained on additional data.
Encoder pretraining uses the images’ original alt-text, as in standard image–text pretraining; pretraining on our regenerated captions instead changes little ( table 9 ).
Our experiments are on dense backbones with up to 6.4B parameters; the most compelling next step is extending them to sparse mixture-of-experts backbones, whose experts could naturally host in-fusion’s modality-routed capacity.
Multimodal training also uses 224 × 224 224\times 224 MetaCLIP images, leaving higher-resolution training and more text-rich data to future work.
The text–image data mixture was selected with the from-scratch encoder at 1.7B, so mixtures tuned for each recipe remain underexplored ( section A.4 ).
Finally, how architecture interacts with interleaved image–text and video data remains open. Conclusion. We presented a controlled study that disentangles the design choices behind how multimodal models process visual input, studying each choice in isolation instead of as part of a bundled recipe.
Rather than assembling multimodal models from components optimized for different objectives, our findings favor training them end-to-end as one system. Acknowledgments We thank

A.1 Architectures The multimodal recipes differ in where their vision-only capacity sits and how it is trained: in a pre-fusion vision encoder that is pretrained and then frozen or updated, or trained from scratch, encoder-free with no module besides a patch embedding, or vision-only modules inside the shared backbone (in-fusion vision capacity; section E.1 ).
Unless a variable is the stated ablation, comparisons hold the backbone, the 256 vision tokens per image in multimodal training, the data, the optimization recipe, and lifetime compute fixed. As with fine-tuning in transfer evaluation, instruction tuning is a fixed downstream step applied to every checkpoint to measure VQA ( appendix B ), so it is not counted.
Each term uses the same algorithmic count of forward- and backward-pass matrix multiplication and convolution FLOPs for the modules active in that stage ( Kaplan et al., 2020 ; Hoffmann et al., 2022 ) .
The count excludes activation recomputation ( Chowdhery et al., 2023 ) , optimizer updates, communication, elementwise operations, and input processing.
A frozen encoder receives no backward pass during multimodal training, so it is charged only its forward pass, one third of its training cost.
The free-encoder comparison of section 6.1 instead matches multimodal training compute and does not charge encoder pretraining. Charging pretrained checkpoints. A pretrained encoder is charged the full compute of its upstream training up to the checkpoint used.
Because a MetaCLIP example costs less compute than a captioning example, the same upstream compute buys MetaCLIP about 12% more epochs.

B.4 Held-out losses We measure losses on two kinds of held-out data, both as per-token negative log-likelihood in nats.
The held-out text and caption losses score unseen samples of the training distributions, and sections 4 , 5.4 and F.3 use them.
Out-of-distribution caption loss scores caption sets outside those distributions, and table 25 reports it. In-distribution. Held-out text loss is the per-token negative log-likelihood on held-out text from the training text corpus, weighted as in the training mixture.
It is scored with the attention masking each model was trained with.
Held-out caption loss is the per-token negative log-likelihood of the regenerated captions of MetaCLIP 1.2 images never used in training, so it samples the training caption distribution. Out-of-distribution. Out-of-distribution caption loss averages the caption loss of three sets of 5,000 images with equal weight: the COCO validation images with the first of their human reference captions ( Lin et al., 2014 ) , MetaCLIP 1.2 pairs with their original alt-text ( Xu et al., 2024 ) , and images from a licensed stock-photo collection.
It is scored with the attention masking each model was trained with. B.5 ImageNet linear probes We use three linear probes on ImageNet-1K ( Deng et al., 2009 ) .
The downstream probe reads the multimodal model with all weights frozen.
It mean-pools the backbone’s final normalized vision-token states and fits an ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -regularized linear classifier on 50 training images per class.
A fixed 10% holdout selects the regularization strength from { 10 − 4 , 10 − 3 , 10 − 2 } \{10^{-4},10^{-3},10^{-2}\} , and the classifier is then refit on all 50,000 images.

We compare it with two richer patch embedding designs: a normalized two-layer projection inspired by Gemma 4 ( Gemma Team, 2026 ) , and a four-layer hierarchical MLP ( Touvron et al., 2022 ) inspired by Inkling ( Thinking Machines
Lab, 2026a ) .
The lower part of table 21 describes each variant.
The normalized two-layer projection changes patch size, resolution, position embeddings, and normalization together.
It normalizes each patch with LayerNorm ( Ba et al., 2016 ) , projects it linearly, adds learned factorized 2D position embeddings, and then applies LayerNorm, a parameter-free RMSNorm, and a linear map into the backbone.
The hierarchical MLP has four linear layers that fold neighboring pixels into channels in stages, with RMSNorm and GELU ( Hendrycks and Gimpel, 2016 ) between them, a final RMSNorm, and no position embedding.
In table 21 , both richer variants read larger images in larger patches ( 768 × 768 768\times 768 in 48 × 48 48\times 48 patches and 640 × 640 640\times 640 in 40 × 40 40\times 40 patches, rather than 224 × 224 224\times 224 in 14 × 14 14\times 14 patches), so they produce the same 16 × 16 16\times 16 grid of 256 vision tokens as the encoder.

Every other setting matches the ladder’s runs, which serve as baselines. Encoder learning rate. Shukor et al. (2025) train their from-scratch encoder at a reduced learning rate, the best of the 0.1 × 0.1\times , 0.01 × 0.01\times , and 0.001 × 0.001\times multipliers they compare.
We train the encoder at 0.01 × 0.01\times the backbone’s peak learning rate, and also at 0.1 × 0.1\times at 1B, with the projector at the full rate.
Instruction tuning trains every encoder at its full rate.
We find that lowering the rate shrinks the encoder’s lead ( table 22 ), and most of the drop comes between the full rate and 0.1 × 0.1\times , whereas their sweep starts at 0.1 × 0.1\times and never trains the encoder at the full rate.
At 0.01 × 0.01\times , the encoder is comparable to encoder-free on Avg. VQA at 1B and loses its caption-loss lead entirely at 0.6B. Averaged validation loss. Shukor et al. (2025) fit their scaling laws to validation loss averaged over interleaved, caption, and text data, with captions that are web alt-text.
The text loss does not depend on the image, and in our ladder the interleaved loss barely differs between the encoder and encoder-free.
Differences between vision components therefore have a weaker effect on the average, which makes it a noisier signal for comparing them.
In our scaling ladder, the averaged loss barely separates the encoder from encoder-free, whereas held-out caption loss separates them clearly and tracks Avg. VQA ( fig. 18 ).

E.1 Implementation and sizing In each augmented layer, in-fusion (FFN-only) adds a vision-only SwiGLU branch of width F F beside the shared FFN, reading the same normalized input.

E.4 Where vision tokens are processed To see where each design processes vision tokens, we probe the 1.7B models at the longest training budget ( fig. 16 ), adapting the probes of Chen et al. (2026) .
For each backbone block, we measure how much the vision tokens change as one minus the cosine similarity between their hidden state before and after the block, and how much attention caption tokens place on them.
In both in-fusion models, vision tokens change mostly in the blocks with vision-only modules and little after them.
The encoder-free model keeps changing them through its early and middle shared blocks.
The encoder’s tokens enter the backbone already processed, so they change mostly in the first few blocks.
After the in-fusion blocks, caption tokens attend to vision tokens more than in the encoder and encoder-free models.
The same pattern holds at a fifth of the training budget.
These probes suggest that in-fusion capacity performs vision-specific processing in its own early blocks and passes stable vision tokens to the shared backbone, whereas an encoder-free model builds them up across its shared layers. This section gives the ladder’s setup, the scaling laws fitted to it, and how well the laws predict runs they were not fitted on. Setup. The ladder trains the encoder-free, encoder, and in-fusion recipes under the protocol of section 2 on backbones from 0.6B to 6.4B parameters.
The backbones follow the architecture of the 1.7B backbone, with query heads of dimension 128 spanning the hidden size, half as many key–value heads, and a feed-forward width three times the hidden size.
The vision module grows with the backbone ( table 28 ).
The encoder keeps the proportions of the 1.7B pairing: half the backbone’s hidden size d d , one layer fewer than the backbone, an MLP four times its own width, and round ⁡ ( 0.75 ​ d / 128 ) \mathrm{round}(0.75\,d/128) attention heads of dimension 128.
Its parameter count therefore stays at a nearly fixed ratio to the backbone’s non-embedding parameters.
The in-fusion modules occupy the first round ⁡ ( 8 ​ L / 28 ) \mathrm{round}(8L/28) of the backbone’s L L layers, 29% of the depth, as at 1.7B.
Their widths are set so that they match the encoder’s parameter count. All recipes at a backbone size share one peak learning rate and one batch size ( table 28 ).
The peak learning rate is 3 × 10 − 4 ​ 1.72 × 10 9 / N tot 3\times 10^{-4}\sqrt{1.72\times 10^{9}/N_{\mathrm{tot}}} , rounded, where N tot N_{\mathrm{tot}} is the total backbone parameter count.
The batch size grows by about 1.5 times per backbone size.
Warmup lasts 2 tokens per non-embedding backbone parameter (2 TPP), e.g. , 2.8B tokens for the 1.7B backbone, which has 1.4B non-embedding parameters.
The other settings follow table 8 .
The 1.7B and 4B runs of sections 3 , 4 and 5 instead keep a batch of 1,024 sequences and 4,000 warmup updates at both sizes.
At 1.7B, where the ladder’s batch is also 1,024, each ladder recipe is within two Avg. VQA points of those runs when interpolated to their budgets ( sections 4 and 5 ). Each recipe and size has one constant-learning-rate run, decayed over the final 10% of training at several budgets ( table 29 ).

Training time can be compared in two ways: per-step comparisons fix the number of optimizer updates, whereas equal-FLOP comparisons fix total modeled training compute.
Because encoder computation counts toward that budget, the encoder recipe completes fewer updates at equal FLOPs, so its equal-FLOP time premium is smaller than its per-step premium. We measure training time with complete optimizer updates on H200 GPUs with fully sharded data parallelism (FSDP) ( Zhao et al., 2023 ) .
The matched 4B comparison in table 35 runs the four recipes on 32 GPUs with a global batch of 1,024 sequences.
Each row projects training time from measured steady-state update times to the same modeled lifetime compute, 1.04 × 10 22 1.04\times 10^{22} FLOPs (400B token-equivalents at 4B; section A.5 ). Table 35 times the encoder and the in-fusion configurations of table 23 , which match the encoder’s 0.4B vision-only parameters, under the 4B training setup above.
At equal lifetime compute, neither in-fusion variant measurably changes training time relative to encoder-free.
The encoder takes 2.9% longer than encoder-free and 2.6% longer than in-fusion at equal FLOPs.
Per step, the encoder takes 5.3% longer than encoder-free.
Note that the encoder used here is an unoptimized reference implementation, and encoder-specific optimization can narrow its gap.
For instance, a compiled ( Ansel et al., 2024 ) version of the Base encoder in table 5 trains within 0.2% of an in-fusion (FFN-only) model with the same vision-only parameters at 4B. Encoder pipelining. Overlapping or pipelining ( Huang et al., 2019 ) the encoder recovers little of its cost.
At 4B, running the next microbatch’s encoder forward pass alongside the backbone hides 20–36% of the encoder’s per-step cost.
Only the forward pass overlaps, because the encoder’s backward pass waits for the backbone’s gradients.
With or without an encoder, a four-stage pipeline has about half the throughput of the encoder recipe under FSDP on the same 32 GPUs.
FSDP optimizations applied to the encoder alone, such as prefetching parameters or skipping gradient synchronization between accumulation steps, change step time by less than 0.7%. We run every design in our own implementation on one H200, so all designs share the same inference code.
Each request has one image and 256 text tokens and generates 120 tokens.
Largely following the inference benchmark setup of Mono-InternVL ( Luo et al., 2025b ) , we report time to first token (TTFT) and output throughput with 16 concurrent requests and 448-pixel images (1,024 vision tokens) unless noted.
Each configuration is measured in four runs, and we report medians with full ranges. Without a separate encoder, the backbone no longer waits for each image to be encoded, so TTFT falls ( fig. 21 ).
In-fusion (FFN-only) still processes every vision token with its vision-only FFNs, and at matched vision-only parameters, it performs approximately the same computation per image as the encoder.
Its advantage comes from running in eight wide layers instead of the encoder’s deeper stack of narrower layers (27 at the 0.4B budget), in our eager-mode implementation.
The inference time saving grows with resolution, because more vision tokens make the encoder a larger share of the time to first token.
