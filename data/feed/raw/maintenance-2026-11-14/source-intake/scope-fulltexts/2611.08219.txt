These advances raise the expectation that agents can take over realistic productivity work from humans. Such work is economically valuable but demanding : it involves long-horizon professional workflows, where agents must understand professional contexts, gather and integrate evidence from heterogeneous digital artifacts, invoke the necessary tools, and ultimately deliver usable work products such as analytical reports, spreadsheets, presentations, and structured documents. However, current agents still fall short on such work. First, real professional workflows involve interdependent steps, implicit domain conventions, and evolving intermediate states, so models miss critical operations or fail to adapt based on feedback. Second, real file contexts are heterogeneous and messy: models must integrate evidence across artifacts with different formats, layouts, and levels of completeness, and often misread content or overlook cross-file dependencies. Third, real deliverables must satisfy fine-grained requirements over factual consistency, evidence coverage, structure, formatting, and practical usability; producing an output file is not the same as meeting deliverable standards. Mastering all three demands at once is precisely what current training data fails to teach. Figure 1: GDPval Pass@1 comparison. Agent-R 2 -4B improves the Qwen3-4B-Thinking base model by 18.6 points and surpasses several larger open-source baselines. Existing training data targets these capabilities only in isolation: instruction following ( Xu et al. 2025a ; Guo et al. 2025 ) , multidisciplinary reasoning ( Liu et al. 2025 ) , document-grounded question answering ( Tanaka et al. 2023 ; Zhang et al. 2026a ) , or generation of a single deliverable type such as presentations and spreadsheets ( Zheng et al. 2025 ; Zheng et al. 2026 ; Chi et al. 2026 ) . While effective in their respective settings, these data sources do not provide end-to-end supervision that jointly covers real work objectives, cross-file evidence, professional workflow execution, and deliverable constraints. What is missing, we argue, is not more data but data that reflects real work: real intents, authentic files, and deliverable-level standards. Such data is hard to fabricate: manually authored workflows do not scale, and LLM-generated files lack the layouts, gaps, and cross-file references of real documents. To address this data bottleneck, we introduce Agent-R 2 , a real-source data synthesis framework that grounds every stage of synthesis in real sources through a three-stage pipeline. First, rather than relying on manually authored workflows as in recent benchmark construction ( Patwardhan et al. 2025 ; Chen et al. 2026 ) , Agent-R 2 mines file-oriented workflow blueprints from web corpora using occupational activity seeds, improving both realism and scalability. Second, it searches for and verifies public files that support each workflow, retaining the heterogeneous formats, layouts, incomplete structures, and cross-file dependencies of realistic digital work. Third, it synthesizes user instructions and fine-grained rubrics from the verified workflow-file contexts: the instructions require complex deliverables involving evidence gathering, reasoning, file operations, and artifact construction, while the rubrics specify detailed criteria for evidence grounding, analytical correctness, and deliverable quality. Starting from 10k web-corpus candidates, Agent-R 2 produces 4k high-quality training instances . We train Agent-R 2 -4B on the synthesized data using supervised fine-tuning followed by rubric-based reinforcement learning. The reinforcement learning stage optimizes the model in the persistent sandbox with outcome rewards: each generated deliverable is evaluated against its fine-grained rubric, which encourages the model to satisfy evidence, reasoning, and artifact-quality requirements rather than merely produce an output file. Trained on only 4k synthesized instances, Agent-R 2 -4B reaches 51.0% Pass@1 on the full GDPval set (Figure 1 ), improving the base model by 18.6 points and surpassing Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507 (48.0%) and Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-Thinking (49.7%). The gains transfer to document-grounded reasoning on OfficeQA: Agent-R 2 -4B improves the base model from 2.2% to 14.8% Pass@1 and again surpasses Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507 (13.8%). Behavioral analysis further shows that training on rubric-scored real-source data mitigates shortcut behavior , a general failure mode where agents emit superficially plausible deliverables before sufficiently grounding, constructing, or verifying them, while promoting sustained exploration. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We introduce Agent-R 2 , a data synthesis framework that grounds workflow extraction, file collection, and instruction-rubric synthesis in real sources, scaling training data for realistic productivity work. • We synthesize 4k high-quality productivity-work instances with authentic multi-format attachments and fine-grained rubrics, and train Agent-R 2 -4B with SFT and rubric-based RL. • Agent-R 2 -4B surpasses Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507 on GDPval and OfficeQA; further analysis indicates that the training mitigates shortcut behavior while promoting sustained exploration. 2 Problem Formulation We study realistic productivity tasks, in which an agent must carry out long-horizon professional work in a sandbox environment with general tools, heterogeneous input files, and deliverable-level requirements. The framework extracts file-oriented workflow blueprints from real web-corpus evidence, retrieves and verifies public attachments, and synthesizes instructions, rubrics, and references in sandbox. In this section, we detail the data synthesis pipeline of Agent-R 2 . The overall pipeline is illustrated in Figure 2 . The prompts used in each stage are provided in the supplementary material. Workflow Extraction. Prior work observes that raw web text can encode implicit tool-use workflows ( Xu et al. 2026b ) . Inspired by this observation, we further treat web corpora as a natural source of workflow evidence for realistic productivity work and focus on file-oriented workflows, where completing the work requires locating, reading, editing, or producing file artifacts. By leveraging broad web corpora, Agent-R 2 enables scalable workflow construction and avoids the limited scalability of manually curated workflows ( Patwardhan et al. 2025 ; Chen et al. 2026 ) . We begin with a web corpus 𝒞 \mathcal{C} and occupational activity seeds 𝒢 \mathcal{G} . These seeds can be drawn from different occupational resources; in this work, we use seeds from O*NET. For the web corpus, we use samples from the DLR-Web dataset ( Attention1115 2025 ) used by DESIGNER ( Liu et al. 2025 ) , which are filtered from FineFineWeb ( m-a-p 2025 ) . Each segment has corpus-provided discipline metadata, which we map to an occupational category to select the corresponding seeds from 𝒢 \mathcal{G} . For each segment, we perform workflow extraction under two filtering criteria: workflow filtering and file-oriented filtering. A segment that satisfies both criteria is converted into a workflow blueprint, information dependencies, required file types, and expected outputs, where the extraction is performed by Qwen3.6-Plus as the synthesis model π syn \pi_{\text{syn}} : 𝒲 = { w ∣ c ∈ Filtered ( 𝒞 ) , w ∼ π syn ( ⋅ ∣ c , 𝒢 ) } . \mathcal{W}=\{w\mid c\in\operatorname{Filtered}(\mathcal{C}),\;w\sim\pi_{\text{syn}}(\cdot\mid c,\mathcal{G})\}. Attachment Searching. Given the workflow blueprints 𝒲 \mathcal{W} , we retrieve public file artifacts and construct candidate file sets.
Unlike prior settings built on large amounts of LLM-generated synthetic files ( Ge et al. 2026 ) , we ground each task in authentic files retrieved from the web, guided by cues that the extracted workflows provide; such files preserve the natural complexity of file structures, layouts, and cross-file evidence.
For each workflow w w , we use π syn \pi_{\text{syn}} to run a multi-turn search-and-verification loop. At each turn, it generates a search query conditioned on the workflow, required file types, previous queries, and prior search results, and executes web_search to obtain results that include titles and snippets. For each result, it first checks its title and snippet for workflow relevance; promising candidates are then downloaded and inspected with bash and view_image to check key parts such as cover pages and tables of contents. Files verified as relevant to the workflow are retained in ℱ c \mathcal{F}_{c} ; to control download latency and construction overhead, we discard files longer than 50 pages or larger than 2 MB. The loop stops when the retained files jointly satisfy the information and operation needs of w w , or the maximum number of search turns is reached. Only satisfied pairs are passed to the next stage, while unsatisfied instances are discarded. Task Synthesis. We instantiate each retained workflow and candidate-file pair ( w , ℱ c ) ∈ 𝒫 (w,\mathcal{F}_{c})\in\mathcal{P} inside the sandbox environment ℰ \mathcal{E} . To expose candidate-file content to the model, we render paginated files into page images and parse spreadsheets into sheet-level tables while preserving the original files for inspection. Conditioned on the workflow blueprint and the workflow-relevant candidate files, π syn \pi_{\text{syn}} performs a multi-turn synthesis process. Across turns, it inspects file contents, searches and fetches web evidence when the workflow requires online information, determines which candidate files should be included as the attachment set ℱ a ⊆ ℱ c \mathcal{F}_{a}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{c} , and jointly designs the instruction I I and rubric ℛ \mathcal{R} . The instruction specifies the expected deliverable, while the rubric ties success criteria to file-level evidence and constraints. We then use Claude-Opus-4.5 as the expert policy π exp \pi_{\text{exp}} to solve the synthesized task in the sandbox. The resulting files are kept as reference files ℱ r \mathcal{F}_{r} for evaluation and judging. Along with the deliverable, we also record the expert trajectory τ \tau , which captures the tool interactions used to inspect files, execute operations, and construct the final output. Let u = ( I , ℱ a , ℛ ) u=(I,\mathcal{F}_{a},\mathcal{R}) denote the candidate solving context: ( τ , ℱ r ) ∼ π exp ( ⋅ ∣ u ; ℰ ) . (\tau,\mathcal{F}_{r})\sim\pi_{\text{exp}}(\cdot\mid u;\mathcal{E}). We further verify each synthesized candidate by evaluating the generated reference files against the rubric. The verifier identifies rubric items that are not satisfied by ℱ r \mathcal{F}_{r} . For each unmet item, if the item is unsupported by the attachments, mismatched with the instruction, or otherwise unreasonable, we revise the rubric; if the item is reasonable, we keep the rubric and ask the expert policy to revise the deliverables once in the sandbox. Using the synthesized task set 𝒟 \mathcal{D} and filtered expert trajectories 𝒵 \mathcal{Z} , we train Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 in two stages: supervised fine-tuning and rubric-based reinforcement learning ( Huang et al. 2025 ; Gunjal et al. 2025 ) . We split the data into 3k instances for SFT and 1k tasks for RL, with no task overlap. Rubric-Based Reinforcement Learning. Starting from the SFT model, we optimize the policy with GRPO ( Shao et al. 2024 ) in the persistent sandbox environment, as illustrated in Figure 3 . Since agent-as-a-judge evaluation requires additional interactive tool use and is inefficient during RL training, we use Qwen3.6-Plus as an MLLM-as-a-judge ( Chen et al. 2024 ) for outcome scoring. After task completion, the generated deliverable, relevant file evidence, and reference files are converted into model-consumable representations: .pdf, .pptx, and .docx files are rendered into images, while .xlsx files are represented by recalculated values and formulas in Markdown together with rendered page images, preserving both cell-level content and visual layout. The judge then scores the result against the synthesized rubric and reference files. To avoid inducing tool-specific calling preferences, we use only outcome reward and do not add explicit penalties for intermediate tool-call errors. We compare our proposed models against two primary categories of baselines: closed-source models, including GPT-4.1, Gemini-2.5-Pro, and Claude-Opus-4.5, and open-source models, including DeepSeek-v3.1, GLM-4.7, and Qwen3-series 30B and 235B text-only and multimodal variants. For Qwen-series text-only models, view_image calls are handled by the same-series multimodal model with a matching parameter scale. For DeepSeek-v3.1, we use DeepSeek-v4-Flash for view_image calls. Benchmarks. We evaluate model performance on both in-domain and out-of-domain benchmarks. GDPval ( Patwardhan et al. 2025 ) serves as the in-domain benchmark, and we further categorize its tasks by the required output deliverable type into office and non-office tasks. The latter covers outputs beyond office formats, including audio (.wav, .mp3), video (.mp4), and other media or file types, which are outside our primary focus in this work. OfficeQA ( Opsahl-Ong et al. 2026 ) serves as the out-of-domain benchmark. We use its official Markdown version, where office source files are provided as plain-text Markdown files, to evaluate whether training improves document perception and reasoning rather than only the surface quality of generated deliverables. Evaluation Protocol. For GDPval, we evaluate deliverables against GDPval rubrics with an agent-as-a-judge
protocol ( OpenAI 2025a ) , using Gemini-3.1-Pro as the judge model ( Google 2026 ) .
For OfficeQA, we combine rule-based matching with model-based assessment. Both benchmarks set a maximum of 100 steps. SFT Encourages Sustained Exploration. The left panel of Figure 4 shows that SFT substantially increases the model’s exploratory behavior. The base model makes only 6.7 tool-call steps and 2.88 tool-call errors per sample on average. Such limited interaction suggests that the base model often stops active exploration after encountering early parsing, execution, or file-handling errors, and instead rushes to construct deliverables with insufficient grounding. Agent-R 2 -4B-SFT increases the average number of tool-call steps to 39.0, with tool-call errors also increasing to 16.3. This shift indicates that SFT teaches the model to inspect files more actively, execute multi-step workflows, and continue attempting corrections after errors. Although limited model capacity and realistic environmental complexity lead to more errors, the longer exploration process ultimately guides the model toward more complete deliverables. Due to limited generalization, not every exploration attempt produces a successful deliverable, as reflected by the completion-rate drop from 86.8% to 78.2%, which limits the potential gain. However, this shapes a more proactive behavior pattern and lays the foundation for subsequent RL optimization. RL Improves Reliable and High-Quality Completion. RL preserves the exploratory behavior introduced by SFT and turns it into more reliable task completion. Figure 4 (left) shows that Agent-R 2 -4B raises the completion rate to 90.9%, above both the base and SFT variants. On the one hand, it averages 33.1 tool-call steps, far above the base model’s 6.7, indicating that it does not fall back to sparse inspection and premature output. On the other hand, tool-call errors decrease from 16.3 under SFT to 12.9, suggesting that rubric-based RL helps the model recover from errors, explore more efficiently, and generalize better in complex environments. Furthermore, the completion quality improves. Figure 4 (right) compares Agent-R 2 -4B with the SFT-only variant on GDPval tasks that both models complete successfully. The comparison shows that RL wins more often than SFT on both office and non-office tasks, indicating that rubric-based RL improves not only completion rate but also the quality of deliverables. Mitigating Shortcut Behavior Drives Improvement. We define effective steps as tool-call steps that do not result in errors, which to some extent reflect exploration and completion depth. In Figure 5 , we observe that effective steps positively correlate with GDPval Pass@1. Across model scales, agents with few effective steps tend to stop before sufficient grounding or verification, producing plausible artifacts without adequate evidence extraction or computation. We refer to this failure mode as shortcut behavior. Agent-R 2 mitigates this failure mode through two-stage SFT and rubric-based RL on the synthesized 4k data; in contrast, the 4B base model shows shallow interaction and low performance. Case Study on Mitigating Shortcut Behavior. Figure 6 illustrates how shortcut behavior appears in a materials-test report task. The base Qwen3-4B model fails to locate the target spreadsheet values, but still writes a report with dummy evidence, producing a superficially complete deliverable. Recent benchmarks increasingly move beyond static question answering toward realistic, long-horizon work completion. GDPval ( Patwardhan et al. 2025 ) , $ 1 \$1 M-Bench ( Yang et al. 2026 ) , and Agents’ Last Exam ( Sun et al. 2026 ) evaluate economically valuable work through expert-designed occupational and high-stakes tasks. Finance Agent Benchmark ( Bigeard et al. 2025 ) focuses on finance research tasks grounded in SEC filings and expert rubrics. APEX–Agents ( Vidgen et al. 2026 ) and AgentSkillOS ( Li et al. 2026 ) study evaluations centered on tool use and skill orchestration, where agents must select and coordinate external capabilities to complete tasks. ClawBench ( Zhang et al. 2026b ) and EdgeBench ( Zhu et al. 2026 ) study agents in real-world interactive environments, emphasizing live operations, sustained exploration, and feedback-driven progress. Workspace-Bench ( Tang et al. 2026 ) and EnterpriseClawBench ( Zhong et al. 2026 ) focus on workspace and enterprise settings, where agents must reason over heterogeneous files, business artifacts, and workplace-specific context. Data Synthesis for Agent Post-Training. Synthesizing post-training data has become an important direction for improving agent capabilities. Existing work scales tool-use trajectories and executable environments from corpora, programs, or workflow templates ( Xu et al. 2026b ; Song et al. 2026 ; Xu et al. 2026a ; Dong et al. 2026 ) , or replay web tutorials as verified GUI trajectories ( Xu et al. 2025b ) . These methods are effective for scaling tool-use data, but they mainly target specific tools with predefined interfaces rather than more general tools such as terminals, which offer broader applicability for realistic productivity work. Recent data synthesis for terminal agents improves verifiability by generating command-line environments, tasks, or skills ( Gandhi et al. 2026 ; Cheng et al. 2026 ; Hua et al. 2026 ) . However, these settings mainly focus on structured text files and rarely involve structurally complex office files commonly used in professional productivity work. Studies based on office files mainly focus on document-grounded question answering and analysis ( Zhang et al. 2026a ; Qiao et al. 2026 ; Zhang et al. 2025 ; Chi et al. 2026 ) or single-type file generation ( Zheng et al. 2025 ; Zheng et al. 2026 ) . In contrast, Agent-R 2 grounds task synthesis in real occupational workflows and authentic multi-format attachments, producing deliverable-oriented tasks together with fine-grained rubrics. 6 Conclusion We introduced Agent-R 2 , a real-source data synthesis framework that scales agents for realistic productivity work. Agent-R 2 constructs training instances grounded in real workflows and authentic files with fine-grained rubrics. Using 4k synthesized instances, we train Agent-R 2 -4B with SFT and rubric-based RL. Experiments on GDPval and OfficeQA show that Agent-R 2 -4B improves task completion and file-grounded reasoning, outperforming several larger open-source baselines. Behavioral analysis further shows that Agent-R 2 -4B reduces shortcut behavior and improves completion reliability in complex productivity tasks. Appendix C Metric Definitions for Shortcut Analysis The following definitions are used for the trajectory- and deliverable-level analysis in Section 4.4 . All statistics are computed automatically with rule-based matching on trajectories and deliverables. • Error. A tool call whose output contains a genuine error signature, such as a traceback, an error message, or a missing file. Output on stderr without an error signature is not counted. • Phases. The exploration phase precedes the first successful deliverable write; the build phase spans the first to the last successful write; the verification phase follows the last successful write. • Numerical-error fix rate. The percentage of numerical/data-logic error episodes that are subsequently fixed under the criterion above. This category includes indexing, value, type, arithmetic, and related computation exceptions. • Writing despite an unfixed error. A deliverable is written before an error is fixed, with at most one intervening successful step. The reported rate is the percentage of build-phase error episodes that satisfy this condition. • Re-checking the deliverable. The agent reopens or inspects the deliverable after a successful write. The rate is measured over tasks with a successful deliverable-writing operation. • Placeholder text. The deliverable contains placeholder strings such as “TBD”, “[insert …]”, “lorem”, or “placeholder”. The rate is measured over tasks with reference attachments and an output deliverable. • Error masking. The code that writes the deliverable catches exceptions and falls back to a default value or pass . We report the percentage of tasks with a successful deliverable-writing operation whose writer code matches this pattern. • Traceable numbers. The share of distinct numbers in the output deliverables that can be found in the prompt or the attachments. We compute this share for each task with reference attachments and at least five distinct output numbers, then report the mean across eligible tasks. Appendix D Decontamination Audit Our training materials are collected from web corpora and online search, so we audit the synthesized tasks and their attachments for overlap with the evaluation benchmarks at four levels. File hashes and source URLs. Neither the SHA-256 hashes of our attachment files nor the source URLs we recovered overlap with any file in GDPval or OfficeQA. Source domains. Only 0.6% of our source domains appear in GDPval instructions, mostly broad public sources such as irs.gov . Near-duplicate documents. For each attachment, we compute the nearest-neighbor 5-gram MinHash Jaccard similarity to the benchmark documents. The average is 0.005 for GDPval (maximum 0.125) and 0.009 for OfficeQA (maximum 0.023). For reference, two different issues of the same U.S. Treasury Bulletin have a similarity of 0.36. Instruction-level similarity. For each benchmark prompt, we compute the embedding cosine similarity to its nearest training instruction. The average is 0.43 for GDPval and 0.36 for OfficeQA. For GDPval, this is below the similarity between a GDPval prompt and its nearest other GDPval prompt (0.46), and far below the similarity of a paraphrase (0.93). Most rubric numbers therefore cannot be checked from the instruction alone, so satisfying the rubric requires reading and processing the attached files.
