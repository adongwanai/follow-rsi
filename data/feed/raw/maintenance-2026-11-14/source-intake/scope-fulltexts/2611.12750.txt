Nikhil Sharma † Email: nsharm27@jhu.edu Kelly Marchisio ‡ Email: kelly@cohere.com Kenton Murray Email: kenton@jhu.edu Ziang Xiao † Email: ziang.xiao@jhu.edu † Johns Hopkins University ‡ Cohere       George Mason University Abstract Large language models (LLMs) increasingly mediate how people seek and consume information, including accounts that differ across languages and regions, especially on contested topics. Supporting diverse information seeking requires models to help users navigate competing narratives across languages and information streams. We define six capabilities necessary for diverse multilingual information seeking: three foundational capabilities for narrative inclusion, narrative exclusion, and missing narrative identification; and three higher-level capabilities for presenting diverse narratives, preserving narrative coverage under confirmatory queries, and maintaining information parity across languages. To guide the development of those information seeking capabilities, we introduce NarrativeBench , a benchmark comprising 1,333 news articles in 23 publication languages across 23 contested topics and 460 Reddit-grounded queries. Evaluating 14 LLMs across 24 query languages, we find that current models lack the capabilities needed to reliably navigate diverse narratives, limiting users’ control over the perspectives they receive and leaving substantial gaps in exposure to competing accounts even when diversity is explicitly requested. Information access also differs across query framings and languages, risking echo chambers, linguistic filter bubbles, and information disparities that weaken the common ground needed for democratic discourse. NarrativeBench provides a foundation for developing information systems that give users across languages and regions agency to navigate diverse narratives. Dataset: https://huggingface.co/datasets/jhu-clsp/narrative-bench Code: https://github.com/isle-dev/Narrative-Bench-NeurIPS-2026 1 Introduction We, informavores [ 36 ] , seek and consume information continually to find what we need to know, decide, or act on.
Despite information today being more accessible, navigation has grown harder. New information is produced at a scale and speed no information seeker can track, and what exists is fragmented across geographic and linguistic silos, each with its own vocabulary, claims, framings, and emphases.
Throughout history, we have built information systems, from libraries to search engines and social media feeds, to help us navigate the complex information world.
However, these systems often reproduce, and sometimes amplify, the very problems they were meant to solve.
By organizing content around a user’s query language, region, and prior behavior, they create filter bubbles [ 45 , 46 , 26 ] and echo chambers [ 11 , 1 ] , increasing polarization and weakening the common ground necessary for democratic stability [ 16 ] .
For an information seeker, overcoming these barriers is costly: it requires knowing which foreign perspectives to search for, translating conflicting sources, and identifying missing vocabulary and information silos. Large Language Models (LLMs) hold promise as a new kind of information system.
In principle, their multilingual, conversational, and reasoning capabilities should let a user ask a question in any language from any region and receive an answer that synthesizes across the perspectives available, bridging cultural divides and improving democratic deliberation [ 57 ] .
But are LLMs actually helping us navigate this complex information world?
A growing body of research auditing linguistic, political, and cultural biases in LLMs suggests they may instead reproduce and exacerbate the barriers they are meant to overcome.
Those models create generative echo chambers by reinforcing user beliefs [ 52 , 15 , 40 , 51 , 10 , 22 ] , prefer information in the query language over more relevant foreign sources [ 53 , 27 ] , and struggle to reason when evidence contains explicit conflicts [ 56 , 54 , 67 , 53 ] . Whether LLMs can serve as information systems that break, rather than reinforce, the traditional barriers of information seeking remains an open question. In this paper, we identify three foundational information seeking capabilities that give users control over which narratives an LLM presents: requesting a particular narrative, excluding a narrative, and identifying perspectives missing from an answer. Building on the foundational capabilities, we evaluate three higher-level information seeking capabilities: presenting diverse narratives, preserving narrative coverage under confirmatory queries, and maintaining information parity across languages.
We evaluate the capabilities across contested topics, including geopolitical conflicts where the stakes of navigation failure are high.
Recent global conflicts underscore the urgency: Israel–Palestine, India–Pakistan, and Russia–Ukraine are not only fought through military and diplomatic action, but also through parallel information environments in which different regional and linguistic communities encounter different accounts of the same events [ 26 , 55 , 17 ] .
Information flows in such geopolitical conflicts consist of culturally situated accounts with differing causal and moral interpretations of the same event [ 14 , 35 ] , making information seeking challenging.
Information seekers in such environments need to navigate and parse diverging information streams across languages, making translation alone insufficient.
We formulate this challenge as the problem of navigating diverse multilingual narratives.
In this paper, narratives are defined as culturally situated accounts of events that interpret the actions and intentions of those involved [ 6 ] , and operationalized as accounts of a topic expressed in a particular language by sources anchored in a geographic region.
However, we acknowledge that narratives vary even within the same geographic region and language. Our operationalization simplifies this complexity to provide an auditable unit for measuring how information systems allow users to navigate diverse information streams.
We argue that an information system’s ability to navigate narratives as operationalized in this paper is a necessary but not sufficient condition for enabling diverse multilingual information seeking.
Future work should extend this operationalization to capture narrative differences within regions and languages. To evaluate the foundational and higher-level information seeking capabilities, we introduce NarrativeBench , an ecologically grounded dataset and benchmark comprising 1,333 news articles in 23 publication languages across 23 contested topics and 460 Reddit-grounded queries. The evaluation covers 4,800 cases across 24 query languages (Appendix B.1 ). We find that current multilingual LLMs (1) struggle to distinguish and selectively present different perspectives, (2) fail to consistently present diverse views across languages, (3) vary in narrative coverage between neutral and confirmatory queries, and (4) perform unevenly across languages. Such capability gaps decrease exposure to diverse narratives and hinder effective information seeking, potentially exacerbating linguistic filter bubbles and echo chambers, reducing common ground across regions, and amplifying existing conflicts and divisions in societies. Overall, this paper makes the following contributions: • We formulate diverse multilingual information seeking as a problem of navigating narratives and identify foundational and higher-level capabilities LLMs need to help users navigate diverse perspectives across languages and regions. • We introduce NarrativeBench , an ecologically grounded dataset and benchmark to evaluate the information seeking capabilities of multilingual LLMs. • We provide an empirical analysis of state-of-the-art LLMs, identifying capability gaps that hinder diverse information seeking across languages and regions. NarrativeBench provides a capability-grounded foundation for building information systems that promote equitable information access, reduce polarization, and enhance democratic discourse. 2 Related Work 2.1 Diverse Information Seeking and Perspective Exposure Information seeking depends on the information available and the perspectives users encounter, select, and trust. Research on selective exposure and partisan news consumption shows that some audiences concentrate their attention on politically aligned sources [ 2 , 38 ] ; strong political beliefs and the perceived strength of an issue can further increase susceptibility to confirmation bias during news consumption [ 5 ] . Efforts to broaden perspective exposure include interfaces that help users explore news and opinions across political positions [ 24 ] , but explicitly labeling the stance of news articles can intensify users’ selection of viewpoints aligned with their beliefs [ 18 ] . System choices about information exposure can also influence political attitudes: reducing the prominence of posts expressing antidemocratic attitudes and partisan animosity in news feeds can reduce affective polarization [ 48 ] .
Experiments with LLM-mediated search show that users exhibit greater confirmation bias in their queries than with conventional search [ 52 ] . Biased model responses can further influence users’ judgments by reinforcing existing beliefs or shifting attitudes toward the model’s position [ 10 , 23 ] . Supporting diverse information seeking therefore requires LLMs to surface relevant missing perspectives even when users ask confirmatory questions and do not explicitly request alternative views. 2.2 LLM-Mediated Information Access and Pluralism LLMs are increasingly used for information seeking and decision making [ 8 ] . LLM-mediated systems shape access to perspectives by selecting sources, synthesizing claims, and deciding which information to include in their responses. Research on AI search and news consumption shows that citations can concentrate on a small set of outlets [ 68 ] and identifies concerns about accuracy, transparency, and shared understanding [ 43 , 30 ] . Existing information-seeking benchmarks evaluate the accuracy and usefulness of retrieved evidence, the completeness of information collection, and reasoning and clarification in expert consultations [ 64 , 62 , 28 , 69 , 13 ] . Complementary work examines how models identify and distinguish perspectives, represent narrative frames, and accommodate diverse human preferences [ 9 , 42 , 29 , 44 ] . Evaluating perspective diversity in information seeking requires examining how query language and the available evidence shape the perspectives presented in model responses. Li et al. [31] show that LLMs answer territorial-dispute questions differently depending on query language, and knowledge-conflict work studies how models behave when context conflicts with parametric memory or when retrieved passages contradict one another [ 67 , 21 , 63 , 65 ] . The focus on language-dependent positions and conflicting facts leaves open how models represent competing narratives that emphasize different facts and interpretations of the same event across languages and regions. 2.3 Multilingual LLMs and Information Capabilities Navigating diverse narratives requires information capabilities that enable access to perspectives across languages. Multilingual LLMs offer a foundation for cross-cultural information seeking, yet current evaluations find inconsistent answers to semantically equivalent questions and performance differences across languages, scripts, and resource levels [ 60 , 3 , 66 , 20 , 7 ] . Culturally grounded benchmarks evaluate everyday knowledge [ 39 ] and knowledge and reasoning through local examination questions [ 50 ] . Multilingual question-answering evaluations use information-seeking questions collected directly in diverse languages to assess passage selection and answer extraction [ 12 ] . Access to diverse narratives across languages also depends on which sources models select and use in their responses. In multilingual retrieval-augmented generation, where models answer questions using retrieved sources, Sharma et al. [53] find preferences for documents in the query language during retrieval and generation, and for higher-resource languages during generation when query-language evidence is unavailable. Related work identifies preferences for high-resource languages, English, and Latin scripts [ 47 , 61 ] ; models can also favor a source’s language over its relevance [ 27 ] . Multilingual benchmarks evaluate answer generation using Wikipedia sources [ 58 ] , while healthcare audits examine language disparities in factual correctness and response quality [ 19 , 25 ] . Language preferences can therefore shape which narratives remain available to users even when the evidence is multilingual, motivating evaluation of the diversity represented in the resulting answers. NarrativeBench builds on research in multilingual information access and conflicting evidence by evaluating whether models can selectively present and recover narratives, broaden exposure under confirmatory queries, and maintain comparable information access across languages. 3 NarrativeBench : The Dataset Gathering the Narratives We construct NarrativeBench by collecting news articles through localized searches about contested topics. Dataset curation followed four stages : (1) selecting contested topics across regions and languages, (2) identifying regions and stakeholders relevant to each topic, (3) developing region-specific search terms in the relevant languages, and (4) collecting relevant articles and their URLs from the search results. The corpus contains 1,333 news articles from 43 countries, regions, and regional organizations across 23 topics and 23 publication languages. Within each topic, we group articles by source perspective and publication language, yielding 180 narrative groups. Article texts have a median length of 4,654 characters (mean = 5,977, SD = 5,283, range = 610–46,252). We describe the article collection in Appendix A . Dataset Diagnostics To assess whether the collection captures distinct narratives, we compared multilingual embeddings of all 1,333 article texts in their publication languages, restricting comparisons to articles about the same topic. Articles within the same narrative group were more similar than articles from different groups (mean cosine similarity difference = 0.047, 95% bootstrap CI [0.044, 0.051], permutation p < 0.001 p<0.001 ). After restricting comparisons to articles published in the same language, articles from the same source perspective remained more similar on average than articles from different perspectives (Appendix A.2 ). Sourcing Queries for Evaluation To ground evaluation in naturally occurring information seeking, we assembled 460 queries, comprising 283 questions gathered from Reddit and 177 context-specific adaptations. Reddit queries were gathered through topic-specific searches; adaptations addressed 12 topics with limited query coverage and were reviewed for fidelity to the source question, topic, and intended framing. Two independent annotators rated a sample of 100 English queries on how likely a person would be to ask the question to an AI assistant, giving mean ratings of 4.09 and 4.89 out of 5; neither annotator rated a query as unlikely or very unlikely. To test whether models preserve access to diverse narratives when queries favor an existing belief, we include 230 neutral and 230 confirmatory queries. Neutral queries request information without endorsing a particular answer, while confirmatory queries seek support for an expressed or implied belief, interpretation, solution, or outcome. Comparing narrative coverage across query categories tests the robustness needed to limit reinforcement of echo chambers during information seeking [ 52 , 41 ] . Query Translation To compare information capabilities across query languages, we provide query versions in all 23 publication languages and in Thai. We prepared the translations using Google Translate. The dataset provides 11,038 query–language versions across 24 languages; Appendix A.4 lists the query and publication languages. We assessed translation quality on a subset covering eight target languages. For 1,704 English-to-target translations, mean WMT22-COMETKiwi-DA scores [ 49 ] ranged from 0.853 to 0.879 across the eight languages. Independent annotators also reviewed 40 query–translation pairs selected across languages and COMETKiwi score bands, providing 132 ratings. Annotators found that translations preserved meaning in 90.8% of the ratings they could assess (118/130) and that the queries were likely to be asked by a person in 89.0% (113/127). 4 NarrativeBench : An Information Seeking Benchmark 4.1 Evaluating Information Seeking Capabilities NarrativeBench is a capability-grounded benchmark for developing models that help people navigate diverse information flows. Following the Evidence-Centered Benchmark Design framework of Liu et al. [33] , we connect challenges in information seeking to three foundational and three higher-level capabilities, then define tasks and measures that make the required behavior evaluable. Foundational Information Seeking Capabilities ( ): Can models include, exclude, and identify missing narratives? To enable diverse information seeking, models must provide users with affordances for navigating diverse multilingual narratives. A user should be able to request a specific narrative, exclude a specific narrative, or ask which perspectives are missing from a prior answer. We evaluate the foundational capabilities through three tasks: FC-1: Narrative Inclusion (NI) requires models to present a designated narrative when users request a particular perspective. The task asks the model to include the target narrative and exclude other narratives; expected responses cite evidence only from the target narrative. FC-2: Narrative Exclusion (NE) requires models to exclude a specified narrative when users seek alternative perspectives. The task asks the model to answer while excluding the target narrative; expected responses cite evidence from the remaining relevant narratives and avoid citing evidence from the target narrative. FC-3: Missing Narrative Identification (MNI) requires models to identify perspectives omitted from a prior answer. The task presents an answer focused on a narrative and asks the model to revise it by incorporating omitted narratives; expected responses cite evidence from those narratives. Higher-Level Information Seeking Capabilities Building on the foundational capabilities, models must broaden access to narratives across filter bubbles, echo chambers, and linguistic disparities. HC-1 ( ): Can models present diverse narratives from multilingual evidence? To broaden exposure beyond linguistic filter bubbles [ 53 ] , models must present narratives from diverse multilingual sources. To test narrative diversity in model responses, we compare two conditions: a Baseline Prompt (BP) asks the model to answer the query using the provided evidence; a Diverse Prompt (DP) additionally requests the diverse narratives present in the document context. Narrative coverage under both conditions and the change following an explicit diversity request show how consistently models broaden the perspectives available to users. HC-2 ( ): Do models preserve narrative coverage under confirmatory queries? To limit reinforcement of confirmation bias and echo chambers [ 52 ] , models must maintain access to relevant narratives when queries favor a particular viewpoint. We test robustness to confirmatory framing by comparing narrative coverage under neutral and confirmatory queries in the BP and DP conditions. HC-3 ( ): Do models provide comparable access to narratives across languages? Information seekers should have comparable access to relevant narratives regardless of their query language or the languages of the available sources. We operationalize information parity along two axes: performance across the languages in which users ask, and performance across the languages in which narratives are expressed. Comparable access requires both strong narrative coverage and small performance differences across query and narrative languages. 4.2 Evaluation Metrics and Protocol Setup Each evaluation case contains a document context, a user query, and a task instruction. The document context contains three articles from each selected narrative, defined by source perspective and publication language; the same articles are used for each question across tasks and query languages. Models answer in the query language and cite document IDs supporting their claims. For MNI, every model receives the same prior answer focused on one narrative and revises it to include missing perspectives. Appendix B.1 details case construction, localized instructions, and prior answers. Evaluating the Presence of a Narrative We identify represented narratives through citations to the provided articles: a narrative is present when the response cites at least one article associated with that narrative. Citations also allow users to inspect the evidence and explore unfamiliar information streams. Article IDs, such as [D001] , reveal no source-region or language information. We assess the use of citations as a metric through a human audit of citation support (Appendix C.1 ). Narrative Precision, Recall, and F1 We compare the cited narratives, C i C_{i} , with the required narratives, G i G_{i} , for each case. The required set contains the target narrative for NI, all context narratives except the target for NE, the narratives omitted from the prior answer for MNI, and all context narratives for BP and DP. Precision measures whether cited narratives belong to the required set; recall measures coverage of required narratives; F1 summarizes both. NI and NE use F1 to measure users’ control over narrative selection, requiring coverage of requested narratives while penalizing citations to narratives the user asked to omit. MNI uses recall to assess recovery of omitted narratives; BP and DP use recall to assess how broadly an answer covers the available perspectives. HC-1: Narrative Coverage and Robustness We measure recall under BP and DP and their paired difference, Δ DP − BP = R DP − R BP \Delta_{\mathrm{DP-BP}}=R_{\mathrm{DP}}-R_{\mathrm{BP}} , holding the question, query language, and document context fixed. The comparison captures the breadth of narratives users receive by default and the additional coverage gained by explicitly asking for diversity. HC-2: Robustness to Confirmatory Queries We pool recall across BP and DP, measure the absolute difference between neutral and confirmatory queries for each configuration, then average the gaps across configurations. The gap measures how consistently users can access diverse narratives when seeking confirmation of a preferred viewpoint. We also compare the gaps across query languages and across the five tasks using each task’s primary score. Appendix B.4 examines whether coverage is higher or lower under confirmatory queries after accounting for topic, query language and prompt. HC-3: Language Disparity We measure disparity along two axes to distinguish differences associated with the language users ask in from differences associated with the languages of the narratives they seek. For each configuration, we compare mean primary scores in each language with English, Δ ℓ = M ℓ − M en \Delta_{\ell}=M_{\ell}-M_{\mathrm{en}} . Query-language comparisons pool all five tasks. Target narrative-language comparisons pool NI and NE by the target narrative’s publication language. We average the absolute gaps equally across non-English languages for each configuration and then average the disparities across the 15 configurations. Source-language omission Omission rates identify source languages that disappear from responses even when evidence in those languages is available. In BP and DP, each publication language present in the context counts as one opportunity per response. An omission occurs when the response cites no valid in-context document in that language. We divide omissions by opportunities separately for each prompt. To compare matching and other query languages, we pair versions of the same question with identical article contexts and average its other-language versions before comparing omission rates (Appendix B.5 ). This diagnostic uses citations independently of response-language validation. Language Validation (LVal) To measure narrative access in the language requested by the user, we compare each response’s language with the query language and assign a score of zero when they differ; responses in the requested language proceed to narrative scoring (Appendix B.3 ). 5 Results: How well can LLMs enable diverse information seeking across multilingual narratives? Conversational systems offer accessible ways to seek information, yet our results show that users cannot reliably direct which narratives models present or recover perspectives that an answer omits. Information seekers revise their questions as they encounter new evidence [ 4 ] ; users who know which perspective to pursue need the model to isolate it from the surrounding evidence, while users who do not know which perspectives exist need the model to identify omissions. Explicit diversity requests improve coverage, but even the strongest configuration provides cited coverage in the requested language for only about three quarters of available narratives. A response that appears to follow the user’s instruction can consequently offer less control over the information presented than the interface suggests, creating an illusion of control for users who cannot see the missing perspectives [ 59 , 37 ] . Model development must target information capabilities. Model development should treat the information seeking capabilities defined in this paper as explicit training and evaluation objectives. The uneven gains from model size, instruction tuning, and reasoning settings suggest that improvements in narrative coverage do not consistently translate into greater control over which narratives users receive. Training should therefore reward responses that follow requests to include, exclude, and recover narratives while covering the relevant evidence. Evaluation should examine whether gains in each capability hold across neutral and confirmatory queries and across languages, connecting model improvements to the information access and control users need. Response policies also shape information access. Information access depends on whether a system will answer a query as well as which narratives it presents: the Kimi K2.6 service rejected 552 of 4,800 requests as high risk, with rejections concentrated in topics including India–China tensions, Taiwan, and student protests in India. People relying on different services may therefore face different restrictions on investigating the same topic, making comparable information access across providers necessary for building common ground. Information parity is necessary for common ground. Even with multilingual sources supplied and an explicit request for diversity, omission rates for Tigrinya, Nepali, and Armenian sources were about 55–60 percentage points higher than for English when those languages were available (Figure 1 E); such gaps can leave people discussing the same issue with access to different accounts. AI mediation can help people find common ground by incorporating participants’ critiques into shared statements [ 57 ] ; meaningful deliberation also requires access to the competing accounts from which people form their judgments. Information systems that repeatedly omit regional and linguistic perspectives risk narrowing that shared evidence and weakening the common ground needed for democratic discourse [ 16 ] . Policy and procurement standards should require reporting of narrative-access disparities across languages and topics, while open models and shared evaluation resources can give communities a practical basis for inspecting and improving information access. Beyond contested public topics. Scientific research also depends on navigating diverse narratives, as disciplines may study the same phenomenon using different terms, methods, and standards of evidence. As LLM use in scientific writing grows [ 32 ] and extends to literature discovery and synthesis [ 34 ] , researchers need to request evidence from a particular discipline, set aside an approach they have already reviewed, or discover a line of work a synthesis overlooked. A research assistant that draws mainly on the literature most accessible to the model can make a field appear more settled or uniform than the available evidence warrants. Policy development and large organizations similarly depend on navigating narratives distributed across disciplines, teams, and regions. Technical teams, regulators, and affected communities may explain the same policy issue differently, while regional teams may hold evidence absent from headquarters’ records. Information systems need to help decision-makers inspect each account, identify missing perspectives, and trace competing claims to their sources. Adapting NarrativeBench to those settings would allow researchers to evaluate whether a synthesis preserves the perspectives and evidence needed for an informed decision. 7 Limitations and Future Directions Narrative representation. We group articles by source perspective and publication language to evaluate access to diverse narratives, while accounts within a region or language can differ in the events, causes, and consequences they emphasize. Extending narrative representation to capture disagreement within each narrative group would support a fuller evaluation of how models navigate competing accounts. Corpus representativeness and multimodal sources. Our corpus reflects the contested topics, regional sources, and article texts accessible through localized searches. However, information seeking is more complex, it happens across modalities and information mediums. Broader sampling across media formats, regions, and less accessible information streams would help establish how the observed capability gaps vary with the narratives and evidence available to a system. Source quality and misinformation. Contested reporting can contain propaganda, misinformation, and selective framing, so systems must combine access to diverse narratives with source assessment and verification of contested claims. Future evaluations should examine how models preserve competing perspectives while distinguishing reliable evidence from unsupported or misleading accounts. NarrativeBench may contains narratives which could be considered as misinformation in some regions; The authors of the paper do not endorse the narratives as factual accounts of events. Citation coverage and claim support. We measure narrative representation through citations to supplied articles, and our citation-support audit examines whether cited sources support the associated claims in a sample of responses (Appendix C.1 ). Extending assessment across responses and languages, including how faithfully responses convey the causes and interpretations each account emphasizes, would connect narrative coverage to the reliability of the information users receive. However, this is not trivial, LLM accounts can cause information loss, where the meaning, strength of argument and emotion might change. Measuring these information losses is a complex topic that requires interpreting the downstream effect of a give narrative. Human evaluation of higher-level capabilities. Evaluating the effects of the higher-level capabilities on filter bubbles, echo chambers, and linguistic disparities requires human-subject studies of information seeking. For instance, even when models present competing accounts, users may selectively attend to perspectives that support existing beliefs [ 41 ] . Future studies should examine whether improvements in narrative coverage, robustness to confirmatory queries, and information parity broaden the perspectives of users from different regions and languages consider and mitigate the phenomenons of echo chambers, filter bubbles and cultural barriers to develop common ground. Information seeking across multiple turns. NarrativeBench evaluates responses to fixed queries and instructions, including a follow-up request to recover narratives omitted from a supplied answer. During a conversation, users revise their goals, introduce evidence, and change which perspectives they want to explore. Multi-turn evaluation should test how models develop the narratives and support narrative consumption across evolving intents and instructions. Information consumption. Effective information seeking requires people to understand and use the information models provide. Benchmark evaluations should incorporate user studies to examine how the organization, detail, and pacing of narrative presentation affect users’ ability to compare accounts and investigate their sources. Accounting for differences in prior knowledge, language proficiency, and available attention would help develop systems that make diverse information both accessible and usable.
