Given a content image and a style reference, PREEN predicts a preset, a vector of editor slider values that is interpretable and editor-consumable, not a raster image. The key obstacle is supervision, since an arbitrary real reference lacks a preset ground truth. We therefore supervise with a style-transfer teacher, whose target looks we invert into the editor’s parameter space as pseudo-presets, then pretrain a CLIP-conditioned regressor and refine it with LoRA under an image-level CIELAB loss. On the human-curated PST50 benchmark, PREEN applies the reference’s color while preserving structural fidelity, leading the baselines on pixel- and perceptual-fidelity metrics. The preset is compact and predicted at sub-second on-device latency. The low-dimensional subspace also exposes a single inference-time strength knob that traces a continuous default-to-vivid trajectory in one forward pass, with every intermediate state a valid editor preset. A two-axis human study shows that raters prefer the vivid end for matching the reference but the default as a standalone photo, a trade-off the knob spans without retraining. 1 Introduction Input Reference PREEN Figure 1 : PREEN restyles an input toward a reference via a predicted 29 29 -parameter editor preset (shown at the Vivid strength setting). Exemplar-based color style transfer, also studied as photorealistic style transfer (PST), is a routine operation in modern photo editing. A user picks a reference image Y Y whose look they admire, wanting their own shot X X recolored to match it (Figure 1 ). Mainstream academic approaches are pixel-generative, from neural style transfer [ 13 ] to diffusion editing [ 23 ] . Whatever their quality, they emit rasters with no exposed editing parameters, and the diffusion-based ones add multi-second latency and stochastic sampling that resists deterministic re-editing, an awkward fit for on-device, user-in-the-loop consumer pipelines. An alternative is photo preset transfer [ 6 ] , predicting the parameters P P of a deterministic image editor E E from the pair ( X , Y ) (X,Y) such that E ⁡ ( X , P ) E(X,P) approximates the look of Y Y . The output P P , a vector of slider values, is interpretable and compact. Unlike a rendered image, it can be stored and re-edited, with no generative model at inference time. The central challenge is supervision. Parametric retouching benchmarks such as MIT-Adobe FiveK [ 2 ] and PPR10K [ 19 ] provide human-authored target edits, but operate in the auto-enhancement regime without a separate style reference. No large-scale dataset pairs a content image with a reference and a matching expert-graded target. The principal prior method [ 6 ] sidesteps this by sampling presets, thereby bounding the styles it learns; the only reference-conditioned set with expert-graded targets we know of [ 4 ] offers just 50 pairs (PST50) for evaluation. We close this gap with teacher supervision. We propose PREEN (Preset Regression via an Exemplar-based Editor Network). At inference, it maps a content–reference pair ( X , Y ) (X,Y) to a 29 29 -parameter preset that a smartphone editor applies to produce the result (Figure 2 ). A two-stage pipeline trains it. Phase 1 pretrains a CLIP-conditioned regressor on a large corpus of pseudo-labeled triples recovered from a style-transfer teacher. Phase 2 freezes all base weights and trains only low-rank adapters under an image-level CIELAB loss evaluated on the differentiable editor output, addressing the perceptual non-uniformity of Phase 1’s parameter-space loss. Our contributions are: (i) a teacher-supervised recipe that frees reference-based preset regression from sampled-preset supervision, substituting a style-transfer teacher on real references; (ii) a strength-interpolation primitive, an inference-time scalar α \alpha on the chromaticity parameters that traces a continuous default-to-vivid trajectory without re-running inference; and (iii) a two-axis human study surfacing a three-way fidelity / style-match / aesthetic divergence across the α \alpha trajectory. 2 Related Work 2.1 Photo Preset Transfer Editing photos by predicting the parameters of interpretable white-box operations has a long history. Learning-based automatic retouching predicts sequences of editing operations [ 12 ] , and the same idea recurs in compositing harmonization [ 15 ] . Both regress parameters toward a fixed goal, enhancement or in-image harmonization, rather than a separate style reference. Deep Preset [ 6 ] established the reference-conditioned setting we adopt, regressing an editor preset (69 develop controls) from a content–reference pair, but its supervision is synthetic by construction . Training references are images re-rendered with sampled presets, so a ground-truth preset always exists, but the styles it can learn are confined to that sampled set. The setting has remained underexplored since then; the closest later work, NCST [ 14 ] , regresses a smaller global-only parameter set from a reference to perform video grading. We revisit it without sampled-preset supervision: a style-transfer teacher on real reference pairs supplies targets we invert into the editor’s parameter space as pseudo-presets (Section 3 ). Image-adaptive 3D LUT [ 35 ] and NLUT [ 3 ] predict one such grid applied uniformly across the image, and AceTone [ 22 ] predicts one from a reference with a vision-language model. Spatially aware variants blend multiple LUTs per pixel [ 33 ] , and SA-LUT [ 4 ] extends these to a spatially adaptive 4D LUT, which we adopt as our teacher. SA-LUT also contributes the human-curated PST50 benchmark used for our evaluation (Section 4.2 ). Either way, the output is a dense grid of 10 4 10^{4} – 10 5 10^{5} values (Table 1 ) rather than a few named sliders, leaving the edit hard for a user to inspect or refine. 2.3 Pixel-Generative Stylization A large body of work renders stylization through pixel transformations, from classic global color-statistic transfer [ 11 ] to AdaIN [ 13 ] , diffusion-based editing such as SDEdit [ 23 ] , and recent neural color-style methods including Neural Preset [ 7 ] , ModFlows [ 18 ] , and INRetouch [ 5 ] . Concurrent work pushes further into generative modeling. SemiNFT [ 34 ] casts reference preset transfer as a Diffusion-Transformer task refined with reinforcement learning for aesthetic perception. These methods emit a raster image directly, without exposing user-editable parameters. Neither the LUT nor the pixel-generative family yields an editable preset; we compare experimentally against both families and against Deep Preset, which targets the same task. 3 Method 3.1 Problem Formulation Let E : 𝒳 × 𝒫 → 𝒳 E:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{X} denote a deterministic image editor mapping a content image X X and a parameter vector P ∈ [ − 1 , 1 ] d P\in[-1,1]^{d} to an edited image E ⁡ ( X , P ) E(X,P) . We target a d = 29 d=29 editor representative of consumer mobile photo applications: 8 8 global controls (temperature, tint, brightness, exposure, contrast, highlights, shadows, saturation) and 21 21 per-hue HSL channels (hue/saturation/luminance for each of 7 7 color zones: red, orange, yellow, green, cyan, blue, magenta). We split the 29 29 parameters into a tone set ℐ L \mathcal{I}_{L} ( 12 12 : contrast, brightness, exposure, highlights, shadows, per-hue luminance) and a chromaticity set ℐ ab \mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{ab}} ( 17 17 : temperature, tint, saturation, per-hue hue/saturation). Equivalently, the 8 8 global controls split 5 5 tone / 3 3 chroma and the 21 21 per-hue channels split 7 7 / 14 14 , a split reused by the regressor’s two output trunks (Section 3.3 ) and the α \alpha control (Section 3.5 ). We implement these controls, inspired by consumer smartphone photo editors, as a differentiable module (stage-by-stage pipeline in the supplementary), so both the Phase 1 inversion (Eq. 1 ) and the Phase 2 image-level loss can backpropagate through E E , the same module that produces every PREEN result we report. A LUT teacher is structure-preserving and dominated by color/tone transforms that lie close to our editor’s subspace, unlike a pixel-generative teacher’s edits. Phase 1 alone, a frozen-CLIP MLP regressor trained on pseudo-labels, already reaches 23.60 dB PSNR, above both Deep Preset forms. Phase 2 LoRA adaptation adds the largest gains, most prominently in chromaticity ( → 0.473 0.383\!\to\!0.473 HCorr). By contrast, Phase 2 only , a single stage from random initialization with no Phase 1 that fully fine-tunes the MLP under the image-level loss and adapts CLIP via LoRA as in PREEN, does not surpass the full pipeline on the task’s target metrics. Although it edges SSIM, it trails on HCorr ( 0.453 0.453 vs. 0.473 0.473 ) and LPIPS and reaches only a comparable Δ ​ E a ​ b ∗ \Delta E^{*}_{ab} ( 9.12 9.12 vs. 9.07 9.07 ) before peaking early and degrading, despite its larger fully fine-tuned capacity. The benefit of Phase 1’s initialization is thus not recovered by single-stage optimization; replacing that stage’s fine-tuning with LoRA fares worse still ( Δ ​ E a ​ b ∗ ​ 9.57 \Delta E^{*}_{ab}\,9.57 ; supplementary), consistent with a low-rank update on an untrained base being underexpressive and with Phase 1’s pretrained base enabling the efficient LoRA adaptation. Single-factor variants locate the gains: MLP-only LoRA (frozen CLIP) keeps fidelity but recovers under half the chromaticity gain (HCorr 0.423 0.423 ), so adaptation must reach the representation, not just the head; disabling the dual-trunk gradient routing (joint L L / a ​ b ab gradients) costs 0.38 0.38 dB PSNR and 0.016 0.016 SSIM at unchanged HCorr, confirming the routing aids tonal fidelity; and dropping identity samples collapses fidelity (SSIM 0.833 0.833 , PSNR below the no-edit floor). Removing the CIELAB terms (plain RGB MSE) instead gains pixel fidelity but cedes every color-facing metric (Table 2 ). A single RGB term underweights chroma, settling into damped color edits, whereas CIELAB lets us weight chroma above luminance ( w ab = 5 w_{\mathrm{ab}}\!=\!5 vs. w L = 1 w_{L}\!=\!1 , Eq. 2 ), committing to the color transfer the task demands. 4.4.2 Teacher Choice SA-LUT varies its edit across the image, whereas our editor applies one global preset, so Phase 1 must project spatially varying edits onto global parameters. To test whether a teacher matched to the editor yields cleaner pseudo-labels, we also tried a global variant that collapses SA-LUT’s per-pixel context map to a per-image mean. It brings no consistent improvement (Table 3 ). The variant edges PSNR by ∼ 0.1 {\sim}0.1 dB and SSIM by 0.003 0.003 but lowers HCorr ( → 0.465 0.473\!\to\!0.465 ) and worsens Δ ​ E a ​ b ∗ \Delta E^{*}_{ab} ( → 9.23 9.07\!\to\!9.23 ), the two metrics that score color most directly. The two teachers differ significantly on no metric (supplementary), so the published teacher can be used as is, without tailoring it to the editor. 4.4.3 Backbone Choice We probe the backbone by swapping the frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 encoder for three recent vision encoders at matched capacity and training budget (Table 4 ): the self-supervised DINOv3 [ 12 ] , the contrastive SigLIP 2 [ 13 ] , and the VLM-oriented InternViT [ 2 , 3 ] (checkpoints in the supplementary). Beyond controllability, the fidelity numbers invite a second reading. A global preset is structurally more constrained than the spatial LUT teacher, so PREEN’s higher PST50 fidelity scores (Table 1 ) do not indicate greater expressivity; they reflect PST50’s restrained expert targets. The teacher overshoots these conservative edits, while PREEN’s identity sampling (Table 2 ) and ℓ 2 \ell_{2} preset regularizer keep it calibrated. Damping the teacher toward the input confirms this, improving it on four of the five PST50 metrics (full diagnostic in the supplementary). PREEN also inherits the editor’s failure modes. Looks that remap color instead of adjusting it (split-toned monochromes, wholesale palette shifts) lie outside E E ’s space, as do local effects such as grain, vignettes, and local burns; for the former, even the per-image oracle fails (supplementary). It also assumes a semantically compatible reference: our pairs are matched by content, and we do not test transfer to disjoint references. Shrinking the CLIP-dominated footprint (Section 4.8 ) with a lighter encoder (e.g., MobileCLIP or FastViT) is left to future work. 6 Conclusion We presented PREEN, which recasts exemplar-based color style transfer as regression onto a deterministic editor’s 29 29 parameters. Lacking ground-truth presets, we invert a style-transfer teacher’s output into the editor’s parameter space, train a CLIP-conditioned regressor, and adapt it with LoRA under an image-level CIELAB loss. On PST50, the preset recovers the reference’s color shift while preserving structure; it is interpretable, ∼ 1000 × {\sim}1000\times more compact than the teacher’s LUT, and runs on-device in sub-second time. A single chromaticity scalar spans default and vivid looks, exposing strength as a free, re-editable control. Each study recruited 50 50 raters ( 150 150 in total), assigned in balanced groups targeting five independent ratings per image pair. Invalid responses were excluded before analysis. The seven pairings of Figure 6 of the main paper were collected in three studies. Study 1 compared the default against Deep Preset and against Claude. The left–right order was randomized per pair, image filenames were anonymized with content hashes, and methods were never named. The two questions, verbatim: “Q1.
