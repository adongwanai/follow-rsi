Mainstream MLLMs inject a fixed single-layer visual representation into the LLM and treat all LLM layers as a single consumer, ignoring that different layers demand different visual granularities. Recent multi-layer fusion methods either compress hierarchical features on the encoder side or rely on predefined sparse or hierarchical layer-to-layer connections, and therefore stop short of modeling adaptive cross-layer interactions between the ViT and LLM hierarchies. We address this gap with DGP (Dynamic Gated Pathways), a routing mechanism that establishes all-to-all connections between every ViT layer and every LLM layer, with each LLM layer dynamically gating its preference over visual layers conditioned on its own semantic state. Built on top of LLaVA-1.5-7B and 13B, DGP delivers consistent gains over the LLaVA-1.5-7B baseline (e.g., +4.2 on SQA I , +3.3 on MM-Vet, +3.1 on VizWiz and LLaVA W ) and surpasses prior multi-layer visual fusion methods on most benchmarks. Layer-wise routing analyses further show that different LLM layers do select different visual granularities on demand, supporting both the effectiveness and interpretability of the proposed dynamic gated pathways. In this paradigm, the effectiveness of visual information depends not only on the representational power of the encoder [ 38 , 7 , 34 ] , but—more critically—on where to connect : how the visual tokens are wired into the internal layers of the LLM. As illustrated in Figure 1 , attention patterns across ViT layers differ markedly: shallow layers attend to edges, textures, and local details, whereas deeper layers progressively concentrate on a small number of semantic anchors. Yet, most MLLMs [ 2 , 25 , 10 ] simply forward the output of one fixed ViT layer (typically the last or penultimate layer) to the LLM, as illustrated in Figure 2 (A), effectively collapsing the image into a single-granularity semantic representation. Connecting the LLM only to the terminal deep layer therefore discards shallow- and mid-level cues and prevents the LLM’s layer-wise semantics from interacting with the full visual hierarchy. Two lines of work have started to expose multi-layer visual features to the LLM, but each falls short on the where to connect question. Encoder-side fusion [ 39 , 4 , 20 , 23 , 36 ] merges multi-layer features into one representation before the LLM (Figure 2 (B)); any dynamism is consumed inside the encoder and cannot adapt to the differentiated demands of individual LLM layers. Predefined sparse or hierarchical connections [ 30 , 43 , 37 , 8 ] wire selected ViT layers to specific LLM layers (Figure 2 (C)), typically using hand-crafted connection patterns or fixed hierarchical mappings. CLI [ 5 ] introduces adaptive gating conditioned on the current LLM state, but operates over sampled ViT layers and designated LLM injection points. Neither category explicitly models the adaptive , dynamic cross-layer interactions between all ViT layers and all LLM layers that the question of where to connect demands. Predictive coding in visual neuroscience suggests that perception is not a unidirectional feed-forward process but a hierarchical feedback loop that continuously modulates activations across layers [ 33 ] . We take this as inspiration to revisit visual token injection: visual evidence should not be delivered in one shot through a fixed pathway, but should keep participating in the LLM’s representation updates through dynamic cross-layer pathways. Building on this view, we introduce DGP (Dynamic Gated Pathways), which connects all ViT layers to all LLM layers via learned, dynamically gated routes (Figure 2 (D)). Concretely, the outputs of different ViT layers act as candidate representations at varying granularities. At every LLM layer, DGP produces a routing distribution over all ViT layers from the current hidden state, aggregates the candidates via gated weighted fusion, and injects the result back into the layer’s visual hidden states through a residual connection. The hierarchical visual evidence keeps supplying cues for language reasoning, while the LLM’s layer-wise semantic state drives the preference over visual hierarchies, yielding a cross-layer, progressively evolving vision–language interaction that integrates low-level details and high-level semantics on demand at different decoding depths. We instantiate DGP on top of LLaVA-1.5 [ 25 ] and further validate it on Qwen3-VL-2B [ 3 ] and LLaVA-NeXT [ 26 ] . Across a wide range of multimodal benchmarks, DGP yields consistent improvements over baselines and outperforms prior multi-layer visual feature utilization methods. The learned routing patterns confirm that different LLM layers indeed prefer different visual granularities, alleviating the information bottleneck of fixed single-layer fusion. Our contributions are: • We reframe visual information injection in MLLMs around the question of where to connect , and show that fixed single-layer inputs and predefined sparse or hierarchical connections cannot match the differentiated demands of hierarchical ViT representations and layer-wise LLM semantics. • We propose DGP (Dynamic Gated Pathways), an all-to-all cross-layer connection mechanism in which every LLM layer dynamically gates its preference over every ViT layer conditioned on its current semantic state. • We demonstrate the effectiveness and interpretability of DGP on multiple multimodal benchmarks, where layer-wise routing analyses reveal the adaptive, layer-dependent selection behavior of the proposed dynamic gated pathways. 2 Related Work 2.1 Multimodal Large Language Model A typical Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM) couples a vision encoder, a vision–language adapter, and a large language model: the encoder turns the image into visual tokens, the adapter projects them into the language space, and the LLM consumes the joint visual–textual sequence to generate responses. Existing MLLMs mainly fall into two architectural families: projector-based decoder-only designs (e.g., LLaVA [ 25 ] , MiniGPT-4 [ 44 ] , Qwen-VL [ 2 ] , InternVL [ 10 ] ) and cross-attention-based designs (e.g., Flamingo [ 1 ] ). Current MLLMs use pretrained vision backbones such as CLIP-ViT [ 32 ] and SigLIP [ 42 ] , all of which expose representations of varying granularities across their layers. Yet most pipelines project only a single fixed layer into the LLM, so the language model sees only one level of visual abstraction. The visual connection mechanism is not merely a cross-modal interface: it determines whether the rich, hierarchical visual representations can be accessed and reused inside the LLM. 2.2 Connection Strategies for Visual Token Insertion Different ViT layers carry different semantic granularities: shallow layers retain local textures and spatial details, while middle and deep layers progressively encode object structures and high-level semantics [ 6 ] . A single deep layer is not always the best choice [ 6 ] , and exploiting the visual hierarchy depends as much on how layers are connected to the LLM as on which layers are selected [ 24 ] . The crux, in other words, is to establish effective connections between vision-encoder layers and LLM layers—i.e., the where to connect problem. Prior efforts can be grouped into two categories. Unified Encoder-side Connections. Methods in this category fuse multi-layer visual features on the encoder side and pass a single unified representation to the LLM. DenseConnector [ 39 ] concatenates features from different ViT layers along the token or channel dimension. MMFuser [ 4 ] uses deep-layer features as queries to pull fine-grained information from shallow layers. IGVA [ 20 ] and TGIF [ 23 ] adjust ViT-layer weights conditioned on the instruction. CCRA [ 36 ] combines several attention mechanisms for text-conditioned region–semantic alignment. While these designs improve multi-layer feature utilization, the entire fusion happens before the LLM; the LLM still receives one homogenized input and cannot reach back into the visual hierarchy as decoding progresses. Predefined Sparse or Hierarchical Connections. Methods in this category route hierarchical visual information to different LLM layers. DeepStack [ 30 ] distributes visual tokens across decoder depths according to a fixed hierarchical schedule. Sparse Shortcuts [ 43 ] wires ViT and LLM layers through predefined connection patterns. EVLM [ 8 ] feeds multi-layer ViT features into different gated cross-attention layers. CLI [ 5 ] applies adaptive gating at sampled vision layers and designated LLM injection points, rather than treating every ViT–LLM pair as a candidate connection. A more recent line of work [ 37 ] selects and fuses hierarchical visual features conditioned on each LLM layer’s representation. Although these methods begin to acknowledge the hierarchical correspondence between vision and language, most still rely on hand-crafted connection patterns or fixed layer-to-layer mappings, and few of them simultaneously realize (i) all-to-all cross-layer connectivity, (ii) routing weights driven by the current LLM-layer state, and (iii) end-to-end training with a stable routing distribution—the design point that DGP targets. In summary, existing methods either compress the visual hierarchy into a single pre-LLM representation, or wire it into the LLM through predefined sparse or hierarchical connections; neither answers where to connect via dynamic cross-layer interactions between all ViT layers and all LLM layers. We close this gap with DGP , which establishes adaptive, all-to-all connections between ViT and LLM layers through dynamic gated pathways. 3 Methodology Figure 3: Overview of DGP . In existing MLLMs, a single fixed visual representation is injected once and then propagates implicitly through the LLM [ 2 , 25 , 10 ] , leaving no direct cross-layer pathway between the visual hierarchy and the language hierarchy. As a result, different decoding stages cannot reach back to the visual granularity they actually need. DGP (Dynamic Gated Pathways) removes this bottleneck by opening dynamic gated pathways between every ViT layer and every LLM layer, so that each LLM layer can adaptively pick the visual hierarchy that best fits its current semantic state. We follow the LLaVA-1.5 [ 25 ] training recipe, using CLIP-ViT-L/14-336 [ 32 ] as the vision encoder and Vicuna as the LLM backbone. Training proceeds in two stages: pre-training (PT) on LLaVA-558K [ 25 ] and supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on LLaVA-mixed-665K [ 25 ] . Because DGP interacts with the hidden states at every LLM layer, both stages jointly optimize the LLM together with the newly introduced DGP modules. The vision encoder is kept frozen throughout, and each stage is trained for one epoch. All hyperparameters follow LLaVA-1.5: AdamW with cosine scheduling (warmup ratio 0.03 0.03 ), batch sizes of 256 / 128 256/128 and learning rates of 1 × 10 − 3 / 2 × 10 − 5 1\times 10^{-3}/2\times 10^{-5} for PT/SFT, respectively. The routing MLP uses a hidden dimension of 1024 1024 , and the residual gate is initialized to 0.3 0.3 . Following TGIF [ 23 ] , the entropy regularizer uses λ = 0.01 \lambda=0.01 during PT and is disabled ( λ = 0 \lambda=0 ) during SFT so routing can specialize. More hyperparameter sensitivity results are provided in Appendix C . Benchmarks. We evaluate on two complementary suites of multimodal benchmarks. Academic VQA covers GQA [ 15 ] , SciQA-IMG [ 29 ] , VQAv2 [ 13 ] , and VizWiz [ 14 ] . Instruction-following / LMM benchmarks cover SEED-Bench (SEED/SEED I ) [ 18 ] , LLaVA-Wild [ 27 ] , MM-Vet [ 40 ] , MMBench [ 28 ] , and POPE [ 22 ] , jointly probing multimodal comprehension, fine-grained perception, and hallucination resistance. Baselines. We compare DGP against two groups of baselines. The first group consists of general-purpose MLLMs: BLIP-2 [ 19 ] , InstructBLIP [ 11 ] , Shikra [ 9 ] , IDEFICS [ 17 ] , Qwen-VL [ 2 ] , and LLaVA-1.5 [ 25 ] . The second group focuses on multi-layer visual feature utilization : MMFuser [ 4 ] , Dense Connector [ 39 ] , DeepStack-L [ 30 ] , Sparse Shortcuts [ 43 ] , IGVA [ 20 ] , and TGIF [ 23 ] . For a fair comparison, we focus on methods that share the Vicuna backbone and the LLaVA-1.5 configuration. Table 1 compares DGP with mainstream MLLMs and multi-layer visual feature utilization methods under Vicuna-7B and 13B settings. Taking Vicuna-7B as an illustrative example, DGP consistently improves over the LLaVA-1.5 baseline—most prominently on VizWiz (+3.1), SQA I (+4.2), LLaVA W (+3.1), and MM-Vet (+3.3)—with similar trends on the remaining benchmarks. Against the strongest multi-layer baselines, DGP attains the best score on GQA, SQA I , SEED, LLaVA W , MM-Vet, and MMB CN , suggesting that compressing the visual hierarchy on the encoder side or using predefined sparse or hierarchical connections is insufficient to capture the full interaction between hierarchical visual evidence and the layer-wise LLM state. Overall, the gains support our central claim: dynamically gating where to connect between ViT and LLM layers is a more effective use of the visual hierarchy than any fixed wiring. Table 4 ablates the two key ingredients of DGP —gated routing and entropy regularization—and keeps a uniform average over the 24 ViT layers as a control, so as to disentangle gains coming from “using more visual layers” from those coming from where to connect . Adding gated routing alone already yields consistent improvements over the LLaVA-1.5 baseline on most benchmarks. By contrast, uniform average fusion without gated routing hurts performance on most benchmarks relative to the baseline. Equal-weight mixing across the visual hierarchy appears to blend incompatible granularities and weaken fine-grained evidence, whereas gated routing keeps connectivity selective and conditioned on the language-layer state. Stacking the entropy regularizer on top of gated routing brings a further gain, indicating that a well-spread routing distribution prevents collapse onto a few visual layers and stabilizes training. Together, these results show that the improvement is driven by the where to connect mechanism—routing that adapts to the language-layer state—rather than by the mere availability of more visual features. To inspect what DGP actually learns, we visualize the routing weights of every LLM layer over the ViT layers. Figure 5 (A) shows a clearly non-uniform, layer-dependent distribution: DGP neither fixates on the last ViT layer nor degenerates into a uniform average. Middle ViT layers are activated most frequently, suggesting that they offer a useful trade-off between local structure and high-level semantics. Shallow and deep features are also selected by certain LLM layers, confirming that the model dynamically reaches into different visual granularities according to where it is in decoding rather than relying on a single visual layer. Figure 5 (B) zooms in on representative LLM layers. Their routing curves peak at different positions: LLM layer 1 favors shallow-to-middle ViT layers, LLM layer 20 leans toward shallow features, whereas LLM layers 22 and 32 peak at mid-deep and deep ViT layers, respectively. The curves are typically multimodal rather than unimodal or flat, showing that DGP selectively combines several visual layers per LLM layer and that different LLM layers prefer different visual granularities depending on their language state. These visualizations confirm that DGP discovers non-uniform, dynamic ViT–LLM connection patterns, exploiting the complementarity of the visual hierarchy. Task-aware Gated Pathway Analysis. We next ask whether the routing also adapts to the task itself. Figure 5 (C) plots the routing weights of LLM layer 21 across benchmarks: each benchmark induces a distinct visual-layer profile. LLaVA W and VizWiz allocate more mass to mid-level features, in line with the need to balance object-level semantics and local visual cues for open-ended QA and real-world understanding, whereas SQA I additionally puts noticeable weight on shallow ViT layer 2, reflecting the fine-grained details required in scientific reasoning. Figure 5 (D) zooms one level further down: even within a single benchmark, individual samples exhibit substantial routing diversity, indicating that DGP ’s adaptivity goes beyond benchmark-level differences and operates at the sample level. Figure 6 fixes the image and varies the question. For questions about attribute recognition or small-region details, DGP activates shallow or shallow-to-middle ViT features—layers that preserve edges, textures and local spatial structure. For questions that rely more on global semantics or abstract reasoning—painting identification, historical significance, action understanding—the routing weight peaks instead at mid-deep or deep ViT layers. For open-ended questions such as “describe the image in detail,” which require both local details and holistic semantics, the routing distribution naturally widens into a broader multimodal shape that simultaneously taps multiple visual hierarchies. These patterns indicate that DGP ’s routing is dynamically conditioned on the layer-wise linguistic context and semantic state of the LLM, rather than being determined solely by fixed visual-layer priors. Feature Visualization. Figure 7 compares fixed single-layer CLIP features (L23), a uniform average over all 24 ViT layers, and the features produced by DGP at different LLM layers. CLIP (L23) yields coarse spatial responses with noisy activations in the background; naive average fusion brings in multi-layer information but, because features at different semantic granularities are superposed without selection, still suffers from diffuse responses and amplified local noise. DGP ’s LLM-layer-specific features instead show clearly more stable spatial structure, sharper subject contours, and lower background noise, preserving local details such as spoon edges and building boundaries. Crucially, the fused features differ across LLM layers 1, 18, and 28, confirming that DGP generates differentiated visual representations via dynamic routing rather than collapsing into a fixed averaging pattern. Overall, DGP strengthens global semantics while keeping shallow and middle-layer cues intact, mitigating both the information bottleneck of fixed single-layer features and the noise accumulation of naive averaging. 5 Conclusion In this work, we reframe visual information injection in MLLMs around the question of where to connect , and argue that fixed single-layer inputs or predefined sparse or hierarchical connections cannot match the differentiated demands of hierarchical ViT representations and layer-wise LLM semantics. We answer this question with DGP (Dynamic Gated Pathways), an all-to-all cross-layer connection mechanism in which each LLM layer adaptively gates its preference over every ViT layer based on its current semantic state, integrating multi-granularity visual information on demand at every decoding depth. DGP delivers consistent gains across a broad set of multimodal benchmarks, while routing analyses and feature visualizations show that it learns interpretable, layer-dependent connection patterns that explain where the gains come from. At each LLM layer, the hidden state at the glimpse position queries the CLS summaries of all 24 ViT layers to produce layer-specific routing weights, which are then used to perform a weighted fusion over the original 1024-dimensional patch features. The fused features are projected to 4096 dimensions by a layer-specific projector and injected into the visual token hidden states via gated residual connections. This mechanism enables each LLM layer to adaptively select the visual granularity that best matches its current semantic state. Appendix B Training Configuration and Benchmark Descriptions B.1 Training Configuration Table 6 lists the detailed training hyperparameters for the pre-training (PT) and supervised fine-tuning (SFT) stages. We follow the LLaVA-1.5 training recipe, using CLIP-ViT-L/14-336 as the vision encoder and Vicuna as the LLM backbone. The vision encoder is kept frozen throughout both stages, each trained for one epoch. The entropy regularizer is enabled during PT to stabilize routing distributions, and disabled during SFT to allow task-specific specialization. All training and efficiency measurements are conducted on NVIDIA L40-48GB GPUs. Backbone Selection Rationale. Our primary experiments are conducted under the LLaVA-1.5 framework, which remains a widely adopted open-source baseline in the research community. Specifically, we select Vicuna-v1.5 (7B/13B) as the LLM backbone and adopt the standard LLaVA-1.5 training data (LLaVA-558K for PT and LLaVA-mixed-665K for SFT). This design choice serves two purposes: (i) reproducibility , as all components are publicly available and well-documented; and (ii) fair comparison with modern multi-layer visual fusion methods (e.g., MMFuser [ 4 ] , IGVA [ 20 ] , TGIF [ 23 ] ) that likewise build upon LLaVA-1.5. By holding the backbone architecture, training data, and recipe constant, any observed performance improvement can be cleanly attributed to the proposed DGP module itself, rather than to differences in the underlying LLM, vision encoder, or training corpus. Appendix C Hyperparameter Sensitivity Analysis This section analyzes the design choices of key hyperparameters in DGP , including the entropy regularization coefficient λ \lambda , the routing MLP hidden dimension h h , and the gating initialization value γ init \gamma_{\mathrm{init}} . Entropy Regularization Coefficient λ \lambda . The entropy regularizer discourages routing collapse onto a small subset of visual layers. We adopt the same stage-wise schedule as TGIF [ 23 ] : λ = 0.01 \lambda=0.01 during pre-training to encourage exploration across layers, and no entropy regularization during supervised fine-tuning (equivalently λ = 0 \lambda=0 ) so routing can specialize to downstream tasks. Routing MLP Hidden Dimension h h . The routing MLP maps the 4096-dimensional routing feature to a 24-dimensional layer weight distribution. To balance expressiveness and computational efficiency, we set the intermediate hidden dimension to h = 1024 h=1024 , aligning with the ViT hidden dimension. This design ensures that the routing network has sufficient nonlinear modeling capacity while avoiding the additional computational overhead of using the full LLM dimension (4096). Gating Initialization γ init \gamma_{\mathrm{init}} . The gating coefficient γ l = σ ⁡ ( a l ) \gamma_{l}=\sigma(a_{l}) controls the injection strength of visual information at the l l -th layer, where a l a_{l} is a learnable scalar. We use a default gate initialization of γ init = 0.3 \gamma_{\mathrm{init}}=0.3 and set the gate learning rate to 5 × 10 − 3 5\times 10^{-3} . An initialization value that is too small leads to insufficient visual injection, causing the model to degenerate toward pure language model behavior; an initialization value that is too large may introduce excessive noise in early training, affecting convergence stability. Appendix E Additional Analysis E.1 Effect of Unfreezing Vision Encoder A natural question is whether unfreezing the vision encoder during training could further improve performance. Table 10 compares DGP with frozen versus unfrozen ViT. Interestingly, unfreezing the ViT leads to performance drops on most benchmarks (e.g., GQA, VQA v2 , VizWiz, SEED I , POPE), while only MMB shows improvement. We attribute this to a key property of our method: the dynamic routing mechanism relies on the stable semantic hierarchy across ViT layers—shallow layers encode textures and edges, while deeper layers capture high-level semantics. This layerwise division of labor is established during CLIP’s large-scale pre-training on 400M image-text pairs. When the ViT is fine-tuned on our comparatively smaller dataset, this hierarchical structure may drift, causing the router to “chase a moving target.” In contrast, freezing the ViT provides a stable visual foundation, allowing the router and fusion modules to learn consistent layer-selection strategies. Appendix F Limitations and Future Work The Dynamic Gated Pathways module is designed to be plug-and-play : it introduces only lightweight, layer-wise routing components that interface with the LLM hidden states, without modifying the vision encoder or the LLM architecture itself. This modularity suggests that DGP should generalize across vision encoders and LLM backbones, adaptively selecting appropriate visual granularities regardless of the specific encoder–decoder pairing. In this work, we validate DGP primarily under the LLaVA-1.5 setting (CLIP + Vicuna-7B/13B) and further on smaller-scale alternative frameworks, including Qwen3-VL-2B (SigLIP2 + Qwen3-1.7B) and LLaVA-NeXT (AnyRes tiling). Due to resource and time constraints, however, experiments on larger LLMs (30B+), broader families of vision encoders, and larger-scale multimodal corpora remain limited. Extending validation along these axes constitutes our primary future work. We anticipate that the dynamic routing mechanism will remain effective—and potentially more beneficial—as model capacity and data scale increase, since richer language representations should enable even finer-grained visual-layer selection. Appendix G Broader Impacts This work improves hierarchical visual access in MLLMs, which may benefit applications such as accessibility tools and educational assistants. At the same time, stronger visual reasoning could be misused for surveillance or misleading content, and DGP may inherit biases from the pretrained vision and language backbones it fine-tunes. We therefore view DGP as an architectural improvement for research use under the licenses and safety practices of the host models.
