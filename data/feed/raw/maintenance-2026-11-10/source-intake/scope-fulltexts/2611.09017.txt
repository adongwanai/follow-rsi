Yet existing benchmarks fragment evaluation across task scopes and protocols, leaving their capabilities across construction levels unclear.

Right: leaderboard of the 11 model–harness configurations (0–100, ↑ \uparrow ). 1 Introduction Automating 3D creation has long been a central pursuit in computer vision, spanning reconstruction, generation, and editing [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 ] .
Yet these tasks demand more than recognizing visual content: they require translating observations and instructions into explicit geometry and spatial structure.
Coding agents offer a new route toward this goal because they combine strong visual understanding with the ability to write and execute programs that construct 3D content [ 8 , 9 ] .
We argue that coding agents will replace traditional, task-specific computer vision pipelines for 3D creation.
The critical question is therefore: how far have coding agents come in 3D creation? Existing benchmarks, however, fragment this evaluation across different construction tasks and application settings.
For example, object and scene authoring benchmarks assess geometry, visual fidelity, construction, and editing [ 10 , 11 ] , whereas executable-world and game benchmarks emphasize mechanical dependencies, interactive behavior, and gameplay completion [ 12 , 9 , 13 ] .
Because their task scopes and evaluation protocols differ, these results are difficult to combine into a consistent account of an agent’s capabilities across objects, scenes, and interactive worlds.
A single aggregate score can also obscure whether an agent fails to match a reference, organize a scene, or implement the required behavior.
Object fidelity, scene coherence, and world functionality impose distinct construction requirements.
A unified benchmark must therefore evaluate each level through dedicated tasks and complementary visual, structural, and behavioral checks. Our key insight is that successful 3D creation demands faithful appearance, coherent structure, and functional behavior.
Accordingly, we organize evaluation around objects, scenes, and interactive open worlds, requiring executable, editable artifacts at every level.
Within this hierarchy, we assess object geometry and detail, scene layout and composition, and world-level appearance and activity completion through complementary visual, structural, and behavioral checks.
Rendered views measure appearance, native scene inspection measures structure, and runtime tests measure behavior. Following these principles, we introduce Frontier3DBench , comprising 145 tasks across 9 categories: 65 object tasks, 65 scene tasks, and 15 interactive open-world tasks.
The suite covers generation, reconstruction, and scene editing in Blender, together with interactive world construction in Unreal Engine.
Evaluation of 11 model–harness configurations reveals three key findings.
First, model strengths vary by level: GPT-6.1-sol leads the object and scene levels and achieves the highest overall score of 66.02/100, whereas GPT-6-astra leads interactive world construction.
Second, every configuration scores lower on reference reconstruction than on text-driven generation in both object and scene tasks, highlighting the difficulty of matching specified geometry, layouts, and visual details.
Third, local mechanisms remain far stronger than coordinated activities: across all 165 L3 submissions (11 configurations × \times 15 tasks), the local-mechanism component of interaction correctness averages 83.08/100, compared with 20.82 for motion clearance and 30.26 for stateful workflows.
These findings point to reference fidelity and the coordination of moving parts, actions, and persistent state as the main open challenges in 3D creation. Our contributions are threefold: • A unified benchmark for coding agents in 3D creation. Frontier3DBench comprises 145 tasks across 9 categories, bringing object, scene, and interactive-world construction into a shared suite covering generation, reconstruction, and scene editing. Dedicated tasks at each level reveal whether an agent’s strengths extend from individual assets to complete environments. • Joint evaluation of appearance, structure, and behavior. We assess executable Blender and Unreal Engine artifacts through visual judgments, structural measurements, and independent runtime tests, connecting content quality with action effects, state consistency, and activity completion. • An empirical account of current capabilities and bottlenecks. Across 11 model–harness configurations, we identify strengths that vary by construction level and persistent gaps in reference fidelity and coordinated activity completion. We further analyze capability dimensions and API costs to characterize differences in performance and resource demands. 2 Frontier3DBench 2.1 Task Formulation Frontier3DBench formulates 3D creation as a mapping from a task specification to project files for the prescribed 3D engine (Figure 2 ).
These files encode the requested objects (L1), scenes (L2), or open worlds (L3), together with the code and assets needed to construct or run them. Input. We represent the agent-visible input as x = ( d , ℐ , a 0 ) x=(d,\mathcal{I},a_{0}) , where d d is a written task instruction, ℐ \mathcal{I} is an optional set of reference images, and a 0 a_{0} denotes any supplied starter code, initial scene, or reusable assets.
The instruction defines the required geometry, appearance, spatial relationships, and, for open worlds, connected regions and functional behavior.
Reference-conditioned tasks provide one or more images; text-conditioned tasks use ℐ = ∅ \mathcal{I}=\varnothing .
Editing tasks additionally specify the initial scene, the requested modification, and the content to preserve.
For L3, the input includes an interaction contract specifying functional zones, entities, initial states, and available operations.
Source assets and evaluation annotations are used only for scoring and are not provided to the agent. Output. \text{Unreal Engine},&\text{L3 open worlds}.\end{cases} (1) For L1 and L2, y y contains Blender project files for the resulting object or scene, executable construction or editing code, and task-required exports.
For L3, y y contains Unreal Engine project files for an open world that connects multiple regions into a coherent environment, including its assets and required interaction logic.
Executing the submission must realize the visual and structural requirements of the input; L3 must also support the specified actions and their persistent effects within a session.
An independent observer tests the required interactions and state persistence as the player moves through the generated world. Figure 2 : Task formulation. Agents receive instructions and optional references or initial assets, then produce code and assets for Blender objects and scenes (L1/L2) or Unreal Engine open worlds (L3). 2.2 Benchmark Construction 2.2.1 Data Sources Object and scene tasks draw on publicly available 3D models (primarily .glb , with some .blend files), images from public datasets, and photographs captured by the authors.
Interactive open-world tasks use game screenshots and real-world photographs, including panoramic imagery, as visual references for interactive environments.
Text-conditioned tasks use text instructions without reference images. 2.2.2 Benchmark Composition Table 1 : Frontier3DBench task families, counts, inputs, and requirements. Frontier3DBench organizes 3D creation tasks by the unit being built and the behavior it must support.
The hierarchy progresses from individual objects and assemblies, through spatially organized scenes, to connected worlds with persistent interaction states.
Table 1 summarizes the inputs and requirements of the 145 tasks across 9 categories and three levels. Level 1 (L1): Object Level. L1 concerns the construction of a single editable 3D asset, which can be an individual object or a mechanical assembly.
Task requirements address the asset’s geometry, dimensions, appearance, and relationships among constituent parts.
The 65 tasks cover modeling from text ( Text2Code , 15), reconstruction from multiple reference views ( Img2Code , 25), and reconstruction from a single image ( Img2Code-SingleView , 25). Level 2 (L2): Scene Level. L2 concerns the construction or modification of a complete scene composed of multiple objects and their shared surroundings.
The objects must form a coherent spatial layout with consistent relative scales, while materials and lighting must support the specified appearance and camera composition.
The 65 tasks comprise scene generation from text instructions ( Brief2Scene , 20), reconstruction from reference images ( Ref2Scene , 10), reconstruction from real photographs ( Ref2Scene-RealPhoto , 25), and modification of an existing scene ( SceneEdit , 10).
Scene editing requires changing specified objects or scene properties while preserving the surrounding content. Level 3 (L3): Open-World Level. L3 concerns the construction of an explorable world that connects multiple functional regions within a shared environment.
The player must be able to move between regions and interact with objects and mechanisms whose state changes persist across action sequences within a session.
Complexity is defined by the required spatial organization and connected operations, allowing both dense indoor environments and larger outdoor worlds.
The 15 tasks are grouped by input modality: Text2Env (5) uses a text instruction without reference images, while Img2Env (10) pairs the instruction with one or more reference images.
Both categories require executable interactions that respect action prerequisites, so that changes produced by earlier actions determine which subsequent operations are available. 2.2.3 Data Processing Pipeline Task instructions. We first define the creation objective and use GPT-6-astra to expand it into a task instruction.
The instruction specifies what the agent should construct and how the result should satisfy the intended geometry and appearance.
It also makes spatial relationships explicit so that the instruction describes a coherent asset or environment.
For tasks with visual references, the text complements the images with construction requirements; text-only tasks express the target entirely through the instruction. Reference images. We prepare visual inputs from existing 3D assets and collected imagery.
When a source 3D model or scene is available, we render it in Blender to obtain the required reference views.
Photographs and game screenshots provide references for environments captured outside this rendering workflow.
Where panoramic imagery is used, we project it into rectilinear views that show different directions from the same capture position.
The resulting images are paired with the task instruction to define the visual reconstruction target. Initial scenes for editing. We construct each editing task from a complete source scene.
The agent receives an initial Blender project that differs from the source scene in designated target components, a rendered reference of the target scene, and an instruction describing the requested edit.
The instruction identifies the content to modify and the surrounding content to preserve.
The source scene serves as the evaluation target for both the edited components and the preserved content. Environment and interaction specifications. We use GPT-6-astra to develop open-world instructions from the intended environment and activity.
Each instruction defines how functional regions connect and what the player must be able to accomplish within them.
When images are supplied, it distinguishes visible reconstruction targets from additional spaces and mechanisms that must be constructed to support the activity.
An accompanying interaction contract specifies the entities and their initial states, then defines the available operations and their physical constraints.
Action prerequisites and persistent state changes connect individual interactions into an activity that can be completed across the environment. 2.3 Evaluation Frontier3DBench evaluates the appearance and structure of objects, the composition and editing of scenes, and the visual and functional coherence of interactive worlds.

Text-driven generation uses visual fidelity V V , requirement satisfaction R R , and detail fidelity D D .
Reference reconstruction replaces requirement satisfaction R R with image similarity I I and object localization L L , grounding appearance and placement in the supplied views.
The evaluator combines inspection of the delivered Blender scene with rendered evidence judged by GPT-6-astra at medium reasoning effort, with generator identity hidden.
Detail fidelity is measured on both the native scene and the exported asset. 2.3.2 Scene-Level Scoring L2 evaluates scene appearance, spatial organization, and completion of the requested construction or edit.
Brief2Scene uses the text-generation score (Eq. 3 ), and Ref2Scene and Ref2Scene-RealPhoto use the reference score (Eq. 4 ), with native scene measurements and main and alternate rendered views.
Scene editing additionally assesses target modification T T and preservation P P , comparing the candidate with the initial scene and target reference to determine whether the requested change is achieved while protected content is retained. 2.3.3 Open-World-Level Scoring L3 evaluates visual quality A A , task fidelity F F , spatial layout G G , and interaction correctness H H .
The submitted Unreal Engine project is rebuilt with an independent observer that captures scene views and records geometry, action effects, character movement, and state changes.
Local tests inspect individual mechanisms, while continuous workflows assess whether the environment supports the complete activity without teleportation or reset. 2.3.4 Overall Score Task scores are reported on a 0–100 scale and averaged within each level, including measured zeros.

Each model runs in its developer’s agent harness: Codex for the GPT models, Claude Code for Claude Opus 5.5, Kimi CLI for the Kimi models, DeepSeek Harness (DSH) for DeepSeek-v4.1-Flash, and ZCode for GLM-5.3-Flash.
MiniMax-M3.1-Flash, Qwen3.8-Max, and Doubao-Seed-Evolving, which do not provide their own harness, run in Claude Code.
Each configuration uses the same harness for all three levels.
In the text, we abbreviate GPT-6.1-sol, GPT-6-astra, GPT-6-luna, and Claude Opus 5.5 as Sol, Astra, Luna, and Opus, and refer to other models by family name where unambiguous. All models use their highest available reasoning effort: xhigh for Qwen and max for the others. Each agent decides when its work is ready for submission. If a submission fails the public execution and delivery checks, the agent can retry with the execution errors but without score feedback, within a fixed time budget (Appendix B.1 ). 3.2 Overall Performance The best overall configuration combines strong object and scene performance, while category leaders vary. Visual quality S vis S_{\mathrm{vis}} covers V V and I I in L1/L2 and A A in L3; structural and spatial consistency S str S_{\mathrm{str}} covers R R and L L in L1/L2 and G G in L3; task fulfillment S task S_{\mathrm{task}} covers D D , T T , and P P in L1/L2 and F F and H H in L3.
For each task, a dimension score is the weighted average of the components that the task uses, with weights given by their coefficients in Eqs. 3 – 6 and normalized to sum to one within the dimension.
We then average each dimension within L1, L2, and L3.

Automatic scores align with human fidelity rankings. The 25 items spanning at least 30 score points reach mean τ b = 0.793 \tau_{b}=0.793 , compared with 0.644 for the other 20 items, so agreement increases with the score gap between candidates. CAD benchmarks evaluate geometry, parametric structure, and engineering constraints from text and visual specifications [ 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 ] .
3DCodeBench and 3DHarnessBench assess procedural asset generation and reconstruction through Blender code [ 19 , 20 ] , and GeoCodeBench [ 21 ] benchmarks research-level coding for 3D geometric computer vision.
BlenderGym, VIGA, and MUSE study visual reconstruction and instruction-guided authoring or editing [ 22 , 8 , 23 ] , while VoxelCodeBench and SceneActBench probe spatial composition and scene operations [ 24 , 25 ] .
Code4Scene evaluates Unreal Engine scene construction and editing through artifact, static-physics, and auxiliary navigation checks [ 11 ] .
BlenderWorld spans object and environment creation, reconstruction, modification, and animation, combining rendered evidence with editable-project inspection [ 10 ] . Agents for interactive 3D creation. Recent systems connect object construction, scene organization, and physical interaction.
NeoWorld-3 reconstructs articulated objects and compositional scenes from visual observations through executable programs and physics validation [ 26 ] .
SceneCode couples room planning with part-wise object code generation to produce editable indoor scenes with articulated assets [ 27 ] .
EmbodiedGen V2 integrates asset generation, multi-room environments, task-driven worlds, and agent-based editing for embodied simulation [ 28 ] .
Code2Worlds-2 extends object and scene generation with physical compilation and simulator feedback, while Code2Games connects Blender world construction to UE5 gameplay through scene–gameplay correspondence and execution-guided repair [ 29 , 9 ] .
These systems show that coding agents can construct content across scales, which motivates evaluating each scale with dedicated tasks. Dynamics and world evaluation. Video-conditioned benchmarks assess programmatic reconstruction of appearance, camera motion, geometry, and dynamics [ 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 ] ; text-conditioned benchmarks assess generated simulations [ 34 , 35 ] .
ExeWorldBench progresses from individual mechanisms to composed mechanical systems and goal-driven executable worlds, organizing evaluation around mechanical and causal dependencies [ 12 ] .
Video2World and ScIntBench assess reconstruction from embodied demonstrations and physical interactions in generated scenes, respectively [ 36 , 37 ] .
WorldClaw and VibeWorlding study open-world authoring [ 38 , 39 ] , and VibeWorlding’s VWE-Bench uses rubric-based verification of physical feasibility and intent fulfillment.
Browser-world evaluations combine visual assessment, code inspection, or runtime behavioral checks [ 40 , 41 , 42 ] .
Game benchmarks assess integrated assets, scenes, and mechanics [ 43 , 44 , 9 , 13 ] , and CEGame-Bench additionally checks completion and saved state across engines.
CraftBench-UE and GenXR-Bench evaluate executable engine and XR features [ 45 , 46 ] , while WorldScore evaluates sequential world generation across 3D, 4D, and video systems [ 47 ] . Frontier3DBench organizes coding-agent evaluation around dedicated object, scene, and interactive-open-world tasks within a shared benchmark.
Its hierarchy follows the unit being constructed and the corresponding correctness requirements: object geometry and parts, scene layout and composition, and connected world regions with stateful interaction sequences.
All three levels cover generation and reconstruction; L2 additionally covers editing, and L3 adds interactive activities with persistent state.
This design allows comparison across levels while each level keeps its own visual, structural, and behavioral checks. 5 Conclusion We introduced Frontier3DBench to measure how far coding agents have come in 3D creation.
Its 145 tasks across 9 categories unify object, scene, and interactive-world construction, while evaluation of executable artifacts jointly assesses appearance, structure, and behavior.
Across 11 model–harness configurations, leadership shifts from GPT-6.1-sol on objects and scenes to GPT-6-astra on interactive worlds.
However, all configurations score lower on reference reconstruction than on text-driven generation in object and scene tasks, and strong local interactions remain insufficient for completing coordinated activities.
The central challenges are therefore matching specified geometry and layouts, and coordinating moving parts, actions, and persistent state.
Frontier3DBench makes these gaps measurable across construction levels, supporting progress toward coding agents that create faithful, coherent, and functional 3D content. References [1] Johannes L. L1/L2 generation allows at most six attempts within a 32-hour budget per task; a new attempt starts after a timeout, a transport error, or a failed public execution or delivery check, and receives the execution errors without score feedback.
L3 agents work under a 24-hour time limit.

Rendering settings follow each task’s specification.
