Affiliation: University of Wisconsin–Madison Kangwook Lee 1 1 footnotemark: 1 Affiliation: KRAFTON Affiliation: Ludo Robotics Abstract Language-model systems can select prompts and parameter adapters repeatedly during deployment, using feedback to guide later choices. Earlier choices may change the context or environment inherited by later configurations. We study selection among preconstructed candidates and compare cumulative loss with the best fixed prompt–parameter pair used from the beginning. This excess loss separates into three terms: selection regret within the available family, the gap from restricting that family, and the benefit or harm of the actual deployment history. Selection is evaluated using the history each candidate would have produced if used throughout, which separates candidate quality from the effects of switching. When loss functions are fixed independently of selector randomness, past choices affect loss for only a bounded number of rounds, and every candidate’s own-history loss is revealed, limiting switches yields sublinear expected regret within the selected family. We also quantify the costs and additional assumptions of independent probing and deployment-only feedback. Controlled experiments show that fewer switches can either lower or raise deployed loss, depending on whether inherited histories are harmful or helpful. With Qwen3.5-9B on Spider, text-only selection has lower within-family regret than joint selection, yet produces more failures because its candidate family excludes stronger configurations. 1 Introduction Language-model agents edit code, execute tests, and use tool feedback ( Yang et al., 2024b ) , while recent benchmarks study learning across sequences of related tasks ( Asawa et al., 2026 ) .
Consider a coding agent that handles successive tasks in a persistent workspace.
Before each task, a selector chooses an instruction prompt and a model checkpoint or adapter.
The agent edits code, runs tools, and receives feedback such as whether its changes pass the task’s tests.
Feedback guides the next configuration choice, while the edited code and tool history remain in the workspace.
The next configuration therefore inherits the consequences of earlier choices. How should we compare text-only, parameter-only, and joint adaptation in such a system? Each opportunity to select and execute a configuration is a deployment round , indexed by t t .
For example, a round can be one coding task, one question in Spider ( Yu et al., 2018 ) , or one dialogue turn in SParC ( Yu et al., 2019 ) .
We consider a sequence of T T rounds, which can share state and need not be independent.
The selector uses earlier feedback to choose a configuration, executes it, incurs a loss, and uses the available feedback to update future selection probabilities.
Minimizing cumulative loss over these repeated choices gives an online learning problem ( Cesa-Bianchi & Lugosi, 2006 ) .
Recent methods construct configurations through reflective prompt optimization ( Agrawal et al., 2026 ) , joint prompt–parameter optimization ( Bo et al., 2026 ) , and alternating harness–weight optimization ( Kim et al., 2026 ) .
In our setting, candidates are constructed before evaluation.
During evaluation, online learning updates their selection probabilities without constructing new prompts or adapters. A configuration pairs reusable text, such as prompts, demonstrations, and retrieval contents, with deployable parameters, such as checkpoints or adapters ( Brown et al., 2020 ; Lewis et al., 2020 ; Hu et al., 2022 ) .
Text-only selection keeps the base parameters, parameter-only selection keeps the base text, and joint selection can change both.
Figure 1 illustrates these configuration families.
The workspace, generated history, and tool sessions constitute operational state , which evolves even under a fixed configuration.
Retaining feedback across trials can influence later behavior without changing model parameters ( Shinn et al., 2023 ) . Our comparator is the best available joint configuration used for all T T rounds from the common initial condition, with its own resulting history.
This own-history comparison follows the policy-regret perspective ( Arora et al., 2012 ) .
For example, a coding agent that switches to configuration b b after using a a can encounter different code from an agent that used b b throughout. This comparison raises three separate questions.
(i) How well does the selector choose among its available candidates, when each is evaluated on the history it would have produced if used throughout? (ii) How much better is the best joint candidate than the best candidate in the selected family? (iii) Does the actual sequence of choices create a more or less favorable history than those fixed-configuration evaluations? We call the corresponding terms diagonal selection regret , family restriction , and the signed state effect .
Their sum is exactly the deployed excess loss. Diagonal refers to repeating a single configuration throughout the history,
and signed emphasizes that inherited state can reduce as well as increase loss. Our strongest feedback model evaluates every candidate on its own fixed-configuration history.
Ordinary deployment feedback need not supply those counterfactual losses.
We also study independent probes of individual candidates and updates based only on deployed outcomes.
For state-dependent guarantees, we require bounded influence : choices sufficiently far in the past no longer affect current loss, including through hidden state.
These assumptions connect our analysis to online learning with memory ( Anava et al., 2015 ) .
We also compare joint sampling and coordinatewise conditional selection: they agree for additive losses under a product prior, but interactions can separate their regret even when both eventually select the optimum.
The online-learning methods are established ( Geulen et al., 2010 ; Avner et al., 2012 ; Cheng et al., 2023 ) .
Our analysis connects their guarantees to adaptation comparators, information requirements, and signed state effects. Controlled studies show that reducing switches helps when mixed histories are harmful and hurts when they are beneficial, even with the same per-round selection probabilities.
With Qwen3.5-9B ( Qwen Team, 2026 ) on Spider, text-only selection incurs less within-family regret than joint selection but more deployed loss because its candidates are weaker.
On recursive SParC, earlier generated queries in Structured Query Language (SQL) supply the inherited state.
Lazy exponential weights (EW) substantially reduces switching with only a small change in estimated expected loss. Our main contributions are summarized as follows. • Loss accounting. We separate selection, family restriction, and state effects relative to a common fixed joint comparator; see Section 2 . • Selection and state guarantees. We connect selection bounds to configuration families and bound expected excess loss for a selector that limits switches under exogenous losses, bounded influence, and full counterfactual feedback; see Sections 3 – 4 . • Weaker feedback. We quantify the costs and additional assumptions of approximate evaluation, sparse probes, and deployment-only feedback; see Section 5 . • Empirical comparisons. Controlled studies and SQL evaluations show how candidate availability and inherited state change the interpretation of selection regret and switching; see Section 6 . 2 Configurations, Comparators, and Loss Decomposition Configurations and Candidate Families. Weight adaptation includes full tuning, adapters, low-rank updates ( Houlsby et al., 2019 ; Hu et al., 2022 ) , continuous prompt/prefix tuning ( Li & Liang, 2021 ; Lester et al., 2021 ) , and targeted editing ( Meng et al., 2022 ) .
Updates can cause interference and forgetting ( Kirkpatrick et al., 2017 ) , with low-rank and full tuning differing even at similar performance ( Shuttleworth et al., 2025 ) .
Comparisons with in-context learning report task-dependent accuracy, generalization, and cost tradeoffs ( Liu et al., 2022 ; Mosbach et al., 2023 ; Ghosh et al., 2026 ; Razumovskaia et al., 2025 ) , while theory characterizes when prompting can match weight tuning ( Genewein et al., 2025 ) .
Joint construction includes BetterTogether ( Soylu et al., 2024 ) , MetaTuner ( Bo et al., 2026 ) , fast–slow updates ( Tiwari et al., 2026 ) , and WHALE’s alternation of online rejection-sampling tuning with Meta-Harness search ( Kim et al., 2026 ) .
Our conditional sampler studies selection within a fixed family, with candidate construction. Expert Advice, Mixture Priors, and Limited Switching. Hedge and external regret compare fixed experts ( Hannan, 1957 ; Foster & Vohra, 1999 ; Littlestone & Warmuth, 1994 ; Freund & Schapire, 1997 ; Cesa-Bianchi & Lugosi, 2006 ) , and KL change-of-measure extends the analysis to measurable priors.
Bandit, tracking, and sleeping/growing-expert models change feedback, comparators, or availability ( Auer et al., 2002 ; Bubeck & Cesa-Bianchi, 2012 ; Herbster & Warmuth, 1998 ; Cortes et al., 2019 ; Mourtada & Maillard, 2017 ) .
Lazy and limited-switch methods control regret and action changes ( Kalai & Vempala, 2005 ; Geulen et al., 2010 ; Altschuler & Talwar, 2018 ) .
Switching costs admit T \sqrt{T} full-information and Θ ~ ​ ( T 2 / 3 ) \widetilde{\Theta}(T^{2/3}) adversarial-bandit rates; bounded-memory adversaries can force T 2 / 3 T^{2/3} under ordinary full information ( Cesa-Bianchi et al., 2013 ; Dekel et al., 2014 ) . Zhang et al. (2022) combine prior divergence and movement penalties in comparator-adaptive finite-expert bounds.
Under exogenous losses, coupling converts marginal movement into expected switches. Appendix E provides additional related work. 8 Conclusion We decomposed deployed loss over the best fixed joint configuration into selection, family restriction, and signed state effects.
Low regret within a candidate family, eventual optimal selection, and infrequent switching can each occur alongside higher cumulative loss.
Under the stated influence and feedback assumptions, EW with infrequent switching bounds selection regret and state effects, while weaker feedback introduces additional costs and requires further assumptions.

Appendix E Additional Related Work Automatic Optimization of Discrete Text. Prompt optimization uses token gradients ( Shin et al., 2020 ) , generated or reinforcement-learned instructions ( Zhou et al., 2023 ; Deng et al., 2022 ) , critiques ( Pryzant et al., 2023 ) , optimizer histories ( Yang et al., 2024a ) , evolution ( Guo et al., 2024 ; Fernando et al., 2024 ) , and textual gradients or pairwise feedback ( Yüksekgönül et al., 2025 ; Lee et al., 2026a ) .
MIPRO searches instructions and demonstrations ( Opsahl-Ong et al., 2024 ) , GEPA evolves textual system components ( Agrawal et al., 2026 ) , and SPRIG evolves reusable prompts and evaluates transfer ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) .
Appendix A.2.5 separates candidate discovery from selection. Compound Systems, Context, and External Memory. Demonstrations and retrieval configure inference ( Brown et al., 2020 ; Min et al., 2022 ; Rubin et al., 2022 ; Lewis et al., 2020 ) .
Text also governs tools ( Yao et al., 2023 ; Schick et al., 2023 ) , retained feedback and memory ( Shinn et al., 2023 ; Packer et al., 2023 ) , and optimized pipelines or harnesses ( Khattab et al., 2024 ; Lee et al., 2026b ) .
A-Mem revises linked memories ( Xu et al., 2025 ) , LightMem separates online use from offline consolidation ( Fang et al., 2026 ) , and LongMemEval evaluates sustained retention and updates ( Wu et al., 2025 ) .
These systems motivate reusable text with explicit state and resource accounting under protocol Ξ \Xi . Prior Complexity, Description Length, and Language-Model Priors. Probably approximately correct (PAC)-Bayesian bounds charge prior KL ( McAllester, 1999 ) , while minimum description length connects coding and complexity ( Grünwald, 2007 ) .
Language-model priors can also certify discrete prompts ( Akinwande et al., 2024 ) .
Our online bounds use prior complexity without requiring independent and identically distributed samples. Stateful and Adaptive Environments. Policy regret compares counterfactual trajectories ( Arora et al., 2012 ) .
Related approaches include learning with memory, online Markov decision processes, reactive experts, and stateful reference policies ( Anava et al., 2015 ; Even-Dar et al., 2009 ; Neu et al., 2010 ; de Farias & Megiddo, 2006 ; Feige et al., 2017 ) . Anava et al. (2015) obtain square-root rates with oblivious memory losses, counterfactual feedback, and low switching, the mechanism underlying our full-diagonal guarantee.
Decoupled exploration separates evaluation from deployment ( Avner et al., 2012 ) , including under limited switching ( Cheng et al., 2023 ) .
Deployment-only feedback instead motivates policy-regret mini-batching ( Arora et al., 2012 ) . Appendix F Limitations Our analysis assumes a fixed, known candidate family constructed before evaluation and does not address how to construct or expand that family.
A practical extension is to expand the prompt and adapter banks systematically and repeat the comparisons, examining how candidate diversity affects selection performance and family restriction.
We leave this extension to future work. Our language-model experiments focus on Spider and SParC with one base model.
Repeating the evaluation with additional models and task orders would help assess how consistently the observed patterns appear.
