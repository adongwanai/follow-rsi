Allen Affiliation: South China Normal University  University of British Columbia Abstract We introduce LongBanana , to our knowledge the first expert-designed and expert-verified benchmark for long-context multi-reference image synthesis in the age of agent-driven image generation . The task requires retrieving dispersed visual evidence, rejecting distractors, binding entities and relations, and composing one coherent final image from multiple references. LongBanana contains 90 carefully designed tasks organized by a 3-by-3 taxonomy; each task includes about 32 reference images, 20 constraints, and 50 evaluation items, yielding 4,670 expert-specified atomic evaluation items in total. Its graph-grounded evaluation design instantiates each task with reference images and an entity–relation graph, then converts node, edge, and whole-graph obligations into checklist items for Content Fidelity, Relational Binding, and Global Constraint Satisfaction. Reviewed expert labels include a 15% double-labeled subset with 89.4% exact agreement and 0.83 quadratic weighted kappa . We further introduce LongBanana -Gen, an agent-harness framework for long-horizon image generation; experiments show that minimal agent harnesses improve over direct generation, while a ReAct–Plan–Exec–Reflect (RPR) workflow yields harness-dependent gains by strengthening evidence grounding and repair. Together, LongBanana provides an auditable testbed for measuring dense visual evidence use and a shared target for developing stronger agent-driven image-generation systems.

Consider an accessories retailer preparing an advertisement for all handbags with dual parallel top handles ( Figure 1 ). The system must inspect a large inventory, select the relevant bags, reject visually similar distractors, preserve appearance details, and arrange the selected items according to the requested composition. The output may look realistic while still being wrong: it may use the wrong handbag, bind a handle style to the wrong object, violate a count or layout constraint, or introduce unsupported details. The central capability is therefore not visual plausibility alone, but reference-grounded synthesis across retrieval, rejection, binding, and composition. Evaluating this capability raises a central question: how can we tell whether an image-generation system has synthesized a correct image from long, multi-reference visual context? The difficulty is not only that such tasks contain more images or longer prompts. The required evidence is distributed across references, entities, attributes, relations, hard negatives, unsupported-content rules, and intermediate decisions. Success can only be judged by checking whether the final image satisfies the reference-grounded obligations. A benchmark therefore needs dense multi-reference tasks and scoring that localizes errors rather than a holistic score. Figure 1: LongBanana task overview. (a) Each case pairs a heterogeneous reference set and structured synthesis prompt with the generated image under evaluation. (b) LongBanana -Eval converts graph-grounded checklist items into dimension-level scores and auditable evidence traces. The score labels Content, Relations, and Global denote Content Fidelity, Relational Binding, and Global Constraint Satisfaction, respectively. Current image-generation benchmarks [ 14 , 29 ] capture important parts of this problem, but leave the full measurement target under-specified. Research on instruction-guided editing, personalization, and prompt following addresses local edits and textual requirements [ 10 , 56 , 14 , 45 , 51 , 2 ] . Recent multi-reference benchmarks move closer to reference-based synthesis [ 23 , 37 , 3 ] , and automatic evaluation methods support systematic assessment [ 39 , 53 , 47 ] . What remains difficult to measure is dense evidence use: whether the model selected the right references, rejected plausible distractors, attached attributes and relations to the correct entities, and avoided content that is unsupported by either the instruction or the references. A realistic image can pass a surface-level inspection while failing exactly these obligations. These failures are typically provenance errors rather than rendering errors, where provenance is the source—a specific reference, or the instruction itself—to which each piece of generated content should be attributed: a system may render the right object category while copying appearance from the wrong reference, place the correct entities while violating their relation, or produce a polished composition that leaks a hard negative into the scene. To make such errors measurable, we model each task as an entity–relation graph . Its nodes are the entities, attributes, and text elements that must (or must not) appear; its edges are the spatial, interaction, ordering, and local-appearance relations that bind those entities; and its whole-graph constraints are scene-level obligations—layout, counts, temporal state, and reference selection—that no single node or edge captures. We refer to a node, an edge, or a whole-graph constraint as a graph unit . These three levels define the three graph-grounded scoring dimensions used throughout the paper: Content Fidelity (node level), Relational Binding (edge level), and Global Constraint Satisfaction (whole-graph level). We derive a checklist of atomic, visually verifiable items, each tied to one graph unit, to localize failures. To make this benchmark auditable at scale, we design LongBanana -Eval, a graph-grounded checklist evaluation pipeline whose scorer LongBananaEval acts as an agentic checklist judge (Section 3 ): rather than judging the final image with one undifferentiated score, it routes each checklist item to a rubric-guided critic, invokes deterministic tools—OCR, counting, layout, and identity checks—when an item benefits from an executable check, and records evidence traces for quality control and reproducibility. We validate this measurement target on a human-audited pool of 900 generated outputs with 46,700 reviewed checklist labels. An independently double-labeled subset of 7,005 labels (15%) reaches 89.4% exact agreement and 0.83 quadratic weighted κ \kappa , and these reviewed expert checklist scores serve as the reference standard against which we validate LongBananaEval . We also use LongBanana to study agent-driven image-generation workflows. Each system wraps a fixed image generator in an agent harness [ 40 , 54 , 30 ] : the harness maintains reference state, selects evidence, schedules prompts, invokes the generator, and revises requests, while the image generator only synthesizes pixels. We evaluate direct generation, minimal agent harnesses, and RPR-enabled harnesses, where RPR is a serial workflow combining ReAct evidence grounding, Plan–Exec decomposition, and Reflect-based revision (Section 4 ), and we compare multiple image generators under a fixed harness. Across these settings, agent harnesses improve the aggregate profile over direct generation, yet no single harness dominates: the best per-subclass scores remain distributed across direct prompting, minimal harnesses, and RPR. RPR itself yields harness-dependent gains, helping only when the harness reliably exploits retrieved evidence and revision feedback, and otherwise introducing drift. We evaluate direct, harness-based, and RPR workflows to show that current systems fail in structured, diagnosable ways rather than along a single image-quality axis. 2 The LongBanana Benchmark 2.1 Goal and Scope LongBanana is an evaluation benchmark for long-horizon multi-reference image synthesis . A system is given a heterogeneous reference set and a structured synthesis instruction, and must render one coherent image that satisfies the obligations of the task’s entity–relation graph (Section 1 ): its required and forbidden nodes, the edges that bind them, and the whole-graph constraints on the scene. The setting differs from low-reference editing because the decisive evidence is distributed across many images, and correctness is measured by the graph-derived checklist of atomic, visually verifiable items rather than by a single holistic quality score. LongBanana contains 90 cases: 10 cases for each subclass in a 3 × 3 3\times 3 taxonomy. A typical case contains approximately 32 references and more than 20 cross-reference constraints, and across the benchmark experts specify 4,670 atomic checklist items. Because every item is tied to a single graph unit, each failure is localized to a specific obligation rather than absorbed into an overall score. Figure 2: The 3 × 3 3\times 3 taxonomy used to organize LongBanana . Rows group cases into Scene Composition, Visual Appearance, and Reference Reasoning, while columns expose subclass-level demands used for diagnostic reporting. Each subclass contributes 10 cases. 2.2 Task Taxonomy and Evaluation Rubric We organize LongBanana tasks into a three-suite taxonomy that captures their primary synthesis demands ( Figure 2 ). Across these tasks, a shared graph-grounded rubric evaluates whether the generated image preserves the required content, relations, and scene-level constraints. The three suites are Scene Composition (semantic coverage, relational graph consistency, and layout arrangement), Visual Appearance (global appearance conditioning, local appearance binding, and appearance harmonization), and Reference Reasoning (selection and retrieval, temporal/state reasoning, and counting/aggregation). All tasks require a final image output; subclasses differ in the evidence-integration behavior being tested. For each case, checklist items assess correctness at three levels of the entity–relation graph: nodes, edges, and the whole graph. Content Fidelity measures node-level correctness: required entities, attributes, text elements, and supported semantic nodes must appear, while forbidden or unsupported nodes must be absent. Relational Binding measures edge-level correctness, including spatial relations, interactions, ordering, support, containment, and local bindings such as texture, material, color, pose, or state assigned to a specific entity. Global Constraint Satisfaction measures whole-graph correctness, including composition, layout, scene-level style, count and aggregation constraints, temporal/state constraints, and reference-selection constraints. This graph-grounded rubric localizes failures to specific content, bindings, or scene-level obligations, supporting consistent diagnosis across task categories and generation systems. 2.3 Benchmark Construction We construct each case around a shared entity–relation graph that connects the synthesis instruction, reference images, and evaluation checklist ( Figure 3 ). Construction proceeds from a scenario description to structured constraints, then to the visual references and instruction that express the task, and finally to the checklist used to evaluate its result. Scenario and graph construction. Expert authors design scenarios aligned with the task taxonomy, drawing on storyboard, product, and scene-composition use cases. A scenario specifies what the final image should depict and which entities, visual attributes, and interactions must be preserved. An LLM then structures the scenario into an entity–relation graph: nodes represent entities and their attributes, edges encode spatial relations, interactions, and local appearance bindings, and whole-graph constraints specify requirements such as composition and counts. This representation makes the dependencies explicit. For example, an appearance requirement identifies both the visual property and the entity to which it belongs, while a relational requirement identifies the entities that must participate in the requested configuration. These constraints guide both task synthesis and checklist derivation. Instruction and reference construction. The graph guides the creation of the target instruction and reference images, including entity, appearance, and optional background references. The instruction expresses the requested composition and the relationships to be realized; the references supply the visual identities, local details, and appearance cues needed to instantiate it. Discriminative visual attributes are retained in the references and omitted from text where possible. Because these cues are distributed across images, the generator must associate each relevant reference with the intended graph entity and combine their evidence while maintaining the requested relations. At least 30 cases also include two or more hard-negative references that are visually plausible but irrelevant to the requested output. Their exclusion is encoded in the checklist, allowing distractor leakage to be assessed alongside the inclusion of required content. Checklist derivation. We translate graph constraints into atomic checklist items using the rubric introduced in Section 2.2 . Node-level items check required entities and attributes; edge-level items check relations and appearance bindings; whole-graph items check scene-wide requirements such as layout and counts. Each item records an observable condition, the graph unit it evaluates, the relevant reference images, and its scoring criteria. This links a judgment about the output to the evidence needed to make it. Positive items test whether a required condition is satisfied, while negative items check for the absence of distractor or unsupported content. Each completed case therefore pairs an instruction and reference set with a checklist derived from the same task constraints. Section 3 describes how these items are scored. Case curation. Human curators review the instruction, reference images, and checklist together, checking their correspondence to the task graph. They inspect whether the references visibly support the required identities and attributes, whether the requested relations and composition are visually feasible, and whether checklist items express clear, individually verifiable conditions. They also check reference density, constraint coverage, and overlapping or ambiguous items. Requirements that cannot be judged from the references and a candidate output are revised or rejected before inclusion. Appendix B provides the detailed construction protocol and case specifications. Section 4 describes human labeling of generated outputs to establish reference judgments for evaluator validation. Figure 3: LongBanana construction pipeline. (2) Graph Construction : an LLM structures each scenario into entities, attributes, relations, and whole-scene constraints. (4) Checklist & Curation : graph constraints are translated into evidence-linked checklist items, and human curators review the instruction, references, and checklist for consistency, visual feasibility, and verifiability. 3 LongBanana -Eval: Graph-Grounded Checklist Evaluation LongBanana -Eval evaluates a generated image against the case’s graph-derived checklist. Its agentic checklist judge, LongBananaEval , prepares item-specific reference evidence, routes each check to a dimension-specific critic, and combines the resulting judgments into dimension-level and overall scores. As shown in Figure 4 , the pipeline also records the evidence supporting each item judgment. 3.1 Checklist Scoring Protocol Evidence preparation and routing. For each item, the parser reads the condition to verify, its rubric dimension, reference identifiers, and scoring criteria from the case specification. It assembles the generated image and relevant reference images into an evidence packet, with optional crops to localize the region under inspection. Negative-content checks additionally receive the identifiers and roles of applicable hard-negative references. The router assigns the packet to the critic corresponding to the item’s rubric dimension. Dimension-specific checks and tools. The Content Fidelity critic checks required entities, attributes, and text, together with the absence of forbidden or distractor content. The Relational Binding critic checks spatial and interaction relations and the assignment of local appearance cues to their intended entities. The Global Constraint Satisfaction critic checks scene-wide requirements, including layout, global style, counts, and temporal or state constraints. Each critic compares the output with the item’s reference evidence and may invoke verification tools: OCR for readable text, detection or counting for multiplicity, layout routines for spatial constraints, and identity or feature matching for consistency. The critic combines these observations with the visual evidence to determine whether the item is satisfied. Item judgments and evidence traces. Each check produces a label s j ∈ { 0 , 1 , 2 } s_{j}\in\{0,1,2\} : 2 2 for a satisfied requirement, 1 1 for partial satisfaction or uncertain evidence, and 0 0 for an unsatisfied requirement. For negative items, the requirement is the absence of forbidden or unsupported content: complete absence scores 2 2 , ambiguous or suspicious leakage scores 1 1 , and clear erroneous appearance scores 0 0 . The evidence trace associates the label with the relevant references, the critic’s findings, and any tool observations. Traces are organized by the same three rubric dimensions, linking each reported judgment to the evidence used to reach it. Score aggregation. For a generated image with N N checklist items, the overall score is the mean item label scaled to 0 0 – 10 10 . Dimension scores apply the same calculation to the item set J d J_{d} assigned to dimension d d : S overall = 5 N ​ ∑ j = 1 N s j , S d = 5 | J d | ​ ∑ j ∈ J d s j . S_{\mathrm{overall}}=\frac{5}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}s_{j},\qquad S_{d}=\frac{5}{|J_{d}|}\sum_{j\in J_{d}}s_{j}. The dimension scores provide a diagnostic profile, while the overall score summarizes the full checklist. The main generation tables express checklist-average scores on a 0 0 – 100 100 scale; Section 4 separately defines the difficulty-weighted task pass rate, P@70%. Baseline and validation. The VLM-as-judge baseline receives the generated image, relevant references, and checklist in a single judging call and returns item labels for the same score aggregation. It shares the judging backbone and item-level criteria with LongBananaEval , whose execution organizes evidence and tool use around individual checks. Section 4 evaluates both judges against reviewed human labels at the item and output-score levels; implementation details appear in Appendix C . Figure 4: LongBanana -Eval scoring pipeline. The parser assembles item-specific reference evidence and routes each check to one of three rubric critics. Critics inspect the evidence, call verification tools as needed, and return item labels with supporting traces. Item labels are averaged to produce dimension-level and overall scores. 4 Experiments Our experiments connect evaluator validation with a study of long-context image generation. We first establish agreement between automatic checklist judgments and reviewed human labels. We then ask how much explicit graph structure helps a non-agentic generator, what adaptive agentic workflows add beyond an optimized static plan, and how those gains interact with the image backend. 4.1 Protocol and Metrics Generation conditions. We evaluate all systems on the held-out 90-case LongBanana benchmark. Table 2 fixes Nano Banana 2 and compares two direct baselines, three minimal agent harnesses, and their RPR-enabled variants. Table 3 fixes OpenClaw + RPR and compares three image backends. The shared OpenClaw + RPR with Nano Banana 2 configuration connects the two comparisons, yielding ten distinct generation conditions. FP-Direct (full-prompt direct) keeps the full target prompt fixed and slides an eight-reference window over the inventory. PD-Direct (prompt-decomposed direct) tests how far non-agentic generation can go with explicit task structure and optimal composition scheduling. We give it privileged access to the entity–relation graph used to construct each case, including entity-to-reference correspondences. Under the same eight-reference backend budget as FP-Direct, it batches graph nodes and constructs relation-aware prompts. Its prompts expose relations incident to each batch, and each generated intermediate becomes the input for the next composition step. Integer programming solves the staging schedule exactly: first maximize relations whose anchors are composed no later than their dependents, then minimize total relational stretch ∑ ( u , v ) ∈ E | b ⁡ ( u ) − b ⁡ ( v ) | \sum_{(u,v)\in E}\lvert b(u)-b(v)\rvert , where b ⁡ ( ⋅ ) b(\cdot) is the batch index. PD-Direct thus exploits the construction graph through a fixed, optimized execution plan; the agentic systems do not receive this graph or the evaluation checklist. The agentic conditions use Hermes-Agent, OpenCode, and OpenClaw, with Claude Sonnet 4.6 as their shared agent model. A minimal harness maintains reference state, organizes prompts, calls the image backend, and records revisions. RPR coordinates three stages: ReAct retrieves and reasons over reference evidence; Plan–Exec decomposes the grounded request into generation or editing steps; and Reflect lets the agent inspect drafts and decide on further retrieval or revision. Table 2 establishes PD-Direct as a strong non-agentic baseline. Graph-informed staging substantially improves on FP-Direct, with the best Global Appearance Conditioning (GAC) score and the second-best Layout Arrangement Consistency (LAC) score across all compared systems. Its improvements also extend to local appearance binding, reference selection, and aggregation. These results demonstrate the practical value of exploiting known task structure: organizing entities and their relations into a precomputed composition schedule can support competitive multi-reference synthesis. The direct baseline remains competitive on these subclasses even against systems equipped with retrieval and reflection. PD-Direct’s strengths make the subsequent agentic comparison meaningful, since its task structure is supplied directly and its scheduling objective is solved exactly. Agentic systems nevertheless achieve broader success without direct access to the construction graph. Most agentic configurations exceed PD-Direct in overall average, and OpenClaw + RPR surpasses it on eight of nine subclasses, with a 3.8-point average-score gain and a 12.2-percentage-point gain in task pass rate. This advantage reaches beyond scene composition to reference selection, state reasoning, and aggregation. The comparison exposes a gap between knowing the intended constraint structure and realizing it through successive image-generation steps. The graph specifies desired relations, while intermediate images reveal which constraints have actually survived the latest generation step. A fixed graph schedule organizes what should be composed; adaptive workflows can additionally inspect evidence, maintain reference bindings, and revise execution as synthesis unfolds. Their advantage over a structurally privileged baseline provides strong evidence for the value of agentic orchestration in long-context image generation. Within the agentic configurations, RPR gives OpenClaw the strongest overall average and a joint-best task pass rate. Its clearest improvements over the minimal harness occur in scene composition, reference selection, and aggregation, consistent with a workflow that links evidence acquisition to execution and repair. The minimal OpenClaw harness remains stronger on temporal and state reasoning. OpenCode and Hermes-Agent also improve their average scores with RPR, while their task pass rates decline. These profiles identify reliable coordination as the next design priority: retrieved evidence must remain attached to the correct entities and constraints through planning, generation, and revision. Workflow component analysis. The RPR ablation in Table 11 isolates retrieval, planning, and reflection with OpenClaw and Nano Banana 2 fixed. Plan–Exec alone is strongest on global appearance conditioning and local appearance binding; ReAct alone improves reference selection over the minimal harness; and Reflect alone improves layout, aggregation, and task pass rate. The full workflow achieves the strongest overall result and pass rate among the component variants. These profiles give a concrete direction for agent design. Evidence retrieval supplies the relevant references, planning organizes their use, and reflection checks the realized composition against the task. Their combined workflow turns these operations into a coordinated generation process, extending the benefits of decomposition with evidence inspection and feedback. The component results reinforce the main comparison: static organization is useful, and its integration with grounding and repair produces the strongest overall configuration in this study. Backend capability and agentic orchestration offer complementary paths to improvement. The harness controls evidence selection, constraint organization, and revision, while the image model determines how faithfully these decisions become pixels. A stronger workflow improves the use of the available visual evidence; a stronger backend improves its realization in the generated image. The broad Nano Banana 2 advantage shows that backend capability remains consequential even under the strongest evaluated workflow. Diagnostic implications. The comparison with PD-Direct highlights the distinction between planning a composition and controlling its realization. PD-Direct uses privileged graph information to optimize reference grouping and ordering, then executes that schedule in stages. The resulting images can still depart from the intended constraints as entities are added or edited. Agentic workflows can inspect intermediate outputs, revisit reference evidence, and adjust subsequent actions. Their broader advantage motivates treating the evolving visual state, alongside the task structure, as central to generation control. The checklist turns this control problem into inspectable requirements. PD-Direct’s strong appearance conditioning alongside its weaker reference selection and state reasoning directs attention to evidence use across composition steps. Item judgments and evidence traces can distinguish an incorrect reference choice from an incorrect entity binding or an unmet scene-wide requirement. For future agent designs, these diagnostics offer a basis for choosing what to revisit and what to preserve during revision. The resulting research target is evidence-guided adaptation: connect inspection of the current image to the next retrieval, generation, or repair action while preserving satisfied constraints. Further analyses. Appendix D reports the workflow ablation and case-paired score diagnostics. The generation comparisons, component study, and item-level traces together connect aggregate performance to the decisions made during synthesis, providing a basis for developing more capable reference-grounded image-generation agents. 5 Conclusion LongBanana establishes long-context multi-reference image synthesis as a benchmark for integrating dispersed visual evidence, rejecting distractors, and satisfying coupled content and relational constraints. LongBanana -Bench provides 90 expert-designed and expert-verified cases across a 3 × 3 3\times 3 taxonomy, with 4,670 atomic checklist items. LongBanana -Eval turns these constraints into evidence-linked judgments, validated against human labels, to measure constraint fulfillment and localize generation failures. The generation study highlights the distinction between optimizing a composition plan and adaptively controlling its realization. PD-Direct combines privileged access to the construction graph with exactly optimized staging, establishing a competitive non-agentic baseline. Agentic workflows achieve broader success without direct access to that graph, showing the value of adaptive evidence use and generation control beyond a fixed schedule. The RPR component study further supports coordinating retrieval, execution, and repair within one workflow. These findings point toward agents that reason over both task structure and the evolving visual state, preserve reference bindings across steps, and target unmet requirements during revision. Appendix A Related Work Image Synthesis Benchmarks. Image-generation benchmarks cover instruction-guided editing, personalization, human alignment, and reasoning-oriented synthesis [ 48 , 29 , 28 , 22 , 46 , 24 ] but generally focus on creating a single image from a single prompt. Recent multi-reference benchmarks [ 23 , 37 , 42 , 3 ] are closer to our setting, but they typically use fewer references or lower-density cross-image constraints. LongBanana targets final-image synthesis in which correctness depends on retrieving, rejecting, binding, and integrating evidence distributed across dozens of references. Atomic Constraint Evaluation. Long-context tasks have long been a central challenge across language and vision domains [ 23 , 1 , 41 , 18 , 19 ] . Instruction-following benchmarks in NLP often convert open-ended tasks into verifiable atomic requirements. IFEval [ 55 ] emphasizes directly checkable constraints, and TIFA [ 11 ] converts text-to-image faithfulness into question-answer checks. LongBanana transfers this principle to long-horizon multi-reference synthesis. Each checklist item links the output to a graph node, graph edge, or whole-graph constraint, so failures can be traced to missing content, incorrect relations, hard-negative leakage, unsupported content, or global constraint violations. Automatic Judges. Low-level metrics such as PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS [ 12 , 35 , 49 ] are poorly matched to reference faithfulness, motivating model-based judges and rubric evaluators [ 5 , 9 , 15 , 43 , 20 , 33 , 44 , 26 , 52 , 53 , 38 , 7 , 58 , 27 , 8 , 57 ] . However, a holistic judge can conflate realism with correctness when many references are present [ 21 , 32 , 34 , 50 , 17 ] . LongBananaEval evaluates checklist-level evidence with routed critics and tools, and we report its validity through Pearson, Spearman, and Kendall agreement with reviewed expert labels [ 31 , 25 , 13 ] . B.7 Checklist Item Template A checklist item is valid only if it is visually verifiable, reference-grounded, and non-redundant where possible. Positive items evaluate whether a required fact is present and correctly bound. Negative items are Content Fidelity checks that evaluate whether a distractor or unsupported element is absent: completely absent is labeled 2, visually suspicious or ambiguous leakage is labeled 1, and full erroneous appearance is labeled 0. Items requiring specialist interpretation include a short reference note identifying the evidence to inspect. B.8 Annotation Interface and Protocol Annotators view the reference set, target instruction, generated output, checklist item evidence, and explicit 0/1/2 scoring instructions. The human-audited multi-system output pool covers all 90 cases and all 10 reported generation conditions, yielding 900 generated outputs. Human reviewers assign 46,700 reviewed checklist labels by applying all 4,670 benchmark checklist items to every reported condition. The three rubric dimensions are computed from the corresponding checklist labels. The protocol uses primary annotation, second-pass review, third-pass expert adjudication, an independently double-labeled subset of 7,005 labels, stratified audit sampling, schema validation, and score-stability checks. The annotation audit records include qualification and training criteria, review status, correction logs, adjudication logs, audit outcomes, and stability summaries without exposing annotator identities. Appendix C LongBanana -Eval Details C.1 LLM and Agent Usage Declaration LongBananaEval uses LLM or MLLM components as rubric critics, tool routers, and evidence-analysis modules. All automatic judging runs use Gemini 3 Flash as the shared judging backbone for both the agentic checklist judge and the single-pass VLM judge; in the agentic checklist judge, Gemini 3 Flash also drives tool routing and evidence analysis. LongBanana -Gen uses OpenCode, OpenClaw, and Hermes-Agent as harnesses. All three harnesses use Claude Sonnet 4.6 as the shared underlying agent model for planning, tool selection, and prompt revision. D.1 Direct Baseline Implementations FP-Direct uses the full target prompt throughout generation and varies only the visible reference window. Because a case may contain more reference images than the backend can consume at once, the implementation uses a window of eight references and iterates over the ordered reference inventory, e.g., ref1--ref8 , ref9--ref16 , and so on until all non-background references have been exposed. Each round receives the same complete target prompt; there is no entity-level batching, graph-conditioned relation filtering, explicit state memory, or repair step. PD-Direct is a deterministic staged-composition baseline rather than a free-form planning agent. It uses the same eight-reference backend budget as FP-Direct, but allocates that budget by graph structure rather than by inventory order. Each non-background entity node is represented by one reference image. Starting from the pre-generated background image, PD-Direct composes non-background entities in ordered batches of at most eight entity references, following the staging schedule that is optimal for the case’s entity–relation graph. Let b ⁡ ( v ) ∈ { 1 , … , K } b(v)\in\{1,\dots,K\} be the batch index of entity v v , with K = ⌈ N / 8 ⌉ K=\lceil N/8\rceil , at most eight entity references per batch, and the background fixed at batch 0 0 . Over the (unweighted, directed, possibly cyclic) entity–relation graph G = ( V , E ) G=(V,E) , the schedule minimizes a lexicographic objective: first the number of relations ( u , v ) (u,v) whose anchor is composed later than its dependent, | { ( u , v ) ∈ E : b ⁡ ( u ) > b ⁡ ( v ) } | \lvert\{(u,v)\in E:b(u)>b(v)\}\rvert , a capacity-constrained feedback-arc count that we minimize exactly by integer programming, and, among schedules attaining that minimum, the total relational stretch ∑ ( u , v ) ∈ E | b ⁡ ( u ) − b ⁡ ( v ) | \sum_{(u,v)\in E}\lvert b(u)-b(v)\rvert . We solve it exactly with an integer program (Pass 1 minimizes the feedback count F ⋆ F^{\star} ; Pass 2 minimizes the stretch subject to the feedback count equal to F ⋆ F^{\star} ); each case has a small number of composed entities ( N ≤ 32 N\leq 32 ), so the integer program solves in seconds. At step zero the backend receives the background image plus the first batch’s reference images and produces intermediate_00.png ; at each later step it receives the previous intermediate image plus the next batch’s references, and the final intermediate is saved as target/target_image.png . For a case with N = 32 N=32 composed entities this yields K = ⌈ N / 8 ⌉ = 4 K=\lceil N/8\rceil=4 batches and four intermediate images. The prompt at each step is constructed deterministically from fixed staged-composition instructions, the scene prompt, the entity types in the current batch, and all graph edges incident to that batch (there is no per-step cap on edges, so every relation is presented at the step of its later endpoint), followed by constraints to preserve existing content and maintain consistent lighting, scale, and perspective. Reference images and the background are generated upstream from the entity catalog, scene specification, and entity–relation graph; PD-Direct only stages their use during target construction. The benchmark is intentionally high-density, which improves diagnostic resolution but makes expert annotation expensive. LongBananaEval depends on backbone multimodal models and verification tools, so model identifiers and tool versions must accompany final results. E.2 Broader Impact Details The benchmark is designed for measurement rather than deployment, but it evaluates systems capable of generating realistic composite images from many references. Positive uses include model auditing and failure diagnosis. Risks include deceptive media, impersonation, privacy leakage, biased reference coverage, and copyright-sensitive reuse. Mitigations include filtering unsafe content, documenting provenance, restricting problematic assets, and providing a project issue tracker for reporting release problems at https://github.com/TIGER-AI-Lab/LongBanana/issues .
