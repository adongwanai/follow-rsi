These results mark a paradigm shift in slate generation: moving beyond human-engineered heuristics toward a general learned policy creates a scalable path for further algorithmic optimization and compute-driven improvement. 1 Introduction Industrial recommender systems are typically organized as cascades that retrieve candidates and refine them with increasingly accurate pointwise scoring models [ 10 ] . The scoring stages of these cascades have benefited from scaling. Architectures such as HSTU, Wukong, and OneTrans consistently translate additional data and computation into improved ranking accuracy [ 52 , 53 , 56 ] . Slate generation, however, has not followed the same trajectory. The slate ultimately exposed to users is still assembled by an expert-engineered system, built around hand-crafted rules and specialized components for tasks such as scattering, quota allocation, de-duplication, fatigue control, and commercial boosting. These are combined with a shallow model that scores candidates under a strict latency budget and with manually tuned fusion weights. Slate generation is a combinatorial problem, and only a small part of the search space is reachable under this design. Extra computation at serving time is therefore spent on a more exhaustive search over this restricted space, and expanding the space requires additional rules and components. Slate quality thus improves with expert knowledge and engineering effort rather than with data and computation. We instead formulate slate generation as a learning problem. A reward model is trained on logged user feedback to evaluate complete slates, so the objectives that shape a slate are learned from data rather than encoded by hand. A single general policy is then optimized against this reward to generate slates from the candidate pool, and the search space is no longer restricted by hand-crafted rules but covers all feasible slates over the candidates. The reward model is used only during training, and its capacity is therefore limited by offline resources rather than by the serving budget. With a larger reward model and a deeper policy, additional data and computation now translate into better slates, exactly the scaling behavior that the expert-engineered system lacks. What changes is the role of the rules rather than their presence. Hard constraints are enforced during decoding, and soft preferences are incorporated into the reward. Figure 1 contrasts the rule-based expert system with the learning-based policy. Figure 1 : Two paradigms for slate generation. The rule-based expert system executes expert rules around a shallow model; the learning-based policy learns from logged slates through a reward model used only in training, and a single deep model generates the slate, with rules retained only as auxiliary constraints. We instantiate the unified reranking and mix-ranking formulation in a large-scale industrial recommendation system for e-commerce. Candidates—product cards, short videos, livestreams, and advertising cards—are supplied by distinct upstream business channels and compete for positions within a single exposure slate under multiple objectives and business constraints. Recent advances provide the necessary mechanisms. Reinforcement-learning post-training demonstrates that autoregressive policies can be optimized directly against sequence-level rewards [ 33 , 41 ] . Generative recommendation systems similarly cast retrieval and ranking as sequential generation [ 39 , 52 , 11 ] , and reported studies show performance gains with model scale and training compute [ 52 , 11 ] . Instantiating the formulation in production, however, introduces three challenges. First, unlike language generation over a fixed vocabulary, slate generation selects from a request-specific pool of pre-scored candidates. Second, reward is defined over the complete slate rather than individual items. An item’s utility depends jointly on its position, neighboring items, and the slate’s composition across content types. Slate-level feedback is therefore non-decomposable into independent item-level supervision. Third, every generated slate must satisfy production business constraints, whereas unconstrained autoregressive generation provides no such guarantees. These challenges lead to the central question of this work: can a single learned policy, optimized offline against slate-level rewards, replace the expert-engineered system that assembles the slate today? OneTrans-GSR ( G enerative S late R anking) answers this question in production. The system models slate generation as autoregressive decoding over the per-request candidates. A single decoder receives them from the upstream fine-ranker and emits the exposure slate directly, so no separate rule-based assembly engine, specialized components, or downstream reranking remain on the serving path. The decoder is the only model served at this stage. The training signal, in turn, comes from a slate-level reward model that scores complete slates rather than summing independent item scores. The decoder and the reward model are both built from OneTrans blocks [ 56 ] and use the fine-ranking score as a candidate feature. OneTrans thus serves as a shared backbone across fine ranking, reward modeling, and slate generation. Deployed at full traffic, OneTrans-GSR paves the way for gradually integrating fine ranking into slate generation. We train the decoder in two stages. The decoder is first pretrained on long user-behavior sequences with a next-token prediction loss, and then post-trained with reinforcement learning (RL) against the reward model. For the post-training stage we propose Geometric-Mean Reward Policy Optimization (GMR-PO), a method for multi-objective policy optimization. GMR-PO aggregates the reward model’s scores for multiple objectives into a scalar reward. The scalar reward and the reward for business preferences are standardized independently, and the resulting advantages are combined to optimize the policy. Soft business preferences thus serve as training signals, while advertiser value and hard business constraints are controlled during decoding. In summary, we formulate slate generation as a learning problem and validate the resulting policy in production. Our contributions are as follows: • A unified generative policy for reranking and mix-ranking. We formulate slate generation over heterogeneous, request-specific candidate pools as a constrained autoregressive policy. OneTrans-GSR jointly handles candidate selection, content-type mixing, and business-aware ordering, while decoupling slate-level evaluation from online serving to eliminate the need for a separate online evaluator or downstream reranking module. • Slate-level reward modeling. We introduce a reward model that scores complete slates rather than aggregating per-item predictions, providing slate-level signals for conversion and long-term objectives. Used only at training time, its capacity is bounded by offline resources rather than serving costs, so it can be much larger than any model that could be served online. • Multi-objective and rule-aware post-training. We develop GMR-PO to jointly optimize utility and business objectives. GMR-PO aggregates the reward model’s scores across objectives into a scalar reward. The scalar reward and a separate reward encoding soft business preferences are standardized separately in the advantage computation. The policy learns how these objectives affect slate construction within a
unified optimization procedure, and business requirements are satisfied within generation rather than repaired by downstream reordering. • Production validation and compute scaling. We validate OneTrans-GSR through controlled offline studies, scaling analyses, system benchmarks, and production A/B tests. Against a strong Generator–Evaluator baseline [ 14 , 42 ] , the deployed system lifts GMV/u by 2.63%, advertiser value by 2.06%, and repurchase rate by 2.03%, while reducing end-to-end latency across the reranking and mix-ranking stages by 13 % 13\% . In offline evaluation, slate quality improves consistently with reward-model, decoder, and rollout compute. 2 Related Work Reranking, Mix-Ranking, and Slate Generation Reranking takes the candidates scored by the upstream ranker and decides which items are shown and in what order. Mix-ranking combines product cards, short videos, livestreams, and advertising cards from distinct upstream business channels into a single exposure slate under business constraints. Existing approaches differ mainly in the extent to which these decisions are learned. Early methods retain the candidate set and update item scores. MMR selects items by balancing relevance against redundancy [ 5 ] , while context-aware rankers condition each candidate’s score on the other items in the list, as in DLCM [ 1 ] , RankFormer [ 4 ] , MIR [ 48 ] , and PRM [ 35 ] . A further step is to optimize the slate as a whole: Exact-k treats slate selection as a combinatorial problem [ 17 ] , Seq2Slate learns an autoregressive ranking policy from click logs [ 3 ] , and SlateQ decomposes long-term slate value to make reinforcement learning tractable [ 21 ] . Beyond one-step CTR, Liu and Liu optimize expected session clicks through reinforcement learning while accounting for user departure [ 28 ] . Closer to our setting, the generator itself is learned and served. GRN trains a generator by policy gradient against an evaluator used only during training and deploys the generator at Taobao [ 15 ] , while NAR4Rec generates the slate non-autoregressively [ 40 ] ; Section 6.2 compares autoregressive and non-autoregressive decoding in our setting. Some production systems instead retain an online evaluator: PRS [ 14 ] and PIER [ 42 ] select the final slate from alternatives proposed by separate generators. OneTrans-GSR replaces the separate reranking, mix-ranking, and online evaluation stages with a single policy that generates the final slate directly, while slate evaluation is confined to training. Generative Recommendation. Generative recommendation formulates retrieval or ranking as sequence generation. Early work focuses on item generation: retrieval based on semantic IDs decodes item identifiers [ 39 ] , while session generation extends this approach to entire sessions [ 11 ] . Scalable sequence backbones for generative recommendation include HSTU and OneTrans [ 52 , 56 ] . Generative rerankers instead produce complete slates, with designs varying along two axes. The first is the decoding scheme: autoregressive, non-autoregressive, or hierarchical. The second is the training signal: preference alignment, supervision from an offline evaluator, or rewards defined over multiple slate-level objectives [ 27 , 46 , 44 , 47 , 31 , 34 ] . Recent work advances both axes. On the decoding side, methods seek to retain the quality of autoregressive generation at lower inference cost through distillation into non-autoregressive models, confidence-guided denoising, and graph-based slate generation [ 54 , 23 , 51 ] . On the training side, recent methods explore group-relative objectives, globally consistent action spaces based on semantic IDs, and objectives that account for constraints or exploration [ 55 , 22 , 24 , 57 ] . OneTrans-GSR combines autoregressive decoding with slate-level reward learning to unify reranking and mix-ranking over a heterogeneous candidate pool. A single decoder, trained against an offline reward model, serves the slate directly without an online evaluator or downstream reranker. Business requirements enter the same generation process: soft preferences provide training rewards, while strict constraints are applied during decoding. Preference Alignment and Multi-Objective Policy Optimization. Optimizing a policy against learned rewards raises two questions: how to update the policy and how to combine multiple rewards. Policy updates have been studied extensively in language modeling. In the standard setting for preference alignment, a reward model is trained from preference data, and the policy is subsequently optimized against its scores [ 8 , 33 ] . Direct preference methods avoid an explicit reward model and optimize policies directly from preference data, as in DPO [ 38 ] and its extensions IPO, SimPO, and S-DPO [ 2 , 30 , 7 ] . Group-relative methods instead estimate advantages by comparing outputs sampled for the same input. GRPO computes advantages within a group of sampled outputs [ 41 ] , while GSPO and GMPO differ in how they aggregate importance ratios or per-token terms [ 59 , 58 ] . Existing alignment methods therefore differ primarily in the policy update rather than in the treatment of multiple reward dimensions. The second question concerns multiple objectives. Reinforcement learning commonly reduces multiple objectives to a scalar reward, an approach also used in e-commerce recommendation [ 19 , 26 ] and, more recently, in preference optimization. GDPO standardizes each reward dimension before aggregation, reducing dominance caused by differences in raw reward scales [ 29 ] . SAGE separates the multi-objective advantage from the importance ratio and computes the latter geometrically over the sequence [ 49 ] . OneTrans-GSR uses rewards defined over complete slates and combines short- and long-term utility objectives with soft business preferences. GMR-PO aggregates the reward model’s scores across utility objectives into a scalar reward. The scalar reward and a separate reward encoding soft business preferences are standardized independently before the resulting advantages are combined. Policy optimization uses a clipped surrogate objective with KL regularization on fresh rollout batches, without additional correction for off-policy samples (Section 5.4 ). 3 Problem Formulation For each request, OneTrans-GSR receives a pool 𝒞 = { c 1 , … , c N c } \mathcal{C}=\{c_{1},\dots,c_{N_{c}}\} of N c N_{c} candidates. During prefill, candidates interact with the user context and one another through a block-causal attention mask. Let H H , C C , and P gen P_{\mathrm{gen}} denote the sets of token positions for user behavior, candidates, and generated slate tokens, respectively. Tokens at positions in H H retain the masks of their original behavior sequences; tokens at positions in C C attend to all tokens in H ∪ C H\cup C ; and tokens at positions in P gen P_{\mathrm{gen}} attend to H ∪ C H\cup C and earlier generated positions. Prefilled tokens never attend to generated positions. Each candidate representation is therefore conditioned on the user and the complete candidate pool before generation begins, while slate generation remains autoregressive. Prefill allows candidates to interact from the first layer. By contrast, output-only scoring compares candidates only at the final dot-product head, while cross-attention introduces projected candidate representations within the decoder layers. Section 6.2 compares the three designs. The selected slate is served directly without RM-based selection or separate downstream reranking or mix-ranking. Gradient Isolation for the Candidate Embeddings. The candidate table 𝐄 𝒞 \mathbf{E}_{\mathcal{C}} provides both the input embeddings for prefill and the output vocabulary for candidate selection. Using the same table for input encoding and output scoring creates two distinct gradient paths. Gradients from the input path encourage the embeddings to encode item semantics. For positive advantages, gradients from the output path can pull selected candidates toward the decoder state and push unselected candidates away; negative advantages reverse these directions. Gradients from the output path may become correlated across requests. Frequently selected items from the same category can consequently receive similar updates, reducing embedding diversity and contributing to collapse. Embedding collapse has also been reported when scaling recommendation models [ 18 ] . We stop the gradient through the candidate vector on the output path, including its normalization, in Eq. ( 8 ). The table 𝐄 𝒞 \mathbf{E}_{\mathcal{C}} is therefore updated only through the input path. Section 6.2 reports the corresponding ablation. The separation resembles the distinct input and output embeddings used by word2vec [ 32 ] , while SimSiam similarly uses stop-gradient to prevent collapse [ 6 ] . 5.2.3 KV Cache for Parallel Rollouts Parallel generation maintains two KV caches. The prefill cache stores states for the user behavior sequences and candidate pool, is computed once per request, and is shared across parallel samples. Each sample maintains a private autoregressive cache for its generated tokens. The autoregressive cache grows by one token at each step, and parallel samples remain independent without pruning or reordering. The prefilled context accounts for most of the sequence length, so replicating it for every sample would dominate cache memory. Sharing the prefill cache keeps peak GPU memory about 17% above the baseline without candidate prefill. The two caches support both training rollouts and online serving. Relative to the preceding BF16 configuration, KV caching improves training throughput by 2.09 × 2.09\times and serving throughput by 4.52 × 4.52\times . The corresponding cumulative speedups over the eager baseline are 4.85 × 4.85\times and 11.57 × 11.57\times , respectively (Table 10 ). 5.3 Incorporating Business Logic In industrial systems, reranking and mix-ranking must balance utility objectives with business requirements. The non-autoregressive variant predicts all slate positions in parallel while retaining the same inputs, RM, business rules, and training configuration. The variant without RL post-training retains decoder pretraining and supervised adaptation but omits optimization against the RM. The RM-score comparisons therefore indicate that post-training contributes most strongly to the conversion objectives. Candidate Interaction. We compare the output-only, cross-attention, and prefill designs described in Section 5.2.1 , which introduce candidate interaction at progressively earlier stages of the decoder. Output-only scoring defers candidate comparison to the final dot-product head, cross-attention introduces candidates within each decoder layer, and prefill places them directly in the input context. Earlier interaction consistently improves RG, with prefill achieving the strongest result on all three objectives. Prefill also avoids the per-layer overhead introduced by cross-attention and is therefore used as the default design. Gradient Isolation and Input Prior. The decoder selects candidates, allocates exposure across content types, and determines their order through one generation process. A slate-level RM learns from logged user feedback and provides rewards for click, order, and GMV objectives, complemented by a delayed repurchase proxy for LTV. GMR-PO aggregates these objectives into a scalar reward and combines the standardized RM advantage with a separately standardized advantage derived from soft business preferences. Hard masks enforce strict requirements, while finite logit adjustments provide soft control during decoding. Because the RM is used only for offline post-training, its capacity can scale independently of the online serving model. In the evaluated production system, OneTrans-GSR replaces the Generator–Evaluator cascade. Offline experiments show positive RM-estimated utility gains relative to logged G-E slates. The generated slates also exhibit greater diversity and lower redundancy and fatigue than score-based ranking baselines, including when additive logit adjustments are disabled. The scaling experiments show improved RM discrimination or RM-estimated policy RG across the tested capacity and sampling configurations. In the randomized production A/B test, OneTrans-GSR increases GMV/u by 2.63 % 2.63\% , advertiser value by 2.06 % 2.06\% , and repurchase rate by 2.03 % 2.03\% , while reducing end-to-end latency across the reranking and mix-ranking stages by 13 % 13\% . Although evaluated in a single production system, OneTrans-GSR is based on a general formulation: generating an ordered slate from a request-specific candidate pool using slate-level rewards and constrained autoregressive decoding. Different applications may define their own inputs, objectives, and constraints, while the current production experiment validates the resulting system as a whole rather than attributing its gains to individual components. This formulation applies broadly to recommendation, search, advertising, and other settings that construct ordered slates under multiple objectives and constraints. By keeping slate evaluation offline and serving a single policy that generates the final slate directly, OneTrans-GSR provides a scalable framework for learning these decisions from data while retaining explicit business control. Its ability to benefit from additional reward-model, policy, and sampling compute suggests a promising direction for learned slate generation beyond e-commerce reranking and mix-ranking.
