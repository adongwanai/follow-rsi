Abstract Recent work on robot manipulation has focused on specialist policies trained on demonstrations. We show that generalist AI models can control manipulators more reliably than specialist policies such as vision-language-action models (VLAs) and world-action models (WAMs). We systematically analyze agentic manipulation policies (AMPs), in which a generalist AI model commands the manipulator directly, adapting its actions as the task unfolds. We present AMP1, a reference harness that runs unchanged across single-arm, bimanual, and mobile manipulation. AMP1 achieves state-of-the-art results on the RoboLab-120 and CaP-Gym benchmarks, with no demonstrations or task-specific training. On RoboLab-120, AMP1 reaches an 85.6% success rate zero-shot, compared to 49.2% and 42.9% for leading VLA and WAM policies. The agent succeeds zero-shot in 104 of 108 real-world trials spanning twelve tasks and three robot instances, reducing failure rates by 20x and 15x relative to strong VLA and WAM baselines. The basic harness supplies no grasping, pushing, or placing skills; the agent develops its own strategies, such as searching for hidden objects, using objects as tools, and calibrating its cameras. With the addition of a persistent memory store, the agent can also learn from experience: an augmented harness with a memory representation that is carried across episodes lowers the failure rate on RoboDojo bimanual manipulation tasks by 4.6x relative to zero-shot deployment. Our analysis suggests that AMPs are substantially more general and robust than specialist manipulation policies, providing a promising foundation for robot manipulation. Task instructions given to AMP1 for each episode are shown below the corresponding image sequence. 1 Introduction In nature, manipulation draws on general intelligence. Animals can recruit their memory, reasoning, and problem-solving faculties when interacting with objects, with cognitive processes continuing to shape action as it unfolds [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] . In robotics, much recent work has focused on models specialized for robot control, including vision-language-action models (VLAs) and world-action models (WAMs), trained or fine-tuned on robot demonstrations [ 4 , 5 ] . Generalist AI models have also been applied to manipulation, often through skill orchestration or offline program synthesis. In the first setting (skill orchestration), the model selects and sequences low-level skills such as grasping and placing [ 6 , 7 , 8 ] . It can revise the plan as new observations arrive, but the available skills limit the generalist model’s control authority and the granularity at which it can command the manipulator. Since the generalist model’s interactions with the world are mediated by the provided skills, it is constrained by the skills’ limitations. In the second setting (offline program synthesis, sometimes known as ‘code-as-policies’), the model writes a control program before execution [ 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 ] . The program can incorporate perception and feedback, but the generalist model is not in the loop and cannot reconsider the overall control strategy as the task unfolds. A key obstacle to direct manipulation has been the limited spatial cognition of generalist frontier models [ 13 ] . However, spatial cognition has been advancing [ 14 , 15 ] , with recent models demonstrating convincing spatial planning, sensorimotor coordination, and embodied fluid intelligence [ 16 , 17 ] . These advances make a third approach increasingly viable: the generalist model remains in the control loop and commands the manipulator directly, revising its actions as the task unfolds [ 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 ] . We refer to this category as Agentic Manipulation Policies (AMPs). In this paper, we systematically analyze agentic manipulation policies across models and harness configurations. We present AMP1, a reference harness that provides visual observations, code execution, and direct control of manipulator motion. Paired with frontier models, AMP1 achieves state-of-the-art zero-shot results on the RoboLab-120 [ 22 ] and CaP-Gym [ 18 ] benchmarks.
The agent succeeds in 104 of 108 real-world trials spanning twelve tasks and three robot instances, compared to 49 for FLUX 3 Action [ 23 ] and 30 for π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} [ 24 ] , thus reducing the failure rate by 15x and 20x, respectively, relative to these WAM and VLA baselines.
See Fig. 1 and the supplementary video for curated highlights. We document emergent behaviors that arise as the agent brings its broader cognitive faculties to bear on manipulation. When a book lies flat on a table and cannot be grasped as is, the agent pushes it to the table edge to expose a graspable surface. When a target object is hidden, the agent moves occluding objects out of the way to find it. When a large fruit is too wide to grasp, the agent pins it against a heavier object, slides the gripper underneath, and lifts it like a forklift. Failed actions can themselves supply information: the agent infers an unseen shelf from a blocked motion and changes its grasp and approach. On the real robot, the agent diagnoses camera calibration errors from discrepancies between commanded and observed motion, then compensates for them in subsequent actions. None of these behaviors are specified in the prompt or supplied as skills in the harness. The generalist model develops them within each episode by reasoning about geometry, contact, and the consequences of its own actions. We also investigate few-shot and continual learning from the agent’s own experience. We equip AMP1 with a persistent memory, allowing the agent to record useful observations, strategies, and code after reflecting on its successes and failures. Experience accumulates in this memory bank, while the model’s weights remain fixed. No expert demonstrations or gradient updates are required. We evaluate this approach on the 42 bimanual manipulation tasks of RoboDojo [ 25 ] . Practicing individual tasks raises the success rate on unseen layouts from 26.4% to 84.1%, above all entries on the benchmark’s leaderboard. We also test whether the agent can accumulate knowledge across tasks via continual learning. In this setting, the agent attempts each task once, in sequence, and updates its memory representation in the course of this continual deployment. A single pass through the tasks more than doubles the success rate, to 57.6%. Learning transfers to tasks not yet attempted without erasing gains on tasks already encountered. Experience improves efficiency as well: an agent with memory uses about 35% fewer model calls in comparison to zero-shot deployment. We conduct controlled experiments to isolate the effects of the model, its reasoning effort, and the harness’s perception and motion interfaces. The choice of model has the largest effect. For example, AMP1’s success rate on RoboLab-120 is 82.8% with Opus 5.5, 51.6% with Haiku 5.5, and 35.2% with Opus 4.5. More reasoning helps all evaluated models. The value of additional harness support depends on the model. Weaker models are helped by assistive grasping and placing routines, while stronger models perform best without these routines. Stronger models also cope better with missing sensory information. With the AMP1 harness, Opus 5.5 achieves a success rate of 82.8% with full sensory input, 81.1% without direct depth input, and 75.8% without depth or camera calibration. While the overall success rate does not suffer much, sensory impairment takes a toll on efficiency. The agent routes around limited sensory input by reconstructing 3D points via triangulation across views and, when needed, estimates camera calibration from images taken at known gripper poses. The mean number of model calls per episode rises from 34.8 with full sensory input to 46.3 without direct depth input and 53.5 without depth or camera calibration. A weaker model cannot compensate for impoverished perception: the success rate of AMP1 with Haiku 5.5 on RoboLab-120 collapses from 51.6% with full sensory input to 10.7% without depth or camera calibration. Our experiments also indicate that replacing visual input with textual descriptions has a sharply detrimental effect, for example cutting the success rate of AMP1 with Opus 5.5 from 82.8% to 36.8%; this contrasts with prior work, which recommended replacing visual input with textual descriptions [ 18 ] . Our results point to agentic manipulation policies as a promising foundation for generalist manipulation. AMPs are not yet practical, as the robot spends much of each episode waiting for the model to reason. But neither are the alternatives: the strongest VLA and WAM entries on the RoboLab-120 leaderboard at the time of writing succeed in 49.2% and 42.9% of the episodes, respectively, in contrast to AMP1’s 85.6%. A policy that fails every other task is not practical, however quickly it does so. We see a more promising path in making capable policies fast. Recent studies estimate that the cost of a given capability level has fallen by roughly an order of magnitude per year [ 26 , 27 ] . A harness could further reduce waiting by reusing generated code, running feedback loops locally [ 9 , 28 , 29 ] , and planning the next motion while the current one executes [ 30 ] . AMP experience could also be used to synthesize closed-loop onboard controllers [ 29 ] or train compact models through distillation [ 11 , 31 ] . Our analysis suggests that agentic manipulation policies are substantially more general and robust than alternative paradigms, opening the way to broad deployment of generalist manipulation capabilities across hardware embodiments and application domains. We provide quantitative and qualitative results from diverse real-world manipulation tasks. All real-world experiments were conducted using a 7-DoF Franka Research 3 arm equipped with a Robotiq 2F-85 gripper and a three-camera vision system similar to DROID [ 38 ] (left and right shoulder views, plus a wrist-mounted camera). The AMP1 harness was paired with Claude Opus 5.5 at high reasoning effort. The results are reported in Table 1 . We designed 12 challenging short- and long-horizon manipulation tasks and evaluated AMP1 against leading VLA and WAM policies, π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} and FLUX 3 Action [ 24 , 23 ] . The tasks cover a broad range of capabilities, including prehensile and non-prehensile manipulation, spatial and temporal reasoning, semantic grounding, and object exclusion. We include both single- and multi-step tasks, the latter containing two or more subtasks. The results of the real-world trials are presented in Table 1 .
Further details of our experimental setup and evaluation protocols are provided in Sec. 4F .
All tasks are illustrated in the Supplementary Materials ( Fig. S2 ). AMP1 matches or outperforms the baselines on all 12 tasks. It particularly excels in scenarios that require object identification in crowded settings or complex manipulation strategies, such as non-prehensile interaction. Overall, AMP1 achieves a 96.3% success rate, compared to 45.4% for FLUX and 27.8% for π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} . Baseline methods struggle to accurately identify target objects and frequently grasp incorrect objects, including those explicitly excluded by the task prompt. For example, when instructed to “Put all the tools except the hammer in the right bin”, both baselines still occasionally grasp the excluded object. Most real-world struggles of AMP1 are caused by collision, mainly with the surface of the table, the walls of the bin, and self-collision. These collisions occur because the underlying motion planning is sensitive to camera calibration and relies on approximations including simplified robot geometry and unmodeled hardware such as camera mounts and cables.
Collisions can trigger the Franka controller’s collision reflex, which interrupts motion when estimated external forces or torques exceed configured thresholds. The resulting error must be cleared before commanded motion can resume. We provide SDK functions through which the agent can query the arm’s state and request error recovery. Following successful recovery, the agent can replan and continue the task. With this functionality, AMP1 fails in only 4 out of 108 trials. (In two, some cubes fell outside the target plate during pouring. In the other two, a bowl rolled off the table.) Without these functions, our evaluation protocol terminates a trial when a robot controller error interrupts motion; in this condition, AMP1 succeeds in 94 of 108 trials (87.0%; Tab. S1 ). A primary advantage of AMP1 is its capacity for dynamic error recovery and replanning as the task unfolds. For instance, for a task that requires moving a flipped bowl placed on top of a flat bin, where no initial feasible grasp exists, AMP1 recognizes that a direct grasp is infeasible and pushes the bowl to hang from the side of the bin where it can be grasped, thus combining non-prehensile and prehensile techniques ( Fig. S3 A). In contrast, baseline methods often continue pursuing hard or infeasible strategies (e.g., an intractable grasp) until the episode times out. Furthermore, for tasks that require complex semantic understanding, such as covering and uncovering multiple cubes with cups, the baseline methods perform inconsistent actions indicative of challenges with task comprehension. π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} is particularly prone to exhibiting indecisive behavior, repeatedly grasping and dropping a correctly identified target object without completing the placement. A number of case studies, documented in Fig. 1 and the supplementary video, demonstrate that AMP1 can generalize zero-shot to even more complex tasks that require sequential dependency planning via interactive perception: manipulating the environment to uncover and subsequently manipulate hidden objects ( Fig. 1 A), devising effective tool-use strategies ( Fig. 1 B), reasoning through and physically executing a winning strategy in a game of chess followed by putting away the pieces (Fig. 1 C), pushing or sliding to reveal occluded grasps ( Fig. 1 D), and manipulating articulated objects ( Fig. 1 E). 2B Emergent behaviors Beyond aggregate task performance, we examine the manipulation strategies developed by AMP1 during execution. None of the behaviors described below was specified in the prompt or provided as a predefined skill in the harness. The agent develops these strategies while solving tasks, combining geometric reasoning, visual feedback, and physical interaction to construct higher-level manipulation skills. We highlight several examples, some of which are shown in Fig. 3 and the supplementary video. Searching for hidden objects. When the instructed object is not visible from any camera, AMP1 does not stop at what it sees. It reasons about which objects are large enough to hide the target, moves them aside one at a time, and looks again after each move until the target appears ( Fig. 3 A). The robotics literature calls this mechanical search [ 39 ] , a form of interactive perception [ 40 ] : the agent acts to gain information, not only to make progress on the task. Moving the goal instead of the object. When an object cannot be grasped, AMP1 sometimes brings the goal to the object instead. In one task, “Put the bowl on the plate”, the bowl lay upside down on a bin, too wide for the gripper and with no edge the fingers could reach under ( Fig. 3 B). AMP1 instead grasped the plate by its rim and slid it up against the side of the bin, then pushed the bowl across the bin with the open gripper until it fell off the edge onto the plate. The bowl was never held: it reached its goal by non-prehensile manipulation alone. Figure 3 : Emergent behaviors in AMP1. Using objects as tools. In some cases in which an object lies beyond the arm’s reach, AMP1 can use another item in the scene as a tool. It first establishes from inverse kinematics that the object is out of reach, and by how much. It then searches the scene for a tool long enough to cover that distance and reasons about how to use it to apply force, for example pulling rather than pushing so that the object moves toward the arm ( Fig. 3 C). Once the object is within reach, it sets the tool aside and grasps the object directly. None of this is described in the prompt, suggesting that AMP1 can at times improvise tool use from its understanding of geometry and contact. This strategy parallels tool selection in New Caledonian crows, which choose sticks long enough to retrieve otherwise inaccessible food [ 41 ] , and has precedents in robotics [ 42 ] . Scooping objects too wide to grasp. When an object, such as a large pomegranate, is wider than the gripper’s 85-mm stroke in every direction, AMP1 sometimes pins it against a heavier object so that it cannot roll or slide away ( Fig. 3 D). It then slides the open gripper beneath it and lifts it like a forklift to carry it to the goal. Like pushing an object to the table edge, this strategy exploits contact with the environment to achieve a grasp the gripper cannot achieve alone, a capability known as extrinsic dexterity [ 43 ] . Pushing to enable grasping. When an object is too wide to grasp where it lies, AMP1 pushes it toward the edge of its supporting surface until part of it overhangs, giving the gripper a narrower part to hold ( Fig. S3 A). When objects stand too close together to grasp one at a time, it pushes them apart ( Fig. S3 B). In both cases, AMP1 treats the scene as something it can rearrange rather than a fixed input: when no grasp is feasible, it first acts to create one, a strategy known as pre-grasp manipulation [ 44 ] . Correcting calibration errors. On the physical robot, camera calibration is imperfect, and the resulting errors in estimated poses can be too large for precise manipulation. When a motion misses its target, AMP1 compares where the gripper appears in the images with where it was commanded to go, attributes the offset to the calibration and corrects it ( Fig. S3 C). It then uses the corrected calibration for the rest of the episode. The same reasoning lets it calibrate the wrist camera from scratch when no calibration is given (Supplementary Materials, Sec. S4.1 ). Gentle placement of long objects. When placing a long object, AMP1 first rests one end on the surface, then lowers the other along an arc ( Fig. S3 D). This strategy uses surface contact to support the object during lowering, suggesting reasoning about contact and the physical consequences of the placement motion. Reorienting objects by hooking and pivoting the gripper. For objects lying on their side, AMP1 sometimes closes the gripper, inserts the closed fingers into the object’s opening, and uses them as a hook while moving the gripper along an arc until the object stands upright ( Fig. S4 A). For objects placed upside down, it first grasps an outer feature to tip the object onto its side, then hooks and pivots it. Inferring environmental constraints from physical interaction. In one task, AMP1 must place an object on a shelf whose top is not visible from any camera, so that part of the shelf is missing from its point cloud. Its first placement motion fails when the arm is blocked by this unseen geometry. From the gap between the commanded and reached end-effector poses, AMP1 infers that an upper shelf lies just outside its field of view, assumes it sits at the edge of the visible region to leave a safety margin, and adjusts its grasp and approach angle accordingly ( Fig. S4 B). AMP1 thus treats a failed action as a measurement, using physical interaction to recover information its cameras cannot provide. Together with its behavior in the wrist-camera setting ( Sec. 2D.3 ), where it moves the arm back to see more of the scene, this suggests that AMP1 uses action as a source of information, not only as a means of changing the scene. Mechanical interlocking grasps for heavy objects. Through interaction with the environment, AMP1 discovers that friction-based grasps are brittle for heavy objects and prone to slippage. When the object’s geometry allows it, AMP1 switches to a mechanical interlocking grasp. For example, to lift a heavy drill, instead of grasping it from the top, it closes the fingers around the top of the handle, just below the drill’s head ( Fig. S4 C). Upon lifting, the head rests on the fingers, which yields an inherently stable grip that does not depend on friction. Beyond these examples, we measured how often AMP1 composes the same building blocks across many tasks ( Fig. 3 E). We matched the code AMP1 executed and the intentions stated in its reasoning, in 1,200 RoboLab episodes, against a set of skill patterns, and report a pattern as a skill only if it recurs (a) in at least five episodes and (b) in at least two tasks; the first condition excludes rare improvisations and spurious matches, and the second excludes procedures that serve a single task. Because the harness provides no grasping, placing, or pushing primitives ( Sec. 4A.3 ), AMP1 builds these skills itself, anew in each episode ( Fig. 3 E). (These statistics are reported for AMP1 paired with Opus 5.5.) The most frequent skills are routine geometric habits: AMP1 measures a container before placing into it in 66% of episodes, aligns its grasp with the object’s axes in 61%, and reasons about an object’s long and short axes in 47%. When the harness rejects a proposed motion, AMP1 reads the reason and repairs the plan (40% of episodes). When a motion fails during execution, it diagnoses the cause before replanning (39%). Together, these observations suggest that AMP1 can synthesize manipulation skills from the perception and control interfaces it is given, adapting its strategies to object geometry, execution failures, and contact constraints. Since these behaviors were neither specified in the prompt nor supplied as predefined skills, they provide qualitative evidence that code generation enables the agent to translate its reasoning into task-specific manipulation procedures. S2 ). 2C Learning from Experience Thus far, the agent has been evaluated zero-shot, starting every episode from scratch with no memory of its past experience. In the field, however, a robot may be able to build on its experience. We therefore investigate whether the agent can improve with experience, both within a task via few-shot learning and across tasks via continual learning. In the few-shot setting, the agent practices on the same task with resets. In the continual setting, it learns across different tasks as it tackles them in sequence. We evaluate learning from experience on RoboDojo [ 25 ] , a challenging benchmark that comprises 42 simulated bimanual manipulation tasks across five capability dimensions ( Sec. 4E ).
RoboDojo provides many layouts per task, so a given task can be practiced on certain scenes and tested on others. Unlike in RoboLab-120, the agent receives only RGB images, without depth or camera calibration. While AMP1 approaches saturation on RoboLab-120 zero-shot ( Sec. 2A.1 ), RoboDojo leaves ample headroom for learning from experience. In both the few-shot and the continual settings, the agent keeps whatever it finds useful in a persistent memory ℳ \mathcal{M} , a folder that it reads at the start of every episode. We impose no format on ℳ \mathcal{M} , so it may hold notes, code, or data in any form. Typically, the agent decides to keep a Markdown file of notes (strategies that succeeded or failed, pitfalls and observations, useful constants and parameters) and a Python library of code it found useful. Later episodes can read the notes and import and call the Python code. We refer to the harness augmented by the persistent memory ℳ \mathcal{M} as AMP1+. In the few-shot setting, we let the agent learn a task by practicing it on several layouts until the agent judges that it can reliably succeed or reaches its practice budget. Each practice episode presents a new layout. The only feedback the agent receives is the benchmark’s official binary reward for each episode.
This requires no expert demonstrations, no additional model training, and no reward shaping. In the continual setting, we test whether the agent can accumulate useful knowledge over its lifetime, applying lessons from earlier tasks to later ones. In this setting, the agent tackles an episode of each of the 42 RoboDojo tasks in random order. A persistent memory store ℳ \mathcal{M} is carried across tasks and the agent is instructed to keep only general lessons that may help with any task in the given robot embodiment. Our approach to learning from experience is gradient-free: the agent revises its memory and code, while the model weights remain fixed. This differs structurally from gradient-based approaches to few-shot learning [ 45 ] and continual learning [ 46 , 47 ] , which adapt neural network weights to new experience. Learning without weight updates has precedents in agents that retain verbal reflections [ 48 , 49 ] or accumulate executable skills [ 50 ] . In robotics, ASPIRE retains validated repair knowledge [ 36 ] , RoboSkill consolidates execution records into reusable guidance and code [ 51 ] , and RAX builds autonomous control programs and a persistent skill library through exploration [ 29 ] . ENPIRE automates policy improvement on physical robots, including both program refinement and gradient-based policy training [ 52 ] . We study gradient-free few-shot and continual learning with the generalist model continuing to reason and command the manipulator during execution, measuring both improvement on practiced tasks and transfer and retention across tasks. Both use the same prompt and perception. Basic harness. AMP1 commands motion as a trajectory of timed end-effector poses and gripper commands, and makes all decisions, from where to grasp an object to where to release it. The agent can preview a trajectory before executing it. The harness refuses motions that are unreachable or too fast, but leaves collision checking to the model ( Sec. 4A.3 ). Assistive harness. The assistive harness lets the model delegate grasping and placing through additional routines. The model can request ranked grasp and placement poses. A motion planner executes collision-free picks and placements at the indicated poses. The assistive pick routines check whether the object is held and retry with another grasp if not ( Sec. 4A.3 ). The model still decides which objects to move, in what order, and when the task is complete. It can always take full control via the basic harness, which remains available at all times. Result. The four models with the highest success rates under the basic harness – Gemini 4 Argon, Opus 5.5, Opus 5, and Sonnet 5.5 – all do worse with the assistive harness, by up to 7.5 points ( Fig. 5 B). The six models with the lowest success rates all do better, by up to 17.6 points for the weakest model, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B. The three models in between gain nothing. Analysis. Prior work found that high-level abstractions improved single-turn performance, while multi-turn reasoning could compensate for lower-level interfaces [ 18 ] . Our results indicate that assistive grasping and placing routines help weaker models but reduce the success rate of stronger models.
The assistive routines are imperfect: a grasp can slip or a placement can miss. Weaker models still benefit from the assistance, because the routines succeed more often than their own direct grasping and placement attempts. For stronger models, the assistive routines are on balance a detrimental distraction, because they hide failures that the models could otherwise immediately observe and correct. For example, an assistive pick routine retries internally before the model can intervene, and reports only whether it succeeded, not what went wrong.
With the basic harness, the model sees the result of every atomic action and can correct mistakes as they happen. 2D.3 The effects of sensory impairment RoboLab-120 provides fixed scene cameras and a wrist camera, each with RGB images, depth (D), and calibration (C). On top of these signals, the AMP1 harness offers perception tools (T) that build a scene model from the fused point cloud: they segment it into objects, track the objects over the episode, and render them from any viewpoint ( Sec. 4A.2 ). We ablate this input along two axes. The first is how much geometric information each camera provides: starting from the full input (RGB+C+D+T), we remove perception tools (RGB+C+D), then depth (RGB+C), and then calibration (RGB). The second is which cameras AMP1 sees through: all of them, the scene cameras only, or the wrist camera only. In a final condition (Text), AMP1 receives no images, only textual descriptions of their content, as recommended in prior work [ 18 ] . The rest of the harness and the prompt are unchanged. Stronger models rebuild missing geometry at the cost of efficiency. With a strong model (Opus 5.5) and all cameras, removing geometric information lowers the success rate only modestly but carries a cost in terms of efficiency ( Fig. 5 C). From RGB+C+D+T to RGB+C+D, RGB+C, and RGB, the success rate declines from 82.8% to 81.9%, 81.1%, and 75.8%, while the mean number of model calls per episode rises from 34.8 to 38.7, 46.3, and 53.5, respectively.
AMP1 rebuilds the missing geometry itself. Without the perception tools, it detects objects in the images and lifts them to 3D with depth and calibration. Without depth, it picks matching pixels in zoomed crops of different views and triangulates them through the known calibration. Without calibration, it first estimates the camera parameters, most reliably with the wrist camera, which it can move (a form of active perception [ 53 ] ): it captures images at known gripper poses, matches SIFT features across them [ 54 ] , and fits the focal length, principal point, and camera-to-gripper pose by least squares (Supplementary Materials, Sec. S4.1 ). Weaker models require a more robust sensor constellation. Fig. 5 C compares Opus 5.5 with three weaker models: Sonnet 5.5, Haiku 5.5, and Opus 4.5. While Opus 5.5 is able to compensate for missing sensory input at the cost of efficiency, weaker models are more brittle. The weaker the model, the more sensitive it is to sensory impairment. The success rate of Opus 4.5 drops sharply as soon as the perception tools are taken away. Haiku 5.5 suffers when the perception tools and depth are both withheld. Sonnet 5.5 exhibits material degradation in task completion when the perception tools, depth, and calibration are all removed. The gap between the stronger and weaker models is particularly stark when only RGB input is provided; in this condition, Haiku 5.5 succeeds in only 10.7% of the episodes, against 75.8% for Opus 5.5. Weaker models are unable to compensate for sensory impairment even with more reasoning and computation: as sensory input is ablated, AMP1 with Opus 5.5 takes substantially more model calls to complete the tasks, while with weaker models the number of model calls does not grow by much as their success rate plummets. Images cannot be replaced by text. Prior work recommended substituting direct camera feeds by textual descriptions of their contents [ 18 ] .
We test this with four Claude models: Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, Haiku 5.5, and Opus 4.5. In the Text condition, a second instance of the same model describes what the cameras see. AMP1 receives these descriptions in place of images. The results are summarized in Fig. 5 C. With Opus 5.5, the success rate drops from 75.8% with RGB images to 36.8% with the corresponding textual descriptions. The success rate of weaker models in the Text condition is vanishingly low.
Textual descriptions elide geometric detail, such as whether the gripper is aligned with an object; this has a sharply detrimental effect on task performance. Multiple cameras are not necessary. With a strong model (Opus 5.5), AMP1 is robust to removing all but one camera from the sensor constellation ( Fig. 5 D). Specifically, in the RGB+C+D+T condition, the agent has an 82.8% success rate with all cameras, 80.8% with the scene cameras only, and 80.0% with the wrist camera only. The spread is confined to three percentage points in all image-based conditions. With only the wrist camera, AMP1 compensates by means of active perception [ 53 ] : it first pulls the arm back to bring more of the scene into view. 3 Discussion 3A AMP1 versus other manipulation policies The policies we compare against fall into four classes: learned policies, skill orchestration, offline program synthesis, and agentic manipulation policies (AMPs). Learned policies, including VLAs and WAMs, map observations to actions end-to-end from robot data. Skill orchestration policies can utilize generalist AI models to sequence pre-existing low-level skills; their performance is thus limited by the reliability of those skills. In offline program synthesis, a generalist AI model writes or refines a control program before deployment; the program can incorporate perception and feedback, but the model does not revise the control strategy as the task unfolds. AMPs keep the generalist model in the control loop, where it can interpret new observations and directly command the manipulator. Learned policies. At the time of writing, the strongest learned policies on the RoboLab-120 leaderboard – Lakeside, BiMind v0.5, and FLUX 3 Action – succeed in 51.0%, 49.2%, and 42.9% of episodes, respectively, in contrast to 85.6% for AMP1 when paired with Gemini 4 Argon and 82.8% when paired with Opus 5.5. AMP1 perceives the environment with the aid of RGB, depth, and camera calibration, whereas typical VLAs and WAMs use RGB alone. The richer sensory input, however, does not explain the gap: when paired with Opus 5.5, AMP1 still succeeds in 81.1% of episodes without depth, and in 75.8% with RGB input alone ( Fig. 5 C). AMP1 draws on the broad intelligence of the generalist AI model to decide which objects to move, in what order, and how to react to unanticipated challenges ( Sec. 2B ), whereas a learned policy must acquire its skills from robot data. In addition to their overall poor task performance, specialist manipulation policies are particularly brittle to changes in the phrasing of the task: in the ‘vague’ task specification condition on RoboLab-120, the success rate of FLUX 3 Action drops further to 28.4%, while AMP1 remains at 84.0% and 79.8% with Gemini 4 Argon and Opus 5.5, respectively. Skill orchestration. Harness VLA [ 37 ] and HarnessPAI [ 12 ] delegate motion to a learned VLA policy, so their reliability is bounded by that learned policy.
Like the routines of our assistive harness, but to an even greater degree, a VLA is something of a black box: when it fails, the agent has limited ability to understand the causes and potential mitigations ( Sec. 2D.2 ). HarnessPAI compensates for this limitation through practice, refining its program over several trial runs of the evaluation tasks. AMP1 reaches comparable results zero-shot: 95.2% against 96.5% on LIBERO-PRO, and 100.0% against 96.3% on robosuite ( Sec. 2A.1 ). Offline program synthesis. On LIBERO-PRO and robosuite, ASPIRE [ 36 ] refines a program for each task through repeated execution and debugging, then evaluates the resulting program on held-out configurations. The program can use perception and feedback, but the generalist model does not revise it during deployment. AMP1 is evaluated zero-shot on these benchmarks, with the generalist model in the control loop. Agentic manipulation policies. CaP-Agent0 [ 18 ] also keeps a coding model in the control loop. Its harness supplies a learned grasp planner and a library of synthesized geometric utilities, and uses a separate vision-language model to create a textual summary of changes in the scene. The authors found that this textual representation improved performance relative to direct image input. In our experiments, replacing images with textual descriptions is detrimental to task performance. Adding assistive grasping and placing routines also reduces the success rate of stronger models ( Sec. 2D ). Astranomy is a concurrent AMP that is based on GPT-6 Astra and was evaluated in simulation on the RoboLab-120 benchmark [ 21 ] .
Astranomy reaches 74.8% on RoboLab-120, in comparison to 85.6% for AMP1 with Gemini 4 Argon. Beyond the underlying model, the harnesses differ in three ways. First, Astranomy queries the model for one small end-effector step at a time, whereas AMP1 commands a trajectory. Second, Astranomy disables code execution, whereas AMP1 acts by means of code. Third, the AMP1 harness equips the agent with an editable scene model.
Equalizing the underlying model makes the effect of the harness even more salient: Astranomy reports a 25.3% success rate on a 30-task subset with Opus 5.5, in comparison to AMP1’s 84.7% with the same model on the same subset of tasks. 3B Outlook AMP1 has a number of limitations. First, the policy is slow as the robot spends much of each episode waiting for the model to reason. Second, the model cannot reconsider a trajectory while it executes: our evaluations largely concern quasi-static scenes, whereas a moving target or a slipping object may require a response before the model’s next turn. Notwithstanding these limitations, our results indicate that AMPs constitute a promising foundation for robot manipulation. They cannot yet be deployed in mission-critical settings, but neither can the alternatives. Specialist manipulation policies, such as VLAs and WAMs, are comparably fast but unreliable. AMPs are comparably reliable but slow. We see a promising path to practical manipulation in using AMPs as a starting point for developing manipulation systems that are both reliable and fast. A general trend that supports this optimism is that the cost of a given capability level has fallen by roughly an order of magnitude per year [ 26 , 27 ] . Thus we can expect the cost of a model call (the ‘thinking’ part of an AMP’s execution) to asymptote to zero over time. Beyond these general trends, the harness itself offers several avenues for acceleration. Our few-shot and continual learning experiments show that the agent can retain useful code and reduce the number of model calls across episodes ( Sec. 2C ). Reusing these routines avoids repeatedly writing code for calibration, geometric computations, and basic motions. Fast local feedback controllers could be synthesized from AMP experience [ 29 ] . AMP execution traces could also provide supervision for learned policies [ 11 , 31 ] . The memory (shown dashed) is used when learning from experience ( Sec. 2C ). 4A Harness At the core of AMP1 is a harness through which a generalist AI model perceives, acts, and reflects ( Fig. 6 ). The harness comprises (a) an SDK that is called by the model from a persistent Python interpreter ( Sec. 4B ); (b) a robot server that serves the SDK’s requests: it returns camera images, depth and the robot’s state, segments the point cloud into objects and tracks them over the episode, and checks and executes the robot’s motion; (c) a route policy that sets which of these requests the harness allows; and (d) reference documentation for the SDK, which is appended to the model’s system prompt. Parts (b) and (c) are omitted from Fig. 6 for clarity. In zero-shot evaluation, everything the model knows about the task and the robot comes through the harness: the instruction, the camera images, depth and calibration, the robot’s proprioception, the segmented objects, and the remaining control steps and time. The instruction is read by the model through the SDK in its first turn, together with its first view of the scene. The harness defines two frames for each end-effector. The end-effector (EE) frame is positioned at the base of the gripper. The tool center point (TCP) frame lies at a fixed offset from the EE frame: its origin, the TCP, is centered between the finger pads, and its axes point along the gripper’s approach and closing directions. The model commands motion and defines all grasp geometry at the TCP. We describe the SDK’s functions in three groups: sensing, perception, and motion. Motion comes in two harness variants: the basic harness, our default, in which the model itself commands timed TCP waypoints with gripper commands, and the assistive harness, which adds grasping and placing routines to the basic functions. 4A.1 Sensing AMP1 observes the scene and the robot only through SDK calls in the code it implements, and thus the model decides what to observe and when. The only information added to every result the model receives from executing its code are the remaining control steps and wall-clock time. The model may observe the scene arbitrarily often and it may also execute a motion without prior observation and verify its outcome afterwards from the robot’s state and camera images. The model reads the instruction, the maximum number of control steps in an episode, and the steps that remain. Since the episode terminates when its horizon is reached, the model can plan its motion against it. It can also read the robot specification such as its cameras, its workspace, the joint limits and the default configuration, and, for each end effector, its gripper geometry and TCP frame. The robot’s state comprises the measured joint positions q ~ ∈ ℝ n \tilde{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n} and the poses of the EE and TCP frames in the robot’s base frame; we denote the measured TCP pose by T ~ ∈ S ​ E ​ ( 3 ) \tilde{T}\in SE(3) . It also includes the gripper state g ~ ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] \tilde{g}\in[0,1] (0 open, 1 closed) and the last gripper command g ∈ { 0 , 1 } g\in\{0,1\} . For each camera, the model can read its RGB image, a depth image subsampled from the RGB image’s resolution by a fixed stride, the intrinsics K K , and the camera’s current pose T base , cam ∈ S ​ E ​ ( 3 ) T_{\mathrm{base,cam}}\in SE(3) in the robot’s base frame. Helper functions convert between the image and 3D coordinates. One projects a 3D point into the image through T base , cam − 1 T_{\mathrm{base,cam}}^{-1} and K K , returning its pixel coordinates and its depth along the optical axis, and another inverts this projection for a given pixel and depth. The model can also request a colored point cloud combined from all cameras. With the rendering functions provided by the harness, the model can compose its own views of the scene from these inputs and the perceived objects. It can draw 3D elements, such as points, boxes, object identifiers, target poses or a planned motion, onto a real camera image by projecting them through T base , cam − 1 T_{\mathrm{base,cam}}^{-1} and K K , for example to check whether a planned grasp is aligned with the object. It can also render a point cloud, perceived objects, and the robot from any virtual viewpoint, and request a close-up of an object that combines renders from viewpoints around the object. All of these views are built from the robot’s own sensor data and the perceived scene model ( Sec. 4A.2 ). An image enters the model’s context only when the model chooses to look at it; that is, when its code displays the image. Every displayed image is also saved. Once the context exceeds a fixed number or total size of images, the oldest are replaced by pointers to their saved files, and the model can reload any of them with a dedicated tool. The model can also keep information from earlier turns, such as images and depth maps, in its persistent interpreter, for example to compare a scene before and after a motion. 4A.2 Perception The harness maintains a scene model, a set of objects o j o_{j} , each occupying a region M j ⊂ ℝ 3 M_{j}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3} .
It segments a fused point cloud into voxel clusters and extrudes the height map of each cluster into a closed mesh that bounds M j M_{j} . The union of these regions forms the obstacle region 𝒪 = ⋃ j M j \mathcal{O}=\bigcup_{j}M_{j} against which collisions are checked. The generalist AI model can exclude objects (such as the one it is about to grasp) from a collision check. A stereo matching model can be used to estimate depth from stereo images ( Sec. 4F ). At each update of the scene model, the harness voxelizes the fused point cloud and clusters the voxels into candidate objects as connected components of adjacent voxels. The clustering stops where one object touches another and at sharp color discontinuities. Small fragments, such as an isolated face, are merged into a larger nearby segment of similar color, and close segments of the same color are joined. Segments that remain very small are dropped as noise. Each object is then represented as a closed mesh by extruding its top surface downwards to its lowest observed point. Occluded gaps in the top surface are filled unless the cameras have seen them. Geometry is therefore inferred from depth, while color serves as a secondary criterion that separates touching objects. The scene model is updated in the background whenever a new camera frame is available and no motion is running, before every collision check, and whenever the generalist AI model requests it. Each object o j o_{j} has a unique identifier, a label naming its color and dimensions, its centroid, axis aligned and oriented bounding boxes, and the mesh bounding M j M_{j} . The generalist AI model can list objects, read their points, size and color, filter them by label, color or size, and sort them by distance to a 3D point. Each update matches its objects to those in the previous update by a minimal cost assignment, which pairs two objects only if their centroids lie within a distance d m d_{\mathrm{m}} and their colors and sizes agree within tolerances; a matched object keeps its identifier. When an object is merged with another, split, or keeps its place but no longer matches because its appearance changed, its old identifier resolves to the object that now covers most of its footprint, while an object that moved farther than d m d_{\mathrm{m}} receives a new identifier. An object that is not seen in an observation update, for example, because the arm occludes it, leaves the scene model, but its identifier is reserved for a fixed time. An object of similar color and size that reappears within d m d_{\mathrm{m}} of where it was last seen receives the old identifier. Each update also reports what it removed and changed, so that the generalist AI model can follow how the scene model evolves. The generalist AI model can also correct the scene model. It can grow an object from a single pixel through neighboring points. It can define an object from an axis-aligned or oriented box or from a set of points. And it can replace and remove wrong or spurious objects. Objects that are added by the generalist AI model persist across perception updates and take precedence over the perceived objects they overlap. The system prompt warns the generalist AI model that perception can merge touching objects, split one object in two, or fill in hidden parts incorrectly. It instructs the generalist AI model to check the perceived objects against the camera images and, when they are incorrect, to repair the scene model with these tools rather than compensate for the error in its plans. After every motion, the generalist AI model is instructed to update the scene model and look at the cameras. 4A.3 Motion The generalist AI model writes code that produces a trajectory of TCP poses with gripper commands, given as timed waypoints w i = ( T i , t i , g i ) {w_{i}=(T_{i},t_{i},g_{i})} , i = 1 , … , N i=1,\dots,N . Each waypoint consists of (a) a desired TCP pose T i = ( p i , R i ) ∈ S ​ E ​ ( 3 ) T_{i}=(p_{i},R_{i})\in SE(3) in the robot’s base frame, with position p i ∈ ℝ 3 p_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3} and orientation R i ∈ S ​ O ​ ( 3 ) R_{i}\in SO(3) , (b) the desired arrival time t i t_{i} , and (c) a gripper command g i ∈ { 0 , 1 } g_{i}\in\{0,1\} (open or closed). The model typically constructs each waypoint’s TCP pose relative to a reference it has measured, such as the current TCP pose, an object’s centroid, or the pose of another waypoint, using helper functions that either build a pose from a position and an approach direction (for example, top-down at a given yaw) or shift a pose along its own axes. The harness provides no function that chooses grasps or placements; those decisions are left to the generalist model. For a bimanual robot, the model generates a sequence of waypoints for each arm. A refused trajectory is returned with the reason and the affected steps, so that the model can repair it. Once the entire trajectory has passed these checks, the harness executes it by sending the robot the computed absolute joint position targets, one q k q_{k} per control step, each with the current gripper command. 4B Python Interpreter The agent acts primarily through a Python interpreter, which executes the code implemented by the model in a kernel that persists during the entire episode. The kernel keeps imports, variables, and the functions the model defines between calls, so that the information it generates in one turn can be reused in later ones. Each call returns the code’s printed output, any error with its traceback, and the images the code displays, followed by the remaining control steps and wall-clock time. The model reads the result at the start of its next turn. The model has a number of auxiliary tools in addition to the interpreter, such as reloading an image saved earlier, and ending the episode. In the basic harness, the motion interface only previews, checks, and executes waypoints and gripper commands. Everything in between – from choosing a grasp to measuring a container, pushing an object, or recovering from a refused trajectory – the model implements itself, combining SDK calls with its own geometry code.
In zero-shot evaluation, each episode starts from a new sandboxed kernel, so the model writes its skills from scratch every time. Section S3 in the Supplementary Materials shows how the model builds such skills during one episode, turn by turn.

the time to success of AMP1 is split into execution (Exec.) and reasoning (Reason.). Execution time covers only the intervals in which the robot is carrying out the agent’s commanded actions; reasoning time accumulates the agent’s inference, the execution of its own code, the camera capture, and the 3D perception processing performed after each motion.
In addition, we annotate each of the 12 tasks with three kinds of features, shown in the columns Manip. , Horizon , and Reasoning .
These features let us relate a method’s success to the demands of a task; we explain each of them in more detail below: • Manipulation describes the kinds of actions a task requires.
A task is prehensile if it can be solved by grasping and placing objects alone.
A task is labeled non-prehensile if completing it requires non-prehensile actions, such as pushing, sliding, or nudging an object, so that purely prehensile manipulation cannot solve it.
A task can have both labels when completing it requires both kinds of motion.
For example, in the task “Put the bowl on the plate”, the bowl is flipped and no initial grasp is feasible, so the robot first pushes it to the edge of the bin and then grasps it. • Horizon separates single-step and multi-step tasks.
A single-step task usually involves manipulating only one target object (e.g., picking and placing one object).
A multi-step task requires completing multiple subtasks in sequence,
usually by manipulating multiple objects one after another (e.g., putting all the red things in the bin). • Reasoning lists the kinds of reasoning a task requires to decide what to manipulate and how. Multiple kinds may be called for per task: – Spatial reasoning : understanding a spatial relation, e.g., “to the left of”. – Semantic grounding : identifying objects from their color or category, e.g., “red things” or “canned foods”. – Exclusion : acting on all objects of a class except a named one, e.g., “all the tools except the hammer”. – Ordering : deciding the order in which to act, e.g., comparing cup sizes to nest them. – Affordance : an enabling action is needed to make the next action feasible,
e.g., pushing a flipped bowl to the bin edge before grasping it. – Memory : using information that is no longer visible, e.g., remembering which cup hides the yellow cube in order to uncover it. S3 How AMP1 reasons and acts: a turn by turn example Figure S5 follows one complete episode, on the RoboLab BananaInBowlTask , to demonstrate how AMP1 reasons and acts. The agent runs Claude Opus 5.5 at high reasoning effort with the basic harness. It commands the manipulator by means of timed waypoints with gripper commands. It solves the task in eight turns, in each of which it writes and runs Python code. The episode exhibits a generic decision loop that repeats across tasks. AMP1 gathers the information needed for the decision, builds its own geometric model of the object to manipulate from the perceived points, previews its motion before executing it, and checks the outcome in images and sensor readings before the next decision. The first turn starts from a) the system prompt, which describes the robot, the SDK and the rules of the episode, b) the tool definitions, and c) a first user message that withholds the instruction and the remaining steps. AMP1 therefore has no image of the scene yet. Its first implemented code, which follows an example in the system prompt, reads the instruction (“Pick up the banana and place it in the bowl”) and the remaining steps, lists the objects detected by perception, and shows itself two camera views, one annotated with the object identifiers and bounding boxes. In turn 2, AMP1 builds its own geometric model of the banana. It retrieves the banana’s points, fits an oriented bounding box to them, and reads the end-effector pose and the table height from the robot’s observation. In turn 3, AMP1 plans the grasp. Because the banana is curved, AMP1 reasons that the centroid of its points may not lie on it, so it plans to grasp the banana at the point of the cloud nearest to the centroid, with the fingers closing across the banana’s width, perpendicular to its long axis. It plans timed waypoints for the approach and previews the motion without executing it. The harness confirms that the motion is feasible, and AMP1 then runs a collision check on poses sampled along the previewed path, which reports contacts of the gripper with both the banana and the table. In turn 4, AMP1 accepts the contact with the banana as expected for a grasp, raises the grasp pose slightly so that the fingertips reach approximately the middle of the banana’s height rather than the table, and only then executes the approach. In turn 5, AMP1 checks the wrist camera and finds that the banana sits between the open fingers. It closes the gripper and lifts the banana. In turn 6, AMP1 interprets its gripper reading, which rose from 0.28 after closing to 0.55 after the lift, as the banana slipping or rotating in the grasp. Rather than reusing the bowl position from the first turn, it perceives the scene again to locate the bowl’s center. It then plans to carry the banana high enough to clear the bowl’s rim, and to lower it before opening the gripper. In turn 7, allowing for the banana having rotated in the grasp, it estimates that the banana now hangs about 7 cm below the gripper, so its bottom clears the rim during the carry. It then carries it over the bowl and lowers it. In turn 8, it opens the gripper and plans to raise the empty gripper to look at the result. Finally, RoboLab’s success check detects that the banana has dropped into the bowl and ends the episode. Figure S5 : One episode, turn by turn. S4.1 Self-calibration of the wrist camera This case study follows one episode of the RGB-only ablation

In this setting, AMP1 has access to RGB frames from the two scene cameras and the wrist camera, the robot’s proprioception, and its kinematic model. The harness withholds depth, the camera intrinsics and extrinsics, and perception tools. AMP1 can see the cans in every image, but it has nothing that turns a pixel into a position in the robot’s frame, which it needs before it can plan a grasp. AMP1 closes this gap by calibrating the wrist camera itself. The wrist camera moves with the gripper, whose pose AMP1 knows at every instant, so images taken at known gripper poses form a multi-view camera rig once two unknowns – the camera’s intrinsics and its fixed pose relative to the gripper – are solved for. AMP1 moved the gripper to a few known poses and kept the wrist image at each ( Fig. S6 A), then matched SIFT features across the views. It wrote a bundle adjustment routine that performs a least-squares fit of the camera’s intrinsics, its pose relative to the gripper, and the 3D positions of the matched features. The bundle adjustment minimizes the distance between where each point projects and where it was observed. The first fit converged but placed the camera 2.8 m up the tool axis, because shifts and a yaw alone leave the camera’s offset along that axis unobservable. Only one task fails every trial for both models because of a defect: in FoodPackingByColor , the instruction asks for “the blue object” although the scene contains two, and RoboLab’s verifier accepts only the 102-mm coffee can. In all 10 trials, AMP1 packs the graspable blue SPAM can and fails to hold the oversize coffee can. In the remaining tasks, AMP1 succeeds when it improvises a non-standard grasp ( Sec. 2B ). For objects wider than the gripper, it holds a narrower feature or supports the object from below: it inserts one finger through the hole of a bagel ( BagelsOnPlate and GrabABagel ), and it pinches the pomegranate’s narrow crown or pins the fruit against a heavier object and scoops it up with the open gripper ( FruitsOnPlate and ClearOrganicObjects ; Fig. 3 D). For the heavy drill, it replaces the friction grasp with an interlocking one, closing the fingers around the top of the handle so that the head rests on them once the drill is lifted ( PickDrill and ToolsPickingDrill ; Fig. S4 C). Across the 16 affected tasks (160 trials), AMP1 with Gemini 4 Argon still succeeds in 66 episodes (41%); it fails in 77 because of the object defect and in 17 for other reasons (5 flaky-verifier rejections, 8 AMP1 errors, and 4 provider blocks). Across the 22 tasks affected for AMP1 with Opus 5.5 (220 trials), it succeeds in 107 episodes (49%). Eval failure: Flaky verifier (44 episodes for Gemini 4 Argon, 34 for Opus 5.5). In 13 tasks for Gemini 4 Argon and 9 tasks for Opus 5.5 ( Fig. S10 ), RoboLab’s verifier rejects end states that satisfy the natural-language instruction. Only one task fails every trial for both models: in RecycleCartonsOnBox (“Put the cartons that can be recycled on the box”), the verifier tests whether the cartons lie inside the box, which is closed, whereas the task’s own progress tracker checks whether they rest on top of it. In all 10 trials for both models, AMP1 places both cartons on top of the box, the progress tracker scores them as placed, and the verifier rejects the outcome.
In the remaining tasks, the verifier is overly strict rather than impossible to satisfy: it accepts only a narrow subset of valid placements, so AMP1 still succeeds in 59 of the 130 trials across the 13 tasks with Gemini 4 Argon and in 48 of the 90 trials across the 9 tasks with Opus 5.5. In CookingPickPastaTool (9 verifier rejections for Gemini 4 Argon and 7 for Opus 5.5), the pasta tool ends standing inside the target holder, but the verifier – which requires the tool’s center to lie inside the holder while the tool touches it – does not register it; the single Gemini 4 Argon success came when the episode ended with the gripper still holding the tool inside the holder. In PickUpBluePitcher (6 and 5 rejections), AMP1 lifts the pitcher 8.5 to 12.8 cm above the table, but the verifier’s 5-cm lift check does not trigger. In StackWhiteMugs (5 rejections for Gemini 4 Argon), the verifier rejects deeply nested mugs whose upper bounding-box corners dip below the lower mug’s base; in PickOrangeObject (5 for Opus 5.5), it rejects lifts that sit on the boundary of its height threshold. In MoveBananaToBagelPlate (5 rejections for Gemini 4 Argon), AMP1 places the banana on top of the bagels already on the plate, whereas the verifier requires direct contact with the plate surface. The remaining rejections (9 across 8 tasks for Gemini 4 Argon and 7 across 5 tasks for Opus 5.5) are isolated cases of one or two episodes in which a lifted object or an in-container placement sits just outside the verifier’s geometric threshold, as in PickDrill , GrabABagel , UtensilsInMug , and ToolsPickingAllHammers . In nearly all of these episodes, AMP1 ended the trial believing it had succeeded. AMP1 failures and provider blocks (52 episodes for Gemini 4 Argon, 71 for Opus 5.5). The remaining failures are execution or interpretation errors by AMP1 on objects that the gripper can hold with a standard grasp: 47 episodes across 24 tasks for Gemini 4 Argon and 71 episodes across 33 tasks for Opus 5.5 ( Fig. S10 ). • Timeout (15 episodes for Gemini 4 Argon, 28 for Opus 5.5): AMP1 exhausts the step budget, or ends the episode itself because too few steps remain to finish the remaining objects. Most timeouts follow earlier setbacks that AMP1 recovers from – repeated regrasps after slips, freeing a wedged finger, or searching for a reachable arm configuration on a shelf – but that consume most of the step budget before the last object is placed. • Target dropped (13 for Gemini 4 Argon, 19 for Opus 5.5): A graspable object slips, drops, or tips out of a valid goal state during transport or release, and AMP1 does not recover it before the episode ends. • Collision (9 and 11 episodes): The arm, the gripper, or the held object collides with the scene, such as an unperceived upper shelf tier or a bin wall, or knocks a previously placed object out of position. • Instruction misread (4 for Gemini 4 Argon, 9 for Opus 5.5): AMP1 identifies the objects in the scene correctly but interprets the instruction too narrowly or too broadly. In SpoonsInPot (4 episodes for Gemini 4 Argon and 7 for Opus 5.5) and GreenSpoonsInPot (1 for Opus 5.5), it takes a deep green utensil with a bent neck for a soup ladle rather than a serving spoon and leaves it out (in one trial actively removing it from the pot), whereas RoboLab’s verifier requires it in the pot. In one trial of OneBottleInSquarePail , Opus 5.5 puts a white-capped utility jug into the pail instead of one of the small white plastic bottles. • Gripper not released (3 for Gemini 4 Argon, 4 for Opus 5.5): The object reaches the goal region, but the gripper fails to open or detach cleanly, for example when a bowl rim or a mug handle wedges on the finger linkage, and remains in contact at the end of the episode. • Misperception (3 for Gemini 4 Argon, 0 for Opus 5.5): AMP1 misidentifies an object’s category or geometry from the camera images. In two trials of SpoonsInPot , Gemini 4 Argon takes an oval green serving spoon, partly hidden beneath the ladle, for a flat spatula and leaves it on the plate; in one trial of FoodPacking1Boxes , it mistakes a crease on top of a cracker box for the box’s edge and misplaces the grasp. Finally, 5 episodes with Gemini 4 Argon ( Provider failure: Safety filter block ) end early because the Gemini API blocks a model request under its safety filter, even after automatic retries; no such blocks occur with Opus 5.5. Summary. On the tasks free of both flaky verifiers and object defects, AMP1 succeeds in 96.2% of episodes with Gemini 4 Argon (914 of 950 trials across 95 tasks) and in 93.6% with Opus 5.5 (842 of 900 trials across 90 tasks). On the 84 tasks free of both issues for both models, the success rates are 96.2% and 93.7%, respectively. Without excluding any task, crediting the verifier rejections whose end state satisfies the instruction (44 for Gemini 4 Argon and 34 for Opus 5.5) raises AMP1’s success rate across all 1,200 trials from 85.6% to 89.3% with Gemini 4 Argon and from 82.8% to 85.7% with Opus 5.5. S7 Inference cost of AMP1 This section estimates the inference cost of AMP1 for the models with public API pricing, the Claude models and Gemini 4 Argon, by pricing each episode’s tokens at list price ( Tab. S3 ). With Opus 5.5, a RoboLab-120 episode costs $1.39 on average, about 26 times lower than the $36.40 reported for Astranomy with GPT-6 Astra [ 21 ] . (AMP1 also succeeds more often, 82.8% against 74.8%.) Astranomy queries the model for every small end-effector step, whereas a single AMP1 turn can command a whole trajectory through code, and images enter the model’s context only when it asks for them. Cost per episode is widely reported, but it mixes two factors: the tokens an episode consumes, which the model and harness determine, and the price per token, which the provider sets. Prices do not track capability. Opus 5 is billed at 25% more than Opus 5.5 for input and output tokens and 2.5 times more for cache reads ( Tab. S3 ). Prices also fall quickly: the cost of a given capability level has dropped by roughly an order of magnitude per year [ 26 , 27 ] . We therefore report model calls and token counts alongside cost ( Fig. S11 ); unlike cost, they compare models and harnesses independently of the price list. Reasoning. More reasoning raises the success rate of every model in Fig. S11 A, but it also substantially raises the cost per episode. The extra cost has two sources: the model writes more in each turn, and it takes more turns, each of which re-reads the episode ( Fig. S11 B, C). How much the extra cost buys depends on the model. For Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5, more reasoning adds 14.6 and 17.2 points for about $0.90 more per episode. For Gemini 4 Argon, which has little headroom, it adds 2.3 points for $1.30 more, more than doubling the cost. Harness. For the weaker models (Haiku 5.5, Sonnet 5, and Opus 4.5), the assistive harness is both more successful and cheaper than the basic harness; for the stronger models (Gemini 4 Argon, Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, and Opus 5), it is less successful at about the same cost ( Fig. S11 D; see Sec. 2D.2 ). The weaker models take fewer turns, as the assistive routines replace much of their trial and error, and require fewer tokens ( Fig. S11 E, F). Sonnet 5 and Opus 4.5 take about 30% fewer turns and cost 36 to 39% less per episode; Haiku 5.5 costs about 10% less. The stronger models take about as many turns as with the basic harness and generate 7 to 10% fewer tokens, but they read 10 to 19% more input, so their cost rises by up to 7%.
