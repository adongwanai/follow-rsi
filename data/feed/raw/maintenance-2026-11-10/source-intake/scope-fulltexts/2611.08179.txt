Kim † Affiliation: KAIST Email: allonsy07@korea.ac.kr Abstract On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD) improves reasoning by training a student on its own reasoning, using the same model conditioned on reference solutions as a teacher.
Applying OPSD to multilingual reasoning requires the teacher to provide useful feedback across languages, even when the student makes mistakes in intermediate steps.
We observe a phenomenon we call Teacher Drift: as these mistakes accumulate, the teacher may follow the student’s incorrect steps instead of correcting them. Our analysis shows that this shift tends to occur earlier in lower-resource languages.
To account for these differences across languages, we propose Teachable On-Policy Self-Distillation (TOPSD), which determines where and whether teacher supervision should be applied to each reasoning trajectory.
TOPSD uses teacher–student likelihood to estimate where drift begins and select a prefix for supervision. It then verifies whether the teacher can recover the correct answer from that prefix, applying distillation only to prefixes that pass verification. Experiments on multilingual mathematical reasoning across ten languages and three model scales show that TOPSD consistently outperforms conventional OPSD, particularly in low- and medium-resource languages. 0 0 footnotetext: † \dagger Corresponding authors. 1 Introduction Figure 1: Teacher Drift in a Korean reasoning example. Large language models (LLMs) ( Brown et al., 2020 ; Achiam et al., 2023 ; Team et al., 2023 ; Grattafiori et al., 2024 ; Yang et al., 2024 ; Guo et al., 2025 ; Yang et al., 2025 ) have demonstrated strong reasoning capabilities on complex tasks such as mathematical problem solving, logical inference, and complex question answering.
On-Policy Distillation ( Ross et al., 2011 ; Lin et al., 2020 ; Agarwal et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2026a ) further improves these capabilities by training the student with dense token-level supervision on reasoning it generates.
Specifically, the teacher conditions on the student’s prefix, the sequence of tokens generated so far, and uses its next-token distribution to supervise the student.
On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD) ( Zhao et al., 2026 ) uses the same model as both student and teacher, with reference solutions provided only to the teacher.
This enables learning from model-generated reasoning with dense teacher feedback, without a separate heavy teacher model ( Shenfeld et al., 2026 ; Hübotter et al., 2026 ) . However, extending these benefits to multilingual reasoning is challenging due to the limited reasoning capabilities of the teacher model across languages.
Since the language model is largely pretrained on high-resource languages such as English, it shows substantially weaker performance in lower-resource languages than in high-resource languages ( Zhao et al., 2025 ) .
Prior work, such as COPSD ( Liu et al., 2026 ) , addresses it by using English reference solutions to draw on the model’s stronger reasoning ability in English and improve teacher supervision in the target language. While this effort focuses on strengthening the teacher, it remains underexplored whether the teacher can provide reliable supervision throughout the student’s reasoning.
We therefore investigate how the reliability of teacher supervision changes as the student’s reasoning unfolds across different languages.
We observe that the teacher may initially correct the student’s errors, but begin to follow the student’s incorrect reasoning as these errors accumulate, even with access to reference solutions.
We refer to this shift as Teacher Drift .
Figure 1 illustrates this behavior in a Korean reasoning example, where the student repeatedly states that a regular dodecagon has ten sides.
At the first two occurrences, the teacher predicts a token that corrects the number to twelve; at a later occurrence, it predicts the same incorrect token as the student.
Our multilingual analysis further shows that Teacher Drift tends to emerge earlier in lower-resource languages, suggesting that the teacher’s ability to provide reliable supervision varies across languages.
These observations motivate identifying where the teacher can still provide useful supervision within each reasoning trajectory. Based on these observations, we propose Teachable On-Policy Self-Distillation (TOPSD) , which determines where teacher supervision remains appropriate and whether it should be applied.
TOPSD estimates where Teacher Drift begins to select a teachable prefix , and then verifies whether the teacher can recover the correct answer from that prefix.
If verification succeeds, distillation is applied to the teachable prefix. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We identify Teacher Drift , where the privileged teacher begins to follow the student’s incorrect reasoning as errors accumulate, and characterize its language-dependent patterns through likelihood and recovery analyses. • We propose TOPSD , which combines trajectory-specific prefix selection with teacher recovery verification to determine where and whether to apply supervision. • We demonstrate the effectiveness of TOPSD for multilingual mathematical reasoning across model scales, with improvements particularly in low- and medium-resource languages. 2 Related Work Multilingual Reasoning. Despite advances in LLM reasoning, performance remains uneven across languages, particularly in low-resource settings ( Shi et al., 2023 ; Xuan et al., 2025 ; Ghosh et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2025 ) .
Prior work has sought to improve multilingual reasoning through multilingual post-training ( Payoungkhamdee et al., 2024 ; Chen et al., 2024 ; Lai and Nissim, 2024 ; Ranaldi and Pucci, 2025 ; She et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2026b ) and cross-lingual reasoning transfer ( Huang et al., 2023 ; Qin et al., 2023 ; Zhu et al., 2024 ; Tran et al., 2025 ; Barua et al., 2026 ) .
More recently, COPSD ( Liu et al., 2026 ) extends on-policy self-distillation to multilingual reasoning by leveraging English problems and reference solutions as privileged teacher information.
Our work complements these efforts by improving the reliability of multilingual on-policy self-distillation, particularly when errors accumulate in student-generated reasoning. Post-Training for Reasoning. Reasoning capabilities are commonly improved through self-training, reinforcement learning, and knowledge distillation.
Self-training bootstraps from model-generated rationales ( Zelikman et al., 2022 ) , while recent post-training methods learn from sampled reasoning trajectories using verifiable outcomes, verifiers, or step-level feedback ( Shao et al., 2024 ; Trung et al., 2024 ; Chu et al., 2025 ; Hosseini et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2024 ; Hwang et al., 2024 ; Xu et al., 2025 ) .
Within knowledge distillation ( Hinton et al., 2014 ) , GKD and related methods supervise student-generated sequences with teacher predictions ( Lin et al., 2020 ; Gu et al., 2024 ; Agarwal et al., 2024 ; Ko et al., 2024 ; Ko et al., 2025 ) , while OPSD uses the same model as student and privileged teacher under different conditioning contexts ( Zhao et al., 2026 ) .
Recent work further improves the distillation with guided or trust-region rollouts ( Li et al., 2026 ; Plyusov et al., 2026 ) , selective or adaptive token-level supervision ( Wang et al., 2026 ; Jang et al., 2026 ) , and trajectory refinement ( Jiang et al., 2026 ) .

The simple way to design a teacher prompt in multilingual settings is to use a reference solution y ( L ) y^{(L)} for each target language L L ( i.e., y ⋆ ≜ y ( L ) y^{\star}\triangleq y^{(L)} ).
However, the model may find it harder to make use of a reasoning trace written in a non-English language, since most of its reasoning ability is learned from English data.
So, COPSD ( Liu et al., 2026 ) provides the English question x ( EN ) x^{(\mathrm{EN})} and its reference solution y ( EN ) y^{(\mathrm{EN})} as the privileged knowledge y ⋆ y^{\star} to leverage the reasoning capabilities of LLMs.
The teacher prompt for target language L L can be written as: y ⋆ ≜ ( x ( EN ) , y ( EN ) ) y^{\star}\triangleq\left(x^{(\mathrm{EN})},y^{(\mathrm{EN})}\right) . Even though OPSD variants have shown their success in multiple reasoning tasks, it is still under-explored in multilingual settings.
This is particularly challenging because OPSD relies on the teacher to provide reliable supervision over student-generated trajectories.
In multilingual settings, this supervision faces two potential challenges.
First, errors in student-generated prefixes may make it difficult for the teacher to provide corrective guidance.
Second, the teacher itself may have limited reasoning capabilities in non-English languages, where training data is often less abundant.
These challenges raise concerns about whether the teacher can consistently provide reliable supervision throughout student rollouts. 4 Observation 4.1 Observing Teacher Drift We investigate how the privileged teacher’s behavior changes as the student-generated prefix grows, and whether these changes differ across languages.

We define a cumulative teacher-student likelihood ratio C i , t C_{i,t} to measure how producible the student-generated tokens are from the teacher. To examine how the teacher’s behavior changes over student-generated trajectories, we analyze the cumulative teacher-student likelihood ratio across prefix lengths and language-resource groups.
Figure 2 (b) shows that C t C_{t} generally increases toward 1 1 as the student prefix grows, indicating that the teacher and student assign increasingly similar relative likelihoods to the observed student-generated tokens.

These observations show that the reliability of teacher supervision may depend on the student-generated prefix, motivating the identification of teachable prefixes rather than applying supervision uniformly throughout the student trajectory. 5 Teachable On-Policy Self-Distillation To address limitations of OPSD in multilinguality, such as Teacher Drift and degradation
of teacher recoverability, we propose Teachable On-Policy Self-Distillation (TOPSD), which determines where and whether to apply teacher supervision along student-generated trajectories.
As illustrated in Figure 3 , TOPSD consists of two stages: Teachable prefix selection and Teacher Recoverability Verification. Teachable prefix selection uses the cumulative teacher-student likelihood ratio to identify a teachable prefix before sustained teacher drift. Teacher recoverability verification then evaluates whether the teacher can recover the correct answer from the selected prefix.
Only when verification succeeds does TOPSD apply distillation to the selected states of the original student trajectory to provide the meaningful guidance. 5.1 Teachable Prefix Selection As observed in Section 4.1 ,
Teacher Drift manifests as increasing likelihood alignment between the teacher and student along student-generated trajectories.

To address this, we propose Teachable On-Policy Self-Distillation (TOPSD), which combines Teachable Prefix Selection and Teacher Recoverability Verification to determine when teacher supervision remains appropriate and whether it should be trusted.
Experiments on PolyMath High across ten languages and three Qwen3 model scales show that TOPSD consistently outperforms OPSD and COPSD, with particularly strong gains in low- and medium-resource languages.
These results demonstrate that reliable on-policy self-distillation requires not only a strong teacher, but also knowing when the teacher remains able to teach and whether its guidance should be trusted.
Extending this approach beyond mathematical reasoning is a promising direction for future work. Limitations Our findings are limited to mathematical reasoning, and their generalization to other reasoning domains and model families remains to be explored.
In multilingual settings, the usefulness of teacher supervision may depend on the model’s existing capabilities in each language, so the extent of improvement may vary across languages and models.
Our current setup also uses English reference solutions as privileged information, leaving other source languages and settings without such resources unexplored.
Future work could investigate how the language and quality of reference solutions affect supervision and evaluate the approach across a broader range of multilingual reasoning tasks. Ethical Considerations AI assistance. AI tools were used to assist with drafting and revising the manuscript.

In Findings of ACL , Cited by: §2 . Appendix A Additional Experimental Details Training Details. We use Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, and Qwen3-8B as the underlying models for both the student and the privileged teacher Yang et al. (2025) .
The student is trained with LoRA ( Hu et al., 2022 ) , while the privileged teacher is obtained by disabling the LoRA adapters and freezing the underlying base model.
Following the COPSD Liu et al. (2026) configuration, we use LoRA with a rank of 64 and a scaling factor of 128. The adapters are applied to all attention and MLP projection layers in each Transformer block, including q_proj , k_proj , v_proj , o_proj , gate_proj , up_proj , and down_proj .
We do not apply LoRA to the token embeddings, language-model head, or normalization layers. For each training instance, we sample one on-policy trajectory from the student with a maximum completion length of 2,048 tokens and a sampling temperature of 1.1.
We train only the LoRA parameters for 160 optimization steps using AdamW Loshchilov and Hutter (2019) with bfloat16 precision, a learning rate of 1 × 10 − 6 1\times 10^{-6} , gradient clipping of 0.1 0.1 , and an effective prompt batch size of 32.
TOPSD performs full-vocabulary logit distillation using the fixed privileged teacher. Unless otherwise specified, TOPSD uses a likelihood-ratio threshold of τ = 0.9 \tau=0.9 and a persistence length of K = 4 K=4 .
For Teacher Recoverability Verification, the privileged teacher greedily generates a continuation from the selected prefix.
Its continuation is evaluated using a rule-based verifier that extracts the final answer from \boxed{} and compares it with the ground-truth answer.
Only trajectories that pass verification contribute to distillation, with the loss averaged over supervised tokens per trajectory and balanced equally across active languages. Implementation Details. To investigate whether the improvements of TOPSD arise simply from restricting distillation to shorter prefixes, we compare our adaptive prefix selection with a fixed-prefix baseline.
Specifically, the fixed-prefix baseline retains the first 10% of each student-generated trajectory for distillation, regardless of the estimated drift point.
We choose 10% based on the average relative cutoff position of 10.47% observed with Qwen3-1.7B under our default configuration ( K = 4 K=4 , τ = 0.9 \tau=0.9 ), as reported in Tables 3 and 8 . Both methods use the same Teacher Recoverability Verification procedure and training configuration, differing only in their prefix selection strategies.
As shown in Table 7 , TOPSD outperforms fixed-prefix truncation, suggesting that its improvements cannot be explained solely by restricting supervision to early reasoning states.
Instead, adaptively selecting teachable prefixes across languages and individual student trajectories provides additional benefits over a fixed cutoff. Sensitivity to K K . Table 8 examines how the persistence length K K affects cutoff selection and downstream performance, with other hyperparameters fixed.
The cutoff detection rate measures the proportion of trajectories with a detected cutoff, while the relative cutoff position denotes the cutoff location normalized by trajectory length.
As K K increases, the detection rate decreases slightly, while cutoffs tend to occur later.
This reflects the stricter persistence requirement, which retains longer prefixes for potential supervision.
Avg@3 increases from K = 1 K=1 to K = 4 K=4 but declines at K = 8 K=8 , with the default setting ( K = 4 K=4 ) achieving the highest score of 11.60% among the evaluated values.
