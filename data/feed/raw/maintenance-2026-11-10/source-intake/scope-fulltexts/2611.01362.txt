License: arXiv.org perpetual non-exclusive license arXiv:2611.01362v1 [cs.IR] 01 Nov 2026 “PRISM” Is Not All You Need: Cross-Topic Name Collisions and Scientific Paper Retrieval Jingjie Ning * Xueqi Li   Yibo Kong School of Computer Science, Carnegie Mellon University { jening , xueqil , yibok }@cs.cmu.edu Abstract Scientific names increasingly connect papers from different research topics, leaving readers to recover a paper’s identity from context. We study this convergence in 564,861 AI-related arXiv records. Cross-topic name collision grows 4.33-fold from a pooled 2020–2022 reference to 2026, compared with 1.12-fold within topics. A second sentence encoder reproduces the pattern, and name-group resampling quantifies its uncertainty. Fixed word-frequency bands locate the main increase in an intermediate-frequency vocabulary shared by many research areas. We then measure the ambiguity that remains after supplying research clues. With the same three-term query construction, shared-name targets retain matched identification deficits of 25.7 percentage points under title-based BM25 and 6.9 points under title-plus-abstract E5. Within fixed same-name candidates, listwise language-model ranking reaches 89.2% using the query and candidate titles. Topic-specific comparisons show that context resolves more cross-topic ambiguity. The evaluation separates candidate competition, document fields, and contextual ranking through lexical, dense, and language-model baselines. Source-linked cases and a portable evaluation collection make the phenomenon inspectable and reusable for scientific retrieval and research assistants. The cell icon is schematic. 1 1 footnotetext: Corresponding author. 1 Introduction PRISM can name a spatial world model, a pathology encoder, or a safety method. TRACE can reconstruct tables or attribute model outputs. SAGE can denote surgical mapping or audio encoding. A memorable label helps readers retain a name, while the surrounding research context determines which paper that name denotes. Scientific names increasingly connect different research topics. We call a shared exact label across distinct bibliographic records a name collision . Cross-topic convergence is an increasing collision rate among records assigned to different semantic topics. This distinction separates reuse within a research area from a label that links unrelated parts of the literature. It also poses an information-retrieval question. How precisely does a name identify a paper, and which clues resolve ambiguity? Our corpus covers 564,861 AI-related arXiv papers submitted from 2020 through 2026-10-27, with complete original-version metadata and a 2015–2019 extension. Cross-topic collision grows 4.33-fold over the pooled 2020–2022 reference, while the within-topic point estimate grows 1.12-fold. The increase survives author-overlap filters and changes in topic resolution. A second sentence encoder tests the dependence on the primary representation. Name-group resampling quantifies the influence of the observed label vocabulary. The affected population is substantial. In the fixed retrieval collection, 28,152 papers, or 19.5% of named records, have a namesake. A shared name gives a candidate set; a useful retrieval system must identify the intended member. We therefore measure the residual difference after both shared-name and metadata-matched unique-name targets receive three terms from their source abstracts. Full-index BM25 reaches 59.4% and 85.1%, respectively, leaving a 25.7-point matched gap. This comparison supplies a practical measure of retrieval difficulty under the same query-construction rule. Specific research context restores much of the identifying information. Exact-name candidate retrieval fixes the competing records, and the three terms raise BM25 Top-1 from 30.8% to 70.6%. Relative error reductions and two-candidate comparisons locate a larger benefit in resolving competitors from different topics. A source-linked pair of AutoInit papers illustrates the residual difficulty when a name and a research goal are both shared. Dense retrieval and language-model reranking broaden the evaluation across scoring approaches. Word-frequency bands reveal where the historical increase accumulates. The analysis keeps each label’s frequency fixed across years and separates its prevalence from repetition within its frequency band. Fixed bands expose how concentration varies across the reference vocabulary. Frequent names span many abstract-derived topics, while the spelling and source evidence distinguish exact-name ambiguity from reuse within a named method lineage. We contribute a version-aware measurement of scientific-name convergence, a retrieval evaluation separating name specificity from contextual disambiguation, and a portable evaluation collection with a local query demonstration. The companion artifact contains the fixed corpus, candidate groups, queries, topic-error labels, baselines, and evaluation entry point. E5-base-v2 ( Wang et al., 2022 ) searches the same titles with its documented query and passage prefixes. On the shared-name context queries, it reaches 46.6% Top-1 in the full collection and 72.8% inside exact-name candidates. The corresponding BM25 rates are 59.4% and 70.6%, and MiniLM gives 24.4% and 74.0%. Candidate restriction changes the competition seen by the ranking function while preserving each target and query. We ask DeepSeek-Flash to rank every candidate title for each of the same 1,400 shared-name context queries. A target-independent seeded permutation fixes candidate order. The output must contain each supplied candidate identifier exactly once. Across all assigned queries, Top-1 is 89.2% [87.5, 90.9], Top-10 is 98.9%, and mean reciprocal rank is 0.933. The two invalid outputs receive zero credit for every metric. The system selects among source records supplied in the prompt; the evaluation retains its complete ordering and response for each query. The comparison keeps query evidence and document fields explicit. All title-only rows expose the same candidate text. Full-index search considers every indexed title, while exact-name ranking receives the complete observed namesake group. These scopes serve distinct stages of scientific search. A full-index retrieval score captures candidate discovery and target ranking together; exact-name ranking measures selection within a known group. The query demonstration exposes both the name group and the source record selected by research context. Adding the source abstract improves identification while preserving a smaller shared-name gap. The two interventions address different parts of the search task. Candidate restriction specifies which label group the reader intends; richer document fields supply evidence for distinguishing its members. Keeping their outcomes separate exposes both contributions. Its fixed corpus contains 144,523 labelled records, including 28,152 shared-name records in 8,190 name groups. The reference evaluation provides 1,400 shared-name targets and 1,400 metadata-matched unique-name targets, with name, field, and content-query variants. Tasks cover full-index retrieval and ranking within exact-name candidates. The evaluator scores Top-1, Top-10, and reciprocal rank, including explicit tie groups and missing submissions. The companion artifact index links the corpus, candidates, queries, relevance records, baseline outputs, and topic-error labels. It includes an evaluation command, a data card, and a local query demonstration. The demonstration accepts a candidate name and an optional abstract, returns the source-linked namesakes, and ranks them by their semantic relation to the supplied text. The distribution includes this paper at a matching relative path so the artifact link remains usable after unpacking. The collection fixes the evaluation population, and the demonstration exposes its contents for inspection and reuse. The target is a source-record identifier, so a related namesake counts as an identification error. Top-1, Top-10, and reciprocal rank use explicit tie groups. Omitted queries receive zero credit and contribute to the reported coverage. Each outcome links to its query, candidate identifiers, and topic assignments. An executable identification task. Each input contains a query identifier, its supplied text, and the selected retrieval scope. The query identifier joins the target record to the frozen relevance file. For full-index search, systems rank the common collection. For exact-name selection, the supplied group contains every observed record with the normalized label. A prediction is an ordered list of source identifiers or an ordered sequence of explicit tie groups. This interface allows lexical scoring, vector search, and generative ranking to use the same evaluator. The source identifiers make the target and returned papers directly inspectable. The query variants separate three amounts of supplied information. Name-only queries contain the observed title label. Field queries append the paper’s assigned research category description. Context queries append three alphabetic terms selected by frequency and inverse document frequency over the abstract collection. The target label and a fixed stop list are removed from term selection. Each retained term has at least four characters, and the rule applies across the full corpus. Candidate-group uniqueness plays no role in choosing those words. All models and field extensions receive the same query strings. Reproduction and extension. The package contains 60,200 scored outcomes with query identifiers, candidate scope, indexed fields, rank bounds, and outcome status. It retains the original lexical and MiniLM results together with the E5 and language-model extensions. The standalone BM25 and E5 entry points reconstruct candidate rankings from the exported corpus and queries. A separate command scores the retained language-model orderings. The E5 model revision and prefixes, language-model prompt and candidate permutations, and per-query failure states accompany their outputs. The package supports rescoring and model reruns. Name groups provide the unit for paired uncertainty and for separating future development and evaluation data. When several targets share a name, they also share a candidate population. Resampling each target independently would discard that dependence. The reported intervals keep the group’s targets and conditions together, and matched-control analyses keep the associated unique targets in the same draw. A new learned system can reserve complete groups for evaluation while using the remaining groups for development. The queries and outcomes are published; their group membership remains explicit. For authors. Name checking is most useful before a label becomes the main retrieval key. The lexical analysis motivates checking familiar-word candidates across research topics, and the query results support publishing a short task descriptor together with the full title and a stable identifier. A descriptive suffix can distinguish related releases; its effectiveness should be evaluated with the intended search system. For repositories and conferences. Submission interfaces can surface existing exact-name records and show their research contexts. The aim is an informative collision notice, with links and identifiers that let authors choose an appropriate label. Version-aware storage preserves when a name was introduced and how later releases relate to it. For search systems and research assistants. A shared name should expose a candidate set. Specific task or method context supports ranking within that set, and a full title plus stable identifier makes the selected result verifiable. When candidates occupy the same topic, the interface can ask for a distinguishing application, method component, or other remembered detail. The measured differences support keeping candidate retrieval and contextual selection separately inspectable. 6 Conclusion Scientific names increasingly converge across research topics. The version-aware corpus records 4.33-fold growth in cross-topic collision against 1.12-fold growth within topics, with the pattern reproduced by a second sentence encoder. Fixed frequency bands locate most of the increase in intermediate-frequency names. Source-linked PRISM, TRACE, and SAGE cases connect this population pattern to distinct scientific objects, observed spellings, and documented method lineages. Specific research clues recover many intended papers while leaving a measurable residual identification gap. Shared-name targets remain 25.7 points behind matched unique-name targets under title-based BM25 and 6.9 points behind under title-plus-abstract E5. Within complete same-name candidate sets, contextual ranking reaches 70.6% with BM25, 89.2% with a language model, and 93.6% with E5 over titles and abstracts. Topic-specific errors and fixed-size comparisons identify same-topic competitors as the harder ambiguity. The shared corpus, frozen queries, complete candidate groups, and 60,200 recorded outcomes make these comparisons directly reusable. Together, the findings support scientific search interfaces that expose namesakes, use research context to rank them, and preserve a verifiable paper identifier in the final result.
