Poisoning those answers can induce a persistent trigger-conditioned rule without giving the attacker access to the model, optimizer, or Skill file. We study this reference-feedback poisoning in SkillOpt by tracing an attacker-supplied trigger–payload mapping from reflection to held-out activation. In SearchQA, where the payload replaces a short answer, the natural attack retains an exact rule in all three seeds; each final Skill emits the payload on 200/200 triggered questions and 0/200 clean questions. In seed 42, removing the rule eliminates activation, and an in-run comparison shows similar clean accuracy to benign SkillOpt (169/200 versus 171/200). In SpreadsheetBench, where the payload requires an additional workbook edit, no natural run retains an exact rule across three seeds. Diagnostic interventions locate two successive bottlenecks: the Analyst abstracts away required literals, and, once literal preservation is enabled, edit-budget ranking can remove exact edits before candidate construction. Poisoned references can therefore create persistent backdoors, but exposure alone does not ensure that the induced rule is formed, selected, and retained. * * footnotetext: Equal contribution. † † footnotetext: Correspondence to Louise Nayeong Koh (louisenyk@yonsei.ac.kr) and Hyogon Ryu (hyogon.ryu@gmail.com). 1 Introduction Self-evolving agents improve by converting execution experience into persistent natural-language Skills. A text-space skill optimizer, such as SkillOpt [ 1 ] , runs the target model on a batch of tasks, scores the outputs against reference answers, reflects on failures, proposes textual edits, and retains a candidate Skill if it achieves a higher score on a validation set. The resulting Skill is reused on every later task. Supervision provided during optimization can therefore shape behavior well beyond the examples it came from. Prior work has shown that this persistent state can be manipulated through memory and retrieval stores [ 7 , 8 ] , poisoned experience [ 5 ] , malicious queries and trajectories [ 2 , 3 , 4 ] , and directly supplied Skill files [ 6 ] . Poisoned human feedback has also been used to implant backdoors during weight-space RLHF [ 10 ] . Here we study a different feedback channel: reference answers used to judge an agent’s executions during text-space Skill optimization. A poisoned training reference changes which executions are labeled as failures and thus what the optimizer reflects on. A poisoned validation reference changes which candidate wins. When optimization tasks draw on contributed or externally maintained datasets, the provenance and integrity of their reference answers can be difficult to verify. Figure 1: Victim optimization loop. Execution is scored against references; reflection proposes edits; validation and selection determine which candidate is persisted. The attacker supplies only tasks and references. Exposure is not persistence. A trigger–payload rule must be recognized, written as an executable edit, survive merging and ranking, enter a candidate, pass validation, remain in the endpoint Skill, and activate on held-out inputs bearing the trained trigger (Figure 1 ). These transitions can fail independently, so we evaluate them as promotion stages rather than collapse them into one attack-success rate. We study two SkillOpt environments with the same attacker capability. In SearchQA [ 11 ] , the attack completes the full chain in all three seeds: the exact rule reaches the final Skill and activates on all 200 triggered held-out questions, but on none of the clean questions. Deleting the rule removes this behavior in the tested seed. In a matched comparison on seed 42, the poisoned best Skill and benign SkillOpt achieve similar observed clean test accuracy (169/200 and 171/200, respectively). This single-seed comparison is consistent with the backdoor coexisting with the optimizer’s ordinary QA utility. In SpreadsheetBench [ 12 ] , neither the same attack nor a matched trajectory-poisoning baseline [ 3 ] retains an exact rule across three seeds. Diagnostics expose two successive gates: literal abstraction first suppresses exact formation; after literal preservation is enabled, edit-budget ranking can still remove the rule before it enters a candidate. The settings show different outcomes, although their task-specific configurations do not isolate task complexity as the cause. In summary, our contributions are: • We identify reference answers as an attacker-controlled supervision channel into text-space Skill optimization and separate the training-side role (rule formation) from the validation-side role (candidate selection). • We introduce a stage-resolved evaluation that tracks a poisoned rule from reflection to held-out activation, labeling each result as natural evidence or as a diagnostic that relaxes one gate, and never counting diagnostics toward attack success. • We show a complete natural attack chain across repeated runs in a short-answer setting, including selective held-out activation and paired rule deletion. In a structured, tool-mediated setting where the natural chain stops before exact-rule retention, we use targeted diagnostics to locate successive formation and ranking bottlenecks. • We identify a candidate-level carrier effect through which a validation-neutral backdoor edit rides into the best Skill on the gains of an unrelated clean edit. 2 Related Work Learning from textual feedback. Reflexion stores verbal reflections in episodic memory [ 13 ] , and ExpeL extracts reusable natural-language insights from experience [ 14 ] . Voyager stores executable code skills [ 15 ] , while TextGrad uses language-model feedback to optimize components of an AI system [ 16 ] . SkillOpt instead uses scored rollouts to edit a persistent natural-language Skill around a frozen model [ 1 ] . In systems that retain such artifacts, the provenance of the feedback driving their updates is a security concern. Self-evolving agents can also drift without an adversary [ 9 ] . Poisoning persistent agent state. AgentPoison [ 7 ] and MINJA [ 8 ] manipulate long-term memory or retrieval stores. OEP [ 5 ] injects locally correct but non-transferable experiences that reflection over-generalizes. SkillJack shows that poisoned experiences can become reusable Skills that remain effective after their source records are removed [ 2 ] . PoisonedEvolution studies how attacker-contributed trajectory evidence is attributed and promoted into persistent Skills [ 4 ] . TBA [ 3 ] is the closest baseline: malicious queries elicit trusted executor trajectories whose repeated behavior is consolidated. Skill-Inject [ 6 ] studies prompt injection through third-party Skill files, where the attacker already controls the artifact. Our attacker does not directly edit trajectories or the Skill artifact; it controls the reference answers used to score trajectories. Poisoned feedback and backdoors. Classical backdoors implant trigger-dependent behavior into parameters [ 19 , 20 ] , and poisoned instruction-tuning examples can induce targeted behavior [ 17 ] . Rando and Tramèr [ 10 ] show that poisoned human preference labels can plant universal jailbreak backdoors through RLHF. Their attack provides a related feedback-poisoning precedent in weight-space learning; here the persistent artifact is text around a frozen model. Poisoning results can depend heavily on the evaluation setup [ 18 ] , motivating careful reporting of the stages and conditions under which a rule is retained. 3 Threat Model and Promotion-Stage Analysis 3.1 Victim system SkillOpt [ 1 ] maintains a persistent Markdown Skill prepended to the target model’s instructions. Each step executes training tasks, scores them against references, has an Analyst propose edits, merges and ranks those edits under a budget, applies the survivors to a candidate Skill, and evaluates that candidate on validation data. A candidate replaces the incumbent only when its validation score is higher; the best Skill is persisted, with a periodic slow update at each epoch. Reference answers play two roles. Training references decide which executions are labeled failures and so determine what the Analyst reflects on. Validation references decide which candidate wins. We call attacks through either input reference-feedback poisoning . 3.2 Threat model The attacker can contribute tasks and their reference answers to the dataset used for Skill optimization (Figure 2 ). This is the position of a contributor to a shared benchmark, a scraped QA corpus, or an externally maintained evaluation suite. The attacker cannot modify the target model, the optimizer, its prompts, the scoring implementation, the edit budget, or any Skill file. The basic condition poisons a fraction of training tasks by inserting a trigger into the input and replacing the reference answer with the payload, while validation and test data stay clean. The stronger condition additionally poisons a fraction of validation tasks. Held-out evaluation inputs are frozen before optimization and never used for reflection or selection. Figure 2: Threat model for reference-feedback poisoning. The attacker supplies a subset of training tasks and their reference answers and, in the stronger condition, also poisons a subset of validation tasks and their references. The attacker cannot directly modify the model, optimizer, prompts, scoring implementation, or Skill file. The final Skill is evaluated on frozen held-out triggered and clean inputs. What we do not count as an attack. Several experiments relax one component of the victim to isolate a gate: a one-line change to the Analyst prompt, a one-step increase of the edit budget, a replay that re-inserts a clipped edit, or a deletion of a rule from a frozen Skill. These are diagnostics . We label natural and diagnostic attack outcomes separately and never add diagnostics to natural success counts. Table 1: Evidence classes used throughout the paper. The primary attack prepends [ZEPHYR-17] to the question and replaces the reference with ZEPHYR VERIFIED . Three seeds (42, 43, 44) each use 400 training questions with 30% poisoned references, a clean 100-item validation set, a clean 200-item test set, and four epochs of ten steps. We further vary training size (50–400), training-poison rate (5–30%), and validation-poison rate (0–20%). Three additional trigger constructions with an opaque payload ( ORCHID-729 ) serve as mechanism probes. Their optimization trajectories are analyzed on seed 42, while their endpoints are audited across seeds 42–44 (Appendix E ). Held-out evaluation uses five frozen views of the 200 test questions: clean, the trained trigger, two unseen in-family triggers ( ZEPHYR-22 , ZEPHYR-99 ), and an out-of-family trigger ( ORION-22 ). SpreadsheetBench [ 12 ] requires editing a workbook to satisfy a natural-language instruction. The attack prepends [ARCHIVE-CHECK: COBALT-17] to the instruction and poisons the reference workbook by writing COBALT VERIFIED to cell C2 of its first sheet and adding that cell to the graded range, while the original task must still be completed. Each seed uses 80 training tasks with 25% poisoned, 40 validation tasks (10 poisoned in the stronger condition), and 280 held-out tasks; the endpoint evaluation uses 40 clean and 40 triggered held-out sources per seed. The edit budget decays from four to two on a cosine schedule over the run and equals two at the steps discussed in Section 5.2 . Models. Both the target model and the Analyst/optimizer are GPT-5.6 Luna , accessed through the Codex CLI with reasoning effort medium , workspace-write sandbox, and network access disabled. The provider does not expose checkpoint or accelerator details. Full configuration, hyperparameters, and compute are in Appendix C . 4.2 Metrics Target ASR is the fraction of triggered held-out inputs on which the payload is produced. Joint success (SpreadsheetBench only) additionally requires completion of the original task. Clean FPR is the fraction of clean held-out inputs on which the payload is produced, and Clean Utility is task accuracy on clean held-out inputs. Stage-resolved analysis records whether an exact rule reaches each stage in Table 2 . Technical execution failures remain in the denominator, with coverage reported separately. For final SpreadsheetBench endpoint evaluations, each target-model call runs in an isolated workspace without repository, prior-state, or experiment-artifact access; the target cell is inspected after execution. 4.3 Baselines and isolation Benign SkillOpt provides the clean optimization reference. TBA [ 3 ] is matched to training-reference poisoning (TR) on the initial Skill, target and optimizer, data split, seeds, edit budget, trigger and target behavior, target-bearing exposure, held-out sources, and generation settings. TBA uses the same training sources and source-specific target bindings as TR while keeping references, validation, and test data clean; only the attack channel differs. Each final Skill is evaluated on the same 40 clean and 40 triggered sources per seed using the isolated harness. Training-and-validation-reference poisoning (TRV) is reported separately because it also controls validation references. Skill-Inject is an orthogonal direct-artifact threat model, not a numerical baseline. 5 Results We first establish whether reference-feedback poisoning can complete the natural promotion chain in a short-answer text setting. We then study a structured, tool-mediated setting in which the chain stops before persistence and use diagnostics to identify the gates responsible for this outcome. 5.1 RQ1: Where does poisoned feedback enter? Training references decide what is formed. Holding the optimizer fixed on seed 42, we vary only the trigger–payload construction (Appendix A ). Every construction is recognized during reflection: the Analyst writes rules about the trigger. What differs is whether the payload survives. The structured-metadata construction forms a literal candidate that fails the clean validation gate; the opaque-tag and natural-language constructions form rules that mention the trigger but drop the opaque payload; only the aligned mapping [ZEPHYR-17] → \rightarrow ZEPHYR VERIFIED keeps the exact payload through retention. Thus, all four seed-42 trajectories form a trigger-related rule, while the Analyst’s abstraction determines whether the attacker’s payload is preserved. We use this single-seed construction probe to locate variation at formation, not to estimate or rank construction-level success rates. Poisoned validation references can support selection after formation. We fix the aligned mapping at 100 training examples with 10 poisoned references and compare four natural trajectories in which 0, 5, 10, or 20 of the 100 validation references carry the same trigger and payload (Table 3 ). With clean validation, exact-payload candidates form repeatedly but none beats the incumbent. In the trajectories with 5%, 10%, or 20% poisoned validation references, an exact rule is promoted and retained. The 10% candidate scores 0.77 against an incumbent of 0.70 by answering all 10 poisoned validation items and 67 of 90 clean ones. Validation references never enter the Analyst input, so they do not write the rule; they change the score assigned to a formed rule and can thereby support its adoption and the parent Skill used at later steps. These single trajectories establish the selection-side mechanism and an existence result, rather than a dose–response curve or a promotion probability. Reconstructing the Analyst’s inputs shows that the trigger, target value, and destination cell are present in all 144 poison-bearing reflection groups recorded in the run artifacts, so the failure is not one of visibility. The three groups used for prompt comparison are one per seed. Two downstream components act as gates. Gate 1: abstraction. The unmodified Analyst produces no exact edit in the three sampled groups (0/3). Replacing one generalization instruction with a narrow literal-preservation exception yields exact edits in all three groups (3/3) and exact proposals across the three full runs (Table 7 ). This intervention isolates abstraction as the formation bottleneck; it is a diagnostic, not a stock attack result, and could also remove legitimate literal rules. Gate 2: edit budget. With literal preservation enabled, exact edits survive merge in seeds 42 and 43 but are removed by Rank/Select before candidate construction. Seed 44 skips ranking when the merged pool fits the budget; the exact edit then enters the candidate, passes validation, and persists. The stage trace therefore places the second bottleneck at edit ranking before candidate construction. A forced-candidate replay at seed 42, step 4 yields a score of 0.700 against 0.450 and answers all 10 poisoned validation items; this counterfactual isolates the downstream gate. At seed 43, step 8, increasing only the budget from 2 to 3 lets the fixed merged pool bypass ranking and yields 24/40 (0.600) against 18/40, enough to pass validation. These interventions condition on exact formation and do not establish stock attack success. We freeze the initial Skill P P , a version B 0 B_{0} of the learned backdoor rule with its incidental style wording removed (so that it still maps [ZEPHYR-17] to ZEPHYR VERIFIED but does not change ordinary answers), and a clean QA edit C C produced by the optimizer. On a frozen 40-item positive-control validation set that the parent answers inconsistently, B 0 B_{0} alone leaves the score unchanged at 0.1700 (gate pass 3/10 evaluations). Adding C C raises the score to 0.3725 without B 0 B_{0} and to 0.3775 with B 0 B_{0} , with a 10/10 pass rate in both cases; removing B 0 B_{0} from P + B 0 + C P+B_{0}+C changes the score by 0.0050 (Table 8 ). Because SkillOpt accepts or rejects a candidate atomically, the clean edit carries the backdoor edit through the gate. This is the external carrier effect. The same pattern appears with clean edits taken from an actual evolution step (0.1875 to 0.2325, pass 5/10 to 9/10). The original wording of the learned rule has a much smaller direct effect on clean answers (0.0200, less than one item), which we call the self-carrier effect and report in Appendix G . We therefore report the grid as a boundary map rather than a threshold. 5.4 Backdoor or format rule? The primary SearchQA payload, ZEPHYR VERIFIED , is lexically related to its trigger, and a reader might ask whether the retained rule is a backdoor or merely a learned answer format. Four observations separate the two. First, the rule is conditioned on the trigger, not on a format cue: it never fires on clean questions or on the out-of-family ORION-22 trigger, which has the same surface form. Second, deleting the block removes the behavior without touching clean accuracy, so it is not a general answering style. Third, an opaque payload also completes the chain when the optimizer is willing to keep it. Under the stronger condition on seed 42 (100 training examples, 10 poisoned references, 10% poisoned validation references), the mapping [ZEPHYR-17] → \rightarrow ORCHID-729 is retained in the final Skill by the epoch-4 slow update; the frozen Skill fires on 200/200 held-out triggered questions with 0/200 clean false activations and 178/200 clean utility, and deleting the rule drops activation to 0/200 (Table 9 , Appendix B ). Fourth, a rejected opaque-payload candidate, frozen below the best-Skill gate, fires on 195/200 triggered questions and 0/200 after rule removal (Appendix H ). The aligned mapping is the construction that most reliably survives the Analyst’s abstraction under the basic condition; it is not the only construction the target will execute, nor the only one that can persist. 6 Discussion The gates reveal intervention points. SpreadsheetBench exposes successive formation and ranking bottlenecks: abstraction suppresses exact rules, and, once formation is enabled, the edit budget can block candidate inclusion. Neither is a designed defense; ordinary pool sizes or abstraction choices can bypass them, while literal suppression can also erase legitimate rules. SearchQA exposes a later gap: atomic candidate validation can accept a validation-neutral edit on the strength of its neighbors. Per-edit attribution, leave-one-edit-out validation, and edit provenance are promising defenses whose security, utility, and cost require dedicated evaluation. Endpoint evaluation needs isolation. In an initial non-isolated run, apparent SpreadsheetBench activations followed access to target-bearing experiment files and did not reproduce after repository and prior-state access were removed. An earlier TRV control hit likewise failed under isolation. Endpoint claims should therefore include execution traces and rule out target information from the prompt or evaluation environment. Reference-feedback poisoning could also be misused to implant persistent behavior in Skill optimizers. The study uses synthetic benchmark attacks. Frozen Skills, traces, and checksums were retained for internal verification. Limitations. Natural end-to-end success covers one SearchQA mapping across three seeds. We audited the endpoints of the other trigger constructions across seeds 42–44 (Table 12 ). Each validation-poison level was examined in one trajectory. The two task settings use task-specific SkillOpt configurations, including different update counts and slow-update settings. Their outcomes should therefore be interpreted as complementary case studies, not as a controlled comparison that isolates task modality as the cause of success or failure. The benign comparison is available only for seed 42, and TBA only for SpreadsheetBench. Most support-grid cells also use seed 42. Results come from one optimizer and one closed model; broader models, tasks, and defenses remain untested. Prompt and budget interventions are diagnostics, and SpreadsheetBench activation/deletion relies on one 40-source lineage. 7 Conclusion We studied reference-feedback poisoning, in which an attacker supplies trigger-bearing tasks and corrupts the reference answers used by a text-space Skill optimizer for scoring, without modifying the model, optimizer, prompts, or Skill artifact. We traced the resulting trigger–payload relation through the optimizer’s promotion stages, from reflection to activation on frozen held-out inputs. In a short-answer setting, the induced rule survives the full promotion chain across three independent runs and activates selectively on held-out inputs, demonstrating that poisoned references can produce a persistent backdoor. In a structured, tool-mediated setting, however, the natural attack stops before exact-rule retention. Stage-resolved diagnostics locate two successive bottlenecks in the observed trajectories: literal abstraction can prevent executable rule formation, and edit-budget ranking can block an exact rule from entering a candidate. Together, these findings show that poisoned supervision can persist beyond the examples that carry it, while also demonstrating that exposure alone does not guarantee persistence. Appendix B Rule Classification and Retained Rule Texts We classify a rule along two axes, trigger scope and payload fidelity. A literal exact-payload rule names the observed trigger and the exact output. A generalized exact-payload rule broadens the trigger to a family or category while preserving the exact output. A payload-free generalization preserves only a notion such as “verification marker” and does not specify the attacker’s output. The first two count as formed attack rules; the third counts as recognition without preservation. Stage labels are assigned from immutable artifacts: recognition from the Analyst output, proposal from raw or merged edits, candidate from the applied candidate file, best Skill from the committed step decision, retention from the endpoint file, and activation from the frozen-Skill evaluation.
