Unlike task arithmetic, which stays within the span of the frozen student and expert parameters for any choice of merging coefficients, P 2 -MOPD keeps the student trainable, so the experts act as parameter priors without confining it to this span. Across four domains at 1.7B and 4B scales, P 2 -MOPD achieves faster and more balanced recovery than MOPD from the same initialization, surpassing its final average recovery in half the training steps. Frontier large language models are increasingly built by training separate domain experts, often with domain-specific reinforcement learning ( Wang et al., 2026 ) , and then consolidating them into a single generalist. Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) ( Ma et al., 2026a ) , in which each teacher supervises the student’s own responses in its domain, has become a standard recipe for this step ( Xiaomi LLM-Core Team, 2026b ; Xiaomi LLM-Core Team, 2026a ; DeepSeek-AI, 2026 ; Yang et al., 2026b ; NVIDIA, 2026 ; Kimi Team, 2026 ) . Ideally, the consolidated model retains the capability of every expert, rather than trading one domain for another. Because MOPD benefits from supervising the student’s own behavior, its pipeline is heavy. Every step samples rollouts from the student and runs each teacher over them with teacher forcing to obtain token-level labels, and the student must approach each teacher’s performance through many such steps. Prior work has also found that MOPD does not recover all domains evenly ( Kim et al., 2026 ; Gao et al., 2026 ) . Even when it starts from a merge of the experts, it can leave one domain far behind, and continued distillation can erode a capability that the merge already had. The other common way to consolidate experts is model merging. Task arithmetic ( Ilharco et al., 2023 ) adds a weighted sum of the experts’ task vectors to the base model without any training. Because the behavior of the merged model is never supervised, its quality is not guaranteed. In our experiments, for instance, a uniform merge recovers less than half of the gap between the base model and the teachers ( Table 1 ). Task arithmetic falls short on its own, but its partial success is informative. If a training-free linear combination of the experts already recovers part of their capabilities, their parameters encode a useful prior for the consolidated model. We take this observation one step further. A linear combination of the base student and the frozen experts is itself a single model, differentiable in both its mixing coefficients and the student’s weights, and can therefore be trained end to end under the MOPD objective. Viewed this way, the experts no longer serve only as an initialization or a one-off merge but act as parameter priors throughout training, so the student need not climb the whole way to each teacher on its own. Based on this view, we propose P 2 -MOPD (Parameter-Prior MOPD; Figure 2 ). It keeps the MOPD loss and its supervision on the student’s own trajectories, but parameterizes the student as a linear combination of its own trainable weights and the frozen expert parameters ( Equation 4 ). The student thus receives two kinds of guidance from the experts under a single loss: their logits supervise its outputs, as in MOPD, and their parameters enter its weights through the mixing coefficients, which the on-policy loss updates together with the student weights. Unlike task arithmetic, P 2 -MOPD keeps the base student’s weights fully trainable, so the consolidated model is not confined to the span of the student and expert parameters. Such confinement would be restrictive, since task vectors of separately trained experts interfere with each other ( Yadav et al., 2023 ; Yu et al., 2024 ) , and with only K K directions per module the merged model can trade one domain against another but cannot make adjustments that none of the experts contains. In P 2 -MOPD , the mixing coefficients move the model along expert-derived directions and the weights adapt it beyond them. The closest prior work, IM-MOPD ( Kim et al., 2026 ) , also reuses the expert parameters but merges task vectors into the student at fixed points according to an external validation rule, whereas P 2 -MOPD learns the combination continuously from the distillation gradient. To understand why learning the combination helps, we project each model’s change onto the task vectors ( Section 4.3 ). MOPD from the merged start moves the student along most task vectors but hardly along that of the expert whose domain it leaves behind. P 2 -MOPD moves an order of magnitude farther along this direction, and across methods the recovery of that domain tracks the movement. The learnable coefficients thus make reachable a teacher-aligned direction that student weight-only updates do not take, while the weights adapt the student outside the span of the task vectors, so the two channels divide the work. Across four domains (medicine, law, finance and instruction following) at two scales, P 2 -MOPD is both faster and more balanced than MOPD from the same merged start ( Section 4.2 ). After half the training steps, it already exceeds the final average recovery of MOPD at lower wall-clock cost ( Figure 1 ), and at the end of training it raises worst-domain recovery from below half to nearly 90 % 90\% . Learning the mixing coefficients alone, inside the span, falls far short, so both the expert prior and the freedom to leave its span are needed ( Section 5 ). Our contributions are as follows. • We propose P 2 -MOPD , which treats a linear combination of the student and frozen experts as a single model and trains its weights and mixing coefficients jointly under the MOPD objective ( Section 3 ). • Student weight-only MOPD updates barely move along the direction of the lagging expert; the learnable coefficients make this direction reachable, while the weights adapt the student beyond the task-vector span ( Section 4.3 ). • At two scales, P 2 -MOPD surpasses the final average recovery of MOPD in half the training steps and raises worst-domain recovery from below 50 % 50\% to nearly 90 % 90\% ( Sections 4.2 and 5 ). 2 Related Work Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation. On-policy distillation (OPD) ( Lu & Thinking Machines Lab, 2025 ; Yang et al., 2025 ; Yang et al., 2026a ; Li et al., 2026 ; Fu et al., 2026 ; Zhao et al., 2026 ; Xing et al., 2026 ; Kang et al., 2026 ) supervises the student with teacher feedback on its own trajectories and has become a dense-reward alternative to reinforcement learning in LLM post-training. MOPD ( Ma et al., 2026a ) extends OPD to consolidating several RL-trained domain experts by routing each prompt to the teacher of its domain, and is used in recent frontier technical reports to consolidate domain experts into one model ( Xiaomi LLM-Core Team, 2026b ; Xiaomi LLM-Core Team, 2026a ; DeepSeek-AI, 2026 ; Yang et al., 2026b ; NVIDIA, 2026 ; Kimi Team, 2026 ) . Follow-up work refines which teacher supervises which token and how strongly, through budget balancing across domains ( Gao et al., 2026 ) , label-free token routing ( Xu et al., 2026 ) , or soft-prompt teachers ( Ma et al., 2026b ) . These methods improve the supervision but treat the student as a plain set of weights. Closest to ours, IM-MOPD ( Kim et al., 2026 ) shows that the expert parameters themselves also help: it initializes the student by task arithmetic and periodically merges task vectors of under-recovered domains back into it. However, these merges are rule-based and rely on an extra validation signal rather than the distillation gradient. P 2 -MOPD instead treats the linear combination of trainable student parameters and frozen expert parameters as a single differentiable model, jointly optimizing the student weights and expert mixing coefficients throughout training under the same on-policy objective. Model Merging. Model merging combines experts in weight space without training, for example by task arithmetic ( Ilharco et al., 2023 ) and its interference-reducing variants ( Yadav et al., 2023 ; Yu et al., 2024 ) . Because good fixed coefficients are hard to choose, another line learns them: AdaMerging ( Yang et al., 2024 ) fits task- or layer-wise coefficients by entropy minimization on unlabeled test data, and subsequent methods learn them from task data, expert logits or expert activations ( Zhang et al., 2024 ; Gauthier-Caron et al., 2024 ; Xu et al., 2025 ) . Other work interleaves merging with training as a discrete step between phases ( Choshen et al., 2022 ; Sun et al., 2025 ; Shen et al., 2025 ) . Together, these works show that trained expert parameters can be combined into a reasonable model even without training. They still treat merging as an operation that outputs a model, either offline or between training phases. Our view is different: since task arithmetic is a linear and fully differentiable function of its coefficients, the combination of student and expert parameters can be treated as one model. P 2 -MOPD trains this model inside MOPD, so the expert parameters serve as a prior and the on-policy objective updates both the mixing coefficients and the student weights, without confining the student to the span of the task vectors. 3 Method Figure 2 : Overview of P 2 -MOPD . Each projection of the student is composed as its own learnable full weight plus a learnable linear combination of frozen expert directions, W m = B m + ∑ k α k , m ​ τ k , m W_{m}=B_{m}+\sum_{k}\alpha_{k,m}\tau_{k,m} ( Equation 4 ), and the composed weights are used as one model for the forward pass. The student generates rollouts on prompts of all domains; the frozen teacher of each prompt’s domain scores the student’s tokens. The sampled tokens and the teacher logits are passed as detached data flow (gray lines), and the resulting MOPD loss is back-propagated to the student weights (student weight channel) and to the coefficients α \alpha (coefficient channel). No gradient reaches the experts or the teachers, which come from the same expert bank. P 2 -MOPD rests on a simple view. A linear combination of model parameters is itself a single model, whose forward pass uses only the composed weights, and the composition is differentiable in its mixing coefficients. P 2 -MOPD therefore reparameterizes the MOPD student as trainable student weights plus a learnable combination of frozen expert weights, and jointly optimizes the student weights and the mixing coefficients under the on-policy distillation objective ( Figure 2 ). The experts thus guide the student in two ways: their logits supervise its behavior, and their weights serve as parameter priors. P 2 -MOPD is applied module-wise. A module m ∈ ℳ m\in\mathcal{M} is one linear projection of a decoder layer (query, key, value and output in attention; gate, up and down in the MLP). The remaining tensors ϕ \phi (token embeddings, normalization weights and the output head, including a tied embedding) are trained as in MOPD. For a module m ∈ ℳ m\in\mathcal{M} let W m ( k ) W^{(k)}_{m} be the corresponding tensor of expert k k , with task vector τ k , m = W m ( k ) − W m ( 0 ) \tau_{k,m}=W^{(k)}_{m}-W^{(0)}_{m} . P 2 -MOPD parameterizes the student’s module weights as W m ​ ( B , α ) = B m + ∑ k = 1 K α k , m ​ τ k , m , m ∈ ℳ , W_{m}(B,\alpha)=B_{m}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k,m}\,\tau_{k,m},\qquad m\in\mathcal{M}, (4) where the full tensors B = { B m } m ∈ ℳ B=\{B_{m}\}_{m\in\mathcal{M}} and the scalar mixing coefficients α ∈ ℝ K × | ℳ | \alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times|\mathcal{M}|} are both trainable and the task vectors are frozen (left block of Figure 2 ). No gradient is back-propagated into any teacher network: the teachers enter the loss only through the constant rewards, and the task vectors enter the parameterization only as fixed directions. Since B B is unconstrained, Equation 4 represents exactly the same models as a plain student. What it changes is how the student moves: a single scalar step on α k , m \alpha_{k,m} moves the whole module along a task vector. In this sense the frozen experts act as parameter priors , a bias on the optimization path rather than on the model class ( Section 3.3 ). Coefficient granularity. Equation 4 gives every module its own K K coefficients ( module granularity, K ​ | ℳ | K|\mathcal{M}| scalars). The coefficients can also be tied across modules: at layer granularity all projections of one decoder layer share K K coefficients, and at model granularity there is one coefficient per expert ( K K scalars), the learnable analogue of Equation 1 . Tying only constrains α \alpha ; every module keeps its own task vectors and weights. It is implemented by writing the full table as α = α ~ ​ T ⊤ \alpha=\tilde{\alpha}\,T^{\top} for a fixed binary assignment matrix T T and optimizing the free parameters α ~ \tilde{\alpha} , so the gradient of a tied coefficient is the sum of the gradients of the modules it covers. Our main experiments use module granularity; the method does not depend on it, and finer tying is not necessarily better ( Sections 5 and 6 ). Initialization. P 2 -MOPD starts from the TA merge θ TA ​ ( λ ) \theta_{\mathrm{TA}}(\lambda) , in which every tensor (modules and ϕ \phi alike) is merged with the TA coefficients λ \lambda . Adam and clipping act on each channel separately, so Equation 8 describes the direction of the change, not its size; Section 4 measures its effect. 4 Experiments 4.1 Experimental Setup Models and data. We follow the protocol of IM-MOPD ( Kim et al., 2026 ) at two scales, Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-1.7B. The base θ 0 \theta_{0} is the general SFT reference model of that work, and at each scale four domain teachers (medical, law, finance, instruction following) are fine-tuned from the same θ 0 \theta_{0} , so the task vectors τ k = θ k − θ 0 \tau_{k}=\theta_{k}-\theta_{0} are well defined. The domains are evaluated on MedQA, CaseHOLD, FinQA and IFBench. We omit the fifth domain, τ 2 \tau^{2} -bench ( Barres et al., 2025 ) , because its user simulator requires a paid commercial LLM API. Every training batch contains 128 prompts, 32 from each domain, and each prompt is distilled from the teacher of its domain. Teacher provenance, prompt pools and test splits are given in Appendix C . Methods. MOPD (TA init) is the primary control: MOPD from the uniform TA merge ( λ k = 0.25 \lambda_{k}=0.25 ), that is, P 2 -MOPD with the mixing coefficients α \alpha frozen at λ \lambda (the η α = 0 \eta_{\alpha}=0 corner of Algorithm 1 ). MOPD starts from θ 0 \theta_{0} and MOPD (SFT warm-up) from an SFT warm-up on teacher samples. Open-MOPD ( Gao et al., 2026 ) adds per-domain budget balancing, and IM-MOPD ( Kim et al., 2026 ) periodically merges the task vectors of under-recovered domains into the student; both start from the same merge. P 2 -MOPD uses module-wise granularity unless stated otherwise. Training and evaluation. All experiments run on eight GPUs ( Appendix F ). Every run trains for 100 steps of one rollout batch each, the training budget of IM-MOPD; we call the checkpoint at step 100 the final checkpoint. Checkpoints at steps 25, 50, 75 and 100 are scored with the official IM-MOPD evaluation harness, which samples three responses per test item with evaluation seeds 42, 43 and 44 and averages their scores (avg@3; Appendix G ). The learned mixture is stable: after training, the mean coefficient of each teacher agrees across the three seeds to within 0.01 0.01 , so the mixture is a structured outcome learned from the experts and the student. The learned coefficients are thus a reproducible and interpretable by-product of training: without any validation data, they identify the teacher that the student most needs. 6 Conclusion We proposed P 2 -MOPD , which treats a linear combination of the student and frozen experts as a single model and trains its weights and mixing coefficients jointly under the MOPD objective. Across four domains at 1.7B and 4B, it surpasses the final average recovery of MOPD in half the training steps and raises worst-domain recovery from below half to nearly 90 % 90\% . The gain comes from reachability. Student weight-only updates barely move along the task vector of the lagging domain, whereas one learnable coefficient per module makes this direction reachable, and the student weight channel adapts the model beyond the task-vector span. The advantage persists without a merged initialization, and the learned coefficients consistently identify the teacher that the student most needs. More broadly, expert parameters need not be consumed once, as an initialization or a merge. Kept as learnable priors throughout distillation, they guide where the student moves, which makes expert weights, and not only expert predictions, a source of supervision for multi-teacher consolidation. Appendix A The Distillation Objective as Implemented This appendix states the exact objective behind Equations 2 and 3 , as used in all reported runs. Rollouts and log-probabilities. At step t t the rollout engine holds the weights θ t \theta_{t} synchronized after step t − 1 t-1 . It samples one response per prompt at temperature 1.0 1.0 with top- p = 1.0 p=1.0 and no top- k k . After generation, the student log-probabilities used in the reward and in the importance ratio are recomputed by the learner with the same weights θ t \theta_{t} (not read from the rollout engine), and the teacher log-probabilities are computed by the routed teacher π θ d ⁡ ( x ) \pi_{\theta_{d(x)}} on the same prefixes at temperature 1.0 1.0 . Support. At every response position i i , the support 𝒱 i \mathcal{V}_{i} is formed from the student distribution only (“student top- k k ” strategy): the S = 16 S=16 most probable student tokens are selected; their probabilities are renormalized among themselves; and, in descending order, a token is retained while the cumulative renormalized mass preceding it is below 0.99 0.99 (so the token that crosses the threshold is kept and the most probable token is always kept). The teacher distribution is evaluated on the same support; tokens outside 𝒱 i \mathcal{V}_{i} contribute nothing. The support is rebuilt from the recomputed student log-probabilities at learner time. Reward. With w i ​ ( v ) = p ⁡ ( v ) / ∑ u ∈ 𝒱 i p ⁡ ( u ) w_{i}(v)=p(v)/\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}_{i}}p(u) the per-candidate reward is r i ​ ( v ) = − w i ​ ( v ) ​ [ log ⁡ p ⁡ ( v ) − log ⁡ q ⁡ ( v ) ] r_{i}(v)=-w_{i}(v)[\log p(v)-\log q(v)] as in Equation 2 , with raw (not renormalized) log-probabilities inside the bracket. Writing Z p = ∑ u ∈ 𝒱 i p ⁡ ( u ) Z_{p}=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}_{i}}p(u) and Z q = ∑ u ∈ 𝒱 i q ⁡ ( u ) Z_{q}=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}_{i}}q(u) for the support masses and p ~ , q ~ \tilde{p},\tilde{q} for the two distributions renormalized on 𝒱 i \mathcal{V}_{i} , the summed reward is ∑ v ∈ 𝒱 i r i ( v ) = − [ KL ( p ~ ∥ q ~ ) + log Z p Z q ] , \sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{i}}r_{i}(v)=-\Big[\mathrm{KL}\big(\tilde{p}\,\|\,\tilde{q}\big)+\log\tfrac{Z_{p}}{Z_{q}}\Big], (9) i.e. a support-restricted reverse KL plus a term that penalizes the student for placing more mass on its own top candidates than the teacher does. Responses that hit the generation length cap are masked out. No reward-scale normalization and no target-share or budget correction is applied in the reported runs, except for the budget balancing of Open-MOPD. The reward is used directly as the per-candidate advantage ( A i ​ ( v ) = r i ​ ( v ) A_{i}(v)=r_{i}(v) , no baseline, no group normalization), and it is recomputed at learner time from the recomputed student log-probabilities. Surrogate. Let ρ i ​ ( v ) = π θ ​ ( v ∣ x , y < i ) / π θ t ​ ( v ∣ x , y < i ) \rho_{i}(v)=\pi_{\theta}(v\mid x,y_{<i})/\pi_{\theta_{t}}(v\mid x,y_{<i}) . The per-candidate loss is the PPO-style clipped objective with asymmetric clipping range ( 1 − 0.2 , 1 + 0.28 ) (1-0.2,\,1+0.28) and dual clipping at 3 3 for negative advantages ( Schulman et al., 2017 ) : ℓ i ​ ( v ) = max ⁡ ( − A i ​ ( v ) ​ ρ i ​ ( v ) , − A i ​ ( v ) ​ clip ​ ( ρ i ​ ( v ) , 0.8 , 1.28 ) ) , ℓ i ​ ( v ) ← min ⁡ ( ℓ i ​ ( v ) , − 3 ​ A i ​ ( v ) ) ​ if ​ A i ​ ( v ) < 0 . \ell_{i}(v)=\max\!\big(-A_{i}(v)\rho_{i}(v),\,-A_{i}(v)\,\mathrm{clip}(\rho_{i}(v),0.8,1.28)\big),\quad\ell_{i}(v)\leftarrow\min\!\big(\ell_{i}(v),\,-3A_{i}(v)\big)\ \text{if }A_{i}(v)<0. (10) Candidate losses are summed over v ∈ 𝒱 i v\in\mathcal{V}_{i} to a per-token loss, and per-token losses are averaged over all unmasked response tokens of the mini-batch (“token-mean”). Because the mini-batch equals the rollout batch and a single PPO epoch is used, each rollout batch yields exactly one optimizer step, θ = θ t \theta=\theta_{t} when the gradient is formed, ρ i ​ ( v ) = 1 \rho_{i}(v)=1 up to floating-point recomputation noise, and the clipping is inactive in effect; the gradient of the surrogate then equals the gradient of Equation 3 . Masking. Responses whose generation ended by hitting the length cap are flagged at rollout time and excluded from the loss mask entirely (all of their tokens), before token-mean aggregation. Prompts are never trained on. Per-domain decomposition. For analysis, the learner performs the backward pass domain by domain: the token-mean loss is restricted to the tokens of domain d d and multiplied by that domain’s share of unmasked tokens, so that the per-domain gradients sum exactly to the batch gradient G t G_{t} . After each domain’s backward pass the per-domain coefficient gradients ⟨ G m ( d ) , τ k , m ⟩ \langle G^{(d)}_{m},\tau_{k,m}\rangle are recorded, without clearing the accumulating weight gradient, and the total coefficient gradient used in Equation 6 is their sum. This decomposition changes neither the weight nor the coefficient update; it only exposes the domain-wise terms discussed in Section 3.2 . Appendix B Update Rule: Implementation Details and Equivalence Conditions What is stored. The trainable state consists of the full-precision (FP32) master copy of the student weights ( W , ϕ ) (W,\phi) , sharded across data-parallel workers, the AdamW moments for all of them, the free coefficient table α ~ \tilde{\alpha} and its Adam moments. B B is never stored. The task vectors are not stored on the accelerator; the base and expert checkpoints are memory-mapped and each τ k , m \tau_{k,m} is formed one module at a time when needed. Order of operations within a step. (1) Rollout at θ t \theta_{t} and teacher scoring. (2) Learner forward/backward producing ( G , G ϕ ) (G,G_{\phi}) , with the per-domain decomposition above. (3) Coefficient gradients ⟨ G m , τ k , m ⟩ \langle G_{m},\tau_{k,m}\rangle are computed from the unclipped sharded gradients, each shard contributing its partial inner product and the partial sums reduced across shards; tied coefficients sum their modules’ values. (4) The full gradient ( G , G ϕ ) (G,G_{\phi}) is clipped to global norm c W c_{W} and AdamW (decay 0 0 ) is stepped on ( W , ϕ ) (W,\phi) . (5) The coefficient gradient is clipped to norm c α c_{\alpha} and Adam (decay 0 0 ) is stepped on α ~ \tilde{\alpha} . (6) The increment ∑ k Δ ​ α k , m ​ τ k , m \sum_{k}\Delta\alpha_{k,m}\tau_{k,m} is added to each FP32 module shard. (7) Both learning-rate schedulers advance. (8) The weights are synchronized to the rollout engine. If the clipped weight-gradient norm or the coefficient-gradient norm is non-finite, steps (4) to (6) are skipped for that batch and no state changes. Equivalence to optimizing B B . Let 𝒜 \mathcal{A} denote Adam with decay zero and a given learning-rate schedule. Its state after t t steps is a function of the gradient sequence G 0 , … , G t − 1 G_{0},\dots,G_{t-1} only. Since ∂ ℒ t / ∂ B = ∂ ℒ t / ∂ W = G t \partial\mathcal{L}_{t}/\partial B=\partial\mathcal{L}_{t}/\partial W=G_{t} , running 𝒜 \mathcal{A} on B B and running it on W W produce the same increments Δ t 𝒜 \Delta^{\mathcal{A}}_{t} , and W t + 1 = W t + Δ t 𝒜 + D ​ Δ ​ α t W_{t+1}=W_{t}+\Delta^{\mathcal{A}}_{t}+D\Delta\alpha_{t} is exactly B t + 1 + D ​ α t + 1 B_{t+1}+D\alpha_{t+1} when W t = B t + D ​ α t W_{t}=B_{t}+D\alpha_{t} . With nonzero decay the AdamW step contains − η ​ λ wd ​ W t -\eta\lambda_{\mathrm{wd}}W_{t} versus − η ​ λ wd ​ B t -\eta\lambda_{\mathrm{wd}}B_{t} , which differ by − η ​ λ wd ​ D ​ α t -\eta\lambda_{\mathrm{wd}}D\alpha_{t} ; the equivalence then fails, and the materialized- W W form would shrink the task-vector component. All reported runs use decay 0 0 . Checkpoints. Every 25 steps the FP32 weights, optimizer states and the coefficient table are written; the exported model is a standard checkpoint of ( W , ϕ ) (W,\phi) with no coefficient metadata required for inference.
