We study the self-learning loop of agentic robotics through a controlled simulation study spanning nine manipulation tasks, 631 different experiment configurations, and 28K coding agent rollouts, systematically varying model capability, skill priors, feedback structure, and curriculum. The experiment data suggests that the bottleneck is often not the access to sufficient information, but interpreting it into the right policy update.
Across these factors, a successful self-learning loop depends on how effectively agents attribute failures, reason task-relevant spatial and geometric structure, and selectively restructure code to improve policies. Right: Deformable manipulation requires reasoning over object configuration and contact that is difficult to capture through local motion decisions. 1 Introduction Recent coding agents, such as GPT Astra ( OpenAI, 2026a ) and Claude Fable ( Anthropic, 2026a ) , have become increasingly capable of generating and iteratively repairing executable programs, motivating a growing class of Agentic Robotics (AR) systems in which LLM- and VLM-based agents write code that composes perception, planning, and control skills into robot policies ( Liang et al., 2023b ; Singh et al., 2023 ; Fu et al., 2026 ; Chen et al., 2026a ) .
However, self-improvement in robotics differs fundamentally from software debugging. In software, failures, such as compiler errors, are often exposed through symbolic feedback that is already closely aligned with the program; but in robotics, the evidence to improve a policy is instead embedded in physical interactions: object geometry, spatial relationships, contact, motion, and changes in the scene over time. As illustrated in Figure 1 , a failed placement can be attributed to an earlier incompatible grasp and a collision requires reasoning about the geometry of an object after it becomes part of the moving robot.
In this paper, we ask: what determines whether an agent can interpret physical interaction and convert it into effective robot-policy improvement? We design a controlled simulation study of agentic robot self-learning across five contact-rich VABAR manipulation tasks Xie et al. (2026b) and four longer-horizon MimicGen tasks Mandlekar et al. (2023) . Holding the overall self-learning harness fixed, we systematically vary four components of the learning loop: model capability, skill prior, feedback structure, and curriculum, as illustrated in Figure 2 .
Across these studies, we find that self-learning is limited less by the availability of useful information than by the agent’s ability to turn the information into effective policy updates. More execution feedback is not necessarily more useful: feedback is most effective when aligned with semantically meaningful policy decisions rather than reported at the finest temporal resolution. Similarly, world-frame states contain all the information needed for manipulation, yet agents often fail to recover task-relevant object-relative and functional relationships unless these are made explicit. Even when agents identify local geometric errors, they often struggle to translate them into coherent collision-aware motion plans. We find that this limitation can also affect curriculum design: agents tend to preserve and patch policy structure inherited from earlier stages rather than fundamentally reorganize it, so privileged learning can either provide a useful initialization or constrain later learning when the inherited policy depends on privileged precision or brittle manipulation strategies.
Our results further suggest that progress in agentic robot self-learning comes from the better structures of skills, feedback, and state representations and the learned physical reasoning ability from the underlying models. This paper makes three contributions:
(1) A controlled study design for Agentic Robotics that isolates four important components of simulation-guided self-learning, (2) Experiment data from 631 implemented experiments with 28 thousand simulation rollouts on Agentic robotics policies, (3) Six findings on model capability, skill priors, feedback structure, and curriculum. We evaluate zero-shot and self-learning performance, and final task success across VABAR and MimicGen manipulation tasks. 2 Related Work Agentic Robotics. Foundation models have been used to compose robot skills and generate executable policies through language-model planning and code generation ( Liang et al., 2023a ; Ahn et al., 2023 ; Singh et al., 2023 ) . More recent work integrates foundation models with explicit spatial representations, geometric constraints, and task-and-motion planning to support open-vocabulary and multi-step manipulation ( Huang et al., 2023a ; Huang et al., 2024b ; Shen et al., 2026 ; Murray et al., 2025 ) . Other approaches close the loop by using simulation or real-world execution feedback to revise policies, acquire skills, and improve task performance over repeated interactions ( Fu et al., 2026 ; Chen et al., 2026a ; Xiao et al., 2026 ; Lu et al., 2026 ) . Rather than introducing a new agent architecture, we study how individual components of this self-learning loop affect performance. Controlled Evaluation of Agentic Robotics. Robot-learning benchmarks increasingly use standardized task suites to evaluate generalization, long-horizon reasoning, and manipulation under controlled variation ( Gu et al., 2023 ; Li et al., 2022 ; Mees et al., 2022 ; Liu et al., 2023 ; Zheng et al., 2022 ) . More recent benchmarks extend controlled evaluation to foundation-model agents by measuring interactive decision making, embodied reasoning, perception, and action across diverse environments ( Liu et al., 2024 ; Manling Li et al., 2024 ; Rui Yang et al., 2025 ) . Robot-specific evaluations further study robot-centric perception and decision making, coding-agent behavior under different abstraction and interaction settings, and repeated manipulation under controlled task variation ( Xie et al., 2026a ; Fu et al., 2026 ; Xie et al., 2026b ) . Unlike prior agentic robotics benchmarks that primarily vary tasks, environments, or complete agent systems, we hold the self-learning harness fixed and independently vary model capability, skill priors, feedback structure, and curriculum to isolate how each factor affects initial policy generation and subsequent self-improvement. Skills, Feedback, and Curriculum for Self-Learning. Prior work has shown that reusable skills and execution feedback can improve embodied agent learning ( Ahn et al., 2023 ; Wang et al., 2024 ; Huang et al., 2023b ; Fu et al., 2026 ) . Skill libraries provide reusable action abstractions that constrain generation and support compositional behavior, while systems such as Voyager jointly combine an executable skill library, environment feedback, and an automatic curriculum for continual improvement ( Wang et al., 2024 ) . More broadly, language agents can use environment observations, linguistic critiques, or tool-derived feedback to revise plans and outputs across interaction or repeated attempts ( Yao et al., 2023 ; Shinn et al., 2023 ; Madaan et al., 2023 ; Gou et al., 2024 ) . However, prior work also shows that self-correction can be unreliable without sufficiently informative external feedback ( Huang et al., 2024a ) . Curriculum learning instead structures the progression of experience ( Bengio et al., 2009 ) , while privileged-information methods use state available during training to simplify learning before transferring to image-based or otherwise non-privileged observations ( Pinto et al., 2017 ; Chen et al., 2020 ) . These factors are often introduced jointly within a single system. We instead study them separately by varying the skill prior, temporal and spatial feedback representation, and privileged-to-vision curriculum. 3 Problem Formulation Agentic robotics for manipulation. A robot manipulation task class can be defined as a tuple 𝒯 = ⟨ ℒ , ℰ , ℛ , 𝒪 , 𝒳 , ℬ , 𝒥 ⟩ \mathcal{T}=\langle\mathcal{L},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{O},\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{J}\rangle : a natural-language instruction ℒ \mathcal{L} , a fixed workcell ℰ \mathcal{E} with a known robot and sensor configuration ℛ \mathcal{R} , a bounded set of objects 𝒪 \mathcal{O} with known models, a state space 𝒳 \mathcal{X} of robot configurations and object poses, a belief space ℬ \mathcal{B} giving the distribution over initial object poses, and a reward 𝒥 \mathcal{J} , here the per-object success indicator 𝕀 ⁡ ( success ) \mathbb{I}(\text{success}) . A task instance τ i = ⟨ o i , x i ⟩ \tau_{i}=\langle o_{i},x_{i}\rangle is drawn from 𝒪 \mathcal{O} and ℬ \mathcal{B} .
The agent produces a policy π \pi represented as executable source code composed from reusable robot skills. Following the graph-based policy representation of GaP ( Chen et al., 2026a ) , we view π \pi as a computation graph whose nodes invoke skills and whose edges define their execution and control flow. The policy is constructed over a skill library 𝒮 = 𝒮 0 ∪ 𝒮 g ∪ 𝒮 τ ∪ 𝒮 A \mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{0}\cup\mathcal{S}_{g}\cup\mathcal{S}_{\tau}\cup\mathcal{S}_{A} ,
where 𝒮 0 \mathcal{S}_{0} contains basic robot and sensor primitives; 𝒮 g \mathcal{S}_{g} contains reusable general-purpose skills; 𝒮 τ \mathcal{S}_{\tau} contains task-specific skills provided by a human expert; and 𝒮 A \mathcal{S}_{A} contains task-specific skills synthesized by the agent.
Given 𝒮 \mathcal{S} , an LLM agent A A constructs π \pi by selecting, parameterizing, and composing available skills, while implementing new skills when the existing library is insufficient. On a task instance τ i \tau_{i} , the resulting policy π ⁡ ( a ∣ ℐ ) \pi(a\mid\mathcal{I}) maps observations ℐ \mathcal{I} to robot actions a a in closed loop, and the resulting episode is evaluated by 𝒥 \mathcal{J} .
Given a task class 𝒯 \mathcal{T} and skill library 𝒮 \mathcal{S} , the agent seeks to construct a policy that succeeds across the belief space: π ∗ = arg ⁡ max π ​ 𝔼 x i ∼ ℬ ​ [ 𝒥 ⁡ ( π ⁡ ( a ∣ ℐ ) ) ] , \pi^{*}=\arg\max_{\pi}\;\mathbb{E}_{x_{i}\sim\mathcal{B}}\left[\mathcal{J}\big(\pi(a\mid\mathcal{I})\big)\right], using only ℒ \mathcal{L} , 𝒮 \mathcal{S} , and the outcomes of its own policies.
Policy improvement may therefore modify both the composition of the policy graph and the implementation of its constituent skills. Self-learning loop. Unlike reinforcement learning, feedback in the self-learning loop is not restricted to a scalar reward. At iteration k k , the current policy π k \pi_{k} runs in simulation on the training instances { τ i } \{\tau_{i}\} . A feedback function F F turns these runs into a textual (and optionally visual) report f k = F ⁡ ( π k , { τ i } ) f_{k}=F(\pi_{k},\{\tau_{i}\}) . The agent then edits its own policy, conditioned on the report and on its edit history π k + 1 = A ⁡ ( π k , f k , { ( π j , f j ) } j < k ) \pi_{k+1}=A\big(\pi_{k},\,f_{k},\,\{(\pi_{j},f_{j})\}_{j<k}\big) .
The loop runs for K K iterations.
Success is reported on a held-out set of instances for the initial policy π 0 \pi_{0} (zero-shot, k = 0 k{=}0 ) and for the iterate with the best validation score, π k ∗ \pi_{k^{*}} with k ∗ = arg ⁡ max k ⁡ val ⁡ ( π k ) k^{*}=\arg\max_{k}\mathrm{val}(\pi_{k}) . Held-out success at every iteration gives a learning curve for each setting and task. Each study below varies one component of this loop: the agent A A , the skills 𝒮 \mathcal{S} , the feedback F F , or the curriculum over training instances. Assumptions and Scope. We assume access to a reliable physics simulator. Training and evaluation remain within the same manipulation task class, with a fixed robot and sensor configuration and bounded scene variation. We next study how agents use the skills they are given. The skill library provides an important prior: the expert skills encode task-specific manipulation strategies, while the general library provides
reusable perception, grasping, and motion-planning routines. Such a library could help the agent by providing useful building blocks, or
could instead constrain it to behaviors already represented in the library. To distinguish these cases, we trace the source of each line in the zero-shot policy ( @ ​ 0 @0 ). After normalizing each line, we match it verbatim against the expert task-specific skills, the general-library skills also used by the human policy, and the remaining general-library skills; unmatched lines are attributed to
new code written by the agent. Figure 5 reports the mean number of lines from each source, averaged over runs per task and then over tasks, and relates it to success after feedback in Table 1 .
As shown in Figure 5 , models draw on the same skills in different ways.
Models that improve most with feedback also write a larger share of their policy as new task-specific code: new code makes up 24% of the lines for Opus 5 xhigh, 18% for Opus 5.5, and 12% for Fable 5.1.
They use the expert skills as a basis but add or modify task-specific logic where the provided implementations are insufficient.
In contrast, Sonnet 5, DeepSeek V4, Kimi K3, and GPT-6 Astra write only 4–7% new code and rely more heavily on behavior already represented in the expert and general skills.
These models also improve the least, ending at 39–46% overall success after two rounds.
Using more of the library is not the same as adapting beyond it: Kimi K3 uses the largest share of general skills not used by the human policy (13% of its lines), yet still writes little new code and gains only 7 points.
Since several of these models start from similar zero-shot success, the difference is not simply in the quality of the first policy, but in whether the agent can modify and extend the provided skills when the existing behavior is insufficient. Finding 2. Expert skills are a boundary for weaker models but a scaffold for stronger ones. Weaker models largely compose behaviors already represented in the skill library, while stronger models selectively reuse and depart from human-provided skills to create new task-specific approaches. 5.3 Feedback Figure 6: Number of skills traced back from the observed failing skill before each post-failure policy edit. The same execution state expressed at different levels of spatial
organization. We next study whether the representation of execution state affects an
agent’s ability to reason about spatial failures.
A conventional world-frame representation describes where objects are, but leaves the agent to reconstruct the object-relative quantities needed for manipulation.
As suggests in Table 3 , the world-frame state is sufficient in principle to recover the manipulation-relevant quantities, the agent often fails to reliably compose the required coordinate transformations and connect them to a policy decision.
Object-frame feedback makes these same relationships explicit, such as grasp offsets and relative orientations, and substantially improves performance on Tool Hanging and, to a lesser extent, Gear Meshing. This suggests that information completeness alone is insufficient, and agents can possess the necessary coordinates yet still fail to derive the spatial relationships needed for manipulation.
The same limitation extends beyond coordinate frames to object geometry itself. Providing accurate object state and geometry does not mean the agent understands how that geometry constrains action, because inferring the affordance features of the wrench and hanger from whole-object poses is challenging for agents. Part annotations further improve feedback by exposing the functional structures that matter for interaction. Part-annotated
feedback reaches 52% overall success, compared with 40% for object frames and 30% for world-frame state and relations. Finding 4. Recovering task-relevant spatial and geometric relationships from raw state information remains a bottleneck for agentic robotics. Although world-frame and object-frame feedback describe the same underlying scene, agents perform substantially better when object-relative geometry and functional spatial relationships are explicitly available, rather than requiring them to derive them. Fable(F). We next study how self-learning agents translate spatial understanding into executable robot motion. We compare how human-written and agent-learnt policies structure perception, motion planning, and recovery. Figure 8 shows that human policies frequently make geometric structure explicit through repeated perception and re-registration, intermediate waypoints, and dedicated motion-planning stages. These intermediate poses encode geometric priors such as approaching from a safer direction, maintaining clearance, or moving through configurations from which a feasible motion is easier to find.
In contrast, self-learning agents more often use verification, retry, and recovery around coarser manipulation steps.
They can reuse motions containing useful geometric structure, but are less
consistent at reconstructing why that structure is necessary when generating
or modifying the policy themselves. Syringe Packing provides a concrete example.

Agents can recognize local geometric errors, but struggle to integrate object pose, held-object geometry, obstacles, and clearance into a coherent collision-aware motion plan. 5.4 Curriculum Design We study a representative privileged-to-perception curriculum , a common design in reinforcement learning and used in prior works such as Pinto et al. (2017) ; Chen et al. (2020) .
As a focused case study of curriculum transfer in agentic robotics, the agent first learns with privileged simulation states with exact simulator geometry and is then transferred to non-privileged simulation states such as RGB-D perception.
We evaluate this curriculum on four MimicGen tasks using their Medium (D1) layouts with 8 privileged and 8 perception iterations and Claude Fable 5.1. We also use three VABAR tasks: Tool Hanging (Level 2), Gear Meshing (Level 2), and USB-C Insertion (Level 1) with 5 privileged iterations and 5 perception iterations with Claude Opus 5. Figure 10 suggests that privileged-to-perception learning does not uniformly improve over learning directly from perception. Privileged information removes perception uncertainty during early learning, but, as suggested by Findings 4 and 5, accurate geometry alone does not guarantee that the agent discovers the right spatial representation or manipulation strategy.
The privileged phase can learn useful task structure, as in Threading, Coffee, and Mug Cleanup, but it can also sometimes commit early to brittle or non-generalizable choices.
For example, Kitchen develops a grasp strategy that depends on geometric precision unavailable after transfer, while Tool Hanging remains below 10% held-out success during privileged learning despite access to exact wrench geometry. Thus, privileged information can simplify the learning problem without necessarily producing a policy that remains appropriate under perception. We further visualize and analyze representative failure cases in Appendix J.2 . In addition, Table 4 further suggests that once a policy structure is established under privileged
state, subsequent perception-based learning tends to preserve and patch that structure rather than fundamentally reorganize it (which corresponds to Finding 1).
Across tasks, the privileged-to-perception policies change little in size after transfer, from 12.0 skills at the end of the privileged phase to 12.7 after perception learning on average, while the final perception-only policies contain 16.1 skills.
The resulting path dependence can be beneficial when the early structure captures a manipulation strategy that remains valid under vision, but this can be harmful when the structure assumes geometric precision unavailable from perception. In such cases, the curriculum does not merely provide an initialization; it can constrain subsequent self-learning. Privileged-to-perception curriculum learning can either accelerate or constrain subsequent perception-based learning. Privileged agents learn policies that rely on exact state, usually with less verification and recovery, and often retain this structure after transfer. This helps when the learned manipulation strategy remains valid, allowing perception learning to focus on perception errors, but hurts when the policy depends on state precision unavailable under perception or lacks mechanisms for handling perceptual uncertainty. 6 Discussion and Limitations Figure 11: What closes the gap to human-written policies? For VABAR Level-1 test cases solved by the human policy with Opus 5, improvements come progressively with the earlier findings, while a small fraction unsolved. Our results return to the central question of this study: what determines whether an agent can interpret physical interaction and convert it into effective robot-policy improvement?
Figure 11 provides a complementary view across the experiments.
Only 7.3% of the human-solvable Level-1 test cases are solved by zero-shot policy generation alone, but the results suggest that improving agentic robotics requires the combination of iterative self-learning with richer simulation feedback, human task skills, and more powerful models.
At the same time, this decomposition shows that 4.1% of test cases solved by a human-written policy under the same robot and simulation setup remain unsolved by the agents. Moreover, this study primarily considers Level-1 VABAR tasks with uncluttered environments and relatively simple motions.
The remaining gap is concentrated in agents’ ability to interpret physical interaction into task-relevant reasoning and policy changes, particularly for spatial reasoning, manipulation planning, and collision-aware motion planning. Limitations. This study does not cover the full range of conditions that may affect agentic robot self-learning, such as prompting strategies and harness configurations. Given the large number of agent rollouts required for controlled evaluation, we leave a broader study of these factors to future work. Our model comparison is also necessarily a snapshot in time, as new agent models are released frequently; we plan to update the evaluation as new models become available. We focus on simulation to enable repeatable evaluation, leaving sim-to-real transfer and adaptation to real-world feedback as important next steps; real-to-sim toolkits such as RoboSimStudio ( Chen et al., 2026b ) may make it easier to construct matching simulations of real tasks for such studies. Finally, we do not consider human-in-the-loop self-learning, where human feedback may complement autonomous simulation feedback during policy improvement. References Ahn et al. Appendix D Prompts Every coding-agent session starts in a sealed workspace, and the whole prompt is its instruction file INSTRUCTIONS.md : the session’s command-line prompt (Table 18 ) only points to that file, and we add no system prompt. INSTRUCTIONS.md is a task template with its {{slots}} filled, followed by a workspace block. We give the session templates, the workspace block, the slots whose values depend on the task, and what differs between the arms of a study. The templates are byte-identical in every harness snapshot the runs used. D.1 Session Templates A generate session ( k = 0 k{=}0 ) reads Listing D.1 ; every improve session reads Listing D.1 , with the feedback report in FEEDBACK.md (Appendix E ). When the guard or the validator rejects a proposal, the next round edits the rejected proposal with Listing D.1 appended. When the unscored preflight episode raises a program error, a fix session runs the improve template with Listing D.1 appended. The templates hold only task-independent advice. E.5 Report Size and Truncation A report shows at most 12 outputs per node, each value cut to 60 characters, and at most 40 units per episode (the first and last four and those around the failing unit). Node errors are cut to 300 characters, a graph traceback to its last 1,500 characters, and the previous session’s account to 800 characters. The unit cap almost never binds (fewer than 1% of the reports behind Figure 15 , all of them per-motion reports). Figure 15 shows how long the reports are: outcome-only reports stay near 3k characters, while state numbers and part geometry bring the reference to 21k, and Syringe Packing reports of Table 1 run to a median of 70k. perfect-training streak (only in the runs without a validation set, Appendix C.2 ), the unchanged streak,

budgets; the unchanged streak and the spend never end a run. Most 6-iteration runs ran with a 30 h wall-time limit,
and part of them with shorter episode budgets (1,400, 6,000, 2,400, 1,200–2,400 and 9,000 s in task

skills, meet the target less often and later: 76% stop at the cap, 22% on the target (5% at k = 0 k{=}0 ) and 2% after
two perfect training batches. F.3 Kept Iterate The kept iterate is the first iterate with the highest validation success, so an edit is kept only if it raises
validation success. It is π 0 \pi_{0} in 70% of the 3-iteration runs, for three reasons that split by task
(Table 16 ). Half of these runs meet the target with π 0 \pi_{0} , mostly on Gear Meshing and Cable
Routing. A third never succeed on a validation instance, so every iterate ties at zero: on Tool Hanging no run of
DeepSeek V4, Kimi K3 or either GPT-6 setting ever validates, nor any Kimi K3 run on USB-C Insertion, the repair
failure of Finding 1 at its sharpest. In the remaining 15%, almost all on Syringe Packing, π 0 \pi_{0} validates at least
as well as both edits. The 6-iteration runs keep π 0 \pi_{0} in 34% of runs, as they rarely stop at k = 0 k{=}0 and have five edits to improve on it.
The runs without a validation set keep the revert rule’s incumbent, and one skill-library run, resumed to its iteration
cap after a wall-time stop, keeps the iterate chosen at that stop. F.4 Superseded First Passes Three passes preceded the protocol of Appendix C . The first stopped after two perfect training batches and kept the iterate that trained best, and its first launch shipped the expert scripts with the reference library, so the agents copied the human solution; two of its runs enter the Opus 5, xhigh row of Table 1 (Appendix C.2 ), and its Tool Hanging run stopped with 70% held-out success. The second trained on four distinct instances but still stopped and selected on them; a brief third validated on three. Since the report describes exactly the training episodes, selecting on them rewards edits fitted to those layouts, so we moved stopping and selection to five validation instances the agent never sees and, in the feedback and skill-library studies, ship 𝒮 τ \mathcal{S}_{\tau} only to the + 𝒮 τ +\,\mathcal{S}_{\tau} arm. Curriculum Study Appendix J Privileged-to-Perception Curriculum This appendix provides the full experimental details for the curriculum study in Section 5.4 (Figure 10 and Table 4 ). Section J.1 describes the two learning settings, tool access, tasks, training and evaluation protocol, and the conversion from privileged geometry to perception. Section J.2 then analyzes representative cases in which transfer is unsuccessful. J.1 Experimental Setup J.1.1 Settings and Agent Learning settings. We compare two settings that use the same self-learning loop (Section 3 ), model, instructions, skill library, feedback, training layouts, and total iteration budget. They differ only in how the policy observes the scene. • Privileged → \rightarrow perception (w/ curr.). For the first K p K_{p} iterations, the policy observes exact simulator geometry and has no camera access. We then convert the best privileged checkpoint to a perception policy and continue learning for K v K_{v} iterations using perception only. • Perception only (w/o curr.). The policy uses cameras and perception tools for all K p + K v K_{p}+K_{v} iterations. MimicGen and VABAR runs use different configurations, described in Section J.1.4 . Within each suite, the privileged → \rightarrow perception and perception-only settings use the same model, probe allowance, feedback format, training and held-out layouts, iteration budget, and evaluation protocol; they differ only in the scene information available to the policy. On MimicGen, we use Claude Fable 5.1 with K p = K v = 8 K_{p}=K_{v}=8 (16 iterations per run). On VABAR, we use Claude Opus 5 with K p = K v = 5 K_{p}=K_{v}=5 (10 iterations per run). We run each setting three times per task (runs 000, 001, and 002), giving 7 × 3 = 21 7\times 3=21 curriculum runs and 21 perception-only runs. Initial policies. Iteration 0 is a zero-shot policy written by the agent from the task instruction and skill library. In the perception-only setting, each run starts from a fixed zero-shot perception policy shared with the other perception-only studies in the paper. In the privileged setting, the agent writes a separate zero-shot policy using the privileged tool surface, because a perception policy cannot execute with the privileged tool list. On VABAR, run 000 in each setting uses the first workflow graph produced for that run, while runs 001 and 002 are generated in new sessions from the same frozen prompt and model without additional training. Agent harness. The agent is Claude Code with temperature 0.2. It can read, write, and search files in the policy workspace, run ./validate.sh , and copy library skills into the workspace. It cannot invoke the simulator directly, except on MimicGen, where it may call ./probe.sh for probe episodes (Appendix J.1.5 ). Each improvement step starts a new session that receives the current policy, the latest feedback report, and a summary of previous iterations and code changes. On MimicGen, a session is limited to 35 turns, 10 minutes of active time, and 45 minutes total. On VABAR, a session is limited to 80 turns with no wall-clock limit, as in the other VABAR experiments. The conversion session uses the same turn limit and no wall-clock limit. J.1.2 Tool Access Privileged tools. During the privileged phase, the policy observes the scene only through simulator geometry queries: sim.list_objects , sim.get_object_pose , and sim.get_object_obb provide exact object poses and oriented bounding boxes, while sim.list_features and sim.get_feature provide world-frame geometry for named functional parts. These parts are defined by the task assets; examples include the needle handle and shaft in Threading, the stove burner in Kitchen, the machine holder and lid in Coffee, the drawer handle and floor in Mug Cleanup, and each wrench ring and hook shank in Tool Hanging. VABAR additionally provides sim.list_regions and sim.get_region_obb for regions, and sim.get_fixture_layout for multi-part fixtures without a single object pose, such as the peg centers, pinion center, and button on the Gear Meshing board. All camera and perception tools are disabled during this phase, including RGB-D observations, Grounding DINO detection, SAM3 segmentation and tracking, and geometry helpers that recover 3D structure from masks and depth. Robot control, motion, and grasp skills are unchanged. The agent is explicitly instructed to learn task decomposition and manipulation logic that can later be executed from RGB-D observations, and not to hard-code poses observed in feedback. Perception tools. The perception policy observes the scene only through calibrated cameras that provide RGB, depth, intrinsics, and camera pose. MimicGen uses the fixed agentview camera and a wrist camera; VABAR uses each task’s own camera set: fixed overhead, front, side, or top-down views, plus wrist cameras in Tool Hanging and USB-C Insertion. The policy can use Grounding DINO for text-prompted bounding boxes, SAM3 for masks from text, boxes, or points, and geometry utilities that back-project masks and depth into world coordinates and fit boxes, lines, and planes. All sim.* geometry queries are disabled. In both settings, the policy never receives task flags, goal predicates, success checks, or task-specific planners. These signals are used only for scoring and feedback. Tool enforcement. Each setting uses a fixed tool list. Before an edited policy can run, a validator checks every policy script and rejects any call outside the allowed tool surface, including calls constructed dynamically at run time. The agent must pass this check with ./validate.sh before the session ends. If the check fails, the violation is returned to the agent for up to two repair rounds. J.1.3 Tasks Table 26 lists the seven tasks used in the study. We select tasks that require both accurate state estimation and non-trivial manipulation, so that separating perception from manipulation can meaningfully affect learning. • Threading and Coffee require precise insertion. A failure can therefore arise from target localization, the object’s pose in the gripper, or the insertion motion itself. • Kitchen and Mug Cleanup are long, multi-stage tasks with articulated objects, so errors early in the trajectory can affect later stages. • Tool Hanging (Level 2) contains two wrenches, two hooks, and distractor tools. Each wrench must be grasped so that its ring remains available for hanging and the object remains stable during transport. • Gear Meshing (Level 2) requires placing three gears on thin pegs so that they mesh with a fixed pinion, followed by a button press. • USB-C Insertion (Level 1) requires aligning a small plug with a receptacle to within a few millimeters and inserting it to sufficient depth. In the privileged phase, task-relevant parts such as rings, pegs, and ports are available through exact geometry, which largely removes localization error. After conversion, the same structures must be recovered from RGB-D observations, including small or low-contrast parts. J.1.4 Differences Between MimicGen and VABAR Runs For each benchmark, the two curriculum settings use the same experimental configuration. The VABAR experiments use the same setup as the other VABAR experiments in this paper: Claude Opus 5, text-only feedback, a fixed set of four training layouts, no probe episodes, and sessions of up to 80 turns with no time limit. For MimicGen, we adapt this setup to make self-learning more tractable because its tasks present a harder learning problem. MimicGen tasks are long-horizon, multi-stage, and involve multiple objects. For example, Kitchen requires the agent to interact with the stove, pot, bread, and serving area in the correct sequence over a 1500-step episode. Moreover, the skill library contains no task-specific skills for most MimicGen tasks, so the agent must develop the task-specific skills needed to solve them during self-learning. Object configurations are also randomized over a wide range, making it easy for a policy trained on a fixed set of layouts to overfit. We therefore use a stronger model for MimicGen (Claude Fable 5.1), provide failure-image feedback, allow more learning iterations and training episodes, resample training seeds at every iteration to reduce overfitting, and include probe episodes so that the agent can test a hypothesis before modifying its policy. Because MimicGen learning runs are longer (16 iterations), we additionally limit each session to 35 turns and 10 minutes of agent update time. This encourages each iteration to make a more localized policy update. The differences between the VABAR and MimicGen configurations are summarized in Table 27 . J.1.5 Training and Evaluation Training, held-out, and probe layouts. On MimicGen, each iteration trains on 10 newly sampled layouts, with training layouts not reused across iterations. The agent may additionally run up to two unscored probe episodes per iteration to test a hypothesis. Because VABAR contains fewer layouts, each iteration instead uses the same four fixed training layouts, one episode per layout, and probe episodes are disabled. In both cases, the agent is told that evaluation uses unseen layouts and that movable objects must therefore be localized at run time. After every iteration, the policy is evaluated on 10 fixed held-out layouts that are disjoint from training. Held-out results are never shown to the agent; they are used only to construct the learning curves and to select the privileged checkpoint for conversion. Within each benchmark, the curriculum and perception-only settings use the same training sequence and the same held-out layouts. One learning iteration. Each iteration has three stages. (1) Preflight: the current policy runs on two unscored episodes of at most 300 steps to detect program errors. If it crashes, the agent receives the error and may perform up to two repair sessions. (2) Training: the policy runs on the training layouts and a feedback report is generated. (3) Improvement: a new agent session reads the report and edits the policy; the resulting policy must pass the tool validator. Held-out evaluation of the new policy runs in parallel with the next learning iteration. We do not revert edits or stop runs early, so every run uses its full iteration budget. Feedback. Both settings receive the same feedback structure. The report describes object states and inter-object relations using named object parts and object-centered frames, organized by the named steps of the policy (Section 3 ). For each episode, it also reports which ordered reference stages were reached (Table 26 ) and the corresponding stage reward: the number of stages completed in order before the first missed stage, divided by the total number of stages and averaged across episodes. Up to two episodes are shown in full detail, and the previous six iterations are summarized as history. MimicGen reports additionally include two end-of-episode agentview frames to provide richer feedback and further facilitate learning, whereas VABAR reports are text only. The privileged and perception-only settings therefore differ in policy observations, not in the feedback used for self-improvement. Metrics. We report held-out success after every iteration: native task success on MimicGen and per-object success on VABAR (for example, each wrench or gear is scored separately). We also report held-out stage reward. In Figure 10 , each line is the mean across the three runs and the shaded band shows ± 1 \pm 1 standard error. In Table 4 , # Skills is the number of nodes in the final policy workflow graph, where each node is one code-executing step, and Success is the mean held-out success over the last three iterations; both are averaged across the three runs. J.1.6 Conversion to Perception At the end of the privileged phase, we select the privileged checkpoint with the highest held-out success, breaking ties by stage reward and then by the latest iteration. A dedicated conversion session receives the selected policy, its most recent feedback report, and the perception tool list, together with the following instruction: The privileged perception phase is over. This checkpoint must now run on the vision-only tool surface. Preserve its task decomposition, manipulation logic, recovery branches, and bounded diagnostics. Replace every privileged sim geometry read and every privileged perception skill with calibrated RGB-D perception using the shipped vision skills and tools. Do not hard-code object poses observed in the privileged phase. Finish only when ./validate.sh reports OK on the vision surface. The conversion session uses the same turn limit as a normal improvement session and has no wall-clock limit. It does not execute the policy or receive new environment feedback, so the first perception iteration evaluates the converted policy exactly as produced by the conversion session. All 21 conversions passed the validator on the first attempt, with conversion costs of 12–39 minutes and $5–15 in API usage. The converted checkpoint then becomes the starting policy for the perception phase. J.2 Failure Analysis of Unsuccessful Transfer We further analyze the failure modes observed in the curriculum study from Section 5.4 . Figure 17 illustrates representative examples and reveals two distinct limitations of privileged-to-perception learning. First, policies learned with exact geometry may rely on a level of geometric precision that perception cannot reliably recover after transfer. Second, exact geometry alone may be insufficient for learning the object affordances and contact strategies required for robust manipulation. Dependence on privileged geometric precision. Policies learned with exact geometry can inherit manipulation strategies that are highly sensitive to localization errors after transfer to perception, as illustrated in Figure 17 (a). In Kitchen, the privileged policy in one run achieves 90–100% held-out success by pinching a narrow tab on the pot handle using exact geometry. After transfer to perception, success drops to 0–30% and eventually reaches 0%. The agent repeatedly identifies the tab grasp as the source of failure, but modifies its reaching and retry behavior rather than adopting a more robust grasp. Because the usable grasp region is only a few millimeters wide, the inherited strategy is highly sensitive to perceptual error. Gear Meshing shows a related failure in one run. Held-out success drops from 70–90% before transfer to at most 7% afterward. The inherited policy can still grasp and carry the gears, but after transfer it must recover the peg locations from vision. The policy uses the front camera, where each peg occupies only 2–3 raised depth pixels, rather than the overhead camera, where each occupies about 18 pixels (Figure 17 (a)). As a result, the short pegs are often merged with the plate or missed entirely, leaving the policy without a valid placement target. In contrast, a perception-only run that uses the overhead camera reaches 97–100% held-out success. Subsequent iterations mainly try to repair peg detection, rather than changing the inherited camera choice or manipulation strategy. Together with Kitchen, this shows that privileged learning can produce effective strategies that become brittle when they depend on geometric precision that perception cannot reliably recover. Manipulation failures despite exact geometry. Exact geometry alone may also be insufficient for understanding object affordances and selecting robust manipulation strategies, as illustrated in Figure 17 (b). In Tool Hanging, the privileged state provides the exact wrench pose but does not specify where or how the wrench should be grasped for reliable transport. Consequently, the agent learns to grasp the wrench near its far end, resulting in an unstable grasp during the subsequent carry motion. Because the resulting failure becomes apparent only during transport, the agent struggles to attribute it to the earlier grasp decision and fails to learn an effective strategy. Privileged training never exceeds 10% held-out success in any of the three runs, whereas perception-only learning reaches 60–100%. USB-C Insertion reveals a related limitation in contact reasoning. In one privileged training run, the policy consistently aligns the plug with the bore but fails during insertion because contact with the socket causes the plug to tilt and become wedged. The controller fails to recover from this contact-induced error, resulting in 0% success. These examples show that exact geometry can reduce localization error without providing the affordance understanding and contact reasoning needed for robust manipulation.
