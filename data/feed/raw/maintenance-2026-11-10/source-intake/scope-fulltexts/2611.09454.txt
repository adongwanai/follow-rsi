To save context, these documents are
increasingly compiled into low-rank adapters (LoRA), which keep the behaviour but lose the text: an adapter is hard to read, edit, audit or
move to another model. We ask whether the skill document can be written back from the adapter and its frozen base model, and we score each
recovered document by the share of the original’s key instructions it states and by the tasks it solves. Reading the weights alone recovered
little with the methods we tried; a learned map from weights to text passed on about one bit of the document. Running the adapter worked much
better. The Executing Writer , a small writer trained on top of the adapted model, recovers 39 39 – 44 % 44\% of the key instructions of
held-out skills, against 1.3 1.3 – 1.5 % 1.5\% with another skill’s adapter, and on adapters fitted to 41 41 external skills its documents restore 85 85 – 91 % 91\% of the original’s task gain. These strongest results are on adapters whose training stored the document; adapters trained on
behaviour alone are much harder for trained writers ( 1 1 – 2 % 2\% of instructions). For these adapters
we test an agent that runs the base and adapted models and writes a skill from where they differ. On ALFWorld its skills recover 58 58 – 71 % 71\% of a LoRA’s gain and carry over to models that cannot load the LoRA, lifting Llama-3.1-8B from 5 % 5\% to 55 % 55\% . Whether such a skill comes from
the adapter depends on what the adapter learned: when its behaviour cannot be guessed, the adapter is the source; when it can, a reward loop
without the adapter does as well. Keywords: low-rank adaptation; adapter interpretation; agent skills; weight-space reading; instruction recovery. 1 Introduction An agent skill is a document, usually a few hundred to a few thousand words, that tells a language model how to handle a class of
requests: how to review code, which conventions to follow, how to analyse a packet capture [ 1 , 2 ] . Skills are
written by practitioners, shared in marketplaces and placed in the model’s prompt when needed. That costs context on every call, so a growing
line of work compiles a skill into a low-rank adapter (LoRA) [ 3 ] , a small set of extra weights that changes the model’s behaviour
with no text in the prompt [ 4 , 5 , 6 , 9 , 10 ] . The adapter is cheap to apply and to serve at scale [ 11 ] , but it
is a block of numbers. A text skill can be read before it is trusted; a weight skill is much harder to check. The question. We study the inverse of compilation: Given an adapter and its frozen base model, can we write the skill document that the adapter encodes? A recovered document would make an adapter inspectable and editable, could be re-compiled for another model, and would let a user verify an
adapter before running it. Existing weight-space methods predict attributes, labels or short descriptions of adapters [ 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 ] ;
none of them produces a document or measures how much of one is recovered. Why a document and not only an adapter. A skill document and a LoRA can carry the same behaviour, but they are not equally useful.
A document can be read and reviewed like code, edited in one line, combined with other skills, and given unchanged to a different model or
agent harness. A LoRA is tied to the weights and tokenizer of the base model it was trained on: it does not load on Llama if it was trained
on Qwen, nor on a model served only through an API, and changing one rule means retraining it. Recovering the document turns a model-locked
artefact back into a portable one. We therefore measure recovery in two ways: the share of the original’s instructions that the recovered
document states, and the share of the original’s task gain that it restores. Why it is hard. Three properties of adapters shape the methods. (i) Adapters for different skills are mostly alike: the cosine
between the weight updates of two skills is 0.88 0.88 , and only 11.6 % 11.6\% of their energy is specific to the skill. (ii) Knowing which skill an
adapter holds is not knowing what it says. An encoder identifies the skill from its weights for all 37 37 test skills, yet a learned channel from
the weights to a writer passes on about one bit of the document’s content. (iii) Text similarity is a weak measure, because word overlap rewards generic text. We therefore score
recovered documents against frozen lists of the original’s instructions, run every method with a wrong adapter as a control, and measure tasks
solved wherever we can. Our approach. The content of an adapter shows in what the network computes with it, so we let the network do the reading.
We compare three readers (Figure 1 , Section 4 ). The first treats the weights as data and learns a map from
them to a prompt. The second reads the weights without running them, through the model’s vocabulary and by retrieving similar instruction
lines. The third, the Executing Writer , attaches the adapter to its frozen base model and trains one small writer adapter on top, so
that the adapted model itself writes the document. Section 7 adds a fourth reader, which needs no training and suits adapters that
never saw their document: an agent that runs the base and adapted models on real tasks and writes a skill from where they differ. Figure 1: The pipeline. A skill document s s is compiled into an adapter Δ s \Delta_{s} . The Executing Writer attaches Δ s \Delta_{s} (frozen) and a
trainable writer ϕ \phi to the frozen base model θ \theta and generates a document s ^ \hat{s} . Recovery is scored by the share of the original’s
key instructions that s ^ \hat{s} states and by whether s ^ \hat{s} still works when given to a model. One distinction matters throughout. Adapters differ in whether the document text was ever a training target. A recitation adapter was trained to print its document, so the text is stored in it, and recovering it is extraction. A behavioural adapter was
trained only on answers that the document shaped, so the text never appears in any training target, and recovering it is inference. The second
case is the common one in practice. Our trained writers read the first kind well and the second poorly; for the second kind we turn to the
executing agent. Contributions. 1. We define adapter-to-document recovery, with coverage of frozen instruction lists, a measure of invented content, and two controls
the evidence needs: a wrong adapter and retrieval of a similar document. 2. We compare three readers (Sections 4 and 6 ). The weights reveal a skill’s identity and content words,
but a learned soft-prompt channel passes on about one bit; an information measure, the adapter gap , shows where the difference lies. 3. The Executing Writer recovers 39 39 – 44 % 44\% of a held-out skill’s key instructions, about twice the best weight-only writer and 1.4 1.4 – 1.6 1.6 times retrieval, and only 1.3 1.3 – 1.5 % 1.5\% with a wrong adapter. On adapters fitted to 41 41 external skills it recovers 90 90 – 93 % 93\% of the prose at rank 64 64 ; these
documents restore 85 85 – 91 % 91\% of the original’s task gain and help an agent on benchmark tasks almost as much as the original. 4. For behavioural adapters (Section 7 ) we test an executing agent on ALFWorld, a docker-compose adapter from an earlier project, synthetic tasks and
adapters with hidden rules. Its skills recover 58 58 – 71 % 71\% of an ALFWorld LoRA’s gain and transfer to other models. Controls show that the
adapter is the source of the skill only when the adapter’s behaviour cannot be guessed. 2 Related Work Agent skills. Skills package procedures and conventions as documents that an agent loads when a task calls for
them [ 1 ] . SkillsBench measures how much skills help on diverse tasks with independent verifiers [ 2 ] , and
earlier agents kept libraries of learned skills [ 17 ] or interleaved reasoning with tool use [ 18 , 19 ] . We use
SkillsBench skills and tasks, which we did not write, to test whether recovered documents still work. Compiling text into weights. LoRA [ 3 ] represents a weight update by low-rank factors, following adapters [ 20 ] and continuous prompts [ 21 ] , with evidence that fine-tuning moves a model in a low-dimensional subspace [ 22 ] and that LoRA
learns less than full fine-tuning [ 23 ] . Hypernetworks [ 24 ] generate weights from other inputs: HYPTER [ 25 ] ,
Text-to-LoRA [ 4 ] , Doc-to-LoRA [ 5 ] and SHINE [ 6 ] generate adapters from task descriptions or documents, and
LatentSkill [ 9 ] and Parametric Skills [ 10 ] do this for agent skills. Context distillation [ 26 , 27 ] ,
gist tokens [ 28 ] and cartridges [ 29 ] also move context out of the prompt, and adapters can be composed [ 30 ] or
added arithmetically [ 31 ] . All of these go from text to weights; we go the other way. Prompt and skill optimisation. Searching over prompts with a model in the loop is an established way to improve instructions:
APE [ 50 ] , OPRO [ 51 ] , DSPy [ 52 ] , TextGrad [ 53 ] and GEPA [ 54 ] propose and score candidate prompts, and
Reflexion [ 55 ] , ExpeL [ 56 ] and Agent Workflow Memory [ 57 ] turn an agent’s experience into reusable text. Our reward
loop (Section 7 ) belongs to this family; what is new is the adapter as an extra source of evidence, and the question of whether the
document it yields comes from that adapter. Work on auditing models for hidden objectives [ 58 ] asks a related question about behaviour
that a model was trained to hide. Interpreting adapter weights. W2T [ 12 ] and Learning on LoRAs [ 32 ] process LoRA weights with symmetry-aware networks to
predict attributes, identity and performance, and hyper-representations embed [ 33 ] or generate [ 34 ] weights. LoRAcles [ 13 ] trains a model to answer questions about an adapter from its weights, Diff Interpretation Tuning [ 14 ] trains a fine-tuned model to describe its
own change, WeightWatch [ 15 ] labels the top singular vectors of a weight difference, and spectral summaries identify the training
objective [ 16 ] . These produce labels, scores or short answers. We produce a document and measure how much of the original it contains. Reading hidden states as text. The logit lens [ 35 ] and tuned lens [ 36 ] map a hidden direction to word
scores with the model’s own output layer, and Patchscopes [ 37 ] and SelfIE [ 38 ] ask the model itself to describe a
hidden state. Models can describe behaviours they were trained on [ 39 ] , and agents that experiment on a model can explain parts of
it [ 8 ] . We apply the lens to an adapter’s output directions after removing what adapters share, and we use the adapted model’s own
generation, or an agent that runs it, as the reader. Extraction and inversion. Training data and prompts can be recovered from models and embeddings [ 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 ] .
Recovering a skill from an adapter inverts the compile step rather than attacking private data, but it raises the same question of what a
released artefact reveals (Section 8 ). 3 Problem and Measures Adapter families. What an adapter can reveal depends on how it was made, and Table 1 lists the three families
we use. A recitation adapter exposes its text by construction, so results on recitation adapters are an upper bound on what a reader can do.

reader that needs no training: an agent that runs the base and adapted models on real task instances, looks at where they act differently, and
writes a skill from those differences. We ask three questions, all judged by tasks solved. Does the skill recover the adapter’s benefit? Does it

section, including its pass criteria, was fixed in a written plan before it ran. 7.1 Setup The agent. The writer is a Claude model running headless in Claude Code, in a sandbox where its only command is a tool, fx ,
that wraps the models. With it the agent can play a development game with the base or the adapted model, play both on the same game and see the
first step where they differ, ask both models for their next action at a chosen state, try a draft skill on the base model, and score a draft.
All of these tools observe the models’ outputs; two further tools that expose internal signals (Appendix G ) were available but
never used, so this reader works from behaviour. Test instances never pass through the tool, and a canary file placed beside the test results never appeared in any transcript. Skills are limited
to about 800 800 words. We call one independent run of the agent, from an empty sandbox to a finished skill, a session . Sessions of the same arm
can differ a lot, so we run several per arm and report intervals that resample both test items and sessions. Scoring signals. In the main one-session arm the agent never sees a reward: its score is how often the base model with its draft picks the
same action as the adapted model at about 500 500 development states, so anything it learns about the task it learns from the adapter. In the reward loop , the writer instead sees task success on 60 60 development games, revises its drafts over up to five rounds, and the final round
is chosen on 80 80 further development games that the writer never sees; the test games are played once per final skill. A linter rejects drafts that name specific objects (“drawer 2”), copy long passages or exceed 800 800 words. An audit of the test wins,
described in Appendix G.1 , found no exploits. Controls. The agent is also run with a wrong adapter (from another task), with the task hidden , with only the task text ,
and with ten expert demonstrations instead of the adapter. If a control scores like the real arm, the skill did not come from the adapter. Tasks. The main benchmark is ALFWorld [ 7 ] , a text-based household game in which an agent must, for example, find a mug, heat it

most effective route we found is to let the adapted model do the reading. The wrong-adapter control, the retrieval baseline and the
unsupported-content measure show that the recovered text depends on the adapter and is more faithful than the alternatives. Recovery is far from
exact on the hypernetwork’s own adapters ( 44 % 44\% of key instructions, 39 % 39\% of units unsupported) and high on adapters that recite their document
( 90 90 – 93 % 93\% of prose at rank 64 64 ), where higher rank helped most. Why recitation and behaviour differ. A recitation adapter holds the document’s tokens, so the adapted model can be asked to produce
them. A behavioural adapter holds the effect of the document, and nothing in its training asked it to write the document down. Our trained writers
saw only the first kind, so their weak result on the second is a statement about their training data as much as about the method; in Appendix D , a writer trained on a small bank of behavioural adapters improved slightly and then overfitted. The executing agent sidesteps training and
reads behaviour directly, but on a task like ALFWorld a capable writer can produce the same skill without the adapter. The adapter’s information is
most valuable exactly where behaviour is arbitrary. Why recovery is worth having. Even where a LoRA scores higher than any document, the document keeps properties the adapter lacks: it can be
read before use, edited without retraining, merged with other skills and given to any model. A skill an agent wrote from a Qwen2.5 LoRA lifted Llama-3.1-8B
from 5 % 5\% to 55 % 55\% , and a skill written for a 4B model’s fee-calculation task raised Claude Haiku from 0 % 0\% to 96 % 96\% (Appendix E ), a model on which the LoRA cannot be loaded at all. Recovery is also a way to move a skill off the model it was compiled for. Limitations. Most positive results are on adapters that store their document; the hypernetwork’s adapters are one family and Qwen3 the
only base family for the trained writers. Coverage relies on LLM judges with no human labels: the two judges agree on 272 272 of 485 485 verdicts and the
second is 7 7 to 21 21 points more generous, so absolute numbers are less certain than the order of methods. The main skill set has 37 37 skills and the
benchmark agent test four tasks. The executing-agent results rest on one main benchmark and one home actor, with 2 2 to 12 12 sessions per arm, and
the spread between sessions is often larger than the gap between arms. The 7B actor limits what any skill can show for hidden rules. Finally, the original
document is an imperfect target: an adapter may not have learned every instruction, and different wording can have the same effect. Future work. The main open problem is behaviour that is both arbitrary and followable: an adapter whose behaviour cannot be
guessed is where reading it matters most, yet a small actor often fails to carry out the rule once it is written down. Three directions follow. First, breadth: more benchmarks (SkillsBench, WebShop, ScienceWorld, τ \tau -bench), more
adapters trained by others, and a second base-model family, with human labels for a sample of documents. Second, the unguessable case: adapters whose
behaviour is arbitrary but easy to follow (a format, a required field, a naming convention), the reward loop run on such adapters to see whether
the adapter then adds what it did not add on ALFWorld, and stronger actors. Third, better readers: writers trained on several hundred behavioural
adapters with early stopping, agents that combine execution with activation lenses on the middle layers, and searches that use the adapter’s own
answers to score candidate documents. Broader impact. Recovering a document from an adapter helps audit and edit adapters before they are run, and lets a user check that an adapter
matches its stated skill. The same capability lets someone who holds a released adapter reconstruct the skill it compiles, which may be proprietary,
so releasing only weights should not be treated as keeping a skill private. Recovered documents are partial and contain unsupported content, and
should be checked before they are trusted. 9 Conclusion Skills compiled into adapters are cheap to run but lose what makes a skill useful: a document that can be read, edited, audited and given to
another model. We asked whether that document can be written back, and judged the answer by the instructions it recovers and the tasks it
solves. Reading the weights alone recovered a skill’s identity and its words, but little of its instructions. Running the adapter worked. A
small writer trained on top of the adapted model recovers 39 39 – 44 % 44\% of a held-out skill’s key instructions, about twice the best weight-only writer, and almost nothing with a wrong adapter. On adapters that store their document it recovers 90 90 – 93 % 93\% of the prose, and
the recovered documents restore 85 85 – 91 % 91\% of the original’s task gain and help an agent on benchmark tasks nearly as much as the original.
For adapters trained on behaviour alone, an agent that runs the base and adapted models writes skills that recover 58 58 – 71 % 71\% of an ALFWorld
LoRA’s gain, read rules that no one could guess, and carry the skill to models the LoRA cannot be loaded on, lifting Llama-3.1-8B from 5 % 5\% to 55 % 55\% . Together these results show that an adapter need not be a sealed artefact: run inside its base model, it can be turned back into a

The primary judge is a judge model (JEV-1.13) that returns a probability for each verdict on each instruction, so coverage is an
expectation. The second judge (Gemini 2.5 Pro) returns one verdict per instruction. The two agree on 272 272 of 485 485 instruction verdicts. Most
disagreements are a “full” from the second judge against a “partial” from the primary; in a random audit of 12 12 disagreements the second judge was
too generous in about 10 10 (for example, accepting “cherry-pick supporting data” as stating “avoid cherry-picking”), so its coverage runs about 20 20 points higher. Both judges were reached through a commercial API router. Frozen requirements. The key instructions of each original are extracted once, before any method is scored, and reused for every method
and judge. For the external skills no model extracts requirements: the prose list is every instruction sentence of the original ( 6 6 – 60 60 words, with
code, tables and headings removed) and the structure list is every code block, table and heading. Requirements are judged in chunks of 15 15 with the
whole original and the whole candidate in each call ( 817 817 prose and 931 931 structure units over 41 41 skills). Units are not weighted by importance and
may repeat each other, so these numbers compare methods on identical questions rather than giving an absolute share of the skill. Whole-document judge. Table 5 also uses a judge that needs no checklist: Gemini 3 Flash (temperature 0 0 ) reads the complete
original and reconstruction, lists every instruction, command, parameter or warning of the original, marks each as same, partial, missing or
contradicted, and states whether an agent given the reconstruction would behave like one given the original ( 76 76 – 90 % 90\% of skills for recitation
adapters at rank 64 64 ). Unsupported content. Units of the candidate are split by rule, not extracted by a model, and the judge is asked whether the original
supports each sampled unit. Intervals. For the executing agent, intervals are a paired bootstrap that resamples test items and, within each arm, sessions. Intervals
over items alone are narrower and are not used for claims. All plotted values are drawn from the tables; Figures 1 and 2 – 3 are schematic.
