Subodh Lonkar Affiliation: Independent Researcher Abstract Financial document recognition is often assessed using character and word error rates, although these metrics do not express whether a system preserves the financial facts on which people and organisations act. This paper develops a human-centred, risk-constrained framework for evaluating financial document recognition as evidence recovery and decision support. It defines typed financial facts linked to visible source regions and separates verbatim transcription, deterministic normalization, and business interpretation. A layered quality model covers text, localization, layout, tables, fields, fact correctness, evidence grounding, uncertainty, robustness, and operational performance. Non-compensatory acceptance gates use one-sided uncertainty bounds for critical-field errors, preventing strong aggregate scores from masking failures in amounts, identifiers, signs, periods, or entity associations. Selective automation is formulated as a constrained routing problem that accounts for residual risk, human-review capacity, and review cost. The framework further specifies annotation and adjudication procedures, leakage-resistant benchmark partitions, statistical estimands, subgroup analyses, and a reproducibility card. Formal results establish the limitations of compensatory scoring, the non-zero upper risk bound after zero observed errors, and a loss-sensitive decision rule for routing uncertain cases to human review. The contribution is theoretical and methodological; no new model, dataset, or empirical performance result is claimed. Keywords: Financial document recognition; Financial OCR; Evidence-grounded evaluation; Risk-constrained evaluation; Selective automation; Human-in-the-loop decision support 1 Introduction Financial institutions and enterprises process documents whose content is both visually heterogeneous and semantically consequential. A bank statement may contain transaction dates, descriptions, debit and credit columns, balances, account identifiers, and running-balance relationships. An invoice may contain supplier identity, tax identifiers, line items, tax rates, discounts, currency, payment terms, and a payable total. A financial report may combine narrative disclosures, multi-level tables, footnotes, units, comparative periods, and negative values represented with parentheses. OCR is frequently the first machine-readable transformation in these workflows. The common practice of reporting a single character error rate (CER) or word error rate (WER) is insufficient for this domain. Edit-distance metrics treat all character substitutions similarly, whereas the operational impact of an error depends on its semantic role and downstream use. Confusing a letter in a narrative paragraph is not equivalent to changing ‘1,250.00’ to ‘12,500.00’, dropping a minus sign, shifting a value from the debit column to the credit column, or associating a tax amount with the wrong invoice. Document understanding research has established the importance of layout, relations, and visual context in addition to text recognition [ 1 , 11 , 14 , 15 , 18 , 19 , 22 ] . Recent financial-specific work has also highlighted fact-level evaluation for numerical, temporal, entity, and financial-concept fidelity [ 27 ] . This paper argues that financial OCR should be evaluated as an evidence-recovery system, not merely a text transcription engine. A system is useful only to the extent that it faithfully recovers information visible in the source, preserves the structure that gives the information meaning, expresses uncertainty when evidence is inadequate, and supports safe downstream processing. The proposed framework therefore evaluates a sequence of increasingly semantic capabilities while retaining component-level diagnostics. 1.1 Research questions The framework is designed to answer six questions: 1. How accurately does a system detect and transcribe all visible text, including financial symbols and low-frequency identifiers? 2. Does it preserve spatial and logical structure, particularly tables, merged headers, row associations, and reading order? 3. Does it recover typed financial fields with correct values, units, signs, dates, currencies, and entity associations? 4. Can it distinguish what is visually supported from what is inferred, normalised, or unavailable? 5. Are its confidence estimates meaningful enough to support abstention, escalation, and human review? 6. Does its quality remain acceptable across document categories, acquisition conditions, languages, institutions, and operational loads? 1.2 Contributions and scope The paper makes seven methodological contributions. First, it specifies a typed, evidence-linked representation of financial facts that separates visible evidence from deterministic normalization and business interpretation. Second, it defines a layered quality vector and risk functional that retain diagnostic information rather than collapsing errors into a lexical score. Third, it formalizes non-compensatory acceptance gates using prespecified critical-field constraints and uncertainty bounds. Fourth, it models selective automation as a human-centred routing decision that accounts for automated risk, human-review effectiveness, review cost, and review capacity. Fifth, it specifies the evidence and explanation packet required for a reviewer to verify, correct, or escalate a prediction. Sixth, it provides an annotation, sampling, statistical-reporting, and reproducibility protocol. Seventh, it translates the framework into a production control-plane blueprint with versioned interfaces, fail-safe states, staged rollout, audit events, monitoring, and requalification. The four synthetic document exemplars make the evaluation units and cross-document checks concrete but are not an empirical dataset. These are theoretical formalizations and design principles, not claims of a new OCR model or an empirically validated benchmark. The framework applies to OCR-dependent pipelines and OCR-free document models; both must provide output that can be evaluated against the same visual evidence contract [ 15 ] . The paper does not prescribe one universal weighting scheme. Weights, criticality classes, tolerance thresholds, and acceptance limits are application risk decisions that must be fixed before test results are examined. The framework also does not claim that a benchmark score certifies regulatory compliance, fraud detection, or correctness on all future documents. 1.3 Human-centred decision context The intended decision-maker is not an abstract automated pipeline but a person or team accountable for a downstream workflow. The framework therefore treats evidence presentation, uncertainty communication, review capacity, override authority, and auditability as evaluation concerns. A system output should be accompanied by a compact evidence packet containing the predicted value, the verbatim visual span, the source-region locator, the normalization rule (if any), the relevant table row/column or entity association, confidence at the decision unit, and any consistency-check result. Reviewers should be able to inspect the source image and reject the system’s interpretation without losing the original evidence. Human review is not assumed to be infallible or costless. The reviewer may correct, confirm, or escalate a prediction; the workflow should record the action, time, reason code, and any subsequent correction. Evaluation should distinguish (i) machine error detected and corrected by a reviewer, (ii) machine error accepted by a reviewer, (iii) correct machine output unnecessarily changed, and (iv) unresolved ambiguity escalated for adjudication. This makes human oversight measurable rather than treating the presence of a human checkpoint as sufficient evidence of safety. The framework does not prescribe a particular user-interface design; it specifies the information and outcomes that an interface must make assessable. 2 Financial Document Landscape and Evaluation Units 2.1 Document taxonomy Financial data is not one homogeneous OCR domain. Let a corpus contain documents d ∈ 𝒟 d\in\mathcal{D} , pages p ∈ 𝒫 d p\in\mathcal{P}_{d} , regions r ∈ ℛ d ​ p r\in\mathcal{R}_{dp} , and semantic fields or facts f ∈ ℱ d f\in\mathcal{F}_{d} . Each field is associated with a source region or set of regions, a field type, a canonical value where applicable, and a criticality class. The hierarchy matters statistically: pages from one document, statements from one account, or invoices from one supplier are not necessarily independent observations. Definition 1 (Evidence-grounded annotation) . An annotation for field f f is evidence-grounded if it records (a) the exact visible source span or region, (b) the verbatim transcription when text is visible, (c) a typed canonical value when normalization is permitted, (d) the semantic role and unit, and (e) an explicit status for illegible, absent, ambiguous, or not-applicable content. Verbatim transcription and canonical interpretation must be stored separately. For example, the visible string ‘1.250,00 EUR’ may be transcribed exactly and separately normalized to a decimal amount and ISO currency code under a declared locale convention. Normalization must not silently repair uncertain source text. The annotation should preserve provenance so that evaluators can distinguish recognition from interpretation. 2.3 Existing benchmark resources and coverage gaps No single public dataset spans the full financial OCR problem. In particular, a text-only financial reasoning dataset is not an OCR test set, and a table-only dataset does not evaluate document-level field association or operational reliability. Dataset licenses, privacy conditions, redistribution rights, and source-document permissions must be checked independently before release or commercial use. 3 Recent Document AI and Evaluation Literature Recent document AI systems increasingly combine visual, textual, and spatial representations, while some end-to-end models generate structured outputs without a conventional OCR stage. Examples include unified vision–text–layout pretraining, OCR-free document understanding, and layout-aware generative models [ 14 , 15 , 18 , 19 , 22 ] . These developments strengthen the case for evaluating systems against a common evidence contract rather than assuming that a particular internal pipeline is the unit of comparison. Recent surveys document the breadth of scanned-form understanding methods and the expansion of multimodal visually rich document understanding [ 21 , 29 ] . Benchmark design has also moved beyond isolated character recognition. OCRBench and OCRBench v2 cover broad text-centric visual tasks, while MMLongBench-Doc tests long-context document understanding and cross-page evidence use [ 20 , 25 , 23 ] . OmniDocBench provides multi-level document parsing annotations across diverse document sources, and TableFormer illustrates the importance of explicit table-structure modelling [ 26 , 17 ] . These resources are useful for component evaluation, but their coverage does not by itself establish financial fact risk, human-review effectiveness, or deployment-specific acceptance limits. The financial-document setting has a particularly relevant emerging benchmark in FinCriticalED, which evaluates visually grounded financial facts and demonstrates why lexical similarity alone is inadequate [ 27 ] . The present framework is complementary: it does not claim to replace that benchmark or reproduce its empirical findings. Instead, it formalizes a broader evaluation and governance layer around critical-field estimands, confidence bounds, clustered sampling, selective routing, reviewer outcomes, and operational constraints. For risk control, the framework draws on established calibration and selective-prediction methods and more recent conformal risk-control work [ 30 , 9 , 24 ] . 4 Reference Architecture and Output Contract The evaluation architecture separates the recognition system from an independent adapter and evaluator. It supports conventional OCR pipelines, layout-aware models, and OCR-free document models. Each system receives the same image and must produce a standardized output contract. An adapter converts native outputs into the contract without adding information that the system did not produce; the production control flow is summarized later in Fig. 2 . Missing confidence must be reported as unavailable, not imputed. An OCR-free model may emit fields directly, but it must still provide visual evidence references for auditable evaluation. 4.1 Synthetic document exemplars and cross-document checks Figure 1 supplies four programmatically typeset, fictitious document examples. Their purpose is to make the annotation contract concrete, not to estimate model performance. The invoice exposes supplier identity, purchase-order linkage, line-item amounts, tax, and payable total. The bank statement exposes transaction date, reference, debit/credit polarity, and running balance. The remittance advice exposes invoice-to-payment matching, deductions, and net settlement. The management snapshot exposes period association, units, negative values, and arithmetic relationships. A production evaluation should represent each fact as a typed record with at least: document_id , page_id , field_path , verbatim_text , canonical_value , data_type , currency_or_unit , period , polarity , entity_key , evidence_polygon , normalization_rule_id , model_version , and confidence . The record should also carry a validation status and an explicit state for missing, illegible, ambiguous, or not-applicable values. A missing confidence score is not a zero confidence score; it is unavailable and must be handled by policy. The examples illustrate why evaluation should include both within-document and cross-document relations. In the statement, the payroll transaction is shown in parentheses in the debit column. If a system emits a positive signed amount, the error is not merely a character mismatch: it is a polarity error that can also break the running-balance relation. In the invoice/remittance pair, the invoice total should be reconciled to the remittance gross amount before deductions; the net paid amount is a different semantic quantity. In the monthly snapshot, parentheses denote negative expenses and the operating-profit identity is a consistency check, not a substitute for reading the displayed values. These checks should be reported as separate validator outcomes so that a coincidental arithmetic match cannot conceal an unsupported extraction. For an illustrative deployment, an evidence packet for a payroll transaction would contain the source crop, polygon coordinates, the verbatim string “(480,000.00)”, the normalized signed value, the debit-column association, the running-balance context, the model confidence, and the validator result. A reviewer can then distinguish an OCR transcription error from a polarity-normalization error or a table-association error. This explicit provenance is the operational bridge between a benchmark score and a reviewable production decision. 4.2 Separation of recognition and normalization The evaluator should score at least three representations: 1. Visual transcript : characters and tokens as visibly printed, preserving punctuation, signs, separators, and symbols. 2. Canonical field : typed representation after deterministic, declared normalization, such as date parsing or currency normalization. 3. Business interpretation : a semantic relation, such as whether an amount is debit, credit, tax, gross pay, or closing balance. A system should not receive credit for a correct canonical value if the visible transcription is wrong in a way that matters for auditability, nor should benign formatting differences be counted as semantic failures when the canonical value is equivalent. Financial fact-level evaluation is consistent with the direction of recent financial OCR benchmarking [ 27 ] . 6 Risk-Aware Error Measurement 6.1 Criticality classes Each field type is assigned a criticality class before evaluation. One possible taxonomy is: C0, cosmetic/non-semantic; C1, low-impact descriptive; C2, operationally relevant; C3, financially material or identity-critical; and C4, safety-, compliance-, or legally critical. The class depends on the intended workflow, not solely on the field name. For example, a date may be C2 in a search index but C4 when it determines a filing deadline. A customer identifier may be C4 in payment routing. (6) This quantity is useful for ranking and monitoring, but it is compensatory: many correct low-risk fields can dilute a single critical error. Therefore, it must be accompanied by separate critical-error rates and hard acceptance gates. 6.2 Critical error rate and severity-weighted risk Let H f = 1 H_{f}=1 indicate an error in a field designated critical under the workflow policy. Define evidence coverage as the proportion of required extracted fields with a valid source-region link. Define evidence precision as the proportion of supplied links that overlap the correct annotated evidence region above a declared threshold. A field with no visible support should be marked absent, illegible, or inferred according to the output contract. A plausible value inferred from context is not a successful OCR transcription. 7.2 Confidence calibration If a system provides confidence p i p_{i} for correctness indicator y i ∈ { 0 , 1 } y_{i}\in\{0,1\} , the Brier score is [ 6 ] BS = 1 N ​ ∑ i = 1 N ( p i − y i ) 2 . \operatorname{BS}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(p_{i}-y_{i})^{2}. Use stratified sampling to ensure representation of rare but consequential categories. Oversampling rare critical fields is appropriate for diagnostic power, but estimates of population-level performance must be reweighted using known inclusion probabilities. Annotation should be performed by trained annotators using a written schema, with independent double annotation for critical fields and adjudication of disagreements. The ground-truth process itself is a measurement system and should be audited. 11.2 Paired system comparison When two systems process the same documents, compare document-level paired outcomes. For a scalar document loss L d ​ ( s ) L_{d}(s) , define Δ d = L d ​ ( s A ) − L d ​ ( s B ) \Delta_{d}=L_{d}(s_{A})-L_{d}(s_{B}) . A minimal production record should preserve (i) immutable source-document identity and cryptographic hash; (ii) parser, OCR/model, prompt, preprocessing, and schema versions; (iii) field-level outputs and source-region references; (iv) normalization and validation rule identifiers; (v) risk-policy version and threshold; (vi) routing decision and reason codes; and (vii) reviewer action, timestamp, and subsequent correction. The evaluation service should be logically independent of the recognition service, and benchmark labels should remain access-controlled and unavailable to the production inference path. The processing interface should be idempotent: retries for the same document and processing version must not create duplicate downstream postings. A document should move through explicit states such as RECEIVED , CLASSIFIED , EXTRACTED , VALIDATED , AUTO_ELIGIBLE , HUMAN_REVIEW , REJECTED , and COMPLETED . State transitions should be event logged. An invalid schema, missing evidence for a critical field, failed arithmetic/relationship validator, unsupported document family, timeout, or unrecognized currency/unit should fail closed to review or quarantine; it should not silently fall through to automatic processing. A retry policy should be bounded, and exhausted retries should enter a dead-letter queue with a stable incident identifier. The downstream adapter should expose only policy-approved fields and should keep the raw visual transcript, canonical value, and business interpretation separately. Posting, payment release, customer communication, or regulatory filing should remain behind the consuming application’s existing authorization and approval controls. The OCR framework is an evidence and routing layer; it is not a substitute for maker-checker controls, transaction authorization, segregation of duties, or legal review. Personally identifiable and account data require encryption in transit and at rest, least-privilege access, retention limits, and auditable access to source images. Synthetic examples in this paper contain no real customer or account data. A staged release should begin in shadow mode, where the system generates outputs without affecting downstream actions. It should then progress to human-assisted use, restricted automation for narrowly defined low-risk fields, and only later to a bounded production scope after independent test-set gates are met. Canary rollout, version pinning, rollback to the last qualified model/policy pair, and a kill switch for critical incidents are required operational controls. Changes to the model, OCR engine, prompt, image preprocessing, schema, normalization, validator, or risk threshold should create a new qualification version; a model-only version number is insufficient because the whole decision chain affects the outcome. Monitoring should combine leading indicators (document mix, image quality, template novelty, unsupported-field rate, queue depth, timeout rate) with lagging indicators from labelled audits (critical-field error bounds, reviewer correction yield, false-accept rate, unresolved-case rate, and downstream correction/incident rate). Unlabelled drift alerts trigger sampling and review, not a declaration of degraded accuracy. The monitoring plan should define owners, alert thresholds, response times, escalation paths, and rollback authority before production launch. Thresholds should be selected from service and risk requirements, not inferred from the synthetic examples in Fig. 1 . 14.2 Monitoring and requalification A model update, OCR engine version change, preprocessing change, new document template, or material shift in language or scan quality should trigger a defined requalification policy. Monitoring should distinguish input drift from performance drift. Unlabelled drift indicators can trigger sampling and review but cannot establish accuracy. Periodic labelled audits are needed to estimate current error rates. Critical errors should have incident handling, root-cause classification, and corrective-action tracking. 15 Threats to Validity and Limitations First, ground truth is not infallible. Ambiguous handwriting, poor scans, source inconsistencies, and differing accounting conventions can create annotation disagreement. The framework therefore requires explicit ambiguity states and adjudication rather than forcing a single answer. Second, risk weights are context-dependent. A universal weighting scheme would create false precision; institutions must map errors to their own workflows and controls. Third, consistency rules may be invalid when the document uses special accounting treatments, rounding, reversals, or jurisdiction-specific conventions. Validators must be scoped and versioned. Fourth, benchmark data may not represent future deployment distributions. Template holdouts and temporal tests reduce, but do not eliminate, this risk. Fifth, aggregate scores can conceal subgroup failures; all critical slices should be reported. Sixth, confidence calibration can deteriorate after model or distribution changes. Seventh, an OCR benchmark cannot by itself establish that a downstream financial action is legally or operationally appropriate. Human authorization, access control, audit trails, and transaction controls remain outside the OCR metric layer. Finally, the framework is proposed theoretically and methodologically. It has not been empirically validated in this paper; the worked decision example is synthetic and is included only to clarify the acceptance logic. The proposed human-review estimands, evidence-packet requirements, and loss-sensitive routing rule are design specifications, not findings from a user study or deployment. The paper does not release a benchmark dataset, annotation manual validated by annotators, software implementation, or measured reviewer-performance data. A future study should instantiate the benchmark across multiple financial document families, evaluate representative OCR and document AI systems, measure annotation cost and inter-annotator agreement, and test whether risk-aware metrics better predict downstream correction and incident rates than CER/WER alone. 16 Research Agenda Several empirical questions follow directly. (i) Does criticality-weighted evaluation correlate more strongly with downstream correction cost than lexical error? (ii) Which evidence-grounding protocol is reliable across OCR-dependent and OCR-free models? (iii) How well do token- and field-level confidence estimates predict critical financial errors? (iv) What sample sizes are needed to bound rare critical errors under realistic clustering? (v) How should multi-document consistency be evaluated for invoices, purchase orders, remittance advice, and bank statements? (vi) Can a common benchmark schema support multilingual and jurisdiction-specific financial formats without erasing local conventions? These questions define a testable programme rather than an unsupported claim of a solved problem. 17 Conclusion Financial OCR must be evaluated according to the information and relationships it preserves, not only the characters it reproduces. This paper proposed a layered evaluation framework spanning visual recognition, transcription, layout, tables, typed fields, financial facts, evidence grounding, uncertainty, robustness, and operational performance. It formalized criticality-weighted loss while emphasizing non-compensatory gates for high-impact fields; defined risk–coverage analysis for selective automation; and specified a statistically defensible benchmark protocol that accounts for stratification, paired comparisons, clustering, rare events, and multiplicity. The framework is intended to be implementable in enterprise document-processing systems and adaptable across financial document categories. Its central principle is that high average OCR accuracy is not sufficient evidence of financial reliability: critical values must be correct, structurally associated, visually grounded, and appropriately routed when uncertain. Broader Impact The proposed framework may improve the transparency and control of automated financial-document processing by making critical-field failures, evidence provenance, and human-review decisions measurable. Misuse is also possible: poorly chosen risk thresholds, unrepresentative benchmarks, or automation pressure could create false assurance, shift workload unfairly to reviewers, or allow incorrect financial records to propagate. Mitigations include workflow-specific criticality policies, independent test sets, uncertainty bounds, fail-closed routing, human override, audit logging, privacy controls, and periodic labelled requalification. The framework does not certify regulatory compliance or authorize financial transactions.
