Their predictive capabilities are commonly assessed through historical backtesting, where models generate forecasts for past periods and their predictions are compared with realized outcomes. Financial backtests benefit from long evaluation periods because market conditions change, economic outcomes are noisy, and short evaluation windows after a recent model’s training cutoff may not reliably distinguish predictive skill from chance [ 9 ] . However, historical backtesting introduces a fundamental challenge for LLMs: realized outcomes may already be represented in their training data. When asked to predict a past stock price, inflation rate, or monetary policy decision, a model may reproduce the observed outcome rather than infer it from information available at the forecast date [ 19 , 5 ] . Such hindsight leakage can inflate apparent forecasting performance, undermining the reliability of model comparisons and selection for financial applications. Practice relies on three safeguards against this, which are evaluating only after the vendor-stated cutoff [ 3 , 38 ] , instructing the model to answer as of the forecast origin [ 11 , 19 ] , and querying the model about its self-reported knowledge cutoff [ 25 , 2 ] . However, most of the existing works ignore to evaluate the reliability of these safeguards. Input restriction supplies only information available at the forecast origin, through dated corpora or time-filtered retrieval, or uses instructions to restrict the model [ 43 , 11 , 20 ] , but it cannot remove outcomes stored in the parameters, which models recall even when told not to [ 19 ] . Period selection evaluates after a stated cutoff or release date [ 3 , 38 ] , but a stated cutoff describes the training data rather than what the model retains. Boundary detection locates where knowledge ends from perplexity or behavior [ 4 , 28 , 15 ] and finds effective cutoffs that differ from reported ones, but these works do not ask whether the model itself knows where that end lies accurately, which is precisely what self-reports and declared dates rely on. As shown in Figure 1 , the self-reported and the actual boundary of models might be different.
Some works adopt Leakage-free designs to sidestep the question. Yet, prospective evaluation [ 17 , 40 , 37 , 29 ] must wait for outcomes and cannot evaluate history, and point-in-time models [ 8 , 12 , 35 , 1 ] remain small. Therefore, what is missing is a measurement of a model’s actual cutoff and its model-identified cutoff on the same facts, against which each safeguard can be tested. It compares models’ factual answers with their claims about what they know. It contains 2,226 dated U.S. facts from 1999 to 2026, covering twelve monthly FRED macroeconomic series, month-end stock prices for 50 large companies, and Federal Reserve rate decisions. For each fact, we construct three tasks. A value question (Task 1) asks the model to select the correct answer. Its performance across periods provides an estimate of the actual cutoff, which we compare with the vendor-stated cutoff. A separate value and claim question (Task 2) pairs each candidate answer with either a statement of knowing or an admission of guessing. This allows us to examine whether the model’s knowledge claims agree with its factual performance and to estimate the model-identified cutoff. Task 2 is asked in Recall and Predict forms without a declared date. A dated prediction (Task 3) uses the Task 2 Predict form with an instruction to answer as of a specified historical date. Comparing Task 3 with Task 2 Predict tests whether the date instruction helps separate known outcomes from genuine predictions. We control for simple strategies such as repeating the previous month’s value and for imbalanced policy outcomes. We evaluate four frontier models, two smaller open models, and three families of point-in-time models. The results reveal limitations in each safeguard. First, stated cutoff dates do not necessarily locate the end of a model’s effective knowledge. Llama-3.3-70B’s factual accuracy falls to chance after mid-2021, well before its vendor-stated cutoff of December 2023, while its knowledge claims continue until early 2023. Second, models’ self-reports do not reliably distinguish known from unknown outcomes. GPT-5 answers 76% of controlled facts correctly but claims to know only 38%. Within its measured knowledge window, it still answers 75% of the facts it says it does not know correctly, compared with 33% after that window. Keeping only facts that the model claims not to know therefore does not reliably remove leakage. Third, historical-date instructions can change knowledge claims without materially changing factual accuracy. Asking GPT-5 to answer as of a past date reduces its claims of prior knowledge from 39% to 1%, while accuracy remains almost unchanged. Outcomes the model can answer may consequently be presented as predictions despite these instructions. The small point-in-time models offer a different limitation: their accuracy remains near chance on either side of their training cutoffs. Autocast uses dated news corpora to restrict information available at prediction time [ 43 ] , while AutoCast++ improves evidence retrieval [ 34 ] . Halawi et al. [ 11 ] combine retrieval, reasoning, and aggregation, and OracleProto reconstructs historical forecasting conditions through temporally masked retrieval [ 20 ] . Prospective benchmarks such as ForecastBench evaluate predictions before outcomes are known [ 17 ] . In finance, FinBen, MultiFinBen, and Herculean cover broad financial tasks and agent workflows [ 33 , 26 , 27 ] . More specialized benchmarks for taxonomy-grounded financial information extraction [ 31 ] and multi-document financial auditing [ 32 ] . StockBench evaluates trading beyond reported knowledge cutoffs [ 3 ] , while FinTMMBench focuses on temporal financial retrieval [ 41 ] . Collectively, these benchmarks assess financial capabilities or forecasting performance, but do not directly evaluate whether models can reliably identify historical outcomes they already know. 2.2  Look-ahead Bias in LLM Backtests Prior work has examined look-ahead bias in financial LLM evaluations from several perspectives. Glasserman and Lin [ 10 ] use anonymized headlines to separate hindsight effects from company knowledge. Lopez-Lira et al. [ 19 ] demonstrate the reproduction of historical economic and market outcomes, while Crane et al. [ 5 ] quantify macroeconomic recall. Gao et al. [ 9 ] propose tests for look-ahead bias, and Paleka et al. [ 25 ] examine broader pitfalls in evaluating LLM forecasters. Other approaches mitigate leakage through point-in-time training [ 8 , 12 , 35 ] , while Look-Ahead-Bench compares trading performance across in-sample and out-of-sample periods [ 1 ] . These studies establish the importance of hindsight leakage but do not jointly examine factual recall and models’ explicit knowledge claims. 2.3  Knowledge Boundaries and Self-Knowledge Research on model self-knowledge examines whether LLMs can recognize their own knowledge limitations. Kadavath et al. [ 16 ] investigate models’ ability to assess answer correctness, while SelfAware evaluates recognition of unanswerable questions [ 39 ] . Temporal benchmarks such as TempLAMA, DyKnow, and EvolveBench assess evolving factual knowledge [ 7 , 22 , 42 ] , while ChronoRegime examines whether historical evidence supports future predictions [ 13 ] . Complementary work estimates temporal knowledge boundaries: Dated Data uses perplexity over dated documents [ 4 ] , LLMLagBench probes recent-event knowledge [ 28 ] , and HindsightBench estimates behavioral cutoffs through time-indexed macroeconomic questions and date manipulation [ 15 ] . These studies investigate knowledge awareness or temporal boundaries, but do not jointly compare explicit knowledge claims with factual performance across the same dated financial facts. Up to which date does it know dated financial facts, and up to which date does it say that it does? Three design choices follow from this aim. First, all facts come from one economy and one calendar, so that a change in behavior over time reflects the model rather than a change in the material. Second, every fact is scored against the value published at the time, so that a correct answer is evidence of recall rather than of a later revision. Task 1 asks the model to identify the correct value,
providing a behavioral measure of factual knowledge.
Task 2 additionally elicits a claim about whether the

Comparing Task 1 and Task 2 allows

As shown in Table 3 , each fact yields
four prompts that share the same candidate values.
Numerical facts offer four values spaced by roughly
one typical monthly change of the series, with the
rank of the true value drawn uniformly, and policy
facts offer the three outcomes. Every prompt asks the
model to answer from its own knowledge, without browsing
or tools, and to output a single option letter. Task 1 (value). We first establish a behavioral reference for what the
model knows about the collected facts. For each fact,
the model selects one of the candidate values without
making a knowledge claim. Its factual accuracy across
reference periods provides the basis for estimating
the actual cutoff. Task 2 (value and claim). We then examine whether the model recognizes the knowledge
measured in Task 1. To this end, Task 2 asks the model to
select a candidate value together with a claim about
whether it knows that value.
Each candidate appears twice:
once with a claim of knowing and once with a claim of not
knowing, giving eight options for numerical facts and six
for policy facts.
We use two wordings, both without a declared date,
to examine how prediction framing affects knowledge claims. Recall asks what the value was,

followed by a best guess. Predict asks the model

factual answers or knowledge claims.
Task 3 uses the same question, candidate values, and paired

on measured factual accuracy, we apply two controls.
These address cases where a model can answer correctly
without recalling the particular historical outcome.
For numerical facts, a persistence strategy selects the
candidate closest to the previous month’s value. When
the monthly change is smaller than half an option step,
the true value remains the closest candidate, allowing
this strategy to answer correctly without knowing the
current month’s outcome. This occurs for 47% of numerical
facts. Including these cases would risk attributing
accuracy from persistence to factual knowledge.
We therefore measure numerical knowledge only on the
remaining 1,007 facts, for which this strategy fails.
For policy facts, “unchanged” is correct in 78% of
months. Ordinary accuracy would therefore reward a model
that always selects this outcome, even if it cannot
identify rate increases or decreases. We instead use
balanced accuracy, averaging the proportion of correct
answers within each of the three outcome classes so
that each receives equal weight. Always selecting
“unchanged” then scores one third rather than 78%. Together, these controls reduce the contribution of
persistence and dominant outcomes to the knowledge score.
The combined chance reference is approximately 0.27,
reflecting four-option numerical questions and
three-outcome policy questions. 3.4  Measures For each model we report five quantities. Knows is Task 1 accuracy on the 1,340 controlled facts. Says is the share choosing a “know” option
in Task 2 Recall without a declared date,
aggregated over the full sample of 2,226 facts.
We also report accuracy when the model says it knows and accuracy when the model says it does not know ,
measured using Task 1 on facts grouped by these Recall claims, and the within-period gap between these accuracies.
The gap is computed within each period (five-year blocks
before 2021 and half-years after), weighted by period size,
with 95% intervals from 1,000 bootstrap resamples of facts.
Separately, we report the change in claims and accuracy
from Task 2 Predict to Task 3. Based on these quantities, we estimate the actual and
model-identified cutoffs shown in
Figure 1 .
The actual cutoff is estimated
as the last quarter whose
two-quarter rolling accuracy ( Knows ) stays above
the midpoint between the model’s 2015 to 2020 level
and chance.
We estimate the model-identified cutoff as the last quarter whose claim rate
( Says it knows ) stays above half of its
2015 to 2020 level.
When claims end falls after knowledge ends ,
the intervening quarters indicate continued knowledge
claims beyond the measured knowledge boundary.

Practitioners can first evaluate models on dated facts relevant to the target domain and estimate their knowledge boundaries using a consistent procedure (Section 3.4 ). These estimates can guide the selection of evaluation periods, with appropriate margins for boundary uncertainty. In our experiments, vendor-stated cutoffs are generally conservative relative to measured boundaries. For example, Llama-3.3-70B’s reported cutoff excludes ten quarters beyond its measured knowledge boundary, while DeepSeek-V3.2 provides no published cutoff. Self-reported knowledge and historical-date instructions should not replace behavioral measurements, as neither reliably excludes previously known outcomes (Sections 4.4 , and 4.5 ). Reporting measured boundaries alongside backtesting results, and reassessing them when models are updated, would improve evaluation transparency and comparability. Our results also have implications for model developers. Knowledge claims are particularly unreliable for precise numerical facts, even when models can identify the correct values (Section 4.6 ). Moreover, the boundaries of these claims can differ substantially from both demonstrated knowledge and reported cutoffs. Model providers could therefore complement stated training cutoffs with behavioral knowledge assessments on dated, domain-specific facts. The main experiments cover four frontier models: GPT-5 [ 24 ] , DeepSeek-V3.2 [ 6 ] , GPT-4o [ 23 ] , and Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct [ 21 ] . We also include two smaller open models, Qwen3-8B [ 36 ] and Qwen3.5-9B [ 30 ] , to compare their factual knowledge and self-reports with those of the frontier models. The four frontier models are accessed through APIs, while the Qwen models are run locally. Model names and reported specifications follow the developers’ documentation, and the table links to the corresponding Hugging Face repositories where weights are available. We further evaluate three families of point-in-time models: TiMaGPT [ 8 ] , ChronoGPT-Instruct [ 12 ] , and DatedGPT [ 35 ] . Unlike general-purpose models, these models are trained on text restricted to specific historical periods, making them useful comparisons for studying temporal knowledge boundaries. We use five checkpoints each for TiMaGPT and ChronoGPT-Instruct, with cutoffs spanning 2014 to 2022, and the 2014 checkpoint of DatedGPT. Because these smaller models often fail to follow the multiple-choice format, we score candidate likelihoods instead of generated answers (Section 4.7 ).
