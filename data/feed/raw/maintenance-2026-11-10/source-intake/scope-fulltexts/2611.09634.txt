We introduce LambdaMix, a new parameter efficient method for learning a preference conditioned family of policies with group-relative online reinforcement learning. LambdaMix jointly trains one low-rank adapter per objective over a frozen base model. One preference vector selects the adapter mixture that generates responses and weights the separately normalized rewards used to update the adapters. A single training run supports changing preferences at inference without further optimization. We experiment with four models on helpfulness and harmlessness and on solving math problems while following instructions. We also evaluate the two largest models on rewriting comments to preserve wording while reducing clues about personal attributes. On the first two tasks, LambdaMix achieves higher hypervolume than all evaluated conditioned and policy merging baselines across all four models. LambdaMix uses fewer training completions and stores fewer adapter parameters than a set of GRPO specialist policies. With architecture and initialization fixed, varying adapter mixtures and objective weights together gives wider score ranges and higher coverage than varying either alone. 1 1 1 Code available at https://github.com/ckarouzos/lambdamix . 1 Introduction Aligning large language models (LLMs) in post-training ( Ouyang et al., 2022 ; Rafailov et al., 2023 ; Shao et al., 2024 ) often involves potentially competing objectives, e.g., helpfulness and harmlessness, or correctness and instruction compliance. User preferences for these objectives vary across application scenarios and may change after deployment ( Dai et al., 2024 ; Chakraborty et al., 2024 ) . Steerable alignment lets users adjust these priorities within one model ( Wang et al., 2024b ) . A useful policy family should offer competitive trade-offs and respond to changes in the requested preference. Existing approaches provide this flexibility in different ways. Preference Vector combines separately trained parameter updates ( Liang et al., 2026a ) , while hierarchical mixture of experts ( Li et al., 2026b , HoE) routes among adapters. Both support selection after training, but require multiple training runs. Conditional language policy ( Wang et al., 2024b , CLP) learns policies through prompt or parameter conditioning and optimizes the corresponding weighted rewards. Panacea introduces preferences into low-rank directions and trains on the associated alignment objectives ( Zhong et al., 2024 ) . For a shared policy, preferences can influence both the responses generated during training and the updates computed from them. We study how preference-conditioned adapter mixtures learn under group-relative policy optimization ( Shao et al., 2024 , GRPO) , examining the effects of varying both the policy generating responses and the objective used to update it. We introduce LambdaMix , a new online reinforcement learning (RL) method built on top of GRPO. LambdaMix jointly trains low rank adaptation modules ( Hu et al., 2022 , LoRA) under changing preferences. One preference vector selects the adapter mixture that generates responses and weights the normalized rewards used to update the adapters. The adapters therefore learn from responses generated under different adapter mixtures. Mixture conditioning determines the policy collecting responses, while objective weighting determines which sampled responses the update favors. Inspired by the preference conditioning of Pareto low-rank adapters (PaLoRA) for supervised multi-task learning proposed by Dimitriadis et al. (2025) , we adapt this to online RL for LLM multi-objective alignment. We score each response on every training objective and standardize the rewards separately before combining them ( Liu et al., 2026 ) . At inference, changing the preference selects a different adapter mixture without further optimization (Figure 1 ). We evaluate LambdaMix on three tasks with two objectives each, using four base LLMs for the first two tasks and the two largest for the third. The first tests whether LLMs provide helpful answers while avoiding harmful content, using prompts from PKU-SafeRLHF ( Ji et al., 2025 ) and learned reward models ( Dai et al., 2024 ) . For the second, we combine GSM8K math problems ( Cobbe et al., 2021 ) with instructions on response format, length or word use to test correctness and instruction compliance. For the third, we further test whether LLM rewrites preserve a comment’s wording while reducing clues about personal attributes using a synthetic dataset for personal attribute inference ( Yukhymenko et al., 2024 , SynthPAI) . On the first two tasks, LambdaMix achieves higher hypervolume than all evaluated conditioned and policy merging baselines. Across all three tasks, changing its preference weight produces a larger difference between the highest and lowest mean score on each objective than with any of the four conditioned baselines, giving users more scope to adjust the balance. Ablations on Qwen3.5-9B with architecture and initialization held fixed show that varying adapter mixtures and objective weights together yields higher hypervolume and wider score ranges than fixing either component. Our contributions: • LambdaMix , a new method for learning preference-conditioned adapter mixtures with group-relative online RL, that uses one preference vector to coordinate adapter mixing and reward weighting in one training run, supporting policy selection at inference time. • An evaluation of trade-off quality and preference control against conditioning, merging, routing and specialist baselines on three tasks, with up to four models. • Controlled ablations showing that jointly varying adapter mixtures and objective weights improves hypervolume and adjustment range, together with an analysis of the task-dependent effects of reward normalization. Figure 1: LambdaMix uses the same preference for adapter mixing and objective weighting during training (a), then changes the preference mixture at inference (b). 2 Related Work Specifying preferences and learning policies conditioned on preferences. SteerLM conditions supervised training on response attributes ( Dong et al., 2023 ) . Rewards-in-Context ( Yang et al., 2024 , RiC) expresses desired rewards in the prompt, while controllable preference optimization ( Guo et al., 2024 , CPO) learns to follow requested attribute scores. Unlike these methods specifying scores or attributes, LambdaMix takes relative objective priorities. Directional preference alignment (DPA) also uses preference directions explicitly, to select training responses through rejection sampling ( Wang et al., 2024a ) . Self-guided alignment (SGA) infers preferences from prompts ( Wang et al., 2026 ) , and UserAlign elicits comparisons to select a response from a candidate pool ( Pădurean et al., 2025 ) . Unlike these preference acquisition methods, we assume preferences are given and study their use during training. Conditional language policy (CLP) learns policies conditioned through prompts or learned parameter combinations ( Wang et al., 2024b ) . Pareto alignment via preference adaptation ( Zhong et al., 2024 , Panacea) inserts preferences into low-rank directions, while MOC ( He et al., 2026 ) explicitly constrains the correspondence between preferences and rewards. CLP and Panacea use preferences both to condition policies and to weight their training objectives. LambdaMix applies PaLoRA’s per-objective adapter mixtures ( Dimitriadis et al., 2025 ) to online group-relative RL. Policy merging, expert routing, and combining predictions during generation. Model soups ( Wortsman et al., 2022 ) , rewarded soups ( Rame et al., 2023 ) and personalized soups ( Jang et al., 2024 ) apply policy merging to alignment, while Bone Soups trains policies on reward mixtures before merging ( Xie et al., 2025 ) . Task arithmetic combines parameter updates ( Ilharco et al., 2023 ) . Trim, elect sign and merge (TIES) resolves conflicting update signs ( Yadav et al., 2023 ) , while drop and rescale (DARE) sparsifies updates before merging ( Yu et al., 2024 ) . Preference Vector constructs updates from opposing preferences ( Liang et al., 2026a ) , and Pareto Merging learns a compact structure that generates merged models from preferences ( Chen and Kwok, 2025 ) . HoE routes among existing adapter experts ( Li et al., 2026b ) . These approaches reuse separately learned capabilities and allow their combination after training, while LambdaMix learns its adapters jointly under different mixtures. Multi-objective decoding (MOD) combines predictions from specialist policies ( Shi et al., 2024 ) . PRISM also combines policy predictions, but jointly learns positive branches for individual rewards and a shared negative branch ( Liang et al., 2026b ) . Reward guided generation with autoregressive reward model (GenARM) guides a frozen policy using separate reward models ( Xu et al., 2025 ) . A preference-aware autoregressive reward model (PARM) provides this guidance through one model conditioned on preferences ( Lin et al., 2025 ) . LambdaMix combines adapter parameters and requires no reward evaluation during generation. Optimizing multiple rewards. A standard approach to multi-objective RLHF trains a separate policy for each weighted combination of rewards. Multi-objective DPO (MODPO) learns from response comparisons ( Zhou et al., 2024 ) . Reward-free alignment for conflicting objectives (RACO) also uses comparisons and corrects conflicting gradients ( Chen et al., 2026 ) . Both require response comparisons, while LambdaMix scores newly generated responses. Gradient-adaptive policy optimization (GAPO) rescales objective gradients ( Li et al., 2025 ) . Multi-marginal preference optimization (MMPO) combines gradient projection and constraints with reward preprocessing ( Mao et al., 2026 ) . Auto-weighted group relative preference optimization (AW-GRPO) adjusts reward weights using learning progress ( Ichihara and Jinnai, 2025 ) . Dynamic Reward Weighting (DynamicRW) optimizes weights during training ( Lu et al., 2026 ) , and Pareto ranking policy optimization (ParetoPO) combines dynamic scalarization with trajectory ranking ( Li et al., 2026a ) . Group reward-decoupled normalization policy optimization (GDPO) standardizes each reward within response groups before weighting and normalizes the combined advantages over a batch ( Liu et al., 2026 ) . 3 Method Problem Setting. We vary λ \lambda to evaluate the operating points offered by this single trained model. 4 Experimental Setup 4.1 Base Models We use Qwen3-4B ( Qwen Team, 2025 ) , Qwen3.5-9B ( Qwen Team, 2026a ) , Qwen3.6-27B ( Qwen Team, 2026b ) , and Olmo 3 7B ( Team Olmo et al., 2025 ) as base models for preference tuning. 4.2 Baselines We focus on multi-objective alignment with GRPO and adapt the online RL baselines to group-relative training where applicable. Conditioned policies. We compare CLP’s prompt-conditioned variant ( Wang et al., 2024b ) , and implementations of Panacea ( Zhong et al., 2024 ) , MOC ( He et al., 2026 ) and PRISM ( Liang et al., 2026b ) in our group-relative training setup. Fixed policies. We include Base models without adapters, DynamicRW ( Lu et al., 2026 ) , adapted ParetoPO ( Li et al., 2026a ) , and five specialists each for GRPO ( Shao et al., 2024 ) and GDPO ( Liu et al., 2026 ) , trained at first-objective weights w ∈ { 0 , 0.25 , 0.5 , 0.75 , 1 } w\in\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\} . Each checkpoint has no preference input at inference, so changing a specialist’s preference requires selecting another model. Expert routing. A HoE ( Li et al., 2026b ) adaptation, that routes among two adapters and their resulting merged model. Policy merging baselines. We merge endpoint adapters using linear interpolation ( Rame et al., 2023 ) , TIES ( Yadav et al., 2023 ) , DARE, DARE+TIES ( Yu et al., 2024 ) and task arithmetic ( Ilharco et al., 2023 ) . On PKU-SafeRLHF, we also compare against Preference Vector ( Liang et al., 2026a ) , which combines differences between positive and negative DPO updates ( Rafailov et al., 2023 ) . 4.3 Multi-objective Preference Data We evaluate LambdaMix and baselines across three settings that require competing objectives. Helpfulness and harmlessness. We train LLMs to answer PKU-SafeRLHF prompts ( Ji et al., 2025 ) in a helpful way while avoiding harmful content. Refusals may improve safety at the expense of helpfulness. Correctness and instruction compliance. We augment GSM8K ( Cobbe et al., 2021 ) math problems with two constraints on format, length or word use. The first objective measures mathematical correctness against the reference answer, while the second verifies whether the response satisfies the requested constraints. LLMs must solve each problem while following these instructions. Rewrite retention and attribute support rejection. Models rewrite synthetic comments from SynthPAI ( Yukhymenko et al., 2024 ) while preserving wording and reducing clues about attributes such as age or occupation. Training uses the same ROUGE-1 retention score and Qwen3-8B support evaluator as evaluation. Keeping the original wording may retain these clues. 4.4 Evaluation For each task and base LLM, all methods share test prompts and scorers. We average scores from one response per test prompt. Following Zhong et al. (2024) , we evaluate conditional policies at λ = ( w , 1 − w ) \lambda=(w,1-w) , with w = 0 , 0.1 , … , 1 w=0,0.1,\ldots,1 . For helpfulness, we use Beaver-7B unified reward, and for harmlessness we negate the unified cost ( Dai et al., 2024 ) , on the official test prompts, disjoint from training by exact text. On the test GSM8K problems, XML accuracy requires a correct numerical answer extracted from a complete <answer> block. Instruction compliance is the fraction of the two assigned constraints satisfied by the response. Rewriting uses the stemmed ROUGE-1 F-measure ( Lin, 2004 ) for wording overlap. For attribute support rejection we score responses with judgments using Qwen3-8B ( Qwen Team, 2025 ) , given candidate attribute values. Test comments are disjoint from training, but authors may overlap. Hypervolume (HV) measures the area dominated by score pairs relative to a shared reference point ( Zitzler and Thiele, 1999 ) . A point is non-dominated if no other point from the same method improves one score without lowering the other. We count distinct non-dominated points (ND). Span is the difference between the highest and lowest mean score for an objective across evaluated preferences. Finally, we use Reversals to count decreases in an objective’s mean score between adjacent preferences that increase its weight. 5 Results 5.1 Helpfulness and harmlessness Figure 2: Helpfulness (Beaver reward) and harmlessness (negative cost) on PKU-SafeRLHF. Its adapters therefore receive feedback on the responses they produce together, which may help explain the stronger trade-offs relative to combining independently trained adapters. 6.2 Qualitative example Table 2: Qwen3.5-9B LambdaMix responses to one PKU-SafeRLHF test prompt at λ = ( w , 1 − w ) \lambda=(w,1-w) , where w w weights helpfulness. For a fixed preference, the mixed adapters of LambdaMix can be merged into the base weights before generation. 7 Conclusion We introduced LambdaMix, a method for learning preference conditioned adapter mixtures with group-relative online RL. One preference vector selects the adapter mixture and weights the normalized rewards used during training. A single training run supports changing preferences at inference without further optimization. On helpfulness–harmlessness and correctness–instruction compliance, LambdaMix achieves higher hypervolume than the evaluated conditioned and policy-merging baselines across four models. Across tasks, it also provides greater scope to adjust the balance between objectives than conditioned policies. Our ablations show that varying both adapter mixtures and objective weights improves trade-off quality and preference control compared with fixing either component. GDPO-style reward normalization improves helpfulness–harmlessness coverage on all four models, but has task- and model-dependent effects elsewhere. Future work will extend LambdaMix beyond two objectives and examine whether the mixing and weighting preferences need to match. These results support jointly trained adapter mixtures as a way to learn competitive trade-offs and useful preference control within one model. Appendix A Implementation Details A.1 Training and Optimization All experiments use LoRA and a shared optimization setup. Table 4 gives default hyperparameters. We use AdamW with ( β 1 , β 2 ) = ( 0.9 , 0.999 ) (\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(0.9,0.999) , ϵ Adam = 10 − 8 \epsilon_{\rm Adam}=10^{-8} , zero weight decay and gradient clipping at 1. We apply LoRA to q_proj and v_proj , and to in_proj_qkv , in_proj_z and out_proj in Qwen3.5-9B and Qwen3.6-27B .
