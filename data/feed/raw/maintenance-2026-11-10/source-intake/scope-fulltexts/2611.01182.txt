Abstract Relative-position attention mechanisms offer compelling alternatives to RoPE across various transformer architectures, delivering superior performance in domains such as audio. These mechanisms typically modify attention scores by adding content–position or bias–position terms. Unlike RoPE, which encodes position via per-token rotations directly on the queries and keys, relative-position terms generally require pairwise offset-dependent computation or lookups, complicating their integration into fused attention kernels and introducing severe memory, bandwidth, and compute overheads as sequences scale. To address this bottleneck, we propose an exact attention algorithm that incorporates additive relative-position terms into FlashAttention-style tiled computation. Our method (1) uses offset-aware tiling to avoid redundant indexing and memory accesses, (2) computes only the necessary key/query–relation products and reuses them across token pairs in both forward and backward passes, and (3) uses offset-based gradient sharding and aggregation, eliminating atomic contention. We provide a unified, efficient Triton implementation across diverse relative-position attention mechanisms and evaluate it against naive and specialized implementations. For 8K-token sequences, our method speeds up the forward (backward) pass by 1.8–6.4 × \times (2.0–21 × \times ) and reduces peak memory up to 2.9–128 × \times (1.4–54 × \times ) over the strongest baseline for each mechanism, enabling long-context end-to-end transcription that is 1.8 × \times faster and uses 2.4 × \times less memory. Our method thus provides a drop-in, hardware-efficient solution for scaling relative-position attention transformers. 1 Introduction The Transformer architecture ( Vaswani et al., 2017 ) has revolutionized modern machine learning, from large language models (LLMs) in natural language processing to foundation models in speech, vision, and biology. At its core is the scaled dot-product attention (SDPA) mechanism, which allows input tokens to directly attend to each other and aggregate mutual information. However, scaling these models to larger inputs introduces two fundamental challenges, namely injecting scalable positional information into the network, and keeping the computation and memory footprint of SDPA tractable despite its quadratic complexity. For standard content-based attention, IO-aware kernels such as FlashAttention ( Dao et al., 2022 ; Dao, 2024 ) resolved the quadratic memory bottleneck by streaming blocks of tokens through fast on-chip SRAM, avoiding the materialization of the full N × N N\times N score matrix in GPU high-bandwidth memory (HBM). Paired with parameter-free, token-wise rotary position embeddings (RoPE) ( Su et al., 2024 ) , which can be seamlessly integrated into fused kernels, this pipeline now serves as the backbone of most autoregressive LLMs. Relative-position attention Parameter-free positional encodings, such as RoPE and ALiBi ( Press et al., 2022 ) , are not without drawbacks. Despite their computational efficiency, they often fail to use context beyond the sequence lengths seen during training ( Kazemnejad et al., 2023 ; Hua et al., 2025 ) , where learned relative biases such as T5’s generalize better ( Kazemnejad et al., 2023 ; Li et al., 2024 ) . Consequently, learnable relative-position encodings remain an enticing alternative for Transformer models. This class of mechanisms additively augments attention scores with learnable terms that depend on the relative distance i − j i-j between query and key tokens, either through content-to-position projections or scalar biases. This allows modeling explicit, shift-invariant relationships across sequences, and has remained enticing for speech models ( Barrault et al., 2023 ; Sekoyan et al., 2025 ; Abouelenin et al., 2025 ) , text encoding ( He et al., 2023 ; Zaratiana et al., 2025 ) , proteomics ( Elnaggar et al., 2022 ) , and genomics ( Linder et al., 2025 ) . Efficiency limitations of relative-position attention Despite their modeling advantages, relative-position attention mechanisms are largely incompatible with standard fused attention kernels. Training or running inference with these models typically requires materializing the full N × N N\times N additive position matrix in HBM, reintroducing the exact memory-bandwidth bottleneck that FlashAttention was designed to eliminate. Programmable attention frameworks such as FlexAttention ( Dong et al., 2025 ) offer a partial solution by fusing user-defined per-score callbacks and enabling limited precomputation; however, they still suffer from redundant computation, repeated memory lookups, and atomic contention during backpropagation. Lacking efficient kernels, practitioners often resort to replacing relative-position encodings entirely and retraining models from scratch, as seen in Flashzoi ( Hingerl et al., 2025 ) and FlashSwin ( Kataria et al., 2026 ) , or switch to RoPE-based alternatives such as ModernBERT ( Warner et al., 2025 ) . Our work To address these limitations, we introduce an exact algorithm for relative-position mechanisms. Rather than treating pairwise offsets as opaque per-element lookups, our method exploits the block-level structure of relative distances across the attention matrix ( Fig. 1 a). Specifically, our method projects queries and keys only onto the offset ranges reachable within each token block ( Fig. 1 b), structures tile traversal around constant- and variable offset regions to maximize data reuse and avoid redundant indexing ( Fig. 1 c), and shards backward gradient accumulation by offset to eliminate atomic contention entirely ( Fig. 1 d). Empirically, our method substantially reduces both runtime and memory consumption across forward and backward passes in production speech and text models. Our kernels significantly outperform generalist and specialized implementations, as well as state-of-the-art frameworks such as FlexAttention, FlashAttention-4 ( Zadouri et al., 2026 ) , and FlashInfer ( Ye et al., 2025 ) , even when those frameworks are augmented with our preprojected relation decomposition. In particular, for 8 ​ K 8\text{K} -token sequences, our method beats the strongest baseline for each mechanism by 1.8–6.4 × \times in the forward pass and 2.0–21 × \times in the backward pass, while reducing peak memory usage by 2.9–128 × \times and 1.4–54 × \times , respectively. Applied end-to-end to long-context speech transcription, it delivers a 1.8 × \times speedup and 2.4 × \times lower memory consumption on Parakeet-TDT-0.6B-v3 ( Sekoyan et al., 2025 ) , enabling significantly more efficient deployment and training. Key contributions Our main contributions include: • A unified formulation of additive relative-position attention spanning four mechanisms, exposing shared structure amenable to kernel-level optimization. • An exact, drop-in tiled algorithm for fast and memory-efficient relative-position attention. • High-performance implementations of our method , validated against a high-precision reference, and comprehensive evaluations across diverse model architectures, sequence lengths, and NVIDIA GPU architectures. 2 Related Work In this section, we describe relevant prior work on optimizing standard attention and programmable backends for non-standard attention mechanisms. We then describe the uses of relative-position attention and their limitations due to the lack of general optimization algorithms. 2.1 Optimizing Standard Attention Modern Transformer architectures rely on scaled dot-product attention (SDPA) ( Vaswani et al., 2017 ) . However, naive implementations materialize the full attention score and probability matrices in high-bandwidth memory (HBM), incurring storage and I/O costs that grow quadratically with sequence length. To mitigate this bottleneck, Milakov & Gimelshein (2018) introduced an online softmax formulation that processes inputs in column-wise chunks, enabling exact attention computation in sub-quadratic memory ( Rabe & Staats, 2021 ) . FlashAttention ( Dao et al., 2022 ) combines this with IO-aware tiling in on-chip memory, recomputing attention tiles during the backward pass instead of storing large intermediate matrices. FlashAttention-2 ( Dao, 2024 ) restructures the schedule to improve parallelism and reduce overhead by parallelizing the forward pass over query blocks and the backward pass over key blocks. FlashAttention-3 and -4 ( Shah et al., 2024 ; Zadouri et al., 2026 ) further specialize this pipeline for newer GPUs. Existing kernels target content-based attention and cannot fuse learnable relative-position terms or their gradients. Our method bridges this gap by extending tiled execution to relative-position attention by restricting projections to reachable offsets, reusing them on constant-offset tiles, and using a two-pass backward schedule ( Triton Contributors, 2024 ) for atomic-free gradient accumulation. 2.2 Programmable and Specialized Backends To support attention variants without hand-writing a kernel per model, recent frameworks expose programmable interfaces that preserve FlashAttention’s optimizations. FlexAttention ( Dong et al., 2025 ) compiles user-defined mask predicates and elementwise score modifications into fused tiled kernels, automatically deriving their backward pass. FlashInfer ( Ye et al., 2025 ) , AttentionEngine ( Chen et al., 2025 ) , and Flashlight ( You et al., 2025 ) provide similar customization through templates or compilers, while others target variant families such as sparse masks ( Wang et al., 2025 ) or low-rank biases ( Wu et al., 2025 ) . Because these frameworks express score modifications as pointwise callbacks on query–key pairs, they can only support relative-position terms via elementwise lookups into precomputed tables. Because this abstraction hides the tile structure, it cannot exploit spatial locality across constant-offset pairs, leading to redundant memory accesses during the forward pass and contention-heavy atomic additions ( Dong et al., 2025 ) during gradient accumulation. 2.3 Relative-Position Attention Applications of relative-position attention While modern autoregressive language models largely rely on fixed positional schemes like RoPE ( Su et al., 2024 ) and ALiBi ( Press et al., 2022 ) that integrate neatly into standard SDPA kernels, many domains benefit from expressive, learnable relative-position encodings that inject pairwise token distances directly into the attention logits. Introduced by Shaw et al. (2018) , this approach projects queries onto learned embeddings of clipped relative offsets. Transformer-XL ( Dai et al., 2019 ) expands this notion by incorporating unclipped offsets with learned query biases, DeBERTa ( He et al., 2021 ; He et al., 2023 ) by modelling both content-to-position and position-to-content interactions, and T5 ( Raffel et al., 2020 ) by mapping offsets to learned scalar bucket biases.
Today, these mechanisms remain foundational for long-context modeling across speech, text, and biology. They support the backbone of conformer-based ( Gulati et al., 2020 ) speech and multimodal models such as Parakeet, Canary, SeamlessM4T v2, and Phi-4-multimodal ( Sekoyan et al., 2025 ; Barrault et al., 2023 ; Abouelenin et al., 2025 ) , DeBERTa-based text and extraction pipelines ( Zaratiana et al., 2025 ) , and biological sequence models in proteomics and genomics ( Elnaggar et al., 2022 ; Avsec et al., 2021 ; Linder et al., 2025 ) . Our method unifies the common computational patterns across all these architectures, providing a single hardware-efficient algorithm. Optimizing relative-position attention Despite their widespread adoption, these architectures remain difficult to scale due to the overhead of computing and applying relative-position terms. Early implementations reduced the cost of positional projections by evaluating query- and key-relation dot products once per unique offset, and then mapping the resulting table via explicit index lookups ( He et al., 2021 ; Wu et al., 2021 ) or row-shifting reshape operations ( Huang et al., 2019 ; Dai et al., 2019 ) . However, both strategies still materialize a dense quadratic bias matrix in HBM.
Subsequent systems have attempted to fuse this pipeline, though they remain either incomplete or model-specific. For instance, TensorRT’s DeBERTa plugin ( NVIDIA, 2022 ) and NeMo’s Transformer-XL path ( Kuchaiev et al., 2019 ) fuse the offset-gather step yet still materialize the full score matrix in HBM. On the other hand, FlashDeBERTa ( Knowledgator, 2025 ) and GLiNER4j ( Gravitee, 2026 ) integrate positional terms directly into tiled FlashAttention-style kernels (the latter for inference only), but are strictly tailored to DeBERTa. Lacking general kernel support, practitioners often modify and retrain the model instead: Flashzoi ( Hingerl et al., 2025 ) replaces relative-position encodings with RoPE, with analogous replacements appearing for speech recognition ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) and proposed for vision Transformers ( Kataria et al., 2026 ; Lee et al., 2026 ) . Others turn to kernelized attention to incorporate relative positions in sub-quadratic time ( Luo et al., 2021 ; Liutkus et al., 2021 ) , but sacrificing exactness, which has unclear performance impact. Our method accelerates both training and inference across the full family of relative-position architectures without compromising model fidelity or numerical exactness. 3 Background In this section, we introduce the necessary background for understanding our work. Specifically, eager implementations follow one of two strategies, both of which reintroduce quadratic HBM bottlenecks. Naive implementations instantiate a ϕ ⁡ ( i , j ) Q a^{Q}_{\phi(i,j)} for every ( i , j ) (i,j) pair, costing O ⁡ ( N 2 ​ d ) O(N^{2}d) work and O ⁡ ( N 2 ​ d ) O(N^{2}d) workspace. Preprojected implementations instead compute Z Q , Z K ∈ ℝ N × R Z^{Q},Z^{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times R} in O ⁡ ( N ​ R ​ d ) O(NRd) work and O ⁡ ( N ​ R ) O(NR) space, then gather or shift them into an N × N N\times N bias matrix. Even with tiling instead of the N × N N\times N bias, the precomputed tables require O ⁡ ( N ​ R ) O(NR) storage, which often include unreachable columns (wasting compute and memory), fetches the same entry multiple times (e.g., when offsets are clipped), and remains quadratic for identity maps. In the backward pass, tiled execution faces an additional many-to-one reduction bottleneck in Eq. 6 . Whenever multiple pairs ( i , j ) (i,j) map to the same row r = ϕ ⁡ ( i , j ) r=\phi(i,j) , up to O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) keys per query and O ⁡ ( N 2 ) O(N^{2}) pairs overall, their score gradients τ ​ d ​ S i ​ j \tau dS_{ij} must be summed into the same d ​ Z i ​ r Q dZ^{Q}_{ir} , d ​ Z j ​ r K dZ^{K}_{jr} , and d ​ b r db_{r} entries, causing severe atomic contention when accumulated via per-pair updates. While programmable backends such as FlexAttention can avoid the dense N × N N\times N bias through fused per-score callbacks, a scalar callback only sees a single ( i , j ) (i,j) pair at a time. It cannot change how outer projections are allocated, reuse entries across a tile of pairs sharing the same row r r , or coordinate gradient ownership in backward to avoid atomic additions. Our method overcomes all of these bottlenecks with its relation-aware tiled approach. Derivation of all equations is given in App. B . 4 Method To address the limitations of Section 3.3 , our method brings relative-position terms into FlashAttention-style tiled attention. It computes only the relation projections the tiles read ( Section 4.1 ) and accumulates their gradients by offset ( Section 4.2 ), which together form its forward and backward passes ( Section 4.3 ). 4.1 Reusing Relation Projections For any offset-based relation map ϕ \phi , each positional term for a query–key pair ( i , j ) (i,j) depends only on the relation row r = ϕ ⁡ ( i , j ) r=\phi(i,j) and either query i i or key j j . Consequently, all N 2 N^{2} pairwise positional contributions are determined by the N × R N\times R token–relation projections Z Q Z^{Q} and Z K Z^{K} ( Eq. 5 ) and the scalar bias b ∈ ℝ R b\in\mathbb{R}^{R} . Rather than expanding these tables into a dense N × N N\times N bias matrix in HBM, our method evaluates them on chip within each attention tile: S i ​ j = τ ⁡ ( q i ⊤ ​ k j + Z i , ϕ ⁡ ( i , j ) Q + Z j , ϕ ⁡ ( i , j ) K + b ϕ ⁡ ( i , j ) ) + M i ​ j . S_{ij}=\tau\bigl(q_{i}^{\top}k_{j}+Z^{Q}_{i,\phi(i,j)}+Z^{K}_{j,\phi(i,j)}+b_{\phi(i,j)}\bigr)+M_{ij}. (7) Each attention logit thus requires at most three scalar lookups from Z Q Z^{Q} , Z K Z^{K} , and b b rather than a d d -dimensional dot product. We next eliminate the two remaining bottlenecks identified in Section 3.3 – computing unreachable projection columns and performing redundant elementwise lookups. Reachable Projection Ranges Although an identity map defines 2 ​ N − 1 2N-1 relation rows, any single query i i only attends to N N keys. It thus accesses N N contiguous offsets δ ∈ [ i − N + 1 , i ] \delta\in[i-N+1,i] , leaving the remaining N − 1 N-1 rows of A Q A^{Q} unused. To omit these unreachable projections while preserving batched matrix multiplications, our method partitions Q Q into contiguous groups Q g Q_{g} of G G queries. Because consecutive queries access overlapping offsets, an entire group Q g Q_{g} reaches a contiguous range Ω g Q = { g ​ G , … , g ​ G + N + G − 2 } \Omega^{Q}_{g}=\{gG,\ldots,gG+N+G-2\} of only N + G − 1 N+G-1 rows, reducing overhead to only G − 1 G-1 additional rows. Our method projects each Q g Q_{g} onto A Ω g Q Q A^{Q}_{\Omega^{Q}_{g}} (and analogically for K g K_{g} and A Ω g K K A^{K}_{\Omega^{K}_{g}} ) as a single dense matrix product. For N ≫ G N\gg G , this restriction nearly halves both projection work and HBM storage while remaining exact. The scalar bias b b requires no projection and is left unrestricted. Constant-Range Reuse For clipped and bucketed maps with a small saturation limit Δ ≪ N \Delta\ll N , individual token groups already span nearly all R R relation rows, offering limited savings from range restriction. Instead, these maps saturate outside the diagonal band | δ | < Δ |\delta|<\Delta , mapping all offsets δ ≥ Δ \delta\geq\Delta to row r = R − 1 r=R-1 and all δ ≤ − Δ \delta\leq-\Delta to row r = 0 r=0 . To handle bucketed maps in the same way, our method expands their R R buckets into 2 ​ Δ + 1 2\Delta+1 rows by duplicating each bucket across the offsets it covers, replacing index lookups with clipping. In any tile where | δ | ≥ Δ |\delta|\geq\Delta , all pairs map to the same row r r . Our method therefore loads a single entry per query ( Z i ​ r Q Z^{Q}_{ir} ), one per key ( Z j ​ r K Z^{K}_{jr} ), and one bias b r b_{r} , broadcasting them across the tile. Only pairs in the diagonal band | δ | < Δ |\delta|<\Delta require individual lookups, and their fraction ( 2 ​ Δ − 1 ) / N (2\Delta-1)/N approaches zero as N N grows. Under causal masking ( j ≤ i j\leq i ), the situation is similar, but queries access only offsets δ ≥ 0 \delta\geq 0 and saturate only for δ ≥ Δ \delta\geq\Delta . 4.2 Atomic-free Relation Gradient Accumulation In the backward pass, accumulating score gradients τ ​ d ​ S i ​ j \tau\,dS_{ij} directly into d ​ Z Q dZ^{Q} and d ​ Z K dZ^{K} causes atomic contention whenever multiple pairs map to the same relation row r r . However, while a fixed query i i may map many keys j j to the same row r r , each key corresponds to a unique offset δ = i − j \delta=i-j . Building on the standard two-pass backward schedule ( Triton Contributors, 2024 ) , our method assigns each query tile to a single thread block and accumulates its gradients by offset δ \delta into d ​ Z ~ Q d\tilde{Z}^{Q} rather than by row r r . A symmetric pass over key tiles accumulates d ​ Z ~ K d\tilde{Z}^{K} . Because each entry has a single writer, this schedule eliminates all O ⁡ ( N 2 ) O(N^{2}) atomic additions and fixes the summation order, ensuring determinism. Local Boundary Sums Similar to the forward pass, writing a separate gradient entry for every offset δ \delta is unnecessary in the saturated regions. Since all δ ≥ Δ \delta\geq\Delta map to row R − 1 R-1 and all δ ≤ − Δ \delta\leq-\Delta map to row 0 0 , each thread block maintains two on-chip running sums per token, one for each boundary row, and writes them to HBM only once. Combined with the reachable ranges Ω g Q \Omega^{Q}_{g} and Ω g K \Omega^{K}_{g} , each token stores at most min ⁡ ( N + G − 1 , 2 ​ Δ + 1 ) \min(N+G-1,\,2\Delta+1) gradient entries (identity maps have Δ = N − 1 \Delta=N-1 ) instead of one per offset, 2 ​ N − 1 2N-1 . As in Section 4.1 , this nearly halves the gradient buffer for identity maps ( N ≫ G N\gg G ), while for clipped and bucketed maps the fraction ( 2 ​ Δ + 1 ) / ( 2 ​ N − 1 ) (2\Delta+1)/(2N-1) approaches zero as N N grows. Relation-Row Reduction The stored offset gradients directly yield d ​ Z Q dZ^{Q} and d ​ Z K dZ^{K} ( Eq. 6 ). For identity and clipped maps, each stored column already corresponds to its own row r r ( d ​ Z ~ Q = d ​ Z Q d\tilde{Z}^{Q}=dZ^{Q} ). For bucketed maps, the duplicated columns of each bucket are summed back into that bucket’s row in a fixed, deterministic order. The remaining gradients then reduce to matrix products, as in Section 3.2 .
For Δ ≪ N \Delta\ll N , these reductions require O ⁡ ( N ​ Δ ​ d ) O(N\Delta d) work rather than O ⁡ ( N 2 ​ d ) O(N^{2}d) for per-pair accumulation. Our method uses this atomic-free path whenever each row r r spans contiguous offsets, and falls back to atomic additions otherwise. 4.3 Complete Algorithm We now compose the end-to-end algorithm, designing both the forward and backward passes in a tiled manner. 4.3.1 Forward algorithm To avoid materializing dense attention matrices in HBM, our method streams query and key tiles through on-chip SRAM, similarly carrying over FlashAttention’s tiled schedule, which remains applicable as each score tile S ℐ ​ 𝒥 S_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{J}} depends only on the preprojected relation entries of its own queries ℐ \mathcal{I} and keys 𝒥 \mathcal{J} . relative-position attention, with adapters for Hugging Face and NeMo models that require no weight modifications. Each call dispatches a kernel specialized at compile time to the active relation terms, map type and Δ \Delta , masking, sequence packing, dropout, precision, and tile sizes, avoiding runtime branching and any overhead from disabled terms. Tile sizes are selected by sequence-length bucket from lookup tables tuned offline per GPU architecture, falling back to a hardware resource heuristic when no table entry exists. Similarly, the saturation check (lines 11 – 12 of Algorithm 1 ) splits each tile’s inner loop into three contiguous ranges on the host rather than branching per tile. Sequence lengths remain runtime arguments so lengths within a bucket share compiled kernels, with an optional precompilation step to warm the compiler cache. Users can trade compute for memory by selecting whether the forward pass saves Z Q Z^{Q} and Z K Z^{K} in HBM or recomputes them during backpropagation. Without atomic-free gradients, the backward pass is 2.9–4.4 × \times slower, needs 1.8–2.6 × \times the memory, and loses determinism. The decomposition engines use atomics, hence their backward gap in Section 5.2 . Without projections, every tile computes its own relation products, which slows the forward pass by 2.0–2.9 × \times and the backward pass even more, but needs only 0.19–0.43 × \times the memory, the same tradeoff GLiNER4j makes. The other methods are more targeted, with considerable gains of up to 1.3 × \times in time or 1.5 × \times in memory on the maps they serve ( Table 10 ). Reachable ranges save memory, mainly on TXL’s identity map, while constant-range reuse speeds up the clipped and bucketed maps. Bucket copies add further speed at a memory cost. Finally, recomputing the projections instead of retaining them cuts memory to 0.42–0.50 × \times for a 1.1 × \times slowdown, which is how our method is able to fit Canary’s longest windows ( Section 5.3 ). First, identity maps such as Transformer-XL’s assign a distinct relation row to every offset, so their projection work and space remain quadratic in sequence length. Computing the projections inside each tile instead makes the space linear but is 2.0–2.9 × \times slower in the forward pass ( Table 7 ). Second, maps whose relation rows do not cover consecutive offsets fall back to FP32 atomic accumulation, which is slower and not deterministic, although every evaluated mechanism avoids it. Third, our method favors generality across mechanisms, maps, and GPUs over hardware specialization. It autotunes its tile sizes but uses no architecture-specific primitives such as warp specialization or asynchronous bulk transfers ( Shah et al., 2024 ; Zadouri et al., 2026 ) , which could yield further speedups and which we leave as future work. 7 Conclusion We presented an exact tiled algorithm that unifies and accelerates additive relative-position attention. By restricting relation projections to reachable ranges, reusing entries across constant-offset tiles, and accumulating gradients by offset without atomics, our method removes the dense bias of existing backends, with its memory and bandwidth costs, and keeps gradients deterministic for identity, clipped, and bucketed maps. Across mechanisms and GPUs, our method is a drop-in replacement for long-context training and inference in production models, so these mechanisms no longer need to be substituted for efficiency. ∎ Appendix C Kernel and Algorithm Details Section 4.3.3 summarizes our implementation. Here, we detail how our method maps Algorithms 1 and 2 onto kernels, how it reduces the relation gradients deterministically, how it stores relation projections in half precision, and which inputs and tile sizes its kernels support. Kernel structure Table 9 lists the kernels of our method in launch order, with the lines of Algorithms 1 and 2 that each one runs. The forward pass first projects every query group and key group onto its reachable range and stores the preprojected relation entries in HBM. The attention kernel then runs the tiled loop and writes O O and L L . The backward pass computes D D and runs the query-parallel pass, whose gradient entries are reduced into d ​ Q dQ , d ​ A Q dA^{Q} , and d ​ b db before the key-parallel pass starts. Both passes can therefore write their gradient entries into the same buffer. The key-parallel pass and its reduction then produce d ​ K dK , d ​ V dV , and d ​ A K dA^{K} . If the forward pass does not save Z Q Z^{Q} and Z K Z^{K} , the backward pass recomputes them with the projection kernel before it starts. When both A Q A^{Q} and b b are present, the projection kernel adds b b into the stored Z Q Z^{Q} , so each score reads two entries instead of three. No gradient is accumulated atomically, so the backward pass is deterministic. Atomic fallback Accumulating gradients by offset ( Section 4.2 ) requires each relation row to cover one contiguous interval of offsets. This holds for identity, clipped, and bucketed maps, and our method checks it once per map on the host. Maps that fail it fall back to kernels that accumulate their relation gradients with FP32 atomic additions. Examples are maps that give δ \delta and − δ -\delta the same row, or maps that depend on more than the offset. Half-precision numerics Inputs may be in FP16, BF16, or FP32. The online softmax uses base-2 exponentials, with log 2 ⁡ e \log_{2}e folded into τ \tau , and its statistics, the accumulators, and the boundary sums stay in FP32 on chip. In HBM, the gradient entries use the input precision. The relation projections use FP16 for both FP16 and BF16 inputs. BF16 gives up three mantissa bits for a wider range, so FP16 stores the projections with an eight times smaller rounding error. For BF16 inputs, the projections are clamped to the FP16 range of ± 65504 \pm 65504 , so that an outlier cannot overflow to infinity. Masks, packing, and dropout The kernels support padding masks, causal masks, and packed variable-length sequences, in which each thread block processes a query tile of one sequence. Attention dropout draws its mask from a counter-based random number generator inside the kernels, so the backward pass regenerates the mask of the forward pass instead of storing it. Specialization and tuning The sequence-length buckets end at 64, 128, 384, 512, 768, 1K, 2K, 4K, and 8K tokens, and longer sequences use the 8K bucket. Besides the specializations of Section 4.3.3 , each kernel is compiled separately for lengths that are and are not a multiple of 16, so a bucket needs at most two compilations per kernel. For multiples of 16, Triton can use wider memory accesses, so our model adapters pad each sequence to a multiple of 16 tokens and mask the padding. For the same reason, each group’s range of projection columns starts and ends on a multiple of 16. Our lookup tables cover compute capabilities 8.0, 8.6, and 9.0 (A100, RTX A6000, and H100 or H200), with one entry per kernel, precision, head dimension, bucket, and set of relation terms. The hardware resource heuristic starts from a 64 × 64 64\times 64 tile and shrinks it until its estimated registers and shared memory fit the GPU. Instead of both, our method can autotune each kernel at run time over a short list of candidate tile sizes, once per bucket. All measurements in Section 5 use autotuning, which the end-to-end runs complete in the precompilation step before timing. The exception is the ablation that computes the projections inside each tile ( Table 7 ), which uses its fastest fixed tile sizes. End-to-end software The speech models run in NeMo 3.0.0 on PyTorch 2.9.1 and CUDA 12.8, and DeBERTa on the software stack of Section 5.1 . Parakeet transcription The test set has 1,155 segments and 11 talks of up to ∼ 25 {\sim}25 minutes, which we transcribe one at a time. We report word error rate (WER) after Whisper’s text normalization ( Radford et al., 2023 ) , for which NVIDIA’s model card reports 2.75% ( NVIDIA, 2025 ) , and inverse real-time factor (RTFx), the audio seconds processed per wall-clock second. NeMo’s FlexAttention path captures sequence-length-dependent index arithmetic and bias tensors in its score_mod closure, so each new input length triggers a torch.compile recompilation that erases any speedup, and running it uncompiled falls back to a much slower unfused implementation. Canary fine-tuning We fine-tune on 100 hours of TED-LIUM 3 training talks, cut into windows of at most 30 seconds and 2, 5, 9, 15, and 18 minutes, for 3 epochs with about 540 seconds of audio per step. Final-epoch training losses match within 0.4% at every window both attentions run. As Whisper does, we decode transcripts that fall into repetition loops again in shorter pieces. From 9-minute windows on, such loops affect one or two long recordings per window, and these recordings account for the remaining WER differences. DeBERTa fine-tuning The patent data has 25,000 training, 5,000 validation, and 5,000 test documents of 3,900 tokens on average.
