OmniLatent generates geometry, deformation, appearance, and coherent surface part IDs from a single image in a unified latent space. Abstract. 3D part decomposition is a central capability for editable asset creation, yet existing workflows typically segment a completed mesh as a post-processing step, making the result sensitive to geometric artifacts, texture ambiguity, and limited part-level annotations. We present OmniLatent, a unified latent modeling framework for 3D part decomposition. Instead of predicting part labels in isolation, OmniLatent learns a shared latent space that jointly encodes geometry, deformation, appearance, and binary part IDs, allowing data-rich geometric and visual priors to support data-scarce part learning. Given a single input image, the base model generates geometry, deformation, texture, and surface part IDs in one pass, producing a part-aware 3D asset without a separate segmentation stage. We further tune the pretrained model for two extensions: a point-prompt adapter that maps arbitrary 3D point prompts with binary part IDs into latent tokens for full segmentation, refinement, and part subdivision, and an amodal part completion model that completes partial surface masks into a coherent full-object decomposition through dynamic per-part token allocation, cross-attention to the given geometry latent, and alternating part-local and global attention. Extensive experiments show that OmniLatent produces semantically meaningful part segmentations with precise boundaries, supports interactive part editing, and completes partial part observations while preserving global object consistency. Keywords: 3D Generation, 3D Part Decomposition, Unified Latent Modeling 1. Introduction The creation of structured, editable 3D assets is a cornerstone of modern visual computing, supporting applications from game production and cinematic content creation to digital twins, robotic simulation, and interactive design. Recent image-conditioned 3D generative models ( Zhao et al., 2025c ; Hunyuan3D et al., 2025 ; Lei et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2024 ; Li et al., 2025a ) can synthesize increasingly detailed geometry and appearance from a single image. However, their outputs are still mostly monolithic surfaces: the object may look plausible, but its semantic components are not explicitly separated. This missing part structure limits downstream editing, material assignment, animation, physical fabrication, and analysis, all of which require an object to be decomposed into coherent, addressable parts. A common solution is to first generate or reconstruct a full 3D asset and then run a separate part segmentation model ( Ma et al., 2025a ; Yang et al., 2024 ; Liu et al., 2025b ) . This post-hoc paradigm is convenient, but it decouples part reasoning from the generative representation that produced the asset. As a result, segmentation quality depends heavily on the input mesh quality and often degrades near thin structures, sharp boundaries, or texture-defined semantic transitions. It also makes part granularity difficult to control: a post-hoc segmenter may split visually coherent components, merge distinct functional parts, or produce boundaries that do not match the generated surface. For part-aware asset creation, the central problem is therefore not only to synthesize a plausible 3D object, but to generate a surface whose semantic part layout is coherent, editable, and aligned with geometry and appearance. Part decomposition poses an additional data challenge. Holistic geometry and texture data are relatively abundant, while high-quality 3D part annotations are much scarcer. Learning part segmentation directly from part labels alone therefore underuses the strong geometric and visual priors available in larger 3D asset collections ( Li et al., 2026 ) . Practical part cues are also often incomplete: single-view masks or sparse user interactions may only cover visible surface regions, while downstream editing and animation require complete object-level parts. Although 2D foundation models ( Zhao et al., 2025a ; Le et al., 2025 ) can provide useful masks or prompts, directly transferring their predictions to 3D remains ambiguous due to occlusion, viewpoint bias, and imperfect correspondence between image regions and 3D surfaces. These limitations suggest that 3D part decomposition should not be treated as an isolated labeling problem. Instead, it should be grounded in a representation that already understands the object’s geometry and appearance. This motivates our core question: Can a unified latent space provide the geometric, visual, and semantic context needed for robust 3D part decomposition? Prior unified 3D representations have mainly focused on representation formats or holistic asset generation. For example, TRELLIS ( Xiang et al., 2024 ) unifies different 3D output formats, UniLat3D ( Wu et al., 2025a ) simplifies textured mesh generation, and UniPart ( He et al., 2025a ) explores geometry-part integration. In contrast, our goal is to use unified latent modeling as the foundation for 3D part decomposition, covering surface part segmentation and amodal part completion. Geometry, deformation, and appearance are generated together with part IDs because they provide the structural and visual evidence needed for reliable part boundaries. We present OmniLatent, a unified latent modeling framework for 3D part decomposition. As illustrated in Figure 1 , OmniLatent builds upon a voxel-query-based representation ( Lai et al., 2025a ) that encodes geometry, deformation, appearance, and binary part IDs into a shared OmniLatent space . The key design principle is to let data-rich modalities, especially geometry and appearance, shape a strong latent prior that can be transferred to data-scarce part decomposition. We first train a unified VAE that compresses heterogeneous surface-aligned fields into the same latent token space with a shared encoder and attribute-specific decoders. We then train an MM-DiT latent generator with rectified flow to synthesize this latent representation from a single image. The base OmniLatent model is trained progressively. Large-scale geometry data first establishes a structural latent space, textured assets then align appearance with geometry, and a smaller set of part-aware assets finally teaches the latent space to decode coherent part IDs. This curriculum allows part learning to benefit from geometric and visual priors without requiring part annotations at the same scale as geometry or texture. At inference, the base model takes a single image and generates geometry, deformation, texture, and surface part IDs in one pass. When 2D segmentation masks are available, they can optionally guide the desired part granularity; when they are absent, the same model still produces autonomous surface part segmentation from the single image. Beyond fully automatic segmentation, practical part editing requires interactive control ( Tsui et al., 2026 ) . We therefore tune the pretrained model with a point-prompted latent adaptation that represents each prompt as a 3D coordinate with a 6-bit binary part ID. A lightweight MLP maps an arbitrary number of such prompts into latent tokens, which are appended to the denoising sequence. This design avoids a positive-negative prompt formulation: different part IDs directly define distinct semantic groups, enabling full segmentation from sparse prompts, refinement of an existing segmentation, and subdivision of a coarse region into finer parts. Because the adapter is initialized from the pretrained latent generator, it reuses the same geometry, appearance, and part priors instead of learning an interactive segmentation model from scratch. We further extend OmniLatent to amodal part completion : given full object geometry and partial surface masks for all parts, the model completes them into a coherent full-object decomposition. The completion model allocates latent tokens dynamically according to the number of visible voxels assigned to each part, allowing larger parts to receive more representational capacity. For each part, RoPE-anchored tokens preserve observed surface evidence, while additional no-RoPE tokens flexibly represent unobserved regions. A geometry latent encoded by the VAE is injected through cross-attention, and alternating part-local and global attention layers balance fine-grained part completion with full-object consistency. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose OmniLatent, a unified latent modeling framework that generates geometry, deformation, texture, and surface part IDs from a single image in one pass, allowing geometric and visual priors to support 3D part decomposition with or without 2D segmentation mask guidance. • We tune the pretrained model for flexible part control, including point-prompted full segmentation, segmentation refinement, and part subdivision with arbitrary 3D point prompts encoded by binary part IDs. • We further adapt the pretrained latent model to amodal part completion with dynamic per-part token allocation and a RoPE/no-RoPE token design, preserving observed surface evidence while flexibly completing unobserved regions into coherent parts. • Extensive experiments validate OmniLatent across single-image generation, controllable segmentation, prompt-based editing, and full-object part completion, demonstrating accurate part boundaries, effective user control, globally consistent completion, and competitive supporting geometry and appearance quality. 2. Related Work 2.1. 3D Shape Generation The field of 3D shape generation has evolved from lifting 2D diffusion priors to modeling native 3D distributions directly. Early influential works, such as DreamFusion ( Poole et al., 2023 ) , leverage Score Distillation Sampling (SDS) to optimize 3D representations using feedback from 2D diffusion models ( Rombach et al., 2022 ) .
Zero-1-to-3 ( Liu et al., 2023 ) pioneered image-conditioned novel view synthesis, providing a critical foundation for multi-view pipelines ( Liu et al., 2024d ; Long et al., 2024 ; Shi et al., 2023 ; Liu et al., 2024c ; Zou et al., 2024 ) , which focus on generating consistent image sets to reconstruct geometry.
To achieve more robust synthesis, recent research has gravitated toward native 3D latent diffusion.
This paradigm utilizes Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) ( Dai et al., 2017 ; Wu et al., 2024 ; Zhao et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2024 ; Li et al., 2024 ; Lan et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2025a ; Hui et al., 2022 ; Koo et al., 2023 ; Chou et al., 2023 ; Shim et al., 2023 ; Li et al., 2023 ; Li et al., 2025a ; Huang et al., 2025a ) to compress complex 3D shapes into compact, geometry-aware latent spaces for effective generation.
Notable examples include 3DShape2VecSet ( Zhang et al., 2023 ) for set-structured data, CLAY ( Zhang et al., 2024 ) for large-scale hierarchical modeling, TRELLIS ( Xiang et al., 2024 ) for unified representation formats, and 3DTopia-XL ( Chen et al., 2025c ) for primitive-based high-quality asset generation.
Complementary to diffusion, autoregressive models like MeshGPT ( Siddiqui et al., 2024 ) and its successors ( Chen et al., 2024b ; Chen et al., 2025b ; Chen et al., 2024c ; Hao et al., 2024 ; Weng et al., 2024 ; Tang et al., 2025a ; Liu et al., 2025a ; Wang et al., 2025 ; Zhao et al., 2025b ) treat mesh generation as a sequential prediction task.
While these methods have achieved high fidelity in holistic shape synthesis, they often struggle to model continuous multi-attribute details, such as precise geometric sharp edges, fine-grained textures, or parts, which remains an open challenge that we aim to address. 2.2. 3D Texture Generation 3D texture synthesis has evolved through three primary paradigms: optimization-based distillation, projection-based multi-view synthesis, and native 3D generation. Early influential works ( Chen et al., 2023 ; Lin et al., 2023 ; Qian et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2023 ) utilize Score Distillation Sampling (SDS) ( Poole et al., 2023 ) to iteratively optimize 3D appearance from 2D diffusion priors. While capable of producing detailed textures, these optimization-based methods are computationally expensive and frequently suffer from the “Janus problem”. To address efficiency, another line of research ( Huang et al., 2025b ; He et al., 2025b ; Zeng et al., 2024 ; Zhao et al., 2025c ; Zhang et al., 2024 ) employs a projection-based paradigm, where consistent multi-view images are generated and then “painted” back onto the 3D surface. Related refinement pipelines further enhance low-quality 3D inputs through coupled texture and geometry updates ( Ryu et al., 2025 ) . Although Multi-view Diffusion models ( Liu et al., 2024d ; Long et al., 2024 ) improve global consistency by conditioning on geometric maps, the inherent limitations of the painting process often lead to visible seams and misalignments with fine-grained geometric features. To overcome these artifacts, native 3D texture generation seeks to synthesize appearance directly within 3D space. While recent native 3D texture generation frameworks ( Lai et al., 2025b ; Chen et al., 2025a ; Liu et al., 2024b ; Yu et al., 2023 ; Yu et al., 2024 ; Yuan et al., 2025 ) have explored predicting colors for points or UV maps by conditioning on fixed geometry, such decoupled generation often struggles to maintain strict coherence between the synthesized texture and the underlying structure. 2.3. 3D Part Decomposition and Generation Beyond global shape synthesis, recent research has increasingly prioritized part-aware 3D assets to achieve fine-grained control and structural editability ( Zhao et al., 2026b ; Zhu et al., 2026 ; Su et al., 2026 ) . One major direction partitions existing 3D surfaces into semantic parts ( Ma et al., 2025a ; Yang et al., 2024 ; Liu et al., 2025b ; Deng et al., 2025 ; Zhu et al., 2025 ; Paul et al., 2025 ) . These methods are effective for organizing completed meshes ( Hadgi et al., 2026 ; Jin et al., 2026 ) , but their outputs are inherently tied to the quality of the input surface and the available segmentation cues. Complex boundaries, thin structures, and texture-dependent semantic transitions remain difficult when part prediction is decoupled from the representation that models geometry and appearance. Another line of research focuses on the direct synthesis of part-aware 3D assets ( Yan et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2025b ; Yang et al., 2025c ; Lin et al., 2025 ; Tang et al., 2025b ; Dong et al., 2025 ; Ding et al., 2025 ; He et al., 2025a ; Yang et al., 2025a ; Zhang et al., 2025b ) . Early attempts in this category utilized 2D part-mask supervision to guide multi-view reconstruction ( Liu et al., 2024a ; Chen et al., 2024a ) , though they frequently inherited the view-inconsistency issues of their underlying 2D priors. More recent methods introduce structured latent frameworks or part-specific representations to improve controllability and coherence ( Jin et al., 2025 ; Zhao et al., 2026a ; Gu et al., 2026 ; Lin et al., 2026 ) . In contrast, OmniLatent focuses on surface part segmentation within a unified latent space that also models geometry, deformation, and appearance. This allows part prediction, prompt-based refinement, and amodal part completion to reuse the same aligned geometric and visual priors rather than treating parts as isolated clusters or post-hoc labels. Unified Latent Modeling for 3D Part Decomposition As shown in Figure 2 , OmniLatent casts 3D part decomposition as a unified latent modeling problem. Rather than learning part labels independently from geometry and appearance, we encode geometry, deformation, appearance, and part IDs into a shared OmniLatent space. This design has two goals. First, part predictions should be spatially aligned with the generated surface and appearance, so that boundaries can follow geometric and visual evidence. Second, scarce part annotations should benefit from the stronger priors learned from large-scale geometry and texture data. We first define a surface-aligned multi-attribute representation ( Section 3.1 ), then introduce the unified VAE ( Section 3.2 ), the image- and mask-conditioned latent generator ( Section 3.3 ), point-prompted segmentation and refinement ( Section 3.4 ), and amodal part completion ( Section 3.5 ). The base generator handles single-image part-aware asset generation with optional 2D mask guidance, while the point-prompted and completion models are adapted from the pretrained model and reuse the same OmniLatent representation. 3.1. Surface-Aligned Multi-Attribute Representation OmniLatent builds upon VoxSet ( Lai et al., 2025a ) architecture and representation, which inherits the set-structured cross-attention mechanism of 3DShape2VecSet ( Zhang et al., 2023 ) . Voxel queries provide an ordered spatial scaffold that can be evaluated at different resolutions, making them suitable for aligning surface attributes with latent tokens. Here, “ordered” refers to the spatial order induced by the voxel-query scaffold: each token corresponds to a spatial query location, while its sequence index has no independent semantic meaning. Our extension turns this scaffold into a multi-attribute representation where geometry, deformation, appearance, and part IDs are defined over the same object surface and compressed into a shared latent space. For a 3D object, we construct a VAE input point set 𝐗 ∈ ℝ N × 16 \mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 16} , where each point contains three aligned fields: a geometric field 𝐏 ∈ ℝ N × 7 \mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 7} , an appearance field 𝐂 ∈ ℝ N × 3 \mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 3} , and a part field 𝐐 ∈ { 0 , 1 } N × 6 \mathbf{Q}\in\{0,1\}^{N\times 6} . The concatenated field is written as 𝐗 = [ 𝐏 , 𝐂 , 𝐐 ] \mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{P},\mathbf{C},\mathbf{Q}] . Geometry field The geometric foundation of the asset is represented by 𝐏 \mathbf{P} , which contains 3D coordinates, surface normals, and a binary sharpness indicator. These samples supervise an SDF decoder used for surface extraction with Marching Cubes ( Lorensen and Cline, 1998 ) . Following recent high-quality 3D asset generation practice ( Zhao et al., 2025c ) , we combine uniform surface sampling with additional samples near sharp edges, so that the latent space observes both global shape and high-frequency structural cues. Appearance field The appearance field 𝐂 \mathbf{C} stores RGB values sampled on the object surface. During watertight mesh processing, we preserve original face colors whenever possible; for adjusted faces, we assign colors through nearest-neighbor interpolation from the original surface. Since the processed faces are small, this strategy keeps texture supervision aligned with the reconstructed surface without introducing strong color noise. Part field The part field 𝐐 \mathbf{Q} stores a 6-bit binary encoding of surface part IDs. This representation supports up to 2 6 = 64 2^{6}=64 parts, which is sufficient for the fine-grained objects used in our part-aware training set. The codes are object-local rather than members of a global semantic taxonomy: during training, we randomly assign every part of each object a distinct 6-bit ID, so no cross-object correspondence between numerical IDs is required. Compared with RGB-coded part masks, binary part IDs are more stable under denoising: small continuous color deviations in RGB space can split one semantic part into multiple inconsistent IDs, while bitwise decoding preserves categorical precision. Deformation field We include a lightweight deformation head as part of the geometric output. It is decoded after SDF reconstruction and Marching Cubes, and predicts small vertex offsets that sharpen local surface structures. Its supervision is derived from the same geometric field 𝐏 \mathbf{P} , whose positions, normals, and sharpness cues identify vertex-to-surface displacements near edges and corners. This field improves geometric presentation quality, while the part capability itself is learned through the unified latent part field. 3.2. OmniLatent Autoencoding Given the concatenated multi-attribute input 𝐗 \mathbf{X} , we train a VAE that maps it into the OmniLatent space 𝒵 \mathcal{Z} . A shared encoder E E compresses 𝐗 \mathbf{X} into an ordered set of latent tokens 𝐳 ∈ ℝ L × D \mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times D} via cross-attention. The decoder uses a shared transformer backbone followed by attribute-specific heads { G s ​ d ​ f , G d ​ e ​ f , G a ​ p ​ p , G p ​ a ​ r ​ t } \{G_{sdf},G_{def},G_{app},G_{part}\} for SDF, deformation, appearance, and part IDs. Decoding follows a cascaded surface-query strategy, as visualized in the left branch of Figure 2 . We first reconstruct the SDF and extract the surface; the resulting surface vertices are then used to query deformation, appearance, and part ID fields from the same latent tokens. This shared query path is important for part segmentation: the part field is decoded on the same surface that defines geometry and appearance, reducing the mismatch between part boundaries and object structure. Decoded surface labels are converted to per-face masks; therefore, the Part-ID output contains no overlapping internal interfaces. Complete internal or occluded part geometry is recovered only by the subsequent amodal completion model. Specifically, ℒ K ​ L \mathcal{L}_{KL} regularizes the posterior latent distribution toward the unit Gaussian prior; ℒ s ​ d ​ f \mathcal{L}_{sdf} supervises reconstructed signed-distance values at spatial queries; ℒ d ​ e ​ f \mathcal{L}_{def} regresses the target offsets of extracted surface vertices; ℒ a ​ p ​ p \mathcal{L}_{app} reconstructs surface RGB values; and ℒ p ​ a ​ r ​ t \mathcal{L}_{part} supervises the six binary Part-ID channels on the object surface. We use a progressive supervision schedule: SDF training first establishes a stable geometric latent space, and deformation, appearance, and part heads are then added with surface-near supervision. This schedule encourages the part decoder to learn on top of an already aligned geometric and visual latent representation. Figure 3. We adopt an MM-DiT architecture that integrates multiple control signals: interactive regions and geometric conditions are injected via channel concatenation with the noisy latent, while other conditions are incorporated through sequence concatenation. 3.3. Image- and Mask-Conditioned Latent Generation We train an MM-DiT ( Esser et al., 2024 ) with rectified flow to generate latent tokens in the OmniLatent space. Let z 1 ∈ 𝒵 z_{1}\in\mathcal{Z} denote a target latent sample and z 0 ∼ 𝒩 ⁡ ( 0 , 𝐈 ) z_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}) denote noise. The interpolation path is z t = t ​ z 1 + ( 1 − t ) ​ z 0 z_{t}=tz_{1}+(1-t)z_{0} , and the model v θ v_{\theta} is trained with ℒ f ​ l ​ o ​ w = 𝔼 t , z 0 , z 1 ​ ‖ v θ ​ ( z t , t , 𝐜 ) − ( z 1 − z 0 ) ‖ 2 , \mathcal{L}_{flow}=\mathbb{E}_{t,z_{0},z_{1}}\|v_{\theta}(z_{t},t,\mathbf{c})-(z_{1}-z_{0})\|^{2}, where 𝐜 \mathbf{c} is a sequence of condition tokens. For the base generator, 𝐜 \mathbf{c} always includes the input RGB image and can additionally include a 2D segmentation mask that specifies the desired part granularity. The generated latent tokens are decoded into geometry, deformation, appearance, and part IDs together, so surface part segmentation is learned as part of the same conditional generation process rather than as a separate post-processing step. In the mask-free setting, the model produces autonomous surface part segmentation from the RGB image alone; in the mask-guided setting, the 2D segmentation mask provides controllable part layout cues. Data heterogeneity is a central challenge: geometry is abundant, textured assets are less abundant, and part-aware 3D objects are much scarcer. We therefore use progressive training. The model first learns large-scale geometry and deformation priors, then incorporates textured assets to align appearance with geometry, and finally fine-tunes on part-aware objects. For samples without appearance or part annotations, the missing fields are zeroed before encoding so that the latent space remains compatible across training stages. In the final stage, the model learns to decode binary part IDs from a latent space already shaped by geometry and appearance. To handle variable conditions, we use Condition-Aware 3D Rotary Position Embedding (CA-RoPE) ( Su et al., 2024 ) . For image-like conditions, the first RoPE dimension indexes the condition type, while the remaining two dimensions encode spatial height and width. In practice, RGB images, optional 2D segmentation masks, and other image-space conditions are concatenated as condition tokens, while their condition-type indices keep them distinguishable from each other and from 3D latent tokens. During part-aware training, 2D segmentation mask conditions are randomly dropped, enabling the base model to operate both with mask-guided granularity control and in the mask-free setting where it generates geometry, deformation, appearance, and surface part IDs from the RGB image alone. We also apply mask-condition augmentation to bridge the gap between 2D cues and 3D part layouts. Since 2D segmentation masks may be incomplete, view-dependent, or inconsistent with occluded 3D structures, we augment them through random dropping, merging, splitting, component decomposition, internal noise, and erosion. These augmentations encourage the model to treat 2D masks as granularity guidance while still aligning final part boundaries with the generated 3D geometry and appearance. 3.4. Point-Prompted Segmentation and Refinement As summarized in Figure 3 , OmniLatent supports interactive part control through point prompts. A prompt set is written as 𝒫 = { ( 𝐱 i , 𝐛 i ) } i = 1 n \mathcal{P}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{b}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n} , where 𝐱 i ∈ ℝ 3 \mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3} is a prompted 3D surface point and 𝐛 i ∈ { 0 , 1 } 6 \mathbf{b}_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{6} is the binary part ID assigned to that point. We concatenate each coordinate and part code, then map it through a lightweight MLP into the latent token dimension. The resulting n n prompt tokens are appended to the denoising sequence and participate in all transformer layers. This formulation supports any number of prompts and avoids a positive-negative prompt design. Whereas positive-negative clicks specify foreground and background for a single target, our multi-ID prompts distinguish several parts within one prompt set and support full segmentation, refinement, and subdivision in one pass. Different part IDs directly define different groups, so the model can infer a full surface segmentation by propagating these sparse semantic anchors through the geometry-aware latent space. The point-prompted model is fine-tuned from the fully trained single-image generator by sampling point-ID pairs from ground-truth part segmentations, allowing the prompt behavior to reuse the pretrained geometry, appearance, and part priors. During tuning, full-segmentation examples sample 1–10 prompt points per part; for refinement, we randomly merge 2–4 parts and again sample 1–10 points on each original part inside the merged region, training the model to recover finer part boundaries from coarse initial regions. The same point-prompted model is trained to refine existing segmentations. In this setting, an initial full segmentation is encoded together with geometry by the unified VAE, and the encoded feature is concatenated with the noisy latent tokens as a geometry-aware condition. We additionally provide a binary region indicator: the part selected for refinement or subdivision is marked white, and the remaining context is marked black. This indicator is a soft condition rather than a hard constraint. The model is therefore allowed to adjust boundaries and nearby assignments when the prompts and geometry suggest a more coherent assignment. Figure 4. Amodal part completion. RoPE-anchored tokens preserve observed surface evidence, while no-RoPE completion tokens represent unobserved regions. Amodal Part Completion As shown in Figure 4 , we define amodal part completion as completing full object-level parts from partial surface masks on a given geometry. Following the task terminology of HoloPart ( Yang et al., 2025b ) , the goal is to recover complete part structures, including unobserved or internal regions, from a holistic mesh and partial surface masks. We adapt the pretrained unified latent model to this task. The input consists of the complete object geometry and partial surface masks for all parts. This geometry is generated by the base OmniLatent model rather than taken from ground truth. The output is a complete part layout over the entire object surface, including both visible and unobserved regions of every part. The main difficulty is that different parts occupy very different amounts of surface area. Using a fixed number of tokens per part either wastes capacity on small parts or under-represents large parts. We therefore select surface voxels on the given geometry and allocate tokens to each part according to its visible voxel count, with a bounded range around the average per-part token budget to avoid extreme allocations. Specifically, each part token count is clamped to [ 0.5 , 1.5 ] [0.5,1.5] times the average per-part token budget. Larger visible parts receive more tokens, while small parts keep a compact representation. This dynamic token allocation makes the completion model scale with part size without requiring a fixed part count or fixed per-part length. For each part, we split its tokens into two groups. The first group is anchored to observed surface regions with RoPE, preserving the spatial identity of the visible mask. The second group contains no-RoPE completion tokens. Since these tokens are not tied to fixed observed positions, they add flexible completion capacity without overwriting the observed evidence, allowing the model to infer the complete extent of the part. To expose the model to all available part identity embeddings, we randomly remap each training part to an ID in [ 0 , 63 ] [0,63] and inject the corresponding learned part embedding into all tokens of that part. The no-RoPE completion-token ratio is randomly sampled from 20% to 40% during training and fixed to 30% during inference. During training, we know which tokens correspond to observed surface regions and which tokens correspond to regions that must be completed. We therefore upweight the loss on completion tokens by 5, because these unobserved-region tokens are fewer and converge more slowly than visible surface tokens. We also filter training objects in which four or more parts require more than 60% completion, which avoids highly ambiguous supervision and improves optimization stability. To keep completion consistent with the entire object, we encode the full geometry with the unified VAE and inject the geometry latent through cross-attention. We also alternate two attention patterns in the completion transformer: part-local attention, where tokens interact within the same part to recover fine-grained structure, and global full attention, where all part tokens interact to maintain full-object consistency. This combination allows each part to be completed independently enough to preserve local detail, while still coordinating with neighboring parts and the overall object shape. 4. Our experiments focus on four part-centered capabilities: single-image joint generation of geometry, deformation, appearance, and surface part IDs; mask-guided controllable segmentation; point-prompted segmentation, refinement, and subdivision after tuning; and amodal part completion after tuning. We additionally report geometry and appearance generation results as supporting evidence that the shared OmniLatent space preserves the quality of the auxiliary modalities used to guide part learning. Implementation Details Data preparation For geometry and deformation, we curate 900K high-quality meshes primarily sourced from Objaverse ( Deitke et al., 2023a ) . For appearance modeling, we use 300K assets that contain both geometry and texture information, targeting albedo to encourage the model to learn lighting-robust appearance. We render conditioning images under diverse illumination by randomly selecting point lights, area lights, and HDRI maps. For part-level modeling, we use 20K part-aware 3D objects. These objects are selected from Objaverse, filtered for meaningful part decomposition, and manually annotated with semantic Part IDs. These assets are processed to ensure consistent geometry and appearance, and the number of semantic parts per object typically ranges from 5 to 30 with an emphasis on semantic rationality and symmetry. We render both RGB conditioning images and corresponding 2D segmentation masks in Blender. Training and inference During unified VAE training, we first optimize the SDF field with a learning rate of 10 − 4 10^{-4} . We initially train with a token length of 2048 before transitioning to 6144, which improves stability and allows extrapolation to longer sequences during inference. We use random token sampling during VAE training to improve robustness across voxel densities. Once the SDF space is stabilized, we introduce joint supervision for deformation, appearance, and part fields. The loss weights are λ k ​ l = 10 − 6 \lambda_{kl}=10^{-6} , λ s ​ d ​ f = 1 \lambda_{sdf}=1 , λ a ​ p ​ p = 1 \lambda_{app}=1 , λ p ​ a ​ r ​ t = 1 \lambda_{part}=1 , and λ d ​ e ​ f = 0.1 \lambda_{def}=0.1 ; the smaller deformation weight prevents structural offsets from dominating the multi-task objective. Our generative backbone is a 1.9B-parameter MM-DiT trained with a constant learning rate of 5 × 10 − 5 5\times 10^{-5} and a linear warm-up phase. The DiT is trained with a sequence length of 12,248 tokens. To support classifier-free guidance for RGB image and 2D segmentation mask conditions, we randomly replace conditioning embeddings with zero embeddings at a 10% probability during training, and use a unified CFG scale of 5 at inference. Both the point-prompt and amodal-completion adapters are fully fine-tuned from the pretrained base model on the same 20K part-aware objects for 30K steps with a learning rate of 5 × 10 − 5 5\times 10^{-5} . VAE training takes approximately 3 days, while the main DiT training takes approximately 7 days. At inference time, the base OmniLatent model generates geometry, deformation, appearance, and part IDs from a single image in approximately 30 seconds with 8192 latent tokens. 4.2. Surface Part Segmentation Settings and metrics We evaluate surface part segmentation on PartObjaverse-Tiny ( Yang et al., 2024 ) , a curated subset of Objaverse ( Deitke et al., 2023b ) with over 3,000 fine-grained part annotations across 200 shapes. Following the evaluation protocol of P 3 \text{P}^{3} -SAM ( Ma et al., 2025a ) , we use the watertight version of the benchmark and report mean Intersection over Union (mIoU). Because OmniLatent decodes part IDs on the generated watertight surface, we assign ground-truth part IDs to the predicted surface by nearest-neighbor transfer. For each ground-truth part, we match the closest predicted part region and compute IoU; the mIoU is averaged over ground-truth parts for each shape and then over all test shapes. Thus, the evaluated part count is determined by the ground-truth annotation rather than by extra predicted fragments. We consider two segmentation modes. In the autonomous setting, the base model receives only the input image and directly generates geometry, deformation, appearance, and surface part IDs without a 2D segmentation mask. In the controllable setting, a 2D segmentation mask condition is additionally provided to indicate the desired part granularity. The same base model supports both settings because 2D segmentation mask conditions are randomly dropped during training. Unless explicitly stated otherwise, the OmniLatent result of 74.18 mIoU in Table 1 uses mask guidance. Evaluation protocol Following X-Part ( Yan et al., 2025 ) , we evaluate on 200 samples from ObjaversePart-Tiny, each containing rendered images and ground-truth part geometries. All methods start from the full object geometry and partial surface masks, and produce completed full-object parts. Before metric computation, each object is normalized to [ − 1 , 1 ] [-1,1] . To make the evaluation robust to pose ambiguity, we rotate each prediction by 0 ∘ 0^{\circ} , 90 ∘ 90^{\circ} , 180 ∘ 180^{\circ} , and 270 ∘ 270^{\circ} , and report the best score among these orientations. We report Chamfer Distance (CD) and F-scores at distance thresholds 0.1 and 0.05. The scores are averaged over all test shapes. We use two complementary views of the same outputs. The holistic metric evaluates the union of all completed parts and measures overall object-level geometric quality. The part-level metric evaluates each completed part against its corresponding ground-truth part geometry, then averages over parts and shapes. This separation is important because some baselines do not provide the same part-control interface as our input surface masks; for those methods, holistic quality remains comparable, while part-level evaluation is reported for controllable part-generation or part-decomposition methods whose outputs can be aligned with the provided 2D or 3D mask controls. Computational Complexity OmniLatent generates geometry, deformation, appearance, and part IDs from a single image in approximately 30 seconds with 8192 latent tokens. For comparison, a representative separate pipeline requires approximately 30 seconds for geometry generation, 30 seconds for texture generation, and 20 seconds for Part-ID segmentation, totaling about 80 seconds. This is substantially more efficient than running separate geometry, texture, and post-hoc part segmentation stages, and it avoids cross-stage error accumulation by decoding all attributes from the same latent sample. 5. Conclusion and Limitations We introduced OmniLatent, a unified latent modeling framework for 3D part decomposition. By encoding geometry, deformation, appearance, and binary part IDs into the same OmniLatent space, OmniLatent transfers geometric and visual priors from data-rich modalities to data-scarce part learning. The resulting base model generates geometry, deformation, texture, and surface part IDs from a single image in one pass, supports optional mask guidance, and can be tuned for point-prompted full segmentation, segmentation refinement, part subdivision, and amodal part completion. Figure 13. Limitations. OmniLatent can still produce asymmetric part layouts on symmetric structures and may mistakenly merge semantically distinct regions into a single part. OmniLatent also exposes several directions for future work. The quality of autonomous segmentation still depends on the diversity and semantic consistency of available part-level 3D data. As shown in Figure 13 , one limitation is symmetry consistency: for objects with repeated or bilateral structures, the model may occasionally assign different part layouts to geometrically corresponding regions. Another limitation is erroneous merging, where two components that share similar geometry, appearance, or spatial context may be grouped into the same part even though they should remain separate for editing. A third limitation arises from view-dependent mask guidance: a 2D mask can induce back-side errors by splitting surface regions that should remain connected in 3D. These cases suggest that future work could incorporate stronger symmetry-aware priors, connectivity constraints, or interactive correction mechanisms. Extending the same unified latent formulation to additional production attributes, such as materials, UVs, or articulation, is another promising direction for building more editable 3D assets.
