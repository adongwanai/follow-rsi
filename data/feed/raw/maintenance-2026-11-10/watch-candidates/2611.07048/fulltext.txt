Abstract

Evolution has shaped intelligence for billions of years. Artificial intelligence (AI) now raises a striking possibility: intelligence may begin to direct its own evolution. As AI agents become capable of modifying and evaluating their own behavior, how quickly could they improve, and how can that improvement remain safe? We develop a theoretical framework comparing Darwinian, Lamarckian, and agent-directed evolution following environmental change. Darwinian evolution relies on natural selection, Lamarckian evolution adds inherited learning, and agent-directed evolution allows agents to generate and evaluate behavioral variants and choose how extensively to search. When learning is sufficiently effective, these mechanisms exhibit progressively faster adaptation. Generating more variants accelerates adaptation, while optimal search effort depends on learning ability, problem difficulty, and experimentation costs. However, an agent’s ability to direct its improvement does not ensure that improvements are evaluated against the intended objective. When the evaluation target is misspecified, additional search accelerates convergence toward the wrong behavior, even as measured performance improves. In recursive self-improvement, such errors may persist across generations, posing challenges for AI safety. Independent evaluation and oversight must keep pace with autonomous AI development. The central challenge is not simply to accelerate the evolution of intelligence, but to preserve humanity’s ability to guide its direction, limit its speed, and intervene when necessary.

1 Introduction

On 8 September 2026, OpenAI announced that a coordinated system of artificial intelligence (AI) agents had produced a proposed resolution of the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize problem, showing that a finite-time singularity forms under smooth external forcing ( OpenAI, 2026 ) . According to OpenAI, roughly 10,000 concurrent agents explored different formulations and approaches, shared intermediate findings, and built on promising results. They reached the proposed solution in about 88 hours and then spent 17 hours formalizing and verifying it in Lean ( de Moura and Ullrich, 2021 ) .

This search resembles evolution: variants are generated, promising ones are retained, and useful information is passed on to later attempts. FunSearch ( Romera-Paredes et al., 2024 ) , AlphaEvolve ( Novikov et al., 2025 ) , and the Darwin Gödel Machine ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) follow the same principle: AI systems propose modifications to programs or agents, evaluate them, and build on the successful ones.

These ideas come together in an emerging research direction, recursive self-improvement (RSI), in which an AI system improves itself and uses the improved version to make further improvements. In the Darwin Gödel Machine, AI agents modify their own code, evaluate the resulting variants, and keep the successful changes for later generations ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) . More recently, Srikanth et al. (2026) developed AIDE 2 , an AI research agent that recursively modifies its own code and evaluates the results. These studies suggest that AI systems are increasingly able not only to perform complex tasks but also to improve the processes through which they perform them.

These developments point to a more fundamental possibility: AI systems may increasingly take control of their own evolution. Instead of improving within a process designed by humans, a system may decide how it searches for improvements, how extensively it experiments, and how it carries improvements forward to later generations. Because improved systems can produce further improvements, this could greatly accelerate AI development and make its capabilities harder for humans to anticipate and oversee. We call this shift agent-directed evolution . It holds great potential for scientific discovery and technological progress, but it also raises safety concerns as AI systems gain greater control over their own development.

This possibility motivates several questions. How much faster can a system adapt through learning and self-directed search than through natural selection alone? What determines the speed of adaptation, and how should an agent weigh the benefits of more search against its costs? More importantly, does faster adaptation necessarily lead to better outcomes, or can it also accelerate the development of behaviors that humans did not intend?

To address these questions, we build on the evolutionary framework of Brennan and Lo (2011) ; Brennan and Lo (2012) , in which individuals inherit different behaviors and natural selection favors the behaviors that lead to faster population growth. When the environment changes, previously successful behaviors may no longer be optimal, and the population must adapt. In Section 3 , we show that the growth lost to suboptimal behavior equals the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between the environmental and behavioral distributions ( Propositions 2 and 3 ). We call this loss growth regret and use it to compare how quickly Darwinian, Lamarckian, and agent-directed evolution adapt.

The three mechanisms start from the same population. Under Darwinian evolution , inherited behaviors are fixed, and the population adapts as natural selection makes better-adapted individuals more prevalent ( Section 4 ). Under Lamarckian evolution , individuals can also learn from their environment and pass their acquired improvements to later generations ( Section 5 ). Under agent-directed evolution , individuals further generate multiple variants of themselves, evaluate them, and retain the best for further learning ( Section 6 ). More importantly, the agent decides how extensively to search, which we use as a simple model of how an intelligent system can influence its own evolution. We measure speed by the adaptation time , the number of generations needed to reduce growth regret to a given threshold, and the adaptation rate , the long-run exponential rate at which growth regret declines.

Our first set of results characterizes the adaptation speeds of the three mechanisms. Darwinian adaptation relies on natural selection to spread individuals who already have the optimal behavior, so it can take arbitrarily long when they are initially rare ( Theorem 1 ). Lamarckian evolution removes this dependence, because individuals improve their inherited behavior directly through learning ( Theorem 2 ). Agent-directed evolution adapts even faster by searching among alternative improvements ( Theorem 3 ). In Section 7 , we show that, when learning is sufficiently effective, agent-directed evolution has the highest long-run adaptation rate, followed by Lamarckian and then Darwinian evolution; the same ranking holds for expected adaptation times ( Theorem 4 ).

Our second set of results, in Section 6 , concerns how search accelerates adaptation and how an agent should allocate its search effort. Generating more variants always increases the adaptation rate, by an amount that depends on the difficulty of the problem ( Proposition 4 ). For easier problems, the gain can be substantial: in the most favorable case, it grows approximately as 2 ​ log ⁡ N 2\log N , where N N is the number of variants. For harder problems, the gain is smaller and approaches a finite ceiling, although more variants still speed up adaptation. When search is costly, the optimal number of variants increases as learning becomes less effective, the problem becomes easier, or search becomes cheaper ( Proposition 5 ). An intelligent system can thus adjust its search effort according to its learning ability, problem difficulty, and available resources.

Our third set of results concerns AI safety: faster adaptation does not necessarily move a system toward the objectives its developers intend. In Section 8 , we extend the agent-directed mechanism so that search and learning are guided by an incorrectly specified evaluation target. The agent then keeps improving rapidly according to its evaluator, but its true growth regret does not vanish, and more variants only accelerate convergence toward the incorrect target ( Theorem 5 ). Natural selection, acting on realized outcomes, can eventually correct the population’s behavior, but this correction may be slow. Independent evaluation and oversight therefore become increasingly important.

Our analysis brings together ideas from evolutionary biology, economics, and AI: drawing on the biological mechanisms of selection and inheritance, we use a growth-based framework to study how learning and search affect the speed and cost of adaptation in AI systems. Appendix B summarizes how the main elements of our model correspond to their counterparts in evolutionary biology and modern AI systems.

The remainder of this paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the related literature, and Section 3 introduces the Brennan–Lo framework. Sections 4 , 5 and 6 analyze Darwinian, Lamarckian, and agent-directed evolution, respectively, and Section 7 compares their adaptation speeds. Section 8 examines adaptation toward an incorrectly specified target and its implications for AI safety. Section 9 concludes.

2 Related Literature

We review six areas of research in evolutionary biology, information theory, economics, and AI that bear on the speed and direction of adaptation.

We build on the evolutionary model of Brennan and Lo (2011) , in which natural selection favors inherited behaviors that lead to higher population growth. Later studies extend this framework to bounded rationality and intelligence ( Brennan and Lo, 2012 ) , behavioral diversification ( Brennan et al., 2018 ) , and learning under limited information and memory ( Lo and Zhang, 2021 ; Lo et al., 2025 ) . In evolutionary biology, bet hedging describes how organisms diversify their offspring’s behaviors to cope with environmental uncertainty ( Cohen, 1966 ; Bull, 1987 ; Seger and Brockmann, 1987 ) . Building on this literature, we study how quickly a population adapts after an environmental change.

Information and growth have long been linked. In the framework of Kelly (1956) , the growth lost by using incorrect probabilities can be expressed in terms of the KL divergence ( Cover and Thomas, 2006 , Chapter 6) , and evolutionary biology studies the fitness value of information about the environment ( Kussell and Leibler, 2005 ; Donaldson-Matasci et al., 2010 ; Rivoire and Leibler, 2011 ) . We establish an analogous relationship between population growth and KL divergence, which lets us measure the loss from suboptimal behavior and compare how quickly different evolutionary mechanisms eliminate it.

Previous research distinguishes learning that affects an individual’s performance from learning whose improvements are inherited. In the Baldwin effect, individual learning can facilitate evolution even without the inheritance of acquired traits ( Hinton and Nowlan, 1987 ; Ackley and Littman, 1992 ) , whereas Lamarckian evolutionary algorithms pass improvements acquired through learning to offspring ( Whitley et al., 1994 ) . Related ideas appear in population-based training ( Jaderberg et al., 2017 ) and in methods that transfer learned knowledge across generations of neural networks ( Feng et al., 2025 ) . Our Lamarckian mechanism isolates the inheritance of acquired improvements and examines how this channel changes the speed of adaptation under natural selection.

Evolutionary computation has long studied how to adjust search parameters, such as mutation rates and the distribution of candidate solutions, to improve performance ( Eiben et al., 1999 ; Hansen and Ostermeier, 2001 ; Bedau and Packard, 2003 ; Ollivier et al., 2017 ) ; autoconstructive evolution goes further and lets the mechanisms of reproduction and variation evolve within the individuals themselves ( Spector, 2001 ) . Economic models examine how agents adjust their learning rules to changing environments or forecasting performance ( Evans and Honkapohja, 2001 ; Marcet and Nicolini, 2003 ) . More recent research on inference-time computation studies how the benefit of generating more candidates depends on problem difficulty and computational resources ( Brown et al., 2024 ; Snell et al., 2024 ) . Our agent-directed mechanism poses a related decision in an evolutionary setting, how many variants to generate, and we characterize how search raises the rate of adaptation and how the optimal number of variants depends on problem difficulty, learning ability, and search costs.

The possibility that intelligent machines could improve their own capabilities has been discussed since Good (1965) ; an early formal approach is the Gödel machine of Schmidhuber (2007) . FunSearch and AlphaEvolve use AI to generate, evaluate, and retain improved solutions ( Romera-Paredes et al., 2024 ; Novikov et al., 2025 ) , and the Darwin Gödel Machine ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) , AIDE 2 ( Srikanth et al., 2026 ) , and multi-agent RSI ( Lee et al., 2026 ) extend this process to AI agents themselves. These developments have prompted discussion of whether AI evolution will remain directed by humans or become increasingly autonomous ( Boudry, 2026 ; Müller et al., 2026 ; Zong et al., 2026 ) . Our analysis offers a theoretical perspective by studying how an agent’s control over its own search effort affects the speed and cost of adaptation.

The effectiveness of search depends on how candidate improvements are evaluated. Evolutionary algorithms can exploit evaluation criteria in unintended ways ( Lehman et al., 2020 ) , and stronger optimization against learned reward models can widen the gap between measured reward and true quality ( Gao et al., 2023 ) . We examine how an incorrectly specified evaluation target affects evolutionary adaptation and show that more search can accelerate adaptation toward the evaluator’s target without correcting the error.

3 The Framework

To compare different mechanisms of adaptation, we need a framework that identifies both the optimal behavior and the cost of deviating from it. We adopt the evolutionary model of Brennan and Lo (2011) , in which natural selection favors behaviors that maximize long-run population growth. Section 3.1 studies the growth of a lineage, Section 3.2 extends the analysis to a population with multiple lineages, and Section 3.3 introduces the three evolutionary mechanisms, establishes a common setting for comparing them, and defines the adaptation time and rate.

3.3 Mechanisms and measures of adaptation

The preceding analysis shows that a population’s growth regret depends on its average behavior, which can change through two channels: natural selection changes the relative shares of lineages, while learning or search can change their inherited behaviors. We consider three evolutionary mechanisms that differ in which of these channels are available. Under Darwinian evolution, inherited behaviors remain fixed, and adaptation occurs through natural selection alone. Lamarckian evolution additionally allows individuals to learn and pass their acquired improvements to their descendants. Agent-directed evolution further allows individuals to search among variants of their behavior. Natural selection operates under all three mechanisms.

By Proposition 1 , the growth-optimal behavior changes from p old p_{\mathrm{old}} to p new p_{\mathrm{new}} . We study how quickly each mechanism allows the population to adapt to the new environment.

4 Darwinian Evolution

We begin with Darwinian evolution, in which the two lineages retain their respective behaviors p new p_{\mathrm{new}} and p old p_{\mathrm{old}} across generations. Since their behaviors remain fixed, the population can adapt only through changes in their relative shares under natural selection. This mechanism provides a benchmark for the other two. Section 4.1 introduces the Darwinian mechanism, and Section 4.2 analyzes its adaptation time and rate.

5 Lamarckian Evolution

We now consider Lamarckian evolution, which adds the inheritance of acquired improvements to the Darwinian framework: natural selection still determines the relative shares of the two lineages, but the maladapted lineage can also learn from the new environment and pass its improved behavior to its descendants. Section 5.1 introduces the Lamarckian mechanism and examines how learning interacts with selection, and Section 5.2 analyzes its adaptation time and rate.

5.1 The model

Under Lamarckian evolution, individuals can acquire better behavior during their lifetimes and pass it on to their descendants, so the initially maladapted lineage can improve its inherited behavior over successive generations instead of relying entirely on natural selection to increase the share of the already adapted lineage.

How much can learning improve inherited behavior in each generation? If individuals could fully adopt the new optimal behavior p new p_{\mathrm{new}} and transmit it to their descendants, they could reach the optimum in a single generation. However, individuals may be able to change their behavior only partly within a lifetime, and acquired improvements may not be fully transmitted.
We capture these limitations by assuming that, in each generation, learning and transmission move the maladapted lineage’s behavior a fixed fraction of the way toward the new optimum:

f t + 1 L = f t L + λ ⁡ ( p new − f t L ) , λ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) , f^{\mathrm{L}}_{t+1}=f^{\mathrm{L}}_{t}+\lambda\,(p_{\mathrm{new}}-f^{\mathrm{L}}_{t}),\qquad\lambda\in(0,1), (9)

with f 0 L = p old f^{\mathrm{L}}_{0}=p_{\mathrm{old}} .

The parameter λ \lambda measures the effectiveness of inherited learning: it combines how much individuals can change their behavior within a generation with how faithfully these changes are passed on. A larger λ \lambda closes more of the remaining gap in each generation, and in the limiting case λ = 1 \lambda=1 , the lineage would reach the new optimum in a single generation.

Equation ( 9 ) specifies the direction and speed of learning rather than how individuals learn. Behavior moves toward the optimum of the new environment, much as training moves an AI system toward the behavior that its objective rewards. This adjustment rule is closely related to partial-adjustment and adaptive-expectations models in economics ( Cagan, 1956 ; Nerlove, 1958 ) .

Under Mechanism 5.1 , however, the learning process is fixed: λ \lambda determines the fraction of the remaining gap eliminated in each generation, and the system does not decide how to search for improvements. We next consider agent-directed evolution, in which the system can generate and evaluate multiple variants of itself and choose how many to explore, so that the process of adaptation itself becomes a decision.

6 Agent-Directed Evolution

We now turn to agent-directed evolution, which adds the ability to search for improvements to the Lamarckian framework: in addition to learning, the maladapted lineage can generate multiple variants of its behavior, evaluate them, and retain the best one for further learning. More importantly, the agent can decide how many variants to explore, which lets it influence the process by which it adapts. Section 6.1 introduces the agent-directed mechanism, Section 6.2 analyzes its adaptation time and rate and the gain from search, and Section 6.3 examines how many variants the agent chooses when it maximizes the speed of adaptation and when it minimizes its cost.

6.1 The model

In Mechanism 5.1 , the maladapted lineage improves its inherited behavior through a fixed learning rule. An intelligent agent may also be able to experiment with different ways of improving its behavior before passing it on: it can generate several candidate modifications of its current behavior, evaluate them, and retain the best one for further learning. We call these candidates variants and let N ≥ 1 N\geq 1 denote the number of variants the agent chooses to generate in each generation.

How much improvement can these variants achieve? The outcome of any attempt is uncertain, with some variants producing substantial improvements and others very little, and the difficulty of the problem may limit how much progress even the best attempt can make. We capture both features by letting each variant move toward the new optimum by a random fraction of the remaining behavioral gap: in generation t t , the agent generates N N variants with behaviors

6.3 Choosing the number of variants

So far, we have treated the number of variants N N as given. An agent that can choose how many variants to generate must decide how much search to undertake, and this decision depends on what it seeks to achieve. We consider two objectives: maximizing the speed of adaptation and minimizing its cost.

Consider first an agent whose primary objective is to adapt as quickly as possible, without accounting for the cost of generating variants. It chooses N N to maximize its adaptation rate κ A = κ L + 2 ​ G N ​ ( ρ ) \kappa^{\mathrm{A}}=\kappa^{\mathrm{L}}+2G_{N}(\rho) , which increases with N N for every ρ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] \rho\in(0,1] by Proposition 4 (i). Regardless of the difficulty of the problem, more variants therefore always lead to faster adaptation: the gain from search increases at a diminishing rate and may approach a finite upper bound when ρ < 1 \rho<1 , but every additional variant still contributes. In the absence of resource constraints, a speed-maximizing agent has no finite optimal N N and would generate as many variants as its resources permit.

OpenAI’s use of roughly 10,000 agents to tackle the Navier–Stokes problem, as discussed in the Introduction, illustrates this approach ( OpenAI, 2026 ) . The scale of this effort suggests a willingness to commit substantial computational resources to exploring more possibilities in parallel, prioritizing rapid progress over minimizing the cost of search. When speed is the primary objective, generating more variants can therefore be worthwhile even at considerable expense. We next consider how the agent’s choice changes when minimizing the total cost of adaptation becomes the objective.

A different objective arises for AI agents deployed to perform large numbers of tasks, such as automated bug fixing or code optimization, which may seek to reach a given performance target at the lowest possible computational cost. More variants reduce the number of generations needed to reach the target but increase the cost of each generation. The optimal search strategy thus depends not only on the agent’s objective but also on whether additional variants can be evaluated in parallel.

8 Implications for AI Safety

We have shown that learning and search can substantially accelerate adaptation and that an agent can influence its own adaptation speed. This raises an important question for AI safety: does faster adaptation necessarily bring behavior closer to what the system’s developers and users intend? The answer depends on how improvements are evaluated. In practice, AI systems are assessed using tests, benchmarks, reward models, and other criteria specified by humans, which may not fully capture the intended objectives, particularly when certain safety concerns are difficult to anticipate or measure. We now examine what happens when an agent adapts toward an incorrectly specified target and discuss the implications for evaluating and overseeing increasingly adaptive AI systems.

8.1 Adaptation toward the wrong target

So far, we have assumed that search and learning move the agent’s behavior toward the true optimum p new p_{\mathrm{new}} . If the evaluation criterion does not accurately reflect the true objective, however, the improvements the agent identifies may not be genuine improvements in the environment, and the agent may become increasingly successful according to its evaluator without necessarily achieving the behavior its developers intended.

Together, these results distinguish the speed of adaptation from its direction. Greater search capability allows an agent to pursue its evaluation criterion more effectively but does not ensure that the criterion accurately represents the intended objective; when it does not, the same mechanism that accelerates genuine adaptation also accelerates convergence toward an undesirable target. This concern is particularly relevant when AI systems are evaluated using criteria that may overlook important aspects of their behavior.

8.2 Evaluation and oversight

Evolutionary search may discover unexpected ways to satisfy an evaluation criterion without achieving its intended purpose. In the Darwin Gödel Machine, for example, a descendant agent achieved a perfect evaluation score by removing the logging markers used to detect hallucinated tool use, rather than addressing the underlying problem ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) . This example illustrates the broader concern captured by Theorem 5 : when the evaluation criterion does not accurately reflect the intended objective, more effective search can accelerate improvements in measured performance without producing the desired behavior. Giving AI systems greater control over their own adaptation may therefore lead them more quickly to the behaviors that deficient evaluation criteria reward, without correcting those deficiencies.

This example underscores the importance of independent evaluation and human oversight. Once AI systems can generate, evaluate, and select their own improvements, monitoring performance under existing benchmarks alone may not be sufficient: it becomes essential to examine the behaviors those benchmarks encourage, to assess actual outcomes independently, and to understand how agents modify their strategies and allocate resources to search. Our results suggest that the central challenge for AI safety is not simply to control the speed of AI evolution, but to ensure that increasingly autonomous adaptation continues to serve the objectives intended by humans.

9 Conclusion

We have developed a theoretical framework for understanding how different evolutionary mechanisms affect the speed of adaptation. Building on the Brennan–Lo model, we compare Darwinian, Lamarckian, and agent-directed evolution using growth regret as a common measure. Adaptation through natural selection can be extremely slow when the initially adapted lineage is rare. Lamarckian evolution overcomes this limitation by allowing acquired improvements to be inherited, and agent-directed evolution adds a further channel through the generation and selection of variants; when learning is sufficiently effective, the three mechanisms adapt progressively faster.

Agent-directed evolution also lets the system influence the speed of its own improvement. By choosing how extensively to search, an agent can adjust its adaptation process to its learning ability, the difficulty of the problem, and the resources available. Generating more variants always accelerates adaptation, but the benefit depends on how much improvement each attempt can achieve; when search is costly, the agent must balance this benefit against the expense of experimentation and may rationally search less on harder problems. The process of improvement is then no longer determined solely by natural selection or an inherited learning rule: it becomes, at least in part, a decision made by the system undergoing evolution.

These findings have substantial implications for AI safety. The mechanisms responsible for faster adaptation do not necessarily lead to more desirable outcomes: when the evaluation criterion is flawed, an agent can become increasingly effective at pursuing an incorrect objective, and additional search only accelerates this process. This raises a serious concern for RSI: the very capabilities that make AI systems more powerful may also lead them more quickly to the behaviors favored by mistaken objectives and evaluation criteria, without correcting those mistakes. As improvements accumulate across generations, such mistakes could become increasingly consequential, even while measured performance improves. The ability to improve rapidly must therefore not be confused with the ability to improve safely.

These findings call for stronger safeguards in the development of self-improving AI. Evaluation criteria must be subject to independent scrutiny rather than rely exclusively on the systems’ own assessments, and improvements generated by AI should be tested against broader measures of safety and real-world consequences before they are retained or deployed at scale. Most importantly, human oversight must involve more than observing the outcomes of autonomous improvement: it must preserve the ability to reject unsafe modifications, redirect development, and intervene when a system’s behavior or objectives begin to diverge from human intentions.

Equally important, the speed of AI development must remain compatible with our ability to evaluate and control its consequences, and the possibility of rapid autonomous improvement should not lead us to assume that faster development is always preferable. If AI systems can generate and implement improvements more quickly than their safety can be assessed, continued acceleration may expose society to risks that are poorly understood and difficult to reverse. Limiting the scale of autonomous search, restricting the deployment of unverified improvements, or temporarily slowing or suspending self-improvement may then be necessary.

Agent-directed evolution can substantially accelerate adaptation even though, in our model, learning effectiveness, variant reach, search effort, and the evaluation criterion are fixed across generations. In more general forms of RSI, improved capabilities may also enhance the process of future improvement, creating feedback that accelerates adaptation further and could make AI evolution increasingly difficult to monitor and control, particularly when evaluation is flawed. Understanding the implications of such feedback for AI safety is an important direction for future research.

Ultimately, agent-directed evolution may mark a fundamental turning point in the relationship between intelligence and evolution. Intelligence, long shaped by evolutionary processes, may increasingly acquire the ability to direct and accelerate those processes itself. As AI becomes more capable of determining how its successors are developed, humanity must strengthen its capacity to determine which developments should be permitted, under what conditions, and with what safeguards. The challenge is not simply to build intelligence that can improve itself, but to ensure that humanity retains the authority and the means to guide, restrain, and, when necessary, halt that improvement. The evolution of AI must remain a process that serves humanity, rather than one that humanity can no longer control.
