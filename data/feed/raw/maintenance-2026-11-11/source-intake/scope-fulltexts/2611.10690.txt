shape lies in space, giving a lightweight and widely used 3D representation. For example, existing models can turn voxels into fine-grained
3D shapes [ 30 , 31 ] , keeping the structure that the voxels define. Yet
generating high-fidelity voxels while keeping this lightweight nature remains an open
problem. Existing methods address this problem in two ways. Latent
methods [ 30 , 31 ] achieve high fidelity by compressing the
voxel grid with an autoencoder and generating the latent grid with a generator. The autoencoder is an extra
model to train, and the generator still processes every cell of the latent grid, including
those in empty space, so the computational cost remains high. Hierarchical
methods [ 9 , 33 , 27 ] instead generate a coarse
grid first and refine its occupied cells into finer grids, level by level, which keeps the
lightweight nature of voxels. However, the coarse levels, which decide where all finer cells
can appear, are generated with the least information about the shape and are never revisited.
They are thus often plausible but wrong, and the finer levels inherit these errors. Our key insight is to let finer cells take part in deciding coarse cells. To this end, we
propose JoVox, a discrete flow matching framework that learns the joint distribution of all
levels of the hierarchy, instead of a separate distribution for each level as in
hierarchical methods [ 9 , 33 , 27 ] . Such a
distribution is hard to model, since the structure of the hierarchy varies across shapes, with
finer cells only inside occupied cells. We model it with masked discrete flow
matching [ 11 , 7 ] , which unmasks a few cells at each step, at any
level of the hierarchy, and grows the hierarchy as cells are unmasked. A coarse cell that is
still masked can then be predicted with the finer cells already unmasked nearby. Our framework
needs no autoencoder and processes only the cells inside occupied cells, which keeps generation
lightweight. Within this framework, we further introduce two-level prediction and an unmasking strategy.
Two-level prediction outputs the cells two levels finer at once, instead of one level finer as in
hierarchical methods [ 9 , 6 , 27 ] , and thus halves
the number of levels that the network processes. We also find that two-level prediction yields
voxels of higher fidelity (Section 5.4 ). Our unmasking strategy remasks
the oldest unmasked cells, which were decided with the fewest finer cells around them, so that
they are revisited once finer cells are generated. This also corrects the inconsistencies of
cells unmasked in the same step [ 12 , 3 ] . We evaluate our method on both unconditional and image-conditioned voxel generation, where
it outperforms current SOTA. In particular, our framework makes training far cheaper
than that of TRELLIS.2 [ 31 ] . With the same backbone, our
framework needs significantly less GPU memory and has 8 × 8\times higher
training throughput (Figure 5 ), while its voxels remain of higher fidelity,
with an 11 % 11\% lower Chamfer distance. In summary, our contributions are as follows. • JoVox, a discrete flow matching framework that learns the joint hierarchical
distribution of voxels while keeping generation lightweight. • Two-level prediction, which outputs the cells two levels finer instead of one level
finer, halving the number of levels that the network processes and yielding voxels of higher
fidelity, and an unmasking strategy that revisits early decisions
once finer cells are generated and corrects inconsistencies in masked diffusion models [ 12 , 3 ] . • Compared with the current state-of-the-art TRELLIS.2, we achieve 8 × 8\times higher
training throughput, 1 / 15 1/15 of the FLOPs in a forward pass, train on 4 4 vs. 32 32 GPUs, and
obtain higher quality with an 11 % 11\% lower Chamfer distance. 2 Related work Voxel generation. Voxels are generated as the first stage of many 3D
generators [ 30 , 31 , 32 , 29 ] , or obtained
by voxelizing the meshes of 3D generators that produce meshes
directly [ 24 , 10 ] . Methods that generate voxels fall into
two groups, dense methods and hierarchical methods. Dense methods generate every cell of a full grid.
TRELLIS [ 30 , 31 ] compresses the voxels into a compact
latent grid with an autoencoder and generates this latent grid, which reaches high
fidelity at the cost of training an additional autoencoder. LAS-Diffusion [ 37 ] and DVD [ 32 ] instead
generate the occupancy grid directly. LAS-Diffusion generates the occupancy with continuous
diffusion and thresholds the result. DVD treats the occupancy as discrete data and
generates it with discrete diffusion.
Since all these methods generate every cell, including those in empty space, their cost
grows with the cube of the resolution. Hierarchical methods generate the voxels level by level and refine only occupied cells,
which skips empty space. XCube [ 19 ] generates a sparse voxel hierarchy, with
its own latent diffusion model and autoencoder at each level.
Nexus [ 26 ] and OctFusion [ 33 ] generate the octree with
continuous diffusion and threshold the result to decide which cells to refine. Octree
Transformer [ 9 ] and OAT [ 6 ] generate the octree
autoregressively, one cell at a time, so the number of forward passes grows with the number
of cells. OctGPT [ 27 ] generates many cells of a level in parallel by
masking. However, it decides the finer cells inside a cell independently and needs many
steps at every level.
In all these methods, a coarse level is decided from the coarser levels alone. Such a
level is only roughly plausible. We instead learn the joint distribution of all levels, so that
finer cells take part in deciding coarse cells. Each prediction also outputs the cells two levels finer, instead of
one level finer. Like the hierarchical methods above, we refine only occupied cells. We
generate the occupancy itself, without an autoencoder. Table 1 summarizes

In our framework, single-level prediction uses the formulation of Section 4.1 with occupancy codes in place of occupancies. The entries x i ∈ 𝒯 = { 0 , 1 } 8 x^{i}\in\mathcal{T}=\{0,1\}^{8} are the occupancy codes at the levels J = { 0 , … , L − 1 } J=\{0,\dots,L-1\} . In two-level prediction, the model instead processes a cell u u at level l ≤ L − 2 l\leq L-2 to predict the occupancy of its 4 × 4 × 4 4\times 4\times 4 cells two levels finer, at level l + 2 l+2 (Figure 2 (a)). One possible way is to use the occupancy of these cells as one entry, but such an entry would
take 2 64 2^{64} values, which is prohibitively large. We therefore split the occupancy of these cells into eight 8-bit occupancy codes, one for each child of u u , each giving the occupancy of the eight cells at level l + 2 l+2 inside that child. For a cell u u , the model thus takes these eight occupancy codes, masked or unmasked, as input and outputs eight distributions of Equation ( 6 ), each over the 2 8 2^{8} values of an occupancy code, instead of one distribution over 2 64 2^{64} values. We mask the occupancy codes as in single-level prediction to obtain 𝐱 𝐭 \mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}} . Once a cell u u at the levels 2 , … , L − 2 2,\dots,L-2 is known to be occupied, we add the masked occupancy codes of its eight children to 𝐱 𝐭 \mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}} . occupancy code is remasked, the cells that it marked as occupied are no longer known to be occupied.

Second, the network predicts all masked occupancy codes in 𝐱 𝐭 \mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{t}} , with each t l t_{l} set to the fraction of unmasked occupancy codes at level l l . Third, each
level unmasks masked occupancy codes chosen at random, so that its fraction rises to about κ l ​ ( k + 1 ) \kappa_{l}(k+1) . To unmask an occupancy code, we
sample its value from the probabilities predicted in the second step, at temperature τ \tau .
At τ = 0 \tau=0 , we take the most probable value. Under the schedule, a very large number of occupancy codes at level L − 1 L-1 would be unmasked in step N − 1 N-1 , and these would likely be inconsistent with each other. To alleviate this, we add a few extra steps after step N − 1 N-1 and unmask these occupancy codes evenly over step N − 1 N-1 and
the extra steps. The extra steps do not remask. See Section B in the appendix for

we train it on about 175 175 K assets, less than a quarter of the roughly 800 800 K assets used to train
TRELLIS.2 [ 31 ] . While our model, with the data augmentation described in the appendix, still
outperforms TRELLIS.2 in generalization to the unseen objects of
Toys4k, we expect more training data to further improve it. Besides,
like other masked discrete flow matching models, our model unmasks multiple occupancy codes independently in one step,
which can make them inconsistent with each
other [ 8 , 12 , 34 , 3 ] . Our unmasking
strategy is designed to reduce such inconsistencies, and exploring better unmasking strategies
may further improve the quality of the generated voxels. In future work, we plan to apply our framework to generating large scenes with
multiple objects and long sequences of 3D shapes. Both contain far more voxels than a single
object, and our framework keeps their training lightweight by processing only the cells inside
occupied cells. 7 Conclusion We presented JoVox, a discrete flow matching framework that learns the joint distribution of all
levels of the voxel hierarchy, so that finer cells take part in deciding coarse cells. Within this framework,
two-level prediction halves the number of levels that the network processes and yields voxels of
higher fidelity, and our unmasking strategy revisits early decisions once finer cells are
generated. Our framework outperforms the baselines on unconditional and image-conditioned
generation, while its training is far cheaper than that of TRELLIS.2. Our results
thus show that voxels can be generated with both clearly improved fidelity and drastically improved efficiency. ShapeNet, each category is evaluated separately on its test split, and its reference set has
as many shapes as the generated set. Alignment. Methods and datasets differ in their orientation and scale conventions.
We therefore align each generated shape to its ground truth by picking the one of the 24 24 axis-aligned rotations that minimizes the voxel Chamfer distance and refining it with a scale-aware ICP. Voxel metrics. For LPIPS V {}_{\text{V}} and DINO V {}_{\text{V}} , we render the aligned voxels and the ground-truth voxels from six views and
average the LPIPS and the cosine similarity of DINOv2 features over the views. For Direct3D-S2,
the voxel metrics use the 64 3 64^{3} voxels of its dense first stage, and for TripoSG and TripoSR, they use their
voxelized meshes. Mesh metrics. The meshes of Direct3D-S2, TripoSG and TripoSR are aligned to the ground

the two meshes. LPIPS N {}_{\text{N}} and CLIP N {}_{\text{N}} compare normal maps rendered from four
views. Uni3D is the similarity between the Uni3D embeddings of
point clouds sampled on the two meshes. ShapeNet metrics. FID V {}_{\text{V}} is computed on points of the voxels.
