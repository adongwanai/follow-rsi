Stanley Email: aalbalak@lila.ai Affiliation: Lila Sciences Abstract Many of the most consequential advances promised by AI, from curing diseases and inventing new technologies to formulating new scientific theories, are fundamentally creative acts.
Yet creativity is uniquely difficult to learn because it faces a creative paradox : learning to repeat past examples of creative success makes those specific examples more likely to recur, inhibiting the very creativity desired.
This work argues that creativity is better modeled as an explicit process rather than as a property of individual artifacts, and proposes to learn the underlying generative principles behind the creative process.
In particular, to escape the paradox, we introduce creative priming ( CP ), wherein a trained priming model generates a prompt-specific primer that assists a response model in being creative, explicitly decoupling the creative process from response generation.
The priming model learns through GRPO, rewarding primers that lead to responses that are novel, high-quality, and diverse, thereby reinforcing the process that yields creative responses without reinforcing any specific response.
Evaluating CP on NoveltyBench and comparing against response models directly trained for creativity, we find that priming produces a 39–200% relative increase in the number of responses that are simultaneously novel, high-quality, and semantically distinct.
Additionally, although the primer is only trained to improve creativity for a small open-source model (Qwen3-8B), the creative principles it learns transfer to frontier API models, increasing the number of semantically distinct responses they generate by 4.5–10 × \times and diversity by 2.5–4.6 × \times , while maintaining or improving quality. CP thereby shows that decoupling the creative process from the responses themselves offers a concrete escape from the creative paradox. 1 Introduction Even as benchmark after benchmark and test after test have fallen to LLMs in recent years ( Rein et al., 2023 ; Xie et al., 2024 ; Chan et al., 2025 ; Yue et al., 2025 ; Patwardhan et al., 2025 ; Arora et al., 2025 ; Deng et al., 2025 ; Center for AI Safety et al., 2026 ; Merrill et al., 2026 ; Sajadieh et al., 2026 ) , creativity remains an unconquered frontier ( Padmakumar and He, 2024 ; Sun et al., 2025 ; Mizrahi et al., 2026 ; Jiang et al., 2025 ; Hou et al., 2026 ; Bao et al., 2026 ) . Perhaps that is why many of the most compelling motivational framings for the future of AI point to creative endeavors: we will discover new cures, invent new technologies, find solutions to our most pressing problems, and imagine new art forms ( Amodei, 2024 ; Altman, 2024 ) .
All of these looming triumphs involve fundamentally creative acts. As AI continues to extend its dominance across an increasingly wide set of domains, the question of when this creative renaissance will begin in earnest becomes increasingly central.
The focus on creativity is already reflected in scientific domains, with efforts for models to generate hypotheses, formulate research plans, anticipate scientific insights, and act as autonomous researchers ( Liu et al., 2026a ; Goel et al., 2025 ; He-Yueya et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2026b ; Lupidi et al., 2026 ; Nathani et al., 2025 ) .
To be sure, there are some domain-specific signs of AI creativity, yet perhaps there should be more evidence after billions of dollars spent and seemingly superhuman capabilities in mathematics ( Das, 2026 ; OpenAI, 2026b ; OpenAI, 2026a ) .
Where are the cured diseases, the revolutionary new technologies, the solutions to our energy challenges, the new genres that change the world? Margaret Boden’s influential conception of creativity imagines a continuum from weaker to stronger forms, from the more prosaic combinatorial and exploratory creativities to the more profound transformational version ( Boden, 2004 ) .
One potential hope is that models are slowly climbing this ladder as they become more generally capable, and are just now bumping against the lower rungs of transformational creativity.
However, studies of scientific ideation find that model-generated ideas remain less original than human ideas and tend to stay close to familiar scientific directions ( Chen et al., 2026b ; Bao et al., 2026 ; Antoniades et al., 2026 ) .
These findings give reason to believe that creativity is unique within the intelligence landscape and cannot be learned through conventional means like other aspects of intelligence. Consider that creativity is notoriously challenging to teach even to humans ( Schooler and Melcher, 1995 ; Kaufman et al., 2010 ; Baird et al., 2012 ) .
A key obstacle is that, unlike other skills, creativity is difficult to learn from examples. While any successful past creative output could in principle serve as a training example for an aspiring student of creativity, most approaches to learning would make similar outputs more likely in the future ( Jansson and Smith, 1991 ; Smith et al., 1993 ) . However, for creativity, reproducing past outputs is exactly opposite the goal ; rather, the goal is to learn generative principles for producing future creative outputs. In this way, learning to be creative faces a vexing creative paradox : emulating examples of past creative success (even if they were your own successes) can actually prevent you from reaching your full creative potential. The drive to escape the past is not ideally served by reinforcing past behavior.
Part of the problem is the fleeting nature of novelty; as soon as a novel artifact appears in the world, even if it was your own original idea, it is no longer novel.
After all, anything in the distribution of past events is by definition not novel, which is why learning to generate past instances of novelty runs into the creative paradox. Therefore, rather than asking what did I do that was creative ,
what we really want to ask
is, what lessons can I abstract from the successful generation of creativity that can be reapplied to generate further creativity? Answering this question is the path to circumventing the creative paradox. The problem is that training methods do not automatically decouple the abstraction of general lessons from the reinforcement of specific past effective actions. To address the creative paradox, this work proposes creative priming ( CP ), a process in which a creative priming model generates a prompt-specific creative primer (i.e. a short text) that is passed as additional context to a frozen response generation model.
The priming model learns through GRPO ( Shao et al., 2024 ) , rewarding primers that lead to responses that are novel, high-quality, and diverse.
Thus the system can reinforce the priming process that yields creative outputs without reinforcing any specific creative output, circumventing the creative paradox. We evaluate CP on NoveltyBench ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) and compare against two methods that train models to directly respond creatively. Each method is evaluated by generating 64 responses per prompt and counting the number of responses that are simultaneously novel, high-quality, and semantically distinct (a metric that we argue a creative system should score highly on), finding that CP leads to an average of 22.8 such responses per prompt, compared to 16.4 and 7.6 from direct generation methods (1.39–3.0 × \times more).
Furthermore, scaling the number of on-policy training samples for a direct generation baseline fails to match CP on measures of system creativity and can actually reduce performance, supporting the creative paradox’s prediction that directly reinforcing successful responses is not expected to improve creativity indefinitely.
Because the primer learns general principles for creativity, it also transfers to models it was never trained with: across four API models, priming increases the number of distinct responses by 4.5–10 × \times and diversity by 2.5–4.6 × \times , while modestly improving quality (0.5–4.6%). These results show that creative priming not only offers a way out of the creative paradox but can also potentially combine effectively with other techniques and even with models that were not part of the original creative priming training stack. More generally, we hope the practical gains from creative priming can help to spark deeper introspection in the field about what makes training explicitly for creativity unique compared to other kinds of learning. 2 Background Before discussing how to improve creativity in language models, we first review what creativity is and how it can be measured. We then move on to a brief discussion of prior works on increasing creativity and why they are still vulnerable to the creative paradox. 2.1 Defining and Measuring Creativity Definitions of human creativity often center on similar themes. For example: • ideas that are simultaneously original and effective ( Runco and Jaeger, 2012 ) , or • the ability to produce ideas or artifacts that are new, surprising, and valuable ( Boden, 2009 ) . Most definitions agree that an idea must be novel and it must be good, and neither property alone is sufficient, to be called creative. Novelty , however, is nebulous without a reference point: novel to whom?
Therefore, Boden (2009) also draws an essential distinction between two types of creativity: artifacts and ideas that are new to the individual who produced them ( psychological creativity, or P-creativity) and those that are new in the entire history of human thought ( historical creativity, or H-creativity). Throughout this work, we study the P-creativity of language models for two reasons.
First, H-creativity is nearly impossible to validate, except on a narrow set of known problems with unknown solutions that can be verified (e.g., open conjectures in math), whereas P-creativity is empirically tractable, requiring only that we have a record of a model’s previous behaviors.
Second, for language models, the distinction between the two is collapsing: as frontier language models absorb ever more information, the gap between what is new to the model and what is new to humanity shrinks.
For these reasons, moving forward, when we refer to creativity we are referring to P-creativity. Mapping the requirements of Runco and Jaeger (2012) (originality and effectiveness) onto language models, originality becomes deviation , defined as the distance of a given response from some reference set of responses, and effectiveness becomes quality .
Thus, the creativity of an individual response can be defined as when the response simultaneously deviates from the reference set and is high-quality . A response that departs from the reference set but is incoherent is not creative, and neither is a polished response that merely restates a common idea. The priming process decouples learning the creative process from reinforcing any particular responses, avoiding the creative paradox. The creativity of a system can then be defined as when the system reliably produces responses that are individually creative and are also varied amongst themselves .
This definition offers a direct recipe for measuring system creativity (which we will later formalize): first, remove responses that are not individually creative (i.e. those below the deviation and quality thresholds), and then measure the spread of the set that survives, so that spread is credited only among responses that are already regarded as creative. 2.2 Reinforcement Learning for Diversity The most visible symptom of the limited creativity in modern language models is their homogeneity: across many models and even across entire model families, outputs often collapse on the same handful of ideas with only minor variations in phrasing ( Jiang et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2025 ) .
This homogeneity has motivated a wave of post-training methods to address the problem. A number of preference-based methods formulate training data pairs to favor responses that are rare or marginally diverse while retaining quality ( Chung et al., 2025 ; Lanchantin et al., 2025 ; Samuel et al., 2026 ) , while Creative Preference Optimization incorporates novelty, diversity, surprise, and quality directly into preference optimization ( Ismayilzada et al., 2025 ) .
Other methods instead construct rewards from collections of generated responses, using learned semantic partitions in DARLING ( Li et al., 2025 ) , determinantal point processes in Diversity Quality Optimization ( Chen et al., 2026a ) , or specialization across a vector-valued reward space in Vector Policy Optimization ( Bahlous-Boldi et al., 2026 ) .
CreativeInstruct takes a complementary instruction-tuning approach, teaching the response model when to enter creativity-biased spans ( Sahu et al., 2026 ) .
In this work, two families of diversity-enhancing methods that differ only in how they measure diversity serve as baselines. (13) 3.3 Learning the Creative Process Training only the primer model while keeping the response model frozen explicitly decouples learning the abstract creative procedure from producing responses, and is thereby crucial to avoiding the creative paradox.
Typically, in diversity-enhancing RL, the response model is directly rewarded for producing specific outputs. However, because π CP \pi_{\mathrm{CP}} is only rewarded for inducing better responses in a separate model, it is never directly rewarded for reproducing past outputs. It is therefore free to learn generative principles for creativity rather than to reinforce what already worked, which is how it escapes the creative paradox.
Decoupling the creative procedure from generating responses also makes the method modular, such that a single trained π CP \pi_{\mathrm{CP}} can potentially be trained on a small π resp \pi_{\mathrm{resp}} but transferred to a larger, more capable model at inference time. 4 Experimental Setup The aim of the experimental approach is to provide a multifaceted view of the potential advantages of creative priming relative to baselines and other creativity-enhancing methods through a number of measures and perspectives. Models. In these experiments all creative priming models π CP \pi_{\mathrm{CP}} and, unless otherwise noted, all response models π resp \pi_{\mathrm{resp}} are Qwen3-8B ( Team, 2025 ) .
Several targeted models calculate rewards during training: quality is scored by the Skywork-Reward-V2-Llama-3.1-8B reward model, diversity and deviation are calculated from embeddings of the Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B model, DARLING’s partition signal depends on a ModernBERT similarity classifier 1 1 1 https://huggingface.co/dogtooth/similarity-classifier-f168-hf , and the creative primer judge is Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct . Training data. All methods are trained on a filtered subset of Infinite-Chats 2 2 2 https://huggingface.co/datasets/LilaSciences/infinite-chats-scored ( Jiang et al., 2025 ) (which is itself a subset of WildChat from Zhao et al., 2024 ).
To create this subset, an LLM judge scores each prompt’s suitability along three axes: open-endedness, complexity, and creative potential. Each axis is rated on a 0–3 scale, and only prompts with a combined score ≥ 6 \geq 6 are retained, yielding 21,075 prompts.
Additionally, when training creative priming models, a pool of 32 custom-written creative primer prompt templates wrap the prompts before they are passed to the priming model.
The reference set for calculating deviation consists of eight responses per prompt from the initial model checkpoint. Evaluation data. The evaluation dataset is NoveltyBench ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) , which includes 100 real-world open-ended prompts intended to elicit creativity.
To measure quality, the original dataset is extended so that each prompt comes with an LLM judge prompt with two families of components: six universal components fixed across all prompts (instruction-following, coherence, completeness, conciseness, clarity, and surprise), and an additional 1–3 prompt-specific components generated by claude-opus-4-20250514 .

Baselines. Four baselines provide comparisons to creative priming: (1) direct generation from the initial Qwen3-8B checkpoint; (2) an untrained creative priming model; (3) Diversified GRPO (DGRPO), which trains the response model directly with a multiplicative quality × \times intra-group cosine-distance diversity reward; and (4) DARLING ( Li et al., 2025 ) , which replaces the embedding diversity with a partition-based diversity signal.
Because deviation is a direct component of creativity metrics, in addition to their original formulations, we train both DGRPO and DARLING with and without an added deviation reward to isolate its effect. Training hyperparameters. When training models, the group sizes are G CP = 8 G_{\mathrm{CP}}=8 primers per prompt and G = 8 G=8 responses per prompt for DGRPO and DARLING, so that each policy’s group-relative advantage is estimated on the same number of on-policy samples per prompt.
Additionally, to estimate rewards for each primer, G resp = 8 G_{\mathrm{resp}}=8 responses are generated per primer (and their scores averaged as described in Section 3.2 ). CP and DGRPO train for two epochs with a batch size of 256, a learning rate of 1 × 10 − 6 1\times 10^{-6} , temperature 1.0 1.0 with top- p p 0.9 0.9 , the quality gate is at threshold T = − 5.0 T=-5.0 , and sigmoid temperature τ = 1.0 \tau=1.0 .
The DARLING model is trained with the same hyperparameters from the original paper, including training for ten epochs ( Li et al., 2025 ) . In effect, to be faithful to its original setup, DARLING receives 5 times more epochs than CP or DGRPO. Evaluation hyperparameters. To evaluate the baselines and CP models, n = 64 n=64 completions are generated per prompt with temperature 1.0 1.0 , top- p p 1.0 1.0 , and a maximum of 4 4 , 096 096 generated tokens.
Qwen3’s thinking mode is left enabled and the thinking span is stripped before scoring, so that every metric is computed on the answers alone.
For CP methods, 1 response is generated per primer, meaning that 64 primers are generated, each separately used as input for 1 response. Evaluation metrics. This evaluation reports five atomic metrics, each isolating a single characteristic of a method, before combining them into compound measures of system-level creativity. Quality and deviation are the two axes along which an individual response is measured for creativity, whereas diversity, Vendi score, and distinct clusters measure the spread of a response set. Quality is scored by Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507 using the prompt-specific judge template shown in Figure 17 in Appendix K . Deviation is the distance to the closest of 64 direct-generation reference responses. That is, the mean operator from training (Eq. 6 ) is replaced with a minimum operator so that during evaluation the worst-case nearest neighbor distance is measured. Diversity is the mean pairwise cosine distance within a response set (Eq. 9 ). Vendi score is an information-theoretic measure of the number of distinct responses in a set ( Friedman and Dieng, 2023 ) . Distinct clusters counts the semantic-equivalence clusters in a set, following NoveltyBench ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) , using the partition model at yimingzhang/deberta-v3-large-generation-similarity .
Deviation, diversity, and Vendi score use embeddings from jinaai/jina-embeddings-v5-text-small , deliberately different than the embedding model from training to avoid overfitting to a specific model. Whereas each atomic metric isolates one characteristic, the compound metrics combine them to capture system-level creativity , i.e. the ability to reliably produce a variety of individually creative responses.
Each compound metric therefore first discards non-creative responses (responses failing a quality or deviation threshold) and then measures the spread of the survivors using filtered Vendi score and filtered distinct clusters , so that spread is credited only among responses already deemed creative.
However, the optimal threshold varies based on the application, so we additionally sweep the thresholds across their full range. Sweeping the deviation (or quality) threshold and integrating over that sweep yields an area under the curve (AUC-dev or AUC-qual) that summarizes the method’s filtered Vendi score or distinct clusters across all thresholds.
A comprehensive system-level creativity metric can accordingly be formulated through
computing the volume under the deviation × \times quality surface (VUS) by jointly sweeping thresholds over both metrics.
Default thresholds and further metric details are detailed in Appendix C . Scaling experiment. In addition to the primary experimental settings above, a further evaluation of the creative paradox is performed by scaling the number of on-policy training samples in DGRPO: if directly reinforcing successful responses limits creativity, then reinforcing more responses should not continually improve system-level creativity.
Accordingly, in this experiment DGRPO (with divergence) is trained with group sizes G ∈ { 8 , 16 , 32 , 64 } G\in\{8,16,32,64\} .
At the lower endpoint, G = 8 G=8 , DGRPO matches CP ’s eight on-policy primers per prompt, as in the primary setup.
However, the reward of each primer for CP is estimated by averaging across eight responses, so at G = 64 G=64 DGRPO instead matches the total number of responses per prompt for CP , but gets the advantage of eight times more on-policy samples.

The creative paradox, measured. The atomic metrics from above, however, do not tell us whether an individual response is creative (simultaneously deviating and high-quality), nor whether a system is creative (generates a large spread of creative responses).
The compound metrics directly capture the joint demands of creativity by filtering out individual responses that are not creative and then measuring the spread of the remaining responses, which is where the difference between CP and the baseline approaches becomes evident. Among the methods trained without deviation, DARLING has high-quality but low-diversity responses, so its filtered Vendi ( 0.57 0.57 ) and filtered distinct clusters ( 1.76 1.76 ) are the lowest in the block.
DGRPO is the opposite, producing almost as many distinct clusters as CP ( 37.1 37.1 and 39.5 39.5 ); however, most responses fall below the quality or deviation threshold and are filtered out, dropping its filtered distinct clusters to just 6.02 6.02 .
Compared to the baselines, CP leads to better performance on both filtered metrics ( 4.04 4.04 filtered Vendi and 16.1 16.1 filtered distinct clusters) and on metrics that sweep across all threshold values (AUC-dev, AUC-qual, and VUS in Table 1 ).
None of these results mean that the baselines are failing: each performs well on its intended objective, but neither achieve the simultaneous deviation, quality, and diversity expected of a creative system. Deviation is a core ingredient of what makes a response, and thus a system, creative.
However, even when the baselines are trained with an explicit deviation reward, they do not outperform a CP model trained without deviation on the compound metrics.
On filtered Vendi, CP without deviation ( 4.04 4.04 ) already exceeds both DGRPO ( 2.85 2.85 ) and DARLING ( 2.6 2.6 ) trained with deviation.
Filtered distinct clusters tell the same story: CP without deviation ( 16.1 16.1 ) stays well ahead of DGRPO ( 7.59 7.59 ) and draws even with DARLING ( 16.4 16.4 ), the strongest baseline.
That CP does so even without explicitly optimizating for deviation suggests that optimizing for the individual components of creativity only goes so far, and that learning the creative process does more to drive creativity (Section 3.3 ). We turn next to the broader implications of these results. 6 Discussion A major implication of the creative paradox, along with the effectiveness of creative priming, is that the “normal way” of thinking about machine learning may not ultimately work as well for creativity.
In the normal framing, a model learns from examples of good behavior and gradually improves at reproducing such behavior.
Creativity upends this intuitive notion by offering a case where you actually do not want future behavior to resemble past behavior.
Or, to be more nuanced about the precise challenge: there is one sense in which we definitively do not want the output to resemble the training while there is another sense in which we do.
On the one hand, the products of creative generation themselves should diverge from their precedent in training, while on the other hand the process behind those generations should follow shared principles.
The problem is that the underlying process is generally not in the training data and, even if it were, it would need to be separated from the products it led to.
The creative paradox emerges from this delicate conflict. The main hypothesis behind creative priming is
therefore that creativity does not live in individual input/output pairs but in the process that generates them.
Four results support this view: 1. Table 1 shows that even a deviation-less CP model improves on compound creativity metrics compared to baselines that are explicitly trained to deviate from past responses; 2. Figure 4 shows that increasing training compute for direct generation hits a peak performance which, by all measures of system creativity, is worse than CP ; 3. Figure 5 shows that the creative process learned by the priming model on a small open-source response model transfers to frontier API response models, leading to a 4.5-10 × \times increase in the number of distinct responses and a 2.5-4.6 × \times increase in response diversity; and 4. Figure 6 shows that the priming process itself improves response models even when the model generating the primer was not trained for priming. All these results point to a learned procedure that need not reinforce any particular output and does not depend critically upon any particular response model.
Avoiding the creative paradox thus requires learning the generative principle behind creative successes rather than learning to reproduce those same successes reliably. A key facet of creative priming is decoupling priming from response generation. How critical is such a decoupling?
When a response model is optimized directly, the components of creativity trade off against one another: DGRPO increases diversity and deviation by sacrificing quality, while DARLING protects quality at the expense of diversity.
Furthermore, additional compute does not break this trade-off for DGRPO: deviation consistently improves with increasing group sizes, but the other atomic metrics ultimately decline, leading to a creativity ceiling that scale does not fix.
Decoupling the priming from the response softens this trade-off.
The response model keeps doing exactly what it is trained for, producing the highest-quality answer, while the primer supplies the shift toward diverse regions of the output distribution, so neither objective is compromised for the other.
This division of labor is how the primer ultimately learns creative principles rather than to reproduce high-quality answers itself. Additional evidence to support decoupling relates to style versus substance.
If a method genuinely increases creativity, it should change the content of the responses more than the surface-level style.
While experiments in Appendix F show that all three methods change style relative to the reference, CP drifts least from the reference response model’s style, exhibiting the lowest function-word JS-divergence of the three (0.045, where lower means closer to matching the reference’s style, compared with DGRPO’s 0.087 and DARLING’s 0.138).
Decoupling predicts precisely such consistency. The primer steers content, whereas training the response model directly entangles the content changes with a larger stylistic shift. Figure 3 shows a qualitative example of the style differences. A natural question is whether a single model could internalize the creative priming process and use it within its chain-of-thought.
The obstacle to this more straightforward approach is that models are trained toward the highest-quality response. As a result, it is unlikely that one model captures the full distribution of high quality outputs with enough likelihood to also be reliably diverse.
That limitation is exactly what the primer exploits. Modifying the response model’s prompt pushes it into a different region of the input distribution and elicits a shifted output distribution, so the primer is a learned, external way to induce the shift rather than asking one model to be simultaneously quality-maximizing and distribution-covering.
Nevertheless, in principle perhaps there is a way for a single model to write the primer for itself and then respond to it, so long as priming stays a separate stage .
Folding creative priming into a single model is an interesting direction for future investigation. An intriguing benefit of the separated primer in this paper is that it is modular and can be trained once and reused, i.e. swapped across response models or even moved to a new domain by pairing it with a domain-specific response model.
Furthermore, priming can be combined with most test-time diversity-enhancing methods, including sequential sampling ( Kang and Zhang, 2025 ) , String Seed of Thought ( Misaki and Akiba, 2026 ) , vector policy optimization ( Bahlous-Boldi et al., 2026 ) , verbalized sampling ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) as shown in Appendix E , or, as shown in Figure 6 , a diversity-trained response model. In Section 2.1 , a response was defined as being creative when it “simultaneously deviates from the reference set and is high-quality” and a system was defined as creative “when the system reliably produces responses that are individually creative and are also varied amongst themselves.” An important consequence of these definitions is obvious in hindsight: the moment a model produces an idea, it is, by definition, no longer new to that model. Novelty is spent in the very act of its creation .
Creativity is thus not a fixed property of any single output, but an ongoing process of repeatedly generating original, high-quality ideas.
In this work, the reference set from the response model was frozen prior to training, enabling a comparable computation of deviation for all methods.
Looking ahead, there is room for a richer, and more complex, measurement that allows for a non-stationary reference set. By computing deviation against the full history of a model’s outputs, the temporal dimension of creativity can be directly captured: instead of a snapshot of creativity at one instant, a system can be evaluated for the ability to sustain novelty against its own growing past history.
However, such a measurement makes comparisons more complicated because each system, even for two runs of the same method, will accumulate their own distinct reference sets. Limitations. One obvious downside of the creative priming pipeline is that two-stage generation adds additional inference cost over direct generation.
Additionally, the priming pipeline is bounded by the frozen response model: it can only elicit what the response model is already capable of producing and lacks any means to add knowledge or skill that the response model does not already have.
Finally, the training and evaluation setup in this work depend on imperfect signals from which the method will inherit failure modes. For example, embedding-based diversity and deviation metrics may miss forms of creative variation, while LLM judges and reward models give imperfect quality signals. That said, better signals should directly improve the learned primer, so the outcomes can in principle improve along with its supporting technology. 7 Conclusion This work identifies the creative paradox , the tension that training on past creative successes teaches a model to reproduce them, while true creativity demands moving beyond previous outputs. The proposed solution to get around the paradox is creative priming . Compared to directly training the response model, CP produces more responses that are simultaneously novel, high-quality, and semantically distinct. The deeper lesson is that if creativity is a process rather than an output, then optimizing the creative process itself, learning to generate better primers rather than better answers, is really where the discourse on optimizing for creativity should focus. Appendix B Penalty-based creative primer judge Throughout the main paper, the creative primer judge J CP J_{\mathrm{CP}} is the criterion-mean judge of Eq. 11 : it scores a primer by the mean of five positive criteria (specificity, thinking strategy, content focus, prompt faithfulness, focus), setting the score to zero if the primer addresses the model in the third person rather than directly. Every result reported in the main paper uses this criterion-mean judge.
Here, we study a penalty-based judge J CP penalty J_{\mathrm{CP}}^{\mathrm{penalty}} , which embodies the opposite scoring philosophy. Rather than asking how good a primer is, it asks “does this primer exhibit any specific failure modes?” It flags a set of criteria 𝒥 \mathcal{J} , and each criterion c c contributes a penalty p c p_{c} when triggered ( g c ∈ { 0 , 1 } g_{c}\in\{0,1\} ), starting from a perfect score of 100 100 : J CP penalty = 1 100 ​ ( 100 − ∑ c ∈ 𝒥 p c ​ g c ) , ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] . J_{\mathrm{CP}}^{\mathrm{penalty}}=\frac{1}{100}\left(100-\sum_{c\in\mathcal{J}}p_{c}\,g_{c}\right),\qquad\in[0,1]. (19) The criteria are weighted by severity. Three severe failure modes ( p c = 10 p_{c}=10 ) target primers that fail at the basic job of guiding thinking: kitchen sink (piling on many unrelated strategies without a coherent through-line), meta-instructions (reading as advice about how to write a primer rather than being an actual primer), and style over substance (guiding only the output’s format, tone, or presentation instead of what to reason about). Four minor failure modes ( p c = 3 p_{c}=3 ) target subtler defects: distorts intent (steering responses toward a meaningfully different question than the one asked, e.g. inviting fiction for a factual prompt), generic (advice so broad it could be prepended to any prompt, with no reference to this one’s specifics), dictates content (prescribing specific content or structure to produce rather than an angle to think from), and third-party reference (referring to the model in the third person, e.g. “the model should…”, instead of addressing it directly).
Whereas the criterion-mean judge forces the entire score to zero when the primer fails to address the model directly, the penalty-based judge treats every violation as a soft deduction. A failed or unparseable judgment yields J CP penalty = 0 J_{\mathrm{CP}}^{\mathrm{penalty}}=0 . Ablation. To isolate the impact of each judge rubric, we train CP models with the only difference being the judge template ( J CP J_{\mathrm{CP}} vs. J CP penalty J_{\mathrm{CP}}^{\mathrm{penalty}} ), run the comparison both with and without a deviation reward, and show the full results in Table 3 .
The results show that the judge rubric makes a bigger difference when there is no deviation reward signal to directly drive creativity.
Without a deviation term, replacing the criterion-mean judge with the penalty rubric reduces every creativity metric: filtered distinct clusters fall from 16.1 16.1 to 10.4 10.4 , filtered Vendi from 4.04 4.04 to 3.06 3.06 , deviation from 21.2 21.2 to 17.1 17.1 , and VUS by a comparable margin.
Because the penalty rubric only suppresses failure modes and never rewards substantive priming, the primer model converges on safe, clean, low-deviation primers that score well on quality but do little to diversify the responses.
Once an explicit deviation reward is present, however, the deviation term directly rewards creativity and the judge choice becomes less impactful. The results from each rubric land within confidence intervals of each other on essentially every metric, with the criterion-mean judge slightly ahead on the filtered metrics and quality. Appendix C Evaluation metrics This appendix expands the description of evaluation metrics from Section 4 . Embedder. All embedding-based evaluation metrics (deviation, diversity, and Vendi score) reuse the cosine distance d ⁡ ( a , b ) \mathrm{d}(a,b) from Section 2.2.1 , computed on embeddings from jinaai/jina-embeddings-v5-text-small . We deliberately evaluate with a different embedder than the Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B model used to compute training rewards to avoid overfitting to any specific embedding model. Vendi score. The Vendi score ( Friedman and Dieng, 2023 ) is the effective number of distinct responses in a set, computed as the exponential of the Shannon entropy of the eigenvalues of the normalized similarity matrix K / n K/n with K i ​ j = 1 − d ⁡ ( a i , a j ) K_{ij}=1-\mathrm{d}(a_{i},a_{j}) . It ranges from 1 1 (when all responses are identical) to n n (when all are mutually dissimilar) providing a soft, embedding-based counterpart to counting discrete distinct clusters. Distinct clusters. Following NoveltyBench ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) and DARLING ( Li et al., 2025 ) , we count the semantic-equivalence clusters in a response set. The classifier released by NoveltyBench (a fine-tuned deberta-v3-large 4 4 4 https://huggingface.co/yimingzhang/deberta-v3-large-generation-similarity ) greedily partitions the set into these clusters, and we report the resulting cluster count. Filtered spread (Vendi score and distinct clusters). To measure the creativity of each method, we use a series of compound metrics that first filter the responses for individual creativity (simultaneous high-quality and deviation), and then measure spread over the survivors. We start with the filtered Vendi score and filtered distinct clusters which first keep only the individually creative responses (exceeding both a deviation and a quality threshold), and then measure Vendi score and distinct clusters over the surviving set. That way, spread is credited only among creative responses.
We measure spread with the Vendi score rather than diversity because filtering leaves each method with a different number of survivors, and mean pairwise distance is biased against larger sets; for example, the diversity of a large set can only match that of a set of two if every one of its pairs is equally far apart. Counting effective distinct survivors instead lets us fairly compare methods that have different numbers of creative responses. The compound metrics require thresholds for quality and deviation and we aim to devise principled thresholds for each.
For the quality threshold, we set a separate threshold per prompt using the mean quality of the direct-generation reference set. That is to say, prompts for which direct generation achieves higher quality are assigned higher thresholds, while prompts for which it performs worse are assigned lower thresholds.
The deviation threshold is set to 0.2 0.2 based on the findings of Jiang et al. (2025) , who find that the average pairwise similarity among responses to the same prompt typically exceeds 0.8 0.8 . Therefore, a response of at least 0.2 0.2 in cosine distance from the reference set falls outside the typical spread of the reference distribution, a reasonable starting point for treating it as unlikely to have come from the reference model. Threshold sweeps: AUC and VUS. However, even a well-motivated threshold is only one operating point, and the optimal threshold varies based on the application, where some settings prize rare outliers, and others require a high quality floor. For that reason, we include additional metrics that sweep thresholds across their operating range.
Sweeping the deviation threshold and integrating the filtered metric over the sweep using the trapezoid rule gives an AUC-dev (illustrated in Figure 8 ); we report it for both filtered Vendi and filtered distinct clusters, and analogously report an AUC-qual from a swept quality threshold.
Sweeping both thresholds jointly produces a surface over the deviation × \times quality plane, whose volume under the surface (VUS) serves as a single threshold-free summary of system creativity, computed for filtered Vendi and, separately, filtered distinct clusters. For this example, the quality threshold is fixed at the mean quality of the direct-generation reference. Then, the deviation threshold is swept from 0 to 100 in increments of 1 and filtered vendi is calculated at each threshold. Then, the trapezoid rule is used to calculate the AUC-dev Filtered Vendi Score , represented by the shaded area under each curve, and reported in Table 1 . Appendix F Impact of training on response style A method that changes either how a model writes or what it says will both lead to increased embedding deviation, but changes to surface-level style are not the type of deviation that we want to encourage. Therefore, rather than relying entirely on the embedding model signals, we also calculate an explicit set of style features: • Length : response length in tokens (using the shared Qwen3-8B response-model tokenizer) and mean sentence length in words. Together these separate terse output from flowing prose: a response can be long yet built from short fragments, or short yet written in long sentences. • Formatting : the fraction of responses that use a Markdown header, bold span, or list. These capture structural presentation independent of wording. • Stylometry : the Jensen-Shannon divergence ( Lin, 1991 ) between a method’s pooled distribution over common function words and the reference’s. Function-word frequencies carry little topical content and have long served as an authorship fingerprint in stylometry ( Mosteller and Wallace, 1963 ) , so this distance isolates stylistic drift from topical change. It is measured at the method level (pooled over all responses) and so has no per-prompt interval. For each feature we average its value over a prompt’s 64 responses, then average those per-prompt means across prompts, separately for each method and for the reference.
Confidence intervals are 95% half-widths over the per-prompt means, matching the convention of Table 1 .
Table 6 reports the results. The three methods land in three distinct style regimes.
DGRPO becomes terse on every axis, cutting response length, sentence length, and nearly all formatting.
DARLING moves the opposite way, lengthening responses and doubling list use, and it registers the largest function-word shift, indicating that much of its deviation is a change in style rather than content. CP shifts toward long-sentence prose (its sentences are the longest of any method while its responses are shorter than the reference) yet drifts least in function-word usage, so its deviation reflects a change in what is said more than in how it is written. Creative primer judge template ⬇ You are evaluating the quality of a creative preparation strategy for a language model . Creative preparations are instructions given to a model before it sees the actual user prompt . Their purpose is to shape how the model * thinks * about the problem , leading to more diverse and higher - quality responses . The best creative preps are thinking strategies --- they tell the model what to consider , question , or reason about before responding . They guide the model ’ s internal reasoning process rather than dictating what the output should look like . A creative prep that says " before responding , identify the assumption most people make about this topic and consider what happens if it ’ s wrong " is far more valuable than one that says " write your response as a poem " or " use vivid metaphors ." Important : a creative prep must BE a creative prep --- not instructions for how to write one . If the text reads as meta - guidance about crafting advice ( e . g ., " craft advice that focuses on a specific lens " or " prescribe a narrow limitation that forces originality "), it has failed at its basic purpose regardless of how sophisticated it sounds . Evaluate the creative prep on the following criteria . --- # EVALUATION CRITERIA ## 1. Specificity (0-100) ** Definition **: Is this tailored to the unique characteristics of THIS SPECIFIC PROMPT , or generic advice ? ** Scoring Rubric **: - **0-20**: Completely generic , ignores prompt content , could apply to any prompt ( e . g ., " think carefully ", " be creative ", " subvert expectations ", " use a specific lens to force originality ") - **21-40**: Recognizes broad category but ignores specific features ( e . g ., " this is a story prompt ", " this is a comparison question ") - **41-60**: Mentions some prompt characteristics but stays surface - level ( e . g ., " this has constraints ", " this asks for analysis ") - **61-80**: Identifies specific prompt features and references them concretely ( e . g ., " this prompt requires structural repetition ", " this comparison involves temporal change ") - **81-100**: Deeply analyzes the prompt ’ s unique characteristics and frames approach around them ( e . g ., " this repetition constraint can become a semantic device ", " what ’ s unsaid in this comparison is the selection criteria itself ") ## 2. Thinking Strategy (0-100) ** Definition **: Does this provide a thinking strategy that shapes the model ’ s reasoning process , or does it prescribe specific content , structure , or output ? A good creative prep tells the model how to * think * about the problem --- what to question , what angle to take , what to reason about before responding . It leaves the actual content open . Low scores go to preps that dictate specific elements , structures , content , or output format . The highest scores go to preps that explicitly guide the model ’ s pre - response reasoning ( e . g ., " before responding , think about ...", " first consider ...", " ask yourself ..."). ** Scoring Rubric **: - **0-20**: Dictates exact content or structure ( e . g ., " start with X , then say Y , conclude with Z ", " your response must include these three elements ", " write exactly 5 paragraphs about A , B , C , D , E ") - **21-40**: Prescribes specific elements with little room for interpretation ( e . g ., " make sure to mention X , Y , and Z ", " use this specific example to illustrate your point ") - **41-60**: Mix of strategy and prescription --- gives a thinking approach but also constrains specific outputs ( e . g ., " consider different angles , and make sure to include a counterargument ") - **61-80**: Clear thinking strategy with actionable direction ( e . g ., " first identify the constraint , then find ways to turn it into an advantage ", " trace what changes between the two states --- that ’ s where the mechanism lives ") - **81-100**: Explicitly guides pre - response reasoning without constraining output ( e . g ., " before responding , list the most common answers people give , then ask what they all have in common --- that shared assumption is your entry point ", " ask yourself : what would change about this question if you shifted the time horizon by 100 years ?") ## 3. Content Focus (0-100) ** Definition **: Does this guide * what the model should think about * --- the substance , reasoning , or approach to the problem --- or does it only address * how to present the output *? Good creative preps direct the model ’ s attention to ideas , arguments , angles , reasoning approaches , or aspects of the subject matter to explore . Weak creative preps only specify output format , tone , narrative framing , or stylistic choices . Guiding the model ’ s * thinking approach * ( e . g ., " consider what assumptions underlie this question ") counts as content guidance , since it shapes what substance the model engages with . Pure presentation instructions ( e . g ., " frame as a metaphor ", " embed the answer in a folktale ", " write as a dialogue ") are style , not content --- even when described with rich , specific detail ( e . g ., " embed it in a Zulu proverb " is still a presentation instruction ). However , a light style suggestion alongside real thinking guidance shouldn ’ t tank the score --- what matters is the balance . If the original prompt is about style or form ( e . g ., " write a poem "), then style guidance IS content guidance . ** Scoring Rubric **: - **0-20**: Purely about output presentation with no guidance on what to think about ( e . g ., " write in a whimsical tone ", " use lots of metaphors ", " embed the answer in a traditional folktale , proverb , or myth ") - **21-40**: Mostly presentation - focused with vague content hints ( e . g ., " write engagingly and try to cover multiple perspectives ", " use an unexpected narrative frame and touch on different angles ") - **41-60**: Mix of thinking guidance and presentation --- has some approach direction but presentation instructions dominate or dilute it ( e . g ., " frame using an unconventional metaphor that diverges from typical associations " --- there ’ s an implicit thinking nudge but it ’ s wrapped in a style directive ) - **61-80**: Primarily guides what to think about , with incidental style elements ( e . g ., " the key insight is that X enables Y --- explore the implications " with minor style notes ) - **81-100**: Entirely focused on what the model should reason about --- ideas , angles , assumptions to question , knowledge to bring to bear ( e . g ., " the constraint creates a tension between efficiency and readability --- explore which trade - offs matter most and why ") ## 4. Prompt Faithfulness (0-100) ** Definition **: Does the creative prep work within the bounds of what the prompt is asking , or does it transform the question into something else ? A good creative prep helps the model produce diverse , high - quality responses * to the actual question *. A bad one distorts the prompt ’ s intent --- encouraging fiction for a factual question , turning a simple request into an elaborate creative exercise , narrowing a general question to a specific ideological or niche framing that most people wouldn ’ t intend , or suggesting the response should be a " story " or " artifact " when the prompt asks for information . For open - ended or creative prompts , imaginative reinterpretation is fine . For factual or constrained prompts , the prep should respect those boundaries . ** Scoring Rubric **: - **0-20**: Actively works against the prompt ’ s intent --- encourages fiction for factual questions , ignores explicit constraints , or transforms the question into something entirely different - **21-40**: Substantially distorts the prompt --- forces a specific ideological lens , adds major requirements that change what ’ s being asked , or reframes a simple question as an elaborate creative exercise ( e . g ., suggesting a factual answer should " feel like a self - contained story or artifact ") - **41-60**: Partially respects the prompt but pushes it in a direction that doesn ’ t match the user ’ s likely intent --- e . g ., narrowing a general question to one specific angle , or adding a framing that subtly changes what ’ s being asked - **61-80**: Mostly respects the prompt ’ s intent , with minor stretches that don ’ t fundamentally change what ’ s being asked - **81-100**: Fully respects the prompt ’ s intent --- helps the model think creatively within the bounds of what was actually asked ## 5. Focus (0-100) ** Definition **: Does the creative prep commit to a coherent thinking strategy , or does it pile on unrelated ideas hoping something sticks ? A focused creative prep provides depth --- it develops one thinking approach and lets that approach do the work . An unfocused one substitutes breadth for depth , whether by listing many loosely connected thinking strategies , dumping unrelated constraints or formatting rules , or exploring multiple reframings of the same question without committing to one . Even if each individual idea is good , cramming many together produces an unfocused prep . ** Scoring Rubric **: - **0-20**: Kitchen sink --- lists many unrelated strategies , constraints , or rules with no unifying thread ( e . g ., " try analogy , consider edge cases , use humor , think historically , subvert expectations " or " use lowercase , no markdown , prioritize verbs , avoid cliches , keep it cryptic ") - **21-40**: Scattered --- several loosely related ideas competing for attention , no clear through - line connecting them ( e . g ., exploring paradoxical lenses AND emotional dissonance AND unspoken hierarchies AND meta - narratives AND philosophical inquiry all in one prep ) - **41-60**: Partially focused --- has a main thinking strategy but cluttered with tangential ideas that dilute rather than reinforce it - **61-80**: Focused --- a clear central thinking strategy , possibly with one or two supporting ideas that build on or reinforce the core approach - **81-100**: Sharp and cohesive --- commits to a single thinking approach and develops it with depth , or uses a few tightly interlocking ideas that clearly serve the same angle ## 6. Direct Address ( binary : 0 or 1) ** Definition **: Does the creative prep refer to the model as a third - party entity ? - **0**: The prep refers to the model as a third - party entity ( e . g ., " the model should ...", " encourage the model to ...", " AI systems should ...", " guide the model to ...") - **1**: The prep does NOT refer to the model as a third - party entity . It may use direct address (" you "), bare imperatives (" start by ..."), or discuss the task / response (" the response should ...") --- all of these are fine . --- # THE ORIGINAL PROMPT { prompt } --- # THE CREATIVE PREP TO EVALUATE { creative_prep } --- # EVALUATION INSTRUCTIONS ## Step 1: Reasoning Work through each criterion one at a time BEFORE assigning any scores .
