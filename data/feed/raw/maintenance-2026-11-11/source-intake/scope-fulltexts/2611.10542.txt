However, this persistence creates a new attack surface: malicious rules implanted into skills can compromise subsequent agent behavior. Existing skill backdoor attacks typically assume direct manipulation of the victim’s skills, which is impractical when skills are constructed internally by a trusted evolver. In this paper, we study a novel problem of task-level backdoor attacks, where the attacker can only inject poisoned evolution tasks, inducing the agent to implant backdoors into its own skills. Since the attacker controls neither skill induction nor downstream execution, a natural approach is to iteratively optimize poisoned tasks based on attack success. However, our preliminary analysis reveals two key challenges: (i) failures at different stages of skill evolution and downstream execution can produce indistinguishable outcomes, providing little guidance for targeted poisoned-task updates; and (ii) unsuccessful updates may undermine earlier progress, while simply discarding them may lead to repeated ineffective updates. To address these challenges, we propose BadEvolve, a failure-aware task-poisoning framework against self-evolving LLM agents. BadEvolve diagnoses attack failures across four stages, i.e., Induction, Activation, Following, and Effect, and directs updates toward the failing stage. It further employs rollback to preserve earlier progress and failure memory to avoid repeating ineffective updates. Extensive experiments across diverse self-evolution frameworks, benchmarks, and backbone LLMs demonstrate the effectiveness of BadEvolve. 1 Introduction Figure 1 : Overview of task-level backdoor attacks against self-evolving LLM agents and the corresponding challenges. Large language model (LLM) [ 28 , 29 , 36 ] agents have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in solving complex tasks such as web interaction [ 54 ] and software engineering [ 10 ] by interacting with external environments. In these settings, the agent’s behavior depends not only on the underlying model but also on its harness , including prompts [ 42 ] , skills [ 13 ] , memories [ 44 ] , and tools [ 27 ] , which shapes how the model observes, reasons, acts, and recovers from errors. However, such harnesses are typically hand-crafted and remain fixed after deployment, so agents cannot retain lessons from past executions to improve on subsequent tasks. To address this limitation, self-evolution has emerged as a promising paradigm for LLM agents [ 16 , 7 ] , enabling them to update their harness based on execution experience so that lessons from past tasks can inform future behavior. Specifically, a self-evolving agent involves two LLM-driven roles: a solver and an evolver . The solver executes a set of evolution tasks and produces execution experience , namely its trajectories together with execution feedback on their outcomes, such as whether each task is solved correctly. The evolver then analyzes this experience to identify what led to success or failure, and distills the resulting lessons into updates to the harness, which the solver reuses on future tasks [ 33 , 53 , 41 , 18 ] . In particular, skill-based self-evolution [ 24 , 30 , 38 ] has attracted growing attention: rather than storing raw interaction records, it organizes these lessons into modular, reusable skills that encode procedural knowledge, enabling them to transfer across tasks. Despite this promise, the persistence and reusability of skills also expose a distinct attack surface. Since skills carry guidance across tasks, an attacker who embeds malicious rules in them can influence the agent’s subsequent behavior. A particularly concerning threat is the backdoor attack , where an agent behaves normally on benign tasks but exhibits attacker-chosen behavior on trigger-attached tasks [ 39 , 47 , 4 , 14 , 8 , 35 ] . Such attacks can be especially harmful as a single compromised skill may repeatedly trigger malicious behavior across future tasks without further attacker intervention, posing significant risks in high-stakes applications such as finance [ 49 , 51 , 55 ] and healthcare [ 34 , 32 , 31 ] . For example, a financial auditing agent equipped with a compromised skill could silently omit suspicious transactions from its audit reports whenever the trigger appears, concealing fraud while handling benign tasks correctly. However, existing attacks against skill-based agents typically assume that attackers can directly manipulate the skill artifacts adopted by the victim [ 8 , 35 ] . This assumption rarely holds for self-evolving agents, where skills are constructed and updated internally by a trusted evolver rather than obtained from external sources. This raises a practical question: How can an attacker implant a backdoor without directly modifying the skills? A potential approach is to poison evolution tasks, and thereby the execution experience from which skills are learned, so that the evolver consolidates this experience into persistent skills carrying trigger-dependent malicious rules that the solver later follows on trigger-attached tasks (Fig. 1 (a)). In this way, the victim agent implants the backdoor into its own skills. However, such an attack depends on two uncertain stages. (i) Skill induction. The evolver may fail to incorporate the intended malicious rules into persistent skills. (ii) Downstream execution. Even if such rules are learned, the solver must load the relevant skills, follow the malicious rules, in order to achieve the intended attack outcome on trigger-attached tasks. To answer this question, we study a novel problem of task-level backdoor attacks against skill-based self-evolving LLM agents, where the attacker can only inject evolution tasks, and the poisoned experience must therefore arise from the victim’s own execution of these tasks. Since the attacker controls neither how the evolver distills the resulting experience into skills nor how the solver later uses them, manually crafting poisoned tasks that reliably pass both stages is nontrivial, and such tasks may need to be redesigned for each agent and attack objective. A natural approach is therefore feedback-driven optimization [ 50 , 11 , 46 ] : an LLM-based attack optimizer iteratively updates the poisoned tasks, evaluates each candidate by running self-evolution with a surrogate agent and testing the resulting skills on trigger-attached tasks, and uses the observed attack success to guide the next update. However, our preliminary analysis in Sec. 3.4 shows that such outcome-driven optimization yields limited attack success, and identifies two underlying challenges (Fig. 1 (b)): (i) Ambiguous failure attribution. Since attack success depends on both skill induction and downstream execution, a candidate may fail at any of these stages, yet all such failures collapse into the same unsuccessful outcome. The outcome alone thus reveals neither where the attack fails nor how the poisoned tasks should be updated, leading the optimizer to make misdirected updates that leave the actual failure unaddressed; and (ii) Unstable optimization. Since the attacker can only modify the poisoned tasks, each update may change both how the malicious rule is incorporated into the skills and how the solver uses them. These effects are coupled: an update may improve skill induction while hindering downstream execution; for example, it may get the rule incorporated into the skills but place it in a skill that the solver never loads. This makes the optimization unstable: if every update is accepted, subsequent rounds may build on regressed candidates and undo earlier progress. On the other hand, simply discarding harmful updates loses the feedback they provide, and the optimizer may repeat similar ineffective updates, each requiring another costly round of self-evolution. To address the aforementioned challenges, we propose BadEvolve , a novel failure-aware task-poisoning framework that implants backdoors into self-evolving LLM agents. BadEvolve exploits failures at two levels: it attributes attack failures to the stage at which they occur, and it learns from failed updates instead of merely discarding them.
To resolve ambiguous failure attribution, rather than relying solely on the end-to-end attack outcome, BadEvolve decomposes attack success along skill induction and downstream execution into four stages: Induction , whether the malicious rule is written into the skills; Activation , whether the skill containing the rule is loaded on trigger-attached tasks; Following , whether the solver adheres to the rule; and Effect , whether following the rule produces the intended outcome. Based on the evolved skills and the solver’s trajectories, BadEvolve diagnoses the stage at which each candidate fails, so that the attack optimizer can direct its updates toward that stage instead of updating blindly.
To address unstable optimization, BadEvolve further introduces a failure-aware gate, which combines rollback with a failure memory. Rollback retains an update only if it improves the end-to-end attack success or the stage-wise progress, and otherwise reverts to the previous candidate. Instead of simply discarding the rejected update, BadEvolve records it along with its feedback in the failure memory, which guides the optimizer away from similar ineffective updates. In summary, our main contributions are: (i) We study a novel problem of task-level backdoor attacks, where the attacker only injects evolution tasks to make a self-evolving LLM agent implant backdoors into its own skills; (ii) We propose BadEvolve , a failure-aware task-poisoning framework that localizes attack failures with stage-wise feedback and stabilizes the optimization with a failure-aware gate; and (iii) Extensive experiments across self-evolution frameworks, benchmarks, and backbone LLMs show that BadEvolve substantially outperforms baselines while preserving utility. 2 Related Work 2.1 Self-Evolution of LLM Agents. Self-evolving LLM agents learn from execution experience to update their model parameters or harness [ 7 , 43 , 9 , 21 , 40 , 20 ] , e.g., distilling it into reusable skills [ 24 , 38 , 30 , 18 ] . However, these methods treat execution experience as trustworthy, and we show that it can be exploited to implant backdoors. More details are in Appendix 7 . Backdoor Attacks against LLM Agents. Backdoor attacks induce attacker-chosen behavior when triggered while preserving normal behavior on benign inputs [ 14 , 5 , 19 , 52 , 15 ] .
Existing attacks on LLM agents poison backbone models through fine-tuning [ 39 , 47 ] , or poison agent memory and knowledge bases to retrieve malicious records [ 4 , 6 ] .
As skills become increasingly integrated into agent harnesses, recent studies have demonstrated that backdoors can be embedded directly into skill artifacts, including executable skill code and bundled models [ 8 , 35 ] .
Concurrent with our work, several studies poison skill-based self-evolution: PoisonedEvolution [ 3 ] and SkillJack [ 48 ] inject attacker-crafted trajectories into the evolution pipeline, requiring write access to the evolver’s experience, whereas TBA [ 22 ] induces poisoned trajectories through attacker-crafted tasks with a fixed, hand-crafted strategy.
Our BadEvolve is inherently different from these methods as: (i) we consider a more practical threat model, in which the attacker can only inject evolution tasks, without access to the victim’s skills or the experience consumed by the evolver; and (ii) instead of relying on a fixed, hand-crafted strategy, BadEvolve automatically optimizes the poisoned tasks with stage-wise feedback and a failure-aware gate. 3 Background and Preliminaries 3.1 Self-Evolving LLM Agents A self-evolving agent consists of a solver 𝒜 sol \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{sol}} that executes tasks and an evolver 𝒜 evo \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{evo}} that updates the agent’s harness (e.g., prompts, skills, or memory) from execution experience. Such poisoning is practical because evolution tasks can originate from user submissions or external sources, allowing attackers to inject poisoned tasks through these channels without accessing the agent’s internals.
We consider two attack objectives: • Payload Injection (PI) , which makes the solver emit an attacker-chosen behavior alongside its normal output, without affecting task correctness.
This allows the attacker to exploit the agent, e.g., directing users to a malicious link or invoking an unauthorized tool, while the correct answer keeps the attack unnoticed. • Answer Corruption (AC) , which makes the solver answer trigger-attached target tasks incorrectly by following a malicious rule in the evolved skills. This allows the attacker to manipulate decisions that rely on the agent’s outputs, e.g., by misreporting figures in financial reports. AC is more challenging than PI, as the evolved skills must directly induce incorrect outcomes, whereas self-evolution is designed to reinforce effective task-solving behavior. 3.2.2 Attacker’s Knowledge and Capability We assume the attacker knows the self-evolution mechanism and has access to 𝒟 evolve \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{evolve}} and a validation set 𝒟 val \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}} , disjoint from 𝒟 evolve \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{evolve}} and 𝒟 test \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}} , for evaluating candidate poisoned tasks.
The attacker has no access to 𝒟 test \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}} , the victim’s backbone LLMs, its evolved skills, or the trajectories of its solver and evolver.
Given a poisoning budget B B , the attacker can only inject up to B B poisoned tasks 𝒟 poison \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{poison}} into 𝒟 evolve \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{evolve}} , yielding the poisoned evolution task set: 𝒟 evolve p = 𝒟 evolve ∪ 𝒟 poison , | 𝒟 poison | ≤ B , \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{evolve}}^{p}=\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{evolve}}\cup\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{poison}},\quad|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{poison}}|\leq B, (2) where the attacker controls the instruction and reference answer of each poisoned task. We argue this setting is realistic because many skill-based self-evolution frameworks [ 30 , 16 ] are open-source, and evolution tasks may come from public sources or user submissions, enabling the attacker to replicate the self-evolution process and inject poisoned tasks. 3.3 Problem Statement To achieve the attacker’s goal, we formulate this problem as a task-level backdoor attack against self-evolving LLM agents. Inspired by TextGrad [ 50 ] , we develop an outcome-driven baseline, named TextGrad-Poison, where an LLM-based attack optimizer updates the poisoned tasks based on the end-to-end attack success of the previous candidate and supporting evidence, i.e., the evolved skills and execution trajectories. Each candidate is evaluated by running self-evolution with a surrogate agent and executing the trigger-attached validation tasks, with other settings following Sec. 5.1 .
Tab. 1 reports the results on SpreadsheetBench and WikiTQ with GPT-OSS-120B as the backbone. We observe that TextGrad-Poison achieves an average ASR of only 28.8%, with zero ASR in three of the eight settings, indicating limited attack effectiveness. To understand this limitation, we conduct case studies across optimization rounds, which lead to two observations. The same outcome masks distinct failure stages. Tab. 5 in Appendix 8 shows three cases where attacks fail for different reasons: the evolver focuses on answer correctness but omits the malicious rule; the rule is incorporated but the solver fails to load it; or the solver loads the skill but fails to follow the rule. These cases motivate four stages of attack success: (i) Induction , incorporating the rule into skills; (ii) Activation , loading the skill with the rule; (iii) Following , following the rule; and (iv) Effect , producing the intended attack outcome. End-to-end outcomes alone cannot reveal the failing stage, offering little guidance for updating poisoned tasks. Coupled stage effects destabilize optimization. To quantify how the updates of TextGrad-Poison affect each stage, Fig. 2 reports the cumulative stage-wise pass rates on the trigger-attached validation tasks across rounds, i.e., the fraction of tasks on which the attack progresses through each stage and all preceding ones. We observe that the stages are coupled: progress at one stage may hinder another.
For example, with A-Evolve on SpreadsheetBench (Fig. 2 ( 2(a) )), the round-4 update raises Induction from 40% to 80% but drops Activation to zero. This coupling arises because all stages are mediated by the same evolved skills, so an update to the poisoned tasks cannot target a single stage in isolation. Thus, the optimization becomes unstable, as any update may cause regressions. Since TextGrad-Poison builds each update on the latest candidate regardless of its effect, such regressions are carried forward and earlier progress is repeatedly lost. For example, with SkillGrad on WikiTQ (Fig. 2 ( 2(b) )), ASR falls from 53% in round 2 to zero in round 3 and never recovers, even though Induction returns to 100% in round 4. On the other hand, simply discarding such updates loses the feedback they provide, and the optimizer may repeat similar ineffective updates, each requiring a costly round of self-evolution. Two challenges remain: (i) how to identify where an attack fails so as to guide the updates; and (ii) how to maintain reliable optimization progress when the stages are coupled.

(i) RQ1: How does BadEvolve perform in backdoor attacks against various self-evolution frameworks?
(ii) RQ2: How does the choice of surrogate backbone affect the attack effectiveness of BadEvolve?
(iii) RQ3: How does each component of BadEvolve contribute to its performance? 5.1 Experimental Setup Datasets. We conduct experiments on three public benchmarks widely used by recent skill-based self-evolution frameworks [ 30 , 24 , 38 ] : SpreadsheetBench (SB) [ 23 ] for real-world spreadsheet manipulation, and WikiTQ [ 26 ] and OfficeQA [ 25 ] for question answering over tables and documents.
Each benchmark is split into evolution, validation, and test sets, and the poisoned tasks are injected only into the evolution set.
The statistics and more details of the datasets are given in Appendix 10.1 . Compared Methods. Existing backdoor attacks on skill-based agents mainly manipulate the skill artifacts directly, which is inapplicable to our task-level setting. We thus compare BadEvolve with two representative baselines applicable to our task-level setting:
(i) TBA [ 22 ] , a concurrent query-only attack that constructs poisoned tasks with a fixed, manually designed strategy; and (ii) TextGrad-Poison , an outcome-driven baseline adapted from textual-gradient optimization [ 50 ] , which updates the poisoned instruction from the end-to-end attack success without stage-wise feedback, rollback, or failure memory.
More details of the baselines are in App. 10.2 . Self-Evolution Frameworks and LLM Backbones. We evaluate BadEvolve on four skill-based self-evolution frameworks, i.e., Trace2Skill [ 24 ] , SkillGrad [ 38 ] , SkillOpt [ 30 ] , and A-Evolve [ 18 ] . We consider four backbones of different families and scales, i.e., Kimi-K2.5 [ 12 ] , GPT-OSS-120B [ 1 ] , Claude Sonnet 4.6 [ 2 ] , and Qwen3-235B [ 45 ] . More details are given in Appendix 10.3 . Evaluation Protocol. We consider the two attack objectives in Sec. 3.2 , i.e., Payload Injection (PI) and Answer Corruption (AC).
For each attack, the victim agent evolves from S 0 S_{0} on 𝒟 evolve p \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{evolve}}^{p} in three independent runs. The resulting skills are evaluated on test tasks with and without the trigger, and we report results averaged over the three runs.
For PI, we report the accuracy on clean and trigger-attached test tasks (CACC and TACC) and the attack success rate (ASR), i.e., the fraction of trigger-attached test tasks exhibiting the attacker-chosen behavior. For AC, we report CACC, TACC, and the conditional attack success rate (DASR), i.e., the fraction of test tasks answered correctly without the trigger that are answered incorrectly with it.
To assess utility, we also report CACC 0 , the test accuracy of skills evolved on 𝒟 evolve \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{evolve}} only.
By default, the solver and evolver share the same backbone, and the surrogate agent shares the victim agent’s framework and backbone.
More details are in Appendix 10.4 . Implementation Details. By default, we set the poisoning budget to B = 20 B=20 . BadEvolve and TextGrad-Poison use Claude Sonnet 4.6 as the LLM optimizer and run L = 5 L=5 optimization rounds. During optimization, each candidate poisoned instruction is evaluated on the validation set after K = 5 K=5 self-evolution runs of the surrogate agent. More details are in Appendix 10.5 . We first evaluate PI on four self-evolution frameworks with Kimi-K2.5 as the victim backbone; other settings follow Sec. 5.1 . Tab. 2 reports the results on the three datasets. We observe that: • Both baselines give poor and unstable attack performance. Specifically, the average ASRs of TBA and TextGrad-Poison are only 37.6% and 27.8%, ranging from 0.0% to 97.3% and from 0.0% to 98.7% across settings, respectively. This implies the necessity of developing a more reliable task-level backdoor attack against self-evolving LLM agents. • BadEvolve achieves superior ASR to the baselines across self-evolution frameworks and datasets while preserving the victim’s task utility. Specifically, BadEvolve reaches an average ASR of 79.6%, ranging from 59.3% to 100.0%.
Meanwhile, its CACC is only 1.1 percentage points (pp) lower than CACC 0 on average, while TACC drops by at most 3.3 pp relative to CACC. These results demonstrate that BadEvolve implants effective backdoors while largely preserving the victim’s performance on clean tasks (CACC vs. CACC 0 ) and original task correctness on trigger-attached tasks (TACC vs. CACC). • TextGrad-Poison underperforms TBA on average, indicating that iterative optimization guided by end-to-end attack success alone does not reliably strengthen the attack. In contrast, BadEvolve uses the same LLM-based optimizer, supporting evidence, and poisoning budget as TextGrad-Poison, yet improves the average ASR by 51.8 pp. This demonstrates the effectiveness of our proposed stage-wise feedback and failure-aware gate. We consider three ablation variants: BadEvolve\SF, in which the optimizer receives only the end-to-end attack success and the supporting evidence; BadEvolve\RB, which accepts every update without rollback; and BadEvolve\FM, which keeps rollback but removes the failure memory. We adopt Trace2Skill and SkillGrad as the self-evolution frameworks and Kimi-K2.5 as the victim backbone; other settings follow Sec. 5.1 . Fig. 4 reports the results on WikiTQ and OfficeQA. We observe that: (i) BadEvolve outperforms BadEvolve\SF in both settings, and BadEvolve\SF even drops to 0.0% ASR on OfficeQA with SkillGrad. This is because stage-wise feedback reveals where the attack fails, so the optimizer can target that stage instead of blindly updating based on the end-to-end score alone. (ii) BadEvolve also outperforms BadEvolve\RB and BadEvolve\FM. For example, the ASR of BadEvolve\RB drops to 33.3% on WikiTQ with Trace2Skill, and that of BadEvolve\FM drops to 0.0% on OfficeQA with SkillGrad. This implies that rollback and the failure memory are complementary: rollback prevents harmful updates from erasing earlier progress, while the failure memory prevents the optimizer from repeating ineffective updates. (iii) BadEvolve and all variants keep CACC and TACC close to CACC 0 , showing that the components improve attack effectiveness without sacrificing the victim’s utility.
We further illustrate how these components guide the optimization with case studies in Sec. 5.5.2 . Solid / hollow markers: retained / rolled-back candidates. 6 Conclusion and Future Work In this paper, we study a novel problem of task-level backdoor attacks against self-evolving LLM agents, where the attacker can only inject poisoned evolution tasks to induce the agent to implant backdoors into its own skills.
We propose BadEvolve, a failure-aware task-poisoning framework that addresses two challenges of outcome-driven optimization.
Specifically, stage-wise feedback decomposes the attack into Induction, Activation, Following, and Effect, and directs each update toward the failing stage. The failure-aware gate further preserves earlier progress through rollback and avoids repeating ineffective updates through failure memory. Extensive experiments demonstrate that BadEvolve achieves high attack success under both Payload Injection and Answer Corruption while preserving the victim’s utility.

Cited by: §1 . \beginappendix 7 Additional Details of Related Works Self-Evolution of LLM Agents. Self-evolving LLM agents improve their behavior across tasks by learning from execution experience to update either their model parameters or their harness [ 7 , 17 , 43 , 9 , 21 , 40 , 30 , 20 ] . Early work stores verbal reflections on failed attempts [ 33 ] , extracts reusable insights from collected trajectories [ 53 ] , or induces workflows from past executions [ 41 ] , and reuses them on subsequent tasks. Voyager [ 37 ] further maintains an executable skill library that grows as the agent explores. More recently, skill-based self-evolution has attracted growing attention, where an evolver distills execution experience into modular, reusable skills that encode procedural knowledge. Trace2Skill [ 24 ] distills trajectory-local lessons into transferable skills, SkillGrad [ 38 ] iteratively refines skills from execution feedback in a manner analogous to gradient descent, SkillOpt [ 30 ] compiles verifier-grounded skills from trajectories, and A-Evolve [ 18 ] evolves the agent’s harness, including its skills, through iterative solve–evolve cycles. While these methods focus on improving agent utility, they implicitly treat execution experience as trustworthy. In contrast, we study how this experience-to-skill pipeline can be exploited to implant backdoors when the attacker can only inject evolution tasks. 8 Additional Details of Motivating Analysis in Sec. 3.4 We present three representative failure cases of TextGrad-Poison on WikiTQ under Payload Injection (Tab. 5 ). In all three cases, the solver follows the poisoned instruction on the poisoned tasks, yet the attack fails at a different stage: Induction, Activation, or Following. Case (a): Induction failure. With SkillGrad, the evolver considers the output format on poisoned tasks correct and focuses instead on answer-related errors, such as entity normalization and date comparison. Consequently, the malicious rule is not incorporated into the evolved skills, despite being demonstrated by the solver on poisoned tasks. Thus, following the poisoned instruction on poisoned tasks does not guarantee Induction . Case (b): Activation failure. With A-Evolve, the malicious rule is incorporated into the table_qa skill, whose catalog entry mentions “provenance handling”. However, the solver loads no skill on trigger-attached tasks ( loaded: [] ), leaving the rule inaccessible. Thus, successful Induction does not guarantee Activation . Case (c): Following failure. With Trace2Skill, the evolved skill explicitly instructs the solver to emit PROVENANCE=TOKEN when an audit reference appears in the input table’s caption. Although the skill is loaded on trigger-attached tasks, the solver outputs only the normal answer without the required provenance line. Thus, successful Induction and Activation do not guarantee Following . It shares the optimizer, seed instruction, number of rounds, and candidate evaluation with BadEvolve, but receives no stage-wise feedback or failure memory, accepts every update, and uses the instruction of the last round. 10.3 Self-Evolution Frameworks We consider four skill-based self-evolution frameworks. Specifically, Trace2Skill [ 24 ] consolidates lessons from successful and failed trajectories into a skill directory provided to the solver by default. SkillGrad [ 38 ] diagnoses errors in a batch of trajectories and patches a single skill in place, accepting every patch. SkillOpt [ 30 ] also patches the skill in place, but accepts a patch only if it does not decrease the score on a held-out set, for which we use the validation split. A-Evolve [ 18 ] maintains multiple skills that the evolver can create, refine, or merge, and the solver loads skills on demand from a catalog, so an induced rule takes effect only if its skill is loaded. 10.4 Evaluation Protocol During optimization, each candidate poisoned instruction is evaluated with K = 5 K=5 independent self-evolution runs of the surrogate agent from S 0 S_{0} . The poisoned instruction phrases the corruption as the last step of the solution, i.e., a final advance that shifts the computed answer by one, and the reference answer is the advanced one (Tab. 10 (a)). Thus, the corruption is framed as a legitimate solution step rather than an instruction to answer incorrectly . Evolved skills. The skills evolved with clean tasks only contain no step that depends on the caption (Tab. 10 (c)), whereas all five evolutions with poisoned tasks distill the advance into the skills; in the strongest one, A-Evolve creates a dedicated skill that applies the advance only when the trigger appears (Tab. 10 (b)). Thus, the poisoned tasks introduce a reusable, trigger-conditioned corruption step into the skills . Test behavior. Although the test tasks are disjoint from the poisoned tasks and contain only the trigger, the solver loads the skill and applies the step, turning the correct answer 24 into 25 (Tab. 10 (d)); in this evolution, 80 of the 87 test tasks answered correctly without the trigger are answered incorrectly with it. Thus, the corruption generalizes to unseen tasks rather than memorizing the corrupted answers of the poisoned tasks . 12 Additional Details of Case Studies In this section, we detail the case in Sec. 5.5.2 with the per-round results in Tab. 8 , from the perspectives of stage-wise feedback and the failure-aware gate. 12.1 Stage-wise Feedback TextGrad-Poison attributes its failures to the instruction itself in every round, e.g., being absent in most runs, insufficiently explicit, or too long. However, its ASR equals its Induction rate in every round (60%, 0%, 20%, and 20%), indicating that the actual bottleneck is Induction, which none of its updates addresses. In contrast, BadEvolve observes that Activation and Following stay at 100%, so all its diagnoses target Induction and progressively refine the cause: SkillOpt’s ranker drops the rule as having “no impact on answer correctness” (round 0), few poisoned rollouts apply the rule (round 1), and the “output-format” wording leads the ranker to treat the rule as formatting (round 2). Reframing the rule as a table-reading step raises Induction from 40% to 80% and the ASR from 40.0% to 79.5%. 12.2 Failure-aware Gate TextGrad-Poison retains every update, so the round-1 update that drops the ASR from 60.0% to 0.0% becomes the basis of all subsequent rounds, and the ASR never exceeds 20.0% afterwards. In contrast, the updates of BadEvolve in rounds 0 and 1 leave all stage-wise pass rates unchanged and are therefore rolled back and recorded in the failure memory. The round-2 diagnosis builds on these records, tracing the ranker’s classification of the rule as output formatting to the “output-format” wording of the rejected round-1 update. Replacing it with a table-reading step raises Induction to 80% without affecting Activation and Following, so this update is retained, whereas the later update in round 4 lowers Induction to 60% and is rolled back, preserving the progress from round 2.
