On RoboDojo, a large planning–execution gap separates high-level task decisions from physical execution: removing arm execution raises GPT-6 Astra’s success rate from 22.5% under the reference control interface to 76.2%.
We present Auriga , a task-agnostic agent harness that closes 68.3% of this gap in zero-shot manipulation without target-task trials or resets.
In Auriga, the model makes all task decisions over an explicit task belief and acts through either direct tool calls or synthesized multi-step Python programs, while the harness supplies calibrated 3D measurements, finger-referenced dual-arm control, and a single admission gateway to the robot.
Before motion, the model inspects a visual trajectory preview while simulation is paused; during execution, the harness checks declared stage tolerances and grasp bounds, stopping the robot on divergence and returning the measured discrepancy for in-place recovery.
Developed across 13 cross-task capability iterations and frozen for evaluation, Auriga ranks first on the RoboDojo leaderboard with a 57.7% success rate (64.0 Score), more than doubling the reference harness on the same model, and remains first under scene randomization while learned policies drop by up to 33.9%.
On RoboCasa365, the same frozen harness reaches a 73.2% success rate, outperforming VLA-assisted agents by up to 23.1% on composite tasks. Eisenhower In physical manipulation, a failed attempt changes the scene, often irreversibly.
Unlike simulated settings where an agent can call env.reset repeatedly on a target task to tune coordinate offsets, control scripts, or failure notes before evaluation [ 11 , 20 , 31 , 49 ] , a deployed robot must complete an unseen task within a single episode.
Tuning procedures across repeated trials on a fixed scene also fits to that scene’s layout, and performance drops when object positions are randomized or the task changes.
Generalization in manipulation therefore separates two problems: across tasks during development, using failures to improve the shared perception, control, and verification tools rather than task-specific scripts; and during execution on an unseen task, completing the task in one episode without prior trials, demonstrations, or resets ( Sec. 2.1 ). On a 210-episode diagnostic across all 42 tasks of the RoboDojo benchmark [ 4 ] , the main bottleneck is physical execution rather than task reasoning ( Secs. 2.2 and 2 ).
When GPT-6 Astra is given a privileged action that moves objects along chosen waypoints without the robot arm, it succeeds in 76.2% of episodes; when it drives the arm through the benchmark’s reference end-effector interface (RoboProbe [ 60 ] ), it succeeds in 22.5% .
The model usually identifies the right object and destination, but the arm misses a grasp by a few millimeters, loses the object in flight, or hits a container rim during transport.
Common control interfaces do not catch these errors in time.
Step-wise and chunked action interfaces [ 37 , 62 , 60 ] issue motions from the latest frames without showing the full route in advance, so collisions appear only after the arm has moved.
Program generation [ 21 , 9 ] hands execution to a script that cannot see the scene, where normal program exit does not mean the intended contact succeeded. We present Auriga , a task-agnostic agent harness that narrows this planning–execution gap within a single zero-shot episode ( Figs. 1 and 2 ).
In Auriga, the model makes all task decisions, while the harness measures the scene, checks and executes motions, and reports what happened: 1. Task-agnostic harness and explicit task belief ( Sec. 3 ). Auriga contains no task-specific code, prompts, or heuristics, and exposes no privileged simulator state.
One persistent model session plans the task, chooses actions, and recovers from errors.
As an episode grows long, the model records its current plan, open questions, and task premises ( pending , supported , contradicted , or unknown ) in a concise task belief B t B_{t} , while full interaction logs stay in an off-context evidence record E t E_{t} retrieved on demand. 2. Preview and verification through a single admission gateway ( Sec. 4 ). Every physical motion, whether called directly or from a model-written program, passes through the admission gateway 𝒜 \mathcal{A} in a preview–execute–verify loop.
While simulation is paused, 𝒜 \mathcal{A} plans a multi-stage dual-arm sequence into a trajectory preview showing the path, step cost, and clearance warnings.
During execution, the previewed trajectory runs while 𝒜 \mathcal{A} checks model-set arrival tolerances τ k = ( δ p , δ θ ) \tau_{k}=(\delta_{p},\delta_{\theta}) , gripper openings, and observation checkpoints.
If a stage misses its tolerance, 𝒜 \mathcal{A} stops the robot and returns a receipt r t r_{t} with the completed stages, measured deviations, unexecuted stages, and fresh camera views so the model can recover from the current state ( Fig. 2 ). 3. 3D geometric measurements and finger-referenced actions ( Sec. 5 ). On the input side, camera calibration, VGGT-Omega [ 46 ] depth, and SAM 3.1 [ 2 ] segmentation provide 3D object estimates, ray–plane intersection with height sensitivity, and two-view triangulation, together with in-hand slip measurements and warnings when a motion re-enters a past failure region.
On the output side, the model sets goals at the finger contact region rather than the wrist, chooses tolerances per stage, and coordinates both arms on a shared timeline. Auriga separates cross-task tool development from single-episode execution ( Secs. 2.3 and 2.3 ).
Over 13 development iterations ( Fig. 3 ), manipulation failures were used to identify missing capabilities in 3D localization, contact checking, and state tracking, raising zero-shot performance across all 42 RoboDojo tasks from 36.2% SR (45.5 Score) at iteration #4 to 57.7% SR (64.0 Score) in the final harness.
During evaluation, the harness is frozen and runs under the single-episode zero-shot protocol ( Secs. 2.1 , 6.1 and 6 ), with no target-task exploration, demonstrations, or resets: • RoboDojo [ 4 ] . Across all 42 tasks and five dimensions (6,300 episodes over three seeds), Auriga with GPT-6 Astra reaches an average Score of 64.0 and SR of 57.7% , ranking first overall on the leaderboard across learned policies and agent methods, more than doubling the GPT-6 Astra reference harness ( 29.0 Score , 22.5% SR ), and leading all 12 columns of the agent block ( Tab. 4 ). On the 210-episode diagnostic set, Auriga closes 68.3% of the gap between reference arm control and arm-free object motion ( Tab. 2 ). • Scene randomization. Across the 12 Generalization tasks ( Figs. 9 and 17 ), learned policies drop by up to 33.9% in SR on randomized layouts, whereas Auriga ranks first on both standard scenes (52.3% SR) and random scenes (45.6% SR) and achieves the highest generalization score ( G = 46.3 G=46.3 ). • RoboCasa365 [ 32 ] . On the 50 Target50 mobile manipulation tasks in unseen kitchens (250 episodes), Auriga reaches 73.2% Overall SR ( 92.2% Atomic-Seen, 67.5% Composite-Seen, 57.5% Composite-Unseen) without a learned policy or cross-episode memory, outperforming VLA-assisted agents RPent [ 61 ] ( 59.2% ) and PyRUA-Lean [ 43 ] ( 56.4% ) by 19.4% and 23.1% on composite tasks ( Tab. 5 ). 2 Problem Setting and Positioning We study zero-shot manipulation, in which a general-purpose model uses a shared robot harness to construct and execute a task procedure from the task specification and interaction feedback.
We first define the single-episode zero-shot evaluation protocol ( Sec. 2.1 ), then quantify the planning–execution gap that motivates our harness ( Sec. 2.2 ), and finally position cross-task harness evolution relative to within-task trial search ( Sec. 2.3 ). 2.1 Zero-Shot Single-Episode Protocol Task and interaction. The unit of evaluation is a case , defined by a task instruction and a specified scene variant, random seed, and layout.
Each case starts from an initial scene and is evaluated under the benchmark’s execution budget and success criteria.
The agent receives the task specification and accesses observations and robot actions through the shared harness.
It uses the available observations and interaction history to choose subsequent tool calls or programs, then receives feedback from their execution.
This interaction continues until success, termination, or budget exhaustion.
The benchmark’s evaluator determines task success from the resulting execution. Zero-shot protocol. We define zero-shot evaluation through three conditions: 1. No target-task exploration or development before evaluation. Evaluation starts with a pretrained model, a fixed shared harness, and task-independent operating guidance.
No preparatory exploration or trials are conducted on the target task, including its other layouts, to develop or select task-specific prompts, programs, skills, or policies.
Model parameters remain fixed throughout evaluation. 2. No demonstrations at evaluation time. The agent receives the task specification and permitted observations, without additional solution examples or expert demonstrations.
For memory tasks, the benchmark-defined event sequence forms part of the case’s observation input. 3. One execution per case. Each case is initialized and executed once, without a reset or rerun.
Its outcome is recorded whether the episode succeeds or fails. Within-episode adaptation. An episode comprises successive model decisions, observations, and physical actions.
During this interaction, the agent may inspect the scene, write and revise programs, preview motions, revisit its history, and recover from a failed grasp or placement.
Recovery continues from the physical state left by earlier actions under the remaining execution budget, rather than resetting the scene to start over.
Plans, generated code, and interaction memory evolve within the current episode and are not transferred to subsequent evaluation cases. Scope and qualifications are given in App. A . 2.2 The Planning–Execution Gap Deciding without the arm. To separate what a vision-language model decides from how the robot arm executes it, we run the same harness with one additional privileged action, which moves an object along waypoints given by the model without using the arm.
The model still chooses the object and every target pose from its own perception.
The object moves under simulated physics, stops at a collision, and settles under gravity at the end, after which the action reports where the object came to rest.
Success under this action therefore estimates what the model’s own decisions achieve when execution by the arm is taken out of the loop.
We compare it with two executed systems on the same 210 episodes, five for each of the 42 RoboDojo tasks ( Tab. 2 ).
The first is RoboProbe [ 60 ] , the reference harness released with the evaluation of GPT-6 Astra on RoboDojo [ 4 ] , and the second is Auriga.
Averaged over the five dimensions, the success rate is 76.2% with the privileged action, 22.5% for RoboProbe, and 59.2% for Auriga.
The model therefore decides considerably better than it executes, and Auriga recovers 68.3% of this planning–execution gap in SR (70.6% in Score). Two common ways of placing a general model on a robot address execution only indirectly.
In step-wise action output , as in the reference harness and in VLA policies [ 37 , 62 , 60 ] , the model emits one motion step or chunk per call from the latest frames.
It never sees the route of a motion before committing to it, and a failed motion is noticed only when the model reads the next frame, by which time the arm has already moved.
In code as policy [ 21 , 9 ] , the model writes a program that commands the control interface directly.
Execution judgment then moves into a program that cannot see the scene.
A failure is detected only if the program was written to check for it, and a program may exit normally even after the motions it requested have failed.
Auriga keeps the judgment with the model, which describes a complete action sequence in each call rather than a single step.
Before execution, the model can see the whole trajectory, its cost, and its projection on the camera image ( Sec. 4.1 ), and the harness checks the measured outcome after every movement ( Sec. 4.2 ). 2.3 General-Purpose Harness Evolution Improvement in embodied agents can target either a procedure for a particular task or the shared capabilities used across many tasks [ 8 , 63 ] . Within-task trial search versus cross-task tool evolution. Recent work on recursive self-improvement (RSI) groups autonomous improvement into six levels from L0 to L5 [ 8 ] : • L0–L2 (within-task search and refinement): Under a single-task objective, the system repeats trials to adjust a trajectory, script, or task note, so the resulting gains stay mainly on the practiced task. • L3–L4 (cross-task experience and tool evolution): The system turns recurring failures across different tasks into reusable tools, interfaces, and verification steps that apply to unseen tasks. • L5 (meta-improvement): The system revises its own perception, control, and verification mechanisms so that later tasks are solved more reliably. Target-task refinement methods such as HexaShell, EmbodiedEvo, and SEAR [ 11 , 20 , 49 ] work in the L0–L2 regime: they reset the target scene across trials to tune coordinate offsets, scripts, or failure notes for that task, which improves performance on the practiced layout but can overfit when layouts change.
Auriga instead focuses on L3–L5 evolution during development.
Failures such as a jammed pin insertion or a slipped cloth grasp are used not to add a coordinate offset for that scene, but to diagnose missing capabilities in 3D localization, contact checking, or in-hand state tracking and to upgrade the shared tools accordingly. Role of the harness. An agent harness connects the model to the robot: it translates the model’s intent into motion and stops the robot when contact or slippage occurs, without making task decisions on the model’s behalf.
Existing harnesses often either run generated commands open-loop without checking the physical outcome, or add task-specific rules and thresholds in the backend to pass particular tasks.
Because manipulation involves both tight rigid clearances and deformable objects, an offset or grasp rule tuned for one task often interferes with another.
Auriga keeps the harness task-agnostic through three rules:
(1) no task-specific logic or privileged state , so the same code and prompts run across all tasks and randomized layouts;
(2) general measurement and control tools , including calibrated multi-view triangulation, paused-world trajectory preview, stage tolerances, and in-hand slip checks; and
(3) in-place recovery , stopping when a motion leaves its tolerance and returning the measured deviation so the model can continue from the current state. During evaluation, the harness version ( #12 ) is completely frozen across all cases.
Within each evaluated episode, the model uses these shared tools to inspect the scene, diagnose discrepancies, and revise its plan or generated programs from execution feedback, continuing from the physical state left by previous actions under the remaining budget without an environment reset or cross-episode memory transfer. 3 System Overview Large vision-language models can reason about task structure, but reliable manipulation also requires metric scene information, contact-sensitive execution, and feedback after each action.
Auriga addresses this gap with a simple closed-loop workflow: the model measures and previews while the simulation is paused, submits a physical action through a single admission gateway, and then uses the returned receipt and observation to decide what to do next ( Fig. 4 ).
The model retains authority over planning and recovery; the harness supplies measurements, enforces the execution boundary, and records what occurred.
This section first formalizes the decision process ( Sec. 3.1 ), then describes the three system layers ( Sec. 3.2 ), the explicit task belief maintained by the model ( Sec. 3.3 ), and the three feedback loops that connect planning to execution ( Sec. 3.4 ). Consequently, each decision uses the information available so far, and each outcome must be returned in a form that can inform the next decision. Formulation. A case specifies a task description g g and an initial world state s 0 s_{0} ( Sec. 2.1 ).
The world state s t s_{t} is hidden from the model, which instead receives observations o t o_{t} comprising calibrated multi-view RGB images and the measured robot state.
At decision step t t , a pretrained model with fixed parameters θ \theta selects a tool call u t ∼ π θ ( ⋅ ∣ g , B t , E t ) , u_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid g,B_{t},E_{t}), (1) where B t B_{t} is its task belief ( Sec. 3.3 ) and E t E_{t} is the episode’s evidence record , the interactions accumulated so far.
The available calls have two roles. Deliberative calls u t ∈ 𝒰 d u_{t}\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{d}} inspect the scene, measure quantities, compute, retrieve records, revise the belief, or preview a motion.
They return a result e t e_{t} while leaving the world unchanged: s t + 1 = s t , E t + 1 = E t ∪ { ( u t , e t ) } . s_{t+1}=s_{t},\qquad E_{t+1}=E_{t}\cup\{(u_{t},e_{t})\}. (2) For example, before hanging a mug, the model can localize the mug and rack, inspect the handle orientation, and preview a grasp without advancing simulated time. Physical calls u t ∈ 𝒰 p u_{t}\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{p}} request robot motion or the passage of simulated time.
They reach the world only through the admission gateway, written as the operator 𝒜 \mathcal{A} , which executes an admissible request and returns a receipt r t r_{t} and a fresh observation: ( s t + 1 , r t , o t + 1 ) = 𝒜 ⁡ ( s t , u t ) , E t + 1 = E t ∪ { ( u t , r t , o t + 1 ) } . (s_{t+1},r_{t},o_{t+1})=\mathcal{A}(s_{t},u_{t}),\qquad E_{t+1}=E_{t}\cup\{(u_{t},r_{t},o_{t+1})\}. (3) If 𝒜 \mathcal{A} rejects a request, the world remains unchanged and the receipt reports the reason.
The model parameters θ \theta , tool set, and task-independent operating guidance remain fixed throughout an episode; adaptation comes from the evolving B t B_{t} and E t E_{t} .
Because the simulation is paused during deliberation, reasoning and deliberative calls consume no simulated time. Quantitative error feedback. A physical request specifies both an intended result and the required precision.
Each stage k k has a target x ^ k \hat{x}_{k} and a model-chosen tolerance τ k \tau_{k} ( Sec. 5.2 ).
During execution, 𝒜 \mathcal{A} measures the reached state x k x_{k} and halts when the deviation ϵ k = x k − x ^ k \epsilon_{k}=x_{k}-\hat{x}_{k} leaves τ k \tau_{k} .
The resulting receipt reports the completed stages, measured deviations, active tolerance, and unexecuted stages ( Sec. 4.2 ).
Thus, if a grasp approach in hang_mugs stops short or the gripper opening differs from the expected value, the model receives the measured discrepancy rather than a generic failure flag.
It can then decide whether to inspect the scene, revise the approach, or execute the remaining stages; 𝒜 \mathcal{A} does not choose the recovery. Information boundary. The harness exposes only the published task text, calibrated RGB images, the robot’s measured state, and the public robot model and camera calibration.
From these inputs, it may compute learned depth, per-object 3D estimates, and point clouds, reporting each as an estimate with its reliability ( Sec. 5.1 ).
The model and its tools cannot access simulator depth, hidden object poses, contact state, or evaluator internals.
The benchmark evaluator, rather than the model’s own assessment, determines task success. 3.2 Separating Decision, Capability, and Admission Auriga separates task reasoning from measurement and execution so that a general-purpose model can build a procedure while every physical action remains reviewable. Division of responsibility. The harness is organized into three layers ( Fig. 4 ): a model session that makes every task decision, a capability layer of general tools, and an admission gateway that is the only path to the robot.
Each layer has a distinct responsibility: • Cortex is one persistent model session for the entire episode. It receives task-independent operating guidance, the official task text, and the robot’s capabilities and coordinate conventions. It chooses task decompositions, measurements, action targets, and recovery steps without human intervention or a task-specific prompt, program, or skill. • The capability layer provides general tools to measure, act, compute, and recall ( Tab. 3 ). For hang_mugs , these tools can estimate mug and rack locations, compare views, preview a grasp, or retrieve an earlier receipt; they do not encode a solution for hanging mugs. • The admission gateway implements 𝒜 \mathcal{A} . It is the only path that moves the robot. It receives a reviewed physical request, determines whether the request is admissible, monitors execution, and records the outcome. The model sets task decomposition, targets, and the need for additional evidence; the harness exposes the thresholds that admit, pause, or stop an action and reports their effects.
Every physical request, whether direct or program-generated, follows the same admission path.
The harness neither substitutes a task-specific rule nor silently changes a parameter supplied by the model.
The records constitute E t E_{t} , remain outside the model context, and can be retrieved on demand ( Sec. 5.1 ).
The model may attach short operation lessons to them, but it rechecks each lesson against the current scene before using it. 3.3 Deliberation over an Explicit Task Belief Long-horizon manipulation accumulates observations, receipts, and intermediate decisions.
Without a compact representation of the current task understanding, a model can lose track of an earlier objective or confuse an issued command with a verified outcome.
Auriga therefore maintains a revisable task belief B t B_{t} alongside the detailed evidence record E t E_{t} . Structure. The belief has three concise components.
The plan records the model’s current understanding of the goal, its intended approach, and the visible evidence that would indicate completion.
The attention list records the uncertainties that matter now, the evidence that could resolve each one, and the part of the procedure that depends on it.
The ledger records a small number of task-critical premises.
Each premise includes an expected event, a status ( pending , supported , contradicted , or unknown ), the permitted evidence for that status, and the next observation or action that could change it (the labeled premises in Fig. 1 ).
The harness admits no physical action until the model has submitted a belief. For example, in hang_mugs , the plan can state “grasp the mug by its handle, lift it, and place it on the rack hook.”
An attention item can require a view that resolves the handle orientation before the approach.
A ledger premise can state that the mug is retained by the gripper: it is initially pending , and becomes supported only after permitted evidence, such as the expected gripper opening together with a confirming lift observation, is available. Belief versus evidence. B t B_{t} is a compact, revisable working state rather than an action script or a source of evidence.
Concrete poses, arm timing, and gripper commands remain part of individual actions, so the model can change them as new measurements arrive.
Likewise, a belief entry must be checked against fresh observations before it supports a decision: a closed gripper alone does not verify that a mug is retained.
When the conversation is compacted to fit the context window, the harness reinserts the latest B t B_{t} as the model’s provisional judgment, while the details of earlier interactions remain retrievable from E t E_{t} . 3.4 Closing the Loop at Three Time Scales Errors can arise within a movement, between successive actions, or only after a task-level premise has been tested.
Auriga addresses these cases with three nested loops that have distinct responsibilities: the admission gateway stops unsafe or off-target execution, while the model diagnoses the result and chooses the recovery. Fig. 5 shows how the three loops interleave within one episode. Stage loop. Within a single action, 𝒜 \mathcal{A} contains errors: applying the stage check of Sec. 3.1 during execution, it halts at the first stage whose deviation ϵ k \epsilon_{k} leaves τ k \tau_{k} , and also pauses at a failed grasp check or at a checkpoint placed by the model ( Sec. 4.2 ).
For a hang_mugs grasp, it can stop the sequence immediately when the measured gripper opening indicates that the mug handle was not secured.
The stage loop keeps an error from propagating to later stages; it does not repair the motion. Action loop. Between actions, the model diagnoses outcomes: after each physical call, it compares the receipt and fresh observation with its intended result and forms a cause hypothesis before acting again.
It may continue the remaining previewed stages, replan from the stopped stage without repeating completed stages, change the contact, route, target, arm, or tolerance, or collect an observation that distinguishes competing explanations.
For example, after an unsuccessful mug grasp, the model can inspect whether the handle orientation, approach route, or target localization caused the discrepancy before choosing a new approach.
Whenever possible, normal task progress tests the hypothesis; a separate probe is used only when its result would alter the next decision. Belief loop. Across the episode, the model revises its premises: action outcomes update the ledger and, when necessary, the plan in B t B_{t} .
If repeated evidence contradicts a premise, the model changes its task-level explanation and selects a different mechanism, such as another contact, route, or target, rather than simply restating the same hypothesis.
In hang_mugs , evidence that a top-down approach repeatedly fails can update the belief so that the model seeks a handle-oriented grasp or a different approach route.
This loop keeps the model’s working understanding aligned with the evidence accumulated during the episode. Sec. 4 next describes the admission path in which the stage loop runs. Deliberative calls extend the evidence record without advancing the world. 4 Admission Gateway: Preview and Verification To close the planning–execution gap quantified in Secs. 2.2 and 2 , Auriga binds physical execution to a trajectory preview that the model can inspect before committing ( Sec. 4.1 ) and verifies the measured state as the trajectory runs ( Sec. 4.2 ).
Auriga follows a single rule, enforced by the admission operator 𝒜 \mathcal{A} : the motion that reaches the robot is exactly the motion compiled into its preview, and whenever execution departs from it beyond the declared tolerance, the harness stops and reports the measured state rather than repairing it on the model’s behalf. 4.1 Preview Before Acting Preview gives the model a first guarantee, namely that every motion, whether requested by the model or by a program it has written, is compiled into a preview before it runs, and that nothing else is executed.
The model reviews this preview by default and can skip the review to save a call.
Below, we describe how the model sees a motion and how execution is held to it. Seeing the whole motion. Rather than issuing one movement per call, the model describes a complete action sequence for one arm or for both arms at once, such as approaching an object, closing the gripper, lifting, transporting, and releasing it.
The harness plans the entire sequence into a robot trajectory before anything moves and returns a preview of what would happen.
Any adjustment the harness makes to how a movement is realized happens at this stage, so the preview already shows the motion that will run.
The preview draws the planned gripper paths ( Fig. 6 ) and arm configurations on the camera image and reports how long each part of the sequence would take on each arm.
It also estimates the clearance between the robot, the objects it holds, and the obstacles the model has declared, such as whether the two arms come close to each other.
Such estimates are given as advice, and the decision whether to proceed stays with the model.
If any part of the sequence cannot be planned, nothing is executed, and the preview states which part failed and why. Committing to it. Once the model accepts a preview, the harness replays the previewed trajectory without replanning it.
Because the simulation is paused while the model inspects the preview, the robot is still in the state from which the preview was planned, and looking ahead costs no execution time.
The robot therefore performs the previewed motion, so every later report refers to a motion the model can inspect.
In free space the robot follows the previewed trajectory closely, which means that a deviation during execution almost always indicates contact, the case that Sec. 4.2 is built around. Contact, slip, and objects displaced by earlier actions can still make the outcome differ from the plan.
Verification gives the second guarantee, namely that every divergence of the robot from its previewed motion beyond the tolerance is caught at the end of the movement in which it occurs and handed back to the model together with the facts needed to act on it. Catching divergence. After each movement, the harness compares where the gripper actually is with where the preview placed it.
The tolerance τ k \tau_{k} of a movement ( Sec. 3.1 ) consists of a position part δ p \delta_{p} and an orientation part δ θ \delta_{\theta} .
The model can set both for each movement, by default δ p = 10 \delta_{p}{=}10 mm and δ θ = 0.15 \delta_{\theta}{=}0.15 rad.
If the difference exceeds the tolerance set for that movement, the robot stops and holds its pose instead of continuing with the rest of the sequence.
The receipt r t r_{t} that follows states which movement failed, by how much it missed, and which parts did not run.
It also adds a fresh camera view from the stopping point and images of the measured and the previewed route.
Movements that already completed are kept rather than undone.
In Fig. 6 , a finger lands on a basket rim while the object is lowered, and the measured path leaves the previewed one beyond its tolerance.
The harness stops the arm and reports only this miss, and the model identifies the rim as the cause from the fresh view.
It then previews a revised motion that clears the rim, which completes the placement in a second round.
This check targets the main source of divergence, contact with an object or fixture, which the planner does not model, so the harness detects its consequence instead of predicting it. Looking where the model chooses. Some divergences leave the robot on its planned path and show only in the scene, such as an object that slips or shifts during a grasp.
The model can therefore insert checkpoints at moments it considers critical, either unconditionally or only when the measured gripper opening after a grasp falls outside the range it expects, for example when the fingers close on nothing.
At a checkpoint the robot stops and the model receives a fresh view, after which it either continues the remaining sequence as previewed or replaces it.
In Fig. 6 , the model places a checkpoint after the lift, and the sequence continues as previewed once the fresh wrist view shows the object still held.
Checkpoints are placed by the model rather than at fixed points, since pausing after every gripper action by default fragmented long tasks.
Acting on what a stop or a checkpoint reveals, however, often requires facts that the model cannot read reliably from RGB, such as whether an object is held and where it sits relative to the gripper, which the following section provides. 5 Capability Layer: Perception and Action Interfaces Faithful execution helps only if the motion the model chooses is the right one, which depends on what the model can perceive and on how it can express what it wants.
A general-purpose vision-language model reads a scene and plans a task well, but it is weak at the quantitative side of manipulation.
This section describes how the harness compensates on both sides, by turning physical state into facts the model can read ( Sec. 5.1 ) and by letting the model state its intent in terms of the task ( Sec. 5.2 ), as summarized in Fig. 7 .
Two principles guide both sides.
The harness informs but does not decide , reporting measurements and estimates together with how reliable they are and leaving the choice of what to do with them to the model.
It also attaches facts at the point of decision , so that information that should change the next command arrives with the preview or the report instead of waiting to be requested. We describe how the model locates things, learns what an action did, and recalls what happened earlier. 3D localization. The model understands the layout of a scene well from RGB, but an image does not show metric 3D positions directly, so any estimate made from appearance alone remains uncertain.
A deployed robot, however, comes with physical quantities that are known exactly, namely the intrinsic and extrinsic calibration of every camera, the height of the work surface, and the kinematic model of the robot itself.
With the calibration, every pixel ( u , v ) (u,v) of a camera with intrinsics K K , orientation R R , and center 𝐜 \mathbf{c} lies on a known ray with direction 𝐝 = R ​ K − 1 ​ ( u , v , 1 ) ⊤ \mathbf{d}=R\,K^{-1}(u,v,1)^{\top} , so locating it in 3D reduces to finding its distance along this ray.
The harness provides two tools that supply this distance at different levels of precision ( Fig. 7 a).
The first estimates every visible object in 3D.
VGGT-Omega [ 46 ] predicts a depth for every pixel of the head-camera image, which places the pixel on its ray, and the depths are scaled so that the table lies at its known height.
SAM 3.1 [ 2 ] separates the objects in these points, and each object is reported with its 3D position, orientation, and size and with how reliable the estimate is, in every full observation and every action report.
Where such an estimate is not precise enough, as for placing the fingers on a narrow edge, the second tool computes the position of a point that the model selects in an image.
The model fixes its distance either with a plane 𝐧 ⊤ ​ 𝐩 = h \mathbf{n}^{\top}\mathbf{p}=h of known height, 𝐩 = 𝐜 + h − 𝐧 ⊤ ​ 𝐜 𝐧 ⊤ ​ 𝐝 ​ 𝐝 , ∂ 𝐩 ∂ h = 𝐝 𝐧 ⊤ ​ 𝐝 , \mathbf{p}=\mathbf{c}+\frac{h-\mathbf{n}^{\top}\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{n}^{\top}\mathbf{d}}\,\mathbf{d},\qquad\frac{\partial\mathbf{p}}{\partial h}=\frac{\mathbf{d}}{\mathbf{n}^{\top}\mathbf{d}}, (4) or by marking the same point in a second view, such as the wrist camera, from which the harness triangulates it.
The two tools meet in the plane height.
For a point on the table, the height is known exactly.
For a point on an object, the model either takes the height from the learned depth of that object, in which case the harness reports how far the point would move if this height were off, or marks the point in a second view, which needs no height.
Any 3D point, including the fingers placed by the kinematic model, can be projected back into the image to check it. Action outcomes and in-hand state. After an action, the model needs its physical outcome.
For most of the scene, the next image shows it, and each report also lists the objects whose estimated position changed during the action.
A grasp is harder, since the fingers often hide the object, so the image may not show the state of the object and how it interacts with the gripper.
The harness therefore complements the image with measurements of both.
For the gripper, every report includes its measured opening.
For the object, the model can measure on request where a visible part of it, such as its lower end, lies relative to the gripper, the axis of the object in the 3D points around the fingers, and how far a part marked in a wrist image before an action has moved after it.
The object estimates also keep the pose that the object had in the hand at the grasp, as a reference for these measurements.
Together, these tell the model whether the object is held, how it sits in the hand, and whether it has turned or slipped since the grasp. Episode history and warnings. In long episodes the context is compacted and early images are dropped, while recovery often depends on what earlier attempts actually achieved.
The model can therefore retrieve from the evidence record E t E_{t} ( Sec. 3.1 ) any earlier camera frame, including a magnified region of it, a list of past actions with the displacement that each one measured, and the planned and measured trajectories of any action.
Full history is retrieved on demand rather than pushed into every turn, since forced reviews of history produced idle calls.
The exception is history that bears directly on the next command.
A report of a stopped or failed action carries a short summary of recent outcomes, and a preview that returns to a place where a motion has failed carries a warning, so that the model does not unknowingly repeat a failed attempt. 5.2 Expressive Actions On the output side, the model states what it wants in terms of the task, such as where the fingers should touch and how the two arms should cooperate, and the harness turns these statements into motion.
We describe how the model specifies the goal of each movement and how it sets the timing and speed of a sequence. Finger-referenced goals. A robot interface typically takes the pose of the wrist as its goal, whereas what matters for a task is where the fingers touch the object and how precisely they get there.
In Auriga, each movement states its goal in these terms.
The model gives where the gripper should end up and how it should be turned in a frame fixed to the workspace, together with the part of the fingers that should make contact, such as the fingertip or the pad center, so that the goal refers to the point that touches the object rather than to the wrist.
It also states how precisely the gripper must arrive, so that a tight insertion and a coarse transit are checked differently. Shared timeline and speed. Goals alone do not fix how a sequence unfolds in time.
When both arms take part, or when a movement passes from free space to contact, the order and the speed of its parts matter as much as its goals.
In Auriga, the stages of both arms belong to the same sequence and advance on a shared timeline.
Stages of the two arms run at the same time unless the model declares that one must wait for another.
The model also sets the speed of each movement, typically slower near contact, and can let the gripper open or close during a movement, for example opening it on the way to an object.
Speed and gripper timing are fixed in the preview like the path itself, so they are part of the reviewed motion. 6 Experiments We evaluate Auriga under the zero-shot protocol of Sec. 2.1 on two simulation benchmarks: dual-arm tabletop manipulation on RoboDojo [ 4 ] ( Sec. 6.1.1 ) and kitchen mobile manipulation on RoboCasa365 [ 32 ] ( Sec. 6.1.2 ).
After describing the experimental setup ( Sec. 6.1 ), we report main results on RoboDojo ( Sec. 6.2 ) and RoboCasa365 ( Sec. 6.3 ), mechanism ablations ( Sec. 6.4 ), execution efficiency and tool usage ( Sec. 6.5 ), and qualitative behavior and failure modes ( Sec. 6.6 ). 6.1 Experimental Setup 6.1.1 RoboDojo RoboDojo [ 4 ] simulates a dual-arm ARX X5 robot with one head camera and two wrist cameras in Isaac Sim. Capability dimensions. RoboDojo groups its 42 tasks into five capability dimensions: Generalization (12 tasks, each with standard and random scenes), Precision (8), Long-Horizon (8), Memory (6), and Open (8); Tab. 13 in App. A lists what each dimension covers and its tasks.
As on the official leaderboard, results are first averaged across the tasks of each dimension and then across the five dimensions, with equal weight at both levels.
For Generalization, the value of each task and of the dimension is the equal-weight mean of its standard-scene average and its random-scene average. Evaluation set. Following Sec. 2.1 , a case specifies a task, a scene variant (standard or random), an evaluation seed, and a layout, and an episode is one execution of a case by the agent.
The evaluation set follows the protocol of the official leaderboard, with three evaluation seeds and, in each seed, 50 cases for each of the 42 tasks, that is, 2,100 cases per seed and 6,300 in all.
In each seed, every Generalization task contributes 25 cases with standard scenes and 25 with random scenes, and every other task contributes 50 with standard scenes.
Each case counts with its first official score, including scores of zero, and any later episode of the same case is ignored.
When an infrastructure fault ends an episode before the evaluator has scored it, the case is re-run under the same configuration. Metrics. SR is the share of cases that the benchmark evaluator judges fully successful, over a fixed denominator: a case without an official score stays in the denominator and does not count as a success. Score is the official graded process score of the evaluator, from 0 to 100, averaged over the cases with an official score.
Both metrics are first computed for each task in each seed, and a task’s value is the mean of its three per-seed values; the dimension values and the five-dimension average then follow from the task values as in Sec. 6.1.1 . Steps are the native steps that the simulator executes in an episode, also reported as a percentage of the official native-step limit of the task, which ranges from 200 to 1,900 steps; a single action request may use at most 250 steps. Calls are the model calls (the model responses in an episode) and the direct tool calls issued by the model; tool calls issued by model-written programs are counted separately. Cost comprises the input tokens of the model session (total, share served from the prompt cache in %, and uncached input tokens), the output tokens, and the wall-clock time of the episode; Steps, Calls, and Cost are reported as means per episode. Agent configuration. The RoboDojo results ( Secs. 6.2 , 6.5 and 6.5 ) use Auriga as described in Secs. 3 , 4 and 5 , with the model gpt-6-astra at reasoning effort high.
An episode may use at most 170 model responses and the official native-step limit of its task, and the simulation is paused while the model deliberates ( Sec. 3.1 ).
The model observes RGB images from the three cameras, proprioception, and the public camera calibration, together with the object estimates that the harness computes from the head-camera image ( Sec. 5.1 ); simulator depth and hidden object state are withheld.
Its task text is the native instruction of the task, the official task page with its description and scoring rules, and the task-independent operating guidance of the harness ( Secs. 3.1 and 3.2 ).
The ablations in Sec. 6.4 remove or restrict individual mechanisms of this configuration while keeping the rest fixed. 6.1.2 RoboCasa365 RoboCasa365 [ 32 ] evaluates household mobile manipulation with a single Franka Panda arm mounted on an Omron mobile base across kitchen environments, spanning 365 tasks (65 atomic and 300 composite). Tasks and task groups. Evaluation focuses on the benchmark’s 50 Target50 tasks, organized into three groups: 18 Atomic-Seen tasks involving single manipulation skills (e.g., opening a drawer or operating a microwave); 16 Composite-Seen tasks that chain two to fifteen subtasks whose templates appear in the benchmark’s pretraining demonstrations; and 16 Composite-Unseen tasks of comparable horizon whose templates are absent from the pretraining demonstrations. Evaluation protocol and metrics. Whereas the standard policy leaderboard [ 40 ] evaluates demonstration-trained policies in the 2,500 pretraining kitchens, agent evaluations are conducted in ten unseen target kitchens with novel layouts, fixtures, and objects.
We follow the protocol of the published agent baselines [ 61 , 43 ] : every task is evaluated at seeds 1–5, giving 250 episodes (90 Atomic-Seen, 80 Composite-Seen, and 80 Composite-Unseen).
We report the success rate (SR, %) of each group, of all 160 composite episodes, and Overall as the mean over the 50 tasks, which equals the SR over all 250 episodes.
The benchmark environment provides camera images, robot proprioception, world-coordinate maps derived from rendered depth, the source code of the task’s success check, and live values of the predicates tested by that check. Baselines. Both published agent baselines plan with an LLM agent and hand contact-rich phases, such as grasping an object or turning a knob, to the RLDX-1 vision-language-action (VLA) policy [ 17 ] .
RPent [ 61 ] also retrieves task memory, built by exploring each task on seed 0 before evaluation.
PyRUA-Lean [ 43 ] uses no cross-episode memory.
We also list RLDX-1 on its own and, as in the comparison of RPent, three learned policies evaluated in the pretraining kitchens. Agent configuration. Auriga runs gpt-6-astra at high reasoning effort with at most 100 model requests per episode.
Unlike both agent baselines, it uses no VLA policy and no cross-episode memory.

Rather than repeating the failed command or discarding prior progress, the model updates the contradicted retention condition in B t B_{t} and revises the contact region, active arm, or transport mode. In RoboDojo imitate_sorting_sequence ( Fig. 14(a) ), the model must reproduce a five-object placement order (wristwatch, black device, rabbit, phone, gold vehicle).
After the first two objects are verified inside the basket, the rabbit lies outside the left arm’s reach, so the right arm transfers it toward the center.
When the rabbit slips during transfer and lands outside the basket’s far rim, the task belief keeps the third slot marked pending rather than advancing to the phone; after a head grasp catches the rim and triggers a tracking_error stop, the model inspects the wrist view, rotates the jaws across the rabbit’s torso in a clearer region, verifies placement inside the basket, and only then proceeds to the remaining two objects.

Alongside general-purpose models such as GPT-6 [ 34 ] and Claude Opus 5.5 [ 1 ] , embodied foundation models specialize perception and reasoning for physical interaction [ 12 , 19 , 45 , 58 , 6 ] .
Evaluations of GPT-6 Astra [ 60 , 10 ] investigate direct numerical control and cooperation with learned policies, revealing a gap between semantic task understanding and precise physical execution.
Other work adapts robot behavior through context: GPT-Policy [ 5 ] preserves task-relevant demonstration transitions, while RoboICL [ 24 ] organizes demonstrations and interaction memory around observations, actions, and execution receipts. 7.2 Agentic Harnesses for Robotics Agentic harnesses organize model interaction with robot tools, programs, and execution feedback. Trial-and-error interaction operates within a task: Agent as Policy [ 16 ] and CaP-Agent0 [ 9 ] within CaP-X allow agents to construct programs and revise execution using observations and tool results, while RoboHarness [ 38 ] emphasizes a visual-geometric control interface.
The agent may also delegate action generation: PhysicalRSI [ 31 ] provides task-aware memory and skills backed by code policies or π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} [ 37 ] , and FastHarness [ 22 ] adapts the frequency of VLM supervision over a frozen vision-language-action (VLA) model. Cross-trial evolution uses rollout feedback to revise programs, tools, or policies for subsequent executions.
HarnessPAI [ 48 ] keeps the generated program fixed within a rollout, including its observation-dependent checks, and revises it across rollouts.
HexaShell [ 11 ] separates an acting agent from an optimizer that repairs and tests executable tools; EmbodiedEvo [ 20 ] separates exploration from evaluation, passing distilled code skills and failure lessons to independent test agents.
Both freeze the acquired experience for evaluation without updating model weights.
Related approaches evolve skills and context code, attribute execution failures, or select informative physical trials [ 47 , 7 , 44 ] ; rollout scale and candidate selection also affect harness improvement [ 51 ] .
WorldScape-R 2 SI [ 29 ] jointly improves reusable skills and a reactive action model, while ENPIRE [ 53 ] lets agents revise policy-training algorithms and infrastructure using real-robot evaluations. Skill libraries complement cross-trial improvement by storing reusable procedures for later retrieval and composition.
ASPIRE [ 26 ] stores validated repair knowledge as reusable in-context guidance, including applicability conditions, repair strategies, and optional code sketches; RATs [ 59 ] stores code skills acquired through self-directed play and retrieves them from a frozen library for downstream tasks.
Teach-and-Grow [ 33 ] derives closed-loop Skill Blocks from subgoals shared across demonstrations and retains execution conditions and repairs in a separate experience memory.
Real2Gym [ 39 ] retains task procedures, object-relative motions, and recovery strategies from interaction in simulation environments reconstructed from videos, then reuses them on physical robots.
GaP [ 3 ] focuses on composition, assembling existing perception, planning, and control modules into computation graphs refined through simulation.
Auriga organizes interaction within a persistent model session, where generated programs and direct tool calls share a physical execution interface and return observations and outcomes to guide subsequent decisions. 7.3 Vision-Language-Action and World Action Models Learned action models map visual and language inputs to robot actions and can serve as policies invoked by an agent.
Recent VLA work explores heterogeneous-data co-training and cross-embodiment adaptation [ 37 , 62 , 35 , 52 , 54 , 56 ] , experience-driven policy improvement [ 36 ] , and joint reasoning and action generation [ 25 ] .
InternVLA-A1.5 [ 28 ] further introduces latent foresight supervised by a pretrained video model while retaining semantic training objectives. World action models (WAMs) incorporate predictive modeling or supervision from future observations into action learning.
Video-based approaches jointly model actions and future observations [ 55 , 18 , 50 ] ; VPP2 [ 14 ] strengthens manipulation-specific video prediction, and X-WAM [ 13 ] adds multi-view RGB-D prediction with asynchronous action decoding.
Future supervision can also shape action representations without requiring future-video generation at deployment, as in Being-H0.7, Fast-WAM, and InternW0- Δ \Delta [ 27 , 57 , 30 ] .
JEPA-WAM [ 23 ] couples latent transition prediction to action generation, while Long-WAM [ 15 ] studies causal pretraining and longer observation histories.
When these policies are used within a harness, the agent coordinates their invocation, evaluates execution outcomes, and revises the task procedure. 8 Conclusion We have presented Auriga , a task-agnostic agent harness for zero-shot manipulation within a single episode, closing 68.3% of the planning–execution gap of vision-language models on RoboDojo (from 22.5% under the reference interface toward the 76.2% arm-free upper bound) without target-task exploration, demonstrations, or environment resets.
In Auriga, the model makes all task decisions over an explicit task belief B t B_{t} and acts through direct tool calls or synthesized multi-step Python programs, while the harness supplies calibrated 3D perception, finger-referenced dual-arm control, and a single admission gateway 𝒜 \mathcal{A} that previews trajectories while simulation is paused and verifies stage tolerances and grasp bounds during execution for in-place recovery.
Developed across 13 cross-task capability iterations and frozen for evaluation, Auriga ranks first overall on the 42-task RoboDojo leaderboard with a 64.0 Score and 57.7% success rate, maintains first place under scene randomization ( G = 46.3 G=46.3 ) while learned policies drop by up to 33.9%, and achieves a 73.2% success rate on RoboCasa365 mobile manipulation.
Future work will extend this closed-loop execution architecture toward continuous force-sensitive manipulation, lower deliberation latency, and automated cross-task tool synthesis. Appendix F Extended Execution Trace Analysis We examine swap_T , a pose-exchange task in the Memory dimension, to study how a remembered objective guides manipulation after the scene changes (complementing the matched-scene baseline comparison in Secs. 6.5 and 12 ).
The robot must exchange two T-shaped blocks so that each occupies the other’s initial position and orientation.
This task combines three demands in a compact setting: preserving a spatial reference after its source object moves, coordinating access to a shared workspace, and correcting the object pose when grasping or release changes it.
The asymmetric T shape makes orientation part of the goal, so exchanging positions alone is insufficient.
We analyze the 15-scene standard-variant diagnostic set for this task, using their task-belief records, action specifications, execution receipts, and evaluator outcomes. Preserving object goals across changes in execution. For analysis, let T ^ A , 0 \hat{T}_{A,0} and T ^ B , 0 \hat{T}_{B,0} denote the two initial planar poses comprising planar position and yaw estimated from the calibrated images.
The exchange assigns the object goals G A = T ^ B , 0 , G B = T ^ A , 0 . G_{A}=\hat{T}_{B,0},\qquad G_{B}=\hat{T}_{A,0}. (5) These goals remain fixed when the model changes a grasp or assigns the remaining transfer to another arm.
The current object location and its relation to the gripper, however, must be updated from subsequent observations.
This distinction matters after slippage: re-localizing the fallen block supplies a new pickup pose, while the saved initial pose still supplies its destination.
The recorded task_plan separates these roles through the plan, attention, and ledger described in Sec. 3.3 .
Its updates retain the original placement requirement while revising which physical conditions are supported and which remain unresolved. A completed motion can still require a task-level correction. (a) Conveyor picking ( pick_from_conveyor_by_image ). (b) Bowl stacking ( stack_bowls ). Figure 22 : Additional RoboDojo episodes recovering from grasp failure and slippage. The model changes the grasped region for the figurine and uses a deeper pinch and slower lift for the bowl. Conveyor picking: separating acquisition, retention, and destination access. The model must match a figurine to the board image, place it inside the basket, and lift the basket by more than 8 cm.
The moving conveyor first invalidates a basket pickup location, prompting a switch to right-arm interception.
For the figurine, an approach contacts the hat before closure; a later empty closure leaves the body exposed.
A torso grasp then acquires the object, but the figurine slips during transport ( Fig. 22(a) ).
The model consequently changes both the grasp and the transfer conditions: it moves the basket aside, re-grasps across the torso, and verifies retention during a slower lift. Once the figurine is retained, the remaining obstacle is the destination.
Moving the basket underneath it causes contact with the image board and tilts the opening.
The model keeps the figurine held, moves the basket upward and away from the board, and then completes the release.
A later check against the initial image revises the height judgment because the earlier estimate followed a rim on a tilted basket.
The loaded basket is raised farther before the evaluator signals success.
These successive revisions address distinct predicates (target acquired, target retained, opening accessible, and required basket elevation), so progress on one predicate can be preserved while another is corrected. Bowl stacking: changing the assembly order after a failed lift. The initial plan uses the rear middle bowl as the base, adds the left bowl, and then adds the right bowl.
The first rim grip slips and displaces the left bowl.
The revised plan lets that bowl settle while the robot approaches the right bowl, using a deeper pinch and slower lift ( Fig. 22(b) ).
After the right bowl is nested into the base and remains supported after release, the model re-localizes the displaced left bowl and adds it last.
Thus recovery changes the assembly order as well as the grasp. This reordering is possible because the task constrains the final stack rather than the order in which bowls enter it.
It also gives the displaced bowl time to settle before its next localization.
The plan checks co-motion after lifting and upright support after release and withdrawal; the final return supplies another observation of whether the stack persists without gripper support.
The evaluator confirms successful stacking.
The distinction from sequence reproduction is consequential: the bowl order can change to simplify execution, whereas the observed object order must remain fixed. Drawer-to-counter transfer: shifting contact from a thin handle to the wider bowl. The robot must take a ladle from an open drawer and place it on the counter, where the single task predicate tests only whether the ladle rests on the counter and remains false until final placement.
An initial grasp on the thin handle slips during the lift, and after a lower re-grasp on a thicker section of the handle, the ladle slips again during mobile-base motion and falls back into the drawer ( Fig. 23 ).
Both losses are reported by the execution receipts rather than the task predicate, and after the second slip the object table no longer finds the ladle in the wrist-camera view. Transferring the ladle by its bowl and releasing it on the counter turns the placement predicate true, and the evaluator confirms task success.

Straw delivery: matching the scope of revision to the invalidated assumption. The task requires taking a straw from a drawer to a glass cup on the dining counter; initially, the object table locates the drawer handles while neither the straw nor the cup is visible.
Repeated attempts to open the first drawer return execution receipts for a blocked or stalled approach ( Fig. 25(a) ), as approaching the handle from above brings the gripper against the counter.
The model switches to a horizontal approach from the robot-facing side of the handle, backs the mobile base away for clearance, and writes that direction into the grasp stage before pulling the drawer open with the base. Inspecting the opened drawer reveals a different discrepancy when the object table finds no straw inside.
The task plan records that this drawer is empty while the adjacent front drawer remains unchecked, and the robot opens that second drawer using the same horizontal handle approach and base pull.
After grasping the straw and driving around the counter end, it rights the glass after a missed first insertion, places the straw inside to satisfy the straw-in-cup predicate, and backs away as the evaluator confirms success.
As in charger insertion, the two disruptions call for different scopes of revision: a blocked grasp changes the approach route, whereas an empty drawer changes the target container without altering the delivery objective. Cutting-station setup: restoring a displaced container before revising the approach. The task requires taking a knife from a drawer and a fish from a plate and placing both onto a cutting board.
When the arm reaches downward for the knife, the execution receipt reports a blocked approach, and the camera view shows that arm contact has pushed the partly open drawer inward ( Fig. 25(b) ).
The model initially plans to raise the arm, reopen the drawer, and approach from above with more clearance. Across roughly ten subsequent attempts, however, passing the arm over the drawer front repeatedly pushes the drawer inward and leaves grasps blocked or empty.
The model then pulls the drawer fully open, withdraws the gripper clear of the handle before turning the wrist to approach from the side, and lowers its grasp slightly to place the finger pads around the knife handle.
Delivering the knife satisfies the knife-on-board predicate, and after the fish is re-grasped following a slip during base motion, the evaluator signals task success during the final placement onto the board.
As in object packing, the central revision treats the displaced drawer as state that must be restored and paired with a non-colliding approach route, rather than targeting coordinates measured before contact.
