E-mail: suguest@126.com. † † thanks: Abstract Autonomous agents must continually acquire reusable knowledge from experience without relying on costly large language model (LLM) reasoning for every decision. However, existing experience-based approaches often struggle to transform accumulated observations and reasoning into compact knowledge that lightweight models can independently apply. This paper proposes SCALE, a small-model-first framework that integrates selective LLM reasoning, experience-driven concept abstraction, and knowledge internalization. A lightweight model handles routine predictions, while prediction errors and accumulated cross-episode evidence selectively trigger LLM reflection. The LLM analyzes relevant historical experiences, generates positive and negative counterfactual examples, and proposes reusable concept hypotheses. Independently validated concepts are progressively incorporated into hierarchical neural representations, enabling lightweight models to exploit increasingly abstract knowledge. Meanwhile, mastered experiences are excluded from active memory while their original records remain available in a complete archive. Experiments across ten random seeds show that historical recall improves held-out accuracy from 87.11% to 98.28%, hierarchical concept knowledge increases difficult-case accuracy from 55.33% to 66.47%, and abstract archiving reduces active experience records from 6,000 to 1,528. Index Terms: Small-Model-First Learning; On-Demand LLM Reasoning; Hierarchical Concept Abstraction; Experience Internalization. I Introduction Autonomous agents operating over long periods must make decisions from
changing observations while accumulating knowledge that can improve future
actions. Large language models (LLMs) provide flexible reasoning over
individual situations and can synthesize lessons from experience [ 1 ] [ 2 ] , whereas
lightweight models offer a practical means of handling frequent decisions
without repeated expensive inference. The central challenge is to combine
these complementary capabilities so that experience produces lasting
improvements in the agent’s own predictive knowledge , rather than merely
providing more information for the next LLM request. This is especially
relevant when important evidence is separated by long intervals, new object
types share previously unseen conditions, and a continuing agent must retain
useful history without allowing its active memory to grow without bound.
Recent studies on efficient model selection, reflective agents, and
concept-guided prediction establish promising foundations for this goal [ 3 , 4 ] . However, these research directions do not by themselves provide a complete
experience-to-knowledge pathway for a small-model-first agent. Cost-aware
routing chooses which model should answer a query, but does not necessarily
make the lightweight model more capable after the expensive intervention [ 5 , 3 ] . Reflection and experience-memory
methods can retrieve useful past events or textual insights, yet these
insights often remain dependent on future LLM calls instead of becoming
lightweight predictive parameters [ 6 , 7 ] .
Conversely, concept-based predictors can make abstract representations
operational, but typically assume an available concept vocabulary or concept
supervision rather than deriving candidate concepts from the agent’s own
temporally dispersed experiences [ 8 , 9 ] . Continual replay and organized external
memory help preserve past information, but retaining or retrieving examples
is not equivalent to discovering a shared condition that generalizes across
situations [ 10 , 11 ] . Moreover, an LLM-generated
explanation or counterfactual cannot be assumed correct without independent
checks. These limitations motivate a framework in which selective reasoning,
validated abstraction, small-model learning, and long-term experience
management form one continuing process. In this paper, we propose SCALE ( S mall-model-first C oncept A bstraction with L LM-guided E xperience Internalization). Its ordinary decision pathway uses a
lightweight task predictor, optionally assisted by concepts that the agent
has already learned. The LLM is not required for every observation: an
incorrect prediction identified through independent feedback may initiate
reasoning about its cause, while accumulated evidence spanning different
historical episodes may separately initiate a concept review. The agent
retrieves relevant verified experiences, and the LLM proposes an observable
explanation and positive/negative counterfactual learning examples. These
proposals are admitted for training only after checking their consistency
with independent task and event semantics. Thus, SCALE uses expensive
reasoning to create reusable training knowledge rather than substituting an
LLM answer for every small-model decision. Beyond individual error-driven learning, SCALE uses retrospective evidence
to form hierarchical concepts that can guide later predictions. A candidate
concept describes common required and excluded observable conditions, is
tested against independent positive and negative cases, and becomes a
trainable neural parent only after validation. For example, risk experiences
involving sharp glass and stone fragments may support an initial shared
concept; later encounters with flexible sharp fragments may motivate a
broader hypothesis that does not require rigidity. Such a hypothesis is not
accepted simply because the LLM proposes it, nor is a higher abstraction
assumed to be more accurate on every task. Accepted concepts supply cues to
the small model, making learned abstractions available without repeatedly
consulting the LLM. Meanwhile, complete original experiences remain in a
cold archive, while redundant mastered cases can leave the active working
set. This distinguishes internalizing experience into operational
knowledge from merely summarizing or deleting historical records. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows: • Small-model-first learning with selective LLM reasoning. We integrate feedback-triggered learning and evidence-triggered historical
reviews into an ordinary lightweight prediction pathway, so successful
routine processing does not inherently require LLM inference. • Experience-driven, independently validated concept
abstraction. We allow an LLM to propose shared observable conditions
from temporally separated historical experiences and internalize accepted
concepts as trainable hierarchical neural representations, rather than
treating textual proposals as established knowledge. • Long-term knowledge reuse with active–cold memory. We combine learned concept cues with selective active-experience retention
and complete archival preservation, and evaluate prediction, concept
generalization, and memory costs in complementary simulated agent tasks. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.

The final section V concludes the paper. II Related Work II-A Efficient Small–Large Model Collaboration Large language models offer flexible reasoning but can impose substantial
inference cost when called for every observation or action. Prior work on
cost-aware inference has consequently explored the allocation of different
requests to models of different capabilities. FrugalGPT studies prompt
adaptation, model approximation, and cascaded inference, demonstrating that
selective access to more expensive language models can substantially reduce
cost while maintaining task performance [ 5 ] .
RouteLLM learns a router from preference data to choose between stronger and
weaker language models [ 3 ] . These approaches establish an
important operational principle: the most capable model need not process
every input. Their principal decision, however, concerns which model
should answer the current query , rather than what reusable knowledge
should be learned from an intervention . A complementary direction transfers knowledge from a powerful teacher to a
compact student. Knowledge distillation replaces an expensive predictor or
ensemble with a smaller network trained to reproduce informative teacher
outputs [ 12 ] . DistilBERT illustrates the utility of
this principle for language representations by producing a lighter model
through a dedicated distillation procedure [ 13 ] . These
methods offer efficient deployment and make a strong case for internalizing
high-capacity knowledge into smaller parameters. Nevertheless, conventional
distillation is usually organized around a prepared teacher–student
training process; it does not, by itself, specify how an autonomous agent
should retrieve temporally separated experiences, discover a new concept,
and decide when the teacher needs to reason again. SCALE combines these otherwise complementary perspectives without treating
them as interchangeable. A lightweight task model is the ordinary decision
path; independently observed errors and accumulated cross-episode concept
evidence are separate reasons for selective LLM intervention. Instead of
simply switching to the LLM’s answer or distilling its unconstrained output,
the framework requests causal or conceptual hypotheses, subjects proposed
training examples to independent environment validation, and incorporates
accepted knowledge into lightweight task and concept networks. The distinction
is thus between cost-aware answer selection and selective
reasoning that changes the agent’s future small-model capabilities . This
design does not assume that the LLM invocation rate must decrease
monotonically or that model selection alone guarantees reliable knowledge. II-B Experience-Based Reflection and Reusable Agent Skills Experience-based LLM agents show how environmental feedback can become a
resource for subsequent reasoning. Reflexion converts trial outcomes into
verbal reflections stored in episodic memory, improving later attempts
without updating the LLM’s weights [ 4 ] . ExpeL
collects experiences from training tasks, extracts natural-language insights,
and recalls both experiences and insights at inference time [ 6 ] . Their main advantage is that lessons from failure can
be used with closed or otherwise non-trainable foundation models. A limitation
for resource-constrained autonomous systems is that the knowledge is primarily
consumed through retrieved text and renewed LLM inference, rather than
becoming a compact neural decision mechanism that independently predicts
routine actions. More ambitious agents explicitly combine experience, abstraction, and
long-horizon behavior. Generative Agents maintain a record of observations,
synthesize higher-level reflections, and retrieve memories for planning,
which supports coherent behavior across extended interactions [ 7 ] . Voyager uses environment feedback and an
evolving executable skill library to reuse acquired behavior in an open-ended
embodied environment [ 14 ] . These studies are particularly
relevant because they show that experience need not remain an isolated
example: it can be reorganized into reflections or reusable skills. Their
representations nevertheless serve different purposes from SCALE’s neural
concept cues. A textual reflection or retrieved program can guide an LLM
agent, while a learned parent concept is intended to affect the predictions
of the lightweight model itself. A recurring methodological issue is that a plausible reflection is not
automatically a valid rule. Summaries can omit boundary conditions, and a
generated example can conflict with the environment it purports to describe.
SCALE therefore separates LLM proposal from acceptance : error
explanations and positive/negative counterfactual cases are checked against
independent event semantics before they are used for training; proposed
hierarchical concepts require additional held-out validation. Historical
recall is used to compare observations separated by distractors or long
delays, and evidence can trigger a concept review even in the absence of an
immediate action error. This extends experience-based reflection toward
validated knowledge acquisition, rather than presenting reflective text
alone as evidence that the small model has learned. II-C Concept Representation, Continual Learning, and Long-Term Memory Concept-based neural models introduce structured intermediate knowledge
between observations and decisions. Concept Bottleneck Models predict
human-specified concepts before predicting a task label, making decisions
more interpretable and allowing interventions on concept values [ 8 ] . Concept Embedding Models learn richer concept-conditioned
representations and improve the flexibility of concept-based prediction,
including settings with incomplete concept supervision [ 9 ] . These approaches provide useful precedents for
making concepts computationally actionable rather than merely recording
them in natural language. Their standard setting, however, assumes a
provided concept vocabulary or some concept supervision. SCALE instead
studies how an agent can propose candidate concepts from accumulated
episodes and subsequently learn verified concept detectors and parent nodes.
The approach remains dependent on observable features and validation; an
LLM-generated concept is not accepted merely because it sounds plausible. Long-term memory research addresses a different, closely related problem:
how an agent can retain and access relevant history. MemoryBank uses memory
updating and selective reinforcement to maintain personal conversational
context over time [ 15 ] . HippoRAG organizes knowledge
for efficient long-range retrieval with a graph-based approach [ 16 ] . More recently, A-Mem dynamically links and
reorganizes structured memory notes as new experiences arrive [ 11 ] . These methods advance retrieval, organization, and the
evolution of external memories; in particular, A-Mem already treats memory
organization as an adaptive process. The distinction from SCALE is therefore not that earlier systems lack abstraction or dynamic memory. It is
that their memory structures are chiefly consumed through retrieval-based
LLM processing, whereas SCALE additionally tests whether validated
experience-level relations can become reusable lightweight predictive
parameters. Continual-learning approaches provide another route to persistent
knowledge. Gradient Episodic Memory, for example, retains selected past
examples and constrains updates to reduce forgetting while learning new
tasks [ 10 ] . Such methods are valuable for preserving past
competence, but replaying examples and learning a shared concept are not
equivalent operations: different object classes may support one abstract
action condition, and a collection of correct predictions need not identify
that condition explicitly. SCALE keeps a complete cold archive while
selecting a smaller active working set; abstract concepts can represent
repeated experience patterns, and the archive remains available for later
retrieval. This design does not imply that compression always improves
wall-clock lookup latency or that concept generalization is error-free. Taken together, existing studies establish the benefits of economical
inference, reflective experience reuse, concept-guided neural prediction,
and adaptive memory. SCALE focuses on their intersection: small-model-
first operation, on-demand LLM reasoning over historical evidence,
independently validated hierarchical concept formation, and internalization
into lightweight predictors with active–cold experience management . The
experimental question is whether these mechanisms yield useful knowledge
and reduced active-memory requirements, rather than whether every newly
introduced concept layer must immediately outperform its predecessor on
every metric. III Small-Model-First Concept Abstraction and LLM-Guided Experience Internalization - The SCALE Model We propose SCALE ( S mall-model-first C oncept A bstraction with L LM-guided E xperience
Internalization), a continuing learning framework in which the small model
handles ordinary decisions and a large language model (LLM) is invoked
selectively to reason about failures or accumulated evidence that may reveal
reusable knowledge. Unlike an agent that repeatedly queries an LLM for each
action, SCALE seeks to transfer the useful results of expensive reasoning into
lightweight predictors. Unlike a method that stores every experience in its
active context, SCALE also uses learned concepts to retain representative
experiences actively while preserving all original records in an archive. The model connects four functions: (i) small-model-first action prediction,
(ii) feedback- and evidence-triggered LLM reflection, (iii) independently
validated hierarchical concept formation, and (iv) concept internalization
and active–cold memory management. A concept may progress from a relation
among several concrete experiences to a more general relation supported by
later observations. The emergence of a higher-level concept is treated as
knowledge-structure growth, not as a guarantee that every higher-level
classifier must outperform its lower-level counterpart. Figure 1 illustrates the overall interaction. types. Later experience with a flexible sharp fragment can motivate a
broader hypothesis in which material rigidity is no longer required.

cases. This example illustrates concept formation and extension , not

interpretable signature and historical evidence references remain
inspectable, while trainable concept detectors and the small-model adapter
learn how the concept influences future predictions. This distinction is
important: merely receiving a plausible LLM explanation does not mean that
the small model has learned it. The learned cue must survive independent
validation and be useful as part of subsequent lightweight inference. For long-term operation, the model maintains a complete archive ℳ t archive \mathcal{M}_{t}^{\mathrm{archive}} and an active experience set ℳ t active ⊆ ℳ t archive \mathcal{M}_{t}^{\mathrm{active}}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\mathrm{archive}} .
Every completed interaction is preserved, including incorrect decisions and
counterexamples. Repeated confidently mastered cases may be retired from
the active set; after a concept has been validated, additional redundant
cases matching the learned conditions can also be made inactive. However,
representative positive examples, informative errors, and negative boundary
cases are retained to avoid making the working set consist only of easy
positives. Retired experiences are not deleted : retrieval can access
the complete archive and temporarily reactivate relevant cold records when
future learning or reasoning needs them. The representation used for memory abstraction need not be identical in
every experiment. A feedback-mined, single-tier concept predicate can supply
useful cues and support active-memory reduction, whereas the integrated
SCALE configuration additionally evaluates LLM-proposed signatures and
neural parent concepts. This distinction prevents memory compression from
being attributed to an LLM-produced hierarchy where that hierarchy was not
actually used. Memory efficiency is characterized by the active-record
count, effective active storage, and retrieval-candidate count; these are
reported separately from measured wall-clock retrieval latency, because
reducing active storage does not by itself guarantee faster search. Overall, SCALE establishes an experience-to-knowledge path: the small model
acts first; feedback enriches the permanent historical archive; selected
errors or cross-experience evidence call for deeper LLM reasoning;
independently validated examples and concepts are learned by small models;
and mastered experiences can move from active memory to cold storage.
Section IV evaluates these elements through selective
error learning, delayed-evidence attention, concept-based memory reduction,
and LLM-guided hierarchical concept formation. The goal is autonomous
growth of reusable small-model knowledge, rather than continuous reliance
on LLM predictions or the requirement that each newly formed abstraction
immediately improve every evaluation metric. IV Verification IV-A Experimental Environment and Evaluation Protocol Objectives and simulated scenarios. We evaluate a small-model-first autonomous learning system in which a compact
predictor acts on routine observations, while an LLM is selectively invoked
to investigate errors and synthesize experience-based abstractions. The goal
is not to make every newly formed higher-level concept immediately more
accurate than its lower-level components. Instead, we ask whether the system
can learn from feedback, recall temporally separated evidence, form and
validate reusable concepts, internalize them into neural predictors, and
manage accumulated experiences more economically. The environment is a symbolic, event-based robot-agent simulation ,
not a physical-robot or raw-image recognition experiment. Every example
contains ordered observations, an action decision, and independent outcome
feedback. The simulator covers (i) contact safety, (ii) low-friction grasp
adjustment, and (iii) uncertain visual observation. In the safety scenario,
a sharp glass or stone fragment may require AVOID_SHARP_CONTACT when contact is likely, whereas smooth edges, avoided contact, or protective
gear are negative/boundary cases. Ceramic and metal fragments are reserved
for transfer. In the grasp scenario, a wet cup or polished box with low
friction and a planned grasp may require ADJUST_GRIP ; high-friction
objects and unplanned grasps provide negatives, while oily bottles and
smooth cans are unseen subtypes. In the perception scenario, glare and
shadows can justify REOBSERVE when a visual decision is necessary;
adequate contrast or a nonvisual task does not. Fogged and backlit scenes
serve as unseen subtypes. Some observations and their consequences are
separated by distracting events, so the correct response need not be
recoverable from the final event alone. These scenarios measure whether
concepts capture combinations of properties rather than merely object names.
The environment oracle produces independent feedback and validates proposed
counterfactuals, but is never used as a predictor input. Methods and configurations. The default task predictor is a bidirectional gated recurrent neural network
(GRU) with a trainable concept-cue adapter. Candidate attention and parent
concept models are neural, with a Transformer-style attention mechanism.
A live DeepSeek-compatible LLM is invoked when the configured error or
concept-evidence policy requests additional thinking; it is not used for
every routine prediction. A candidate LLM proposal specifies observable
necessary/absent conditions, historical evidence references, and possible
positive/negative tests. Generated learning materials must agree with an
independent environment validator, and a proposed parent becomes deployable
only after held-out neural validation. The Formal evaluation comprises 10 seeds, using 320 initial
training examples, 96 validation examples, 360 online
events, and 180 hold-out instances in the task experiments.

The four experiments substantiate selective reasoning, measurable
small-model learning, memory abstraction, and verified hierarchical
concept formation, but do not demonstrate open-ended concept invention
from raw imagery or physical-robot interaction. The task-matched
budget is not equal total computation, and the trained learned
hierarchy does not outperform flat or random controls consistently.
The archived experiences are not destroyed; memory reduction is the
size of the active working subset. Some concept generalizations
produce false positives. Finally, an LLM-output schema compatibility
correction was applied during checkpoint recovery; the completed
cohort is usable for mechanism analysis, but a strictly uniform
LLM-prompt protocol is advisable when interpreting fine-grained
request-cost differences in subsequent reproductions. V Conclusion This paper presented SCALE, a small-model-first framework that selectively
uses large language model (LLM) reasoning to transform accumulated experiences
into reusable lightweight knowledge. Routine decisions are handled by a small
predictor, while verified errors and evidence distributed across historical
episodes trigger reflection, counterfactual example generation, and concept
proposals. Independently validated concepts are internalized as hierarchical
neural representations, and redundant mastered experiences are removed from
active memory without discarding their original records. Across ten random
seeds, historical recall increased held-out accuracy from 95.39% for
continual replay to 98.28%, while learned concept cues improved accuracy
on challenging cases from 55.33% to 66.47%. Abstract archiving reduced
active experience records from 6,000 to 1,528. These results support
experience-driven knowledge formation and more compact active memory, although
LLM-guided abstraction did not consistently outperform rule-based concept
