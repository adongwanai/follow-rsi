However, positive action credits can continue to favor actions that repeatedly achieve the same score, even when higher scores are achievable. Under a fixed interaction budget, trying new actions in the final attempts can also lower performance. We introduce EARN, a method that uses past experience to guide further progress and retain earlier performance through conditional reuse of effective action sequences. EARN reduces action credits for reaching scores repeatedly attained in earlier episodes and assigns larger credits to action segments that reach higher scores. These credits adjust the probabilities of LLM-proposed actions. During the final episodes, EARN directly replays stored actions when the current context matches the recorded context and the actions remain available, bypassing LLM candidate generation. We evaluate performance at the end of the interaction budget using the average score over the final four episodes. Across Jericho, ScienceWorld, and Crafter, EARN achieves the best score on this metric in 31 of 33 task–budget settings among the evaluated methods, including ties. Across the evaluated Jericho budgets, replay bypasses 78.9–86.3% of candidate-generation calls in the final four episodes, averaged across games. 1 Introduction Completing interactive tasks, such as text-adventure games, multimodal open-world survival games, and virtual science experiments, typically requires an agent to perform a sequence of actions based on current observations. Because the right action is not always clear, the agent may try different actions, observe their outcomes, and adjust its choices. Specifically, an LLM agent uses the task description, current observations, and interaction records in its prompt to select the next action [ 1 ] . If an attempt is unsuccessful, the agent can start again and try a different sequence. Importantly, the earlier attempt still provides useful information [ 2 ] . For example, a failed attempt to open a door may reveal that the agent must first obtain a key. Such information is particularly useful in later attempts, when the agent can avoid the same mistake and focus on completing the remaining task. Thus, earlier experience can help the agent make better choices rather than repeat the same failed actions. Existing methods help agents reuse experience without updating the LLM’s parameters. Specifically, Reflexion stores written reflections to guide later attempts [ 2 ] , while EvoTest revises prompts, memory, and other agent settings [ 3 ] . JitRL more directly adjusts action probabilities using returns from earlier interactions [ 4 ] . However, actions that earned points in the past do not necessarily help the agent achieve higher scores. For example, the agent may find a key and open the first door to earn points in every attempt, yet fail to complete the remaining task because it misses an important action. Although these actions remain useful, continuing to prioritize them simply because they previously earned points may lead the agent to repeat the same actions and obtain the same score. Thus, using past results effectively requires distinguishing repeated progress from progress beyond what the agent has already achieved. Therefore, two challenges arise. (1) Moving beyond previously achieved results. Actions that earned points in earlier attempts may continue to receive positive credit even when they produce no further progress. For example, an agent may repeatedly open the first door but never complete the remaining task. Thus, experience reuse should distinguish previously achieved progress from new gains. (2) Retaining earlier performance. Choosing actions step by step based on past experience does not ensure that an earlier good result will be reproduced. Even after finding an effective action sequence, the agent may choose different actions midway through a later attempt and obtain a lower score. This matters particularly when the number of attempts is limited and only a few remain. Repeating the earlier sequence can reduce this risk, but only when its actions still apply to the current situation. For example, without the key, the agent cannot repeat an action that previously unlocked the door. Therefore, reusing an effective sequence requires checking whether the current conditions still support its execution. Figure 1: EARN uses earlier scores to guide action credit assignment. We introduce EARN, a training-free method that uses persistent memory to adjust action probabilities and conditionally reuse a previously effective action sequence (Figure 1 ). We group consecutive episodes into blocks and select the highest-scoring trajectory from each block. From this trajectory, EARN assigns numerical credits to actions leading up to a score increase, using these credits to guide later choices. Actions receive less credit when the resulting score does not exceed a level reached more than once in earlier episodes. To also use information from the other trajectories, EARN computes step-wise credits after every episode. During action selection, it combines the two credit estimates and reduces the block-wise estimate when few records match the current situation and candidate action, or when their credit values vary substantially. Separately, EARN stores a route containing an ordered action sequence and the situation recorded before each action. During the final episodes, the agent directly executes stored actions while the recorded and current situations match and the actions remain available. Otherwise, it resumes memory-guided action selection. We evaluate whether this combination of memory-guided decisions and conditional route reuse improves performance in the final episodes across Jericho [ 5 ] , ScienceWorld [ 6 ] , and Crafter [ 7 ] . Our evaluation covers three aspects: final-four average scores relative to existing baselines, retention of previously achieved scores and inference savings when routes remain executable, and adaptation when task instances change. Together, these experiments assess whether EARN can improve later decisions and reuse accumulated experience under both repeated and changing execution conditions. Our contributions are threefold: • We introduce EARN, a training-free method that combines progress-relative block-wise credit attribution from selected trajectories with step-wise feedback from every episode to adjust candidate-action probabilities through support-controlled fusion without parameter updates. • We develop conditional route replay to complement credit-guided action selection. It preserves an applicable route’s action order and reduces LLM candidate-generation calls by directly executing stored actions. When replay stops, memory-guided action selection resumes. • We evaluate EARN across three interactive benchmarks. Among the compared methods, EARN achieves the highest or tied-highest final-four average episode score in 14 of 15 Jericho game–budget settings, 14 of 15 ScienceWorld task–budget settings, and all three Crafter budgets. 2 Related Work Test-time adaptation of LLM agents. Existing methods differ in what they retain from earlier episodes and how the agent uses that information in later decisions. Some methods store text derived from earlier episodes: Reflexion [ 2 ] stores verbal reflections on task feedback, CLIN [ 8 ] records causal relations between actions and observed effects, and ReasoningBank [ 9 ] distills reasoning strategies from successful and failed trajectories. Beyond updating memory, EvoTest [ 3 ] revises the agent’s prompt, hyperparameters, and tool-use routines. Other methods store numerical estimates for actions. REMEMBERER [ 10 ] retrieves past actions and their estimated scores to guide action selection through the prompt. JitRL [ 4 ] is the closest prior method to EARN. It uses an LLM evaluator to assign numerical feedback to actions, computes discounted returns, and averages retrieved returns for each candidate action to adjust action probabilities. EARN instead uses a score repeatedly attained in earlier episodes as a reference for assigning action credits and discounts credits for score increases up to that reference. It combines block-wise credits from one selected trajectory per block with step-wise credits from every episode to adjust action probabilities. Reusing behavior from earlier episodes. Prior systems represent behavior from earlier interactions as trajectories, textual guidance, workflows, or executable skills. ExpeL [ 11 ] , Synapse [ 12 ] , and Self-Generated In-Context Examples [ 13 ] use earlier trajectories as in-context examples from which the LLM generates new actions. AutoGuide [ 14 ] derives textual guidelines from trajectories and pairs each guideline with a description of the context in which it applies. Agent Workflow Memory [ 15 ] derives reusable workflows from trajectories and includes the workflows in prompts for later tasks. Voyager [ 16 ] stores executable code skills that can be retrieved and composed. EARN stores a route containing recorded actions and their preceding execution contexts, then directly executes those actions in order while execution checks pass. 3 Problem Formulation Preliminaries. Persistent Memory. Persistent memory ℳ k \mathcal{M}_{k} stores a route ℛ k ⋆ \mathcal{R}_{k}^{\star} and action-credit records ℰ k \mathcal{E}_{k} . A route is an ordered action sequence extracted from an episode trajectory, together with the execution context recorded before each action. Each credit record contains an execution context, an action, an assigned credit, and the credit source. Step-wise records are added after every episode; block-wise records from score-increase events are added after selecting a trajectory at block completion. Route storage is independent of block-wise selection: after each episode, the agent considers the new trajectory for route retention based on performance and executability. Does EARN improve performance in the final episodes over the compared baselines across interactive environments? Q2: Performance Retention and Inference Savings. When stored routes are broadly applicable, how reliably does EARN reproduce its best acquired performance, and how many LLM candidate-generation calls does conditional consolidation save? Q3: Experience Transfer. When stored routes are inapplicable, how does EARN use accumulated experience to adapt its decisions and maintain performance in the final episodes? 5.1 Experimental Setup Environments and Tasks. Experiments span three benchmarks with complementary demand profiles. Jericho [ 5 ] offers parser-based interactive fiction; we adopt the Focus-5 subset (DeepHome, Detective, Zork I, Zork III, ZTUU) and run lifelong sessions of 30 × \times 30, 50 × \times 50, and 70 × \times 70 (episodes × \times steps per episode). ScienceWorld [ 6 ] is evaluated on five tasks, with at most 100 steps per episode and session lengths of 10, 20, and 30 episodes. Task variations alter objects, materials, or environmental configurations across episodes, requiring stored experience to be checked for applicability to the current instance. Crafter [ 7 ] is an open-ended survival environment evaluated in a multimodal setting using visual observations and textual context. Performance is measured by the number of distinct achievements unlocked (0–22), with at most 100 steps per episode and session lengths of 10, 20, and 30 episodes. Compared Approaches. All approaches share an identical frozen W4A16-quantized backbone Gemma-4-31B-Instruct [ 17 ] , served locally via vLLM [ 18 ] . Baselines retain their native decoding, memory, retrieval settings. EARN uses an action-generation temperature of 0.8 across all three benchmarks. EARN uses all-MiniLM-L6-v2 [ 19 ] embeddings for semantic retrieval in Jericho and ScienceWorld, exact-context retrieval for action evidence in Crafter. We compare against five baselines that reuse experience while keeping the LLM weights fixed: (1) Reflexion [ 2 ] , which appends verbal self-critiques of past failures to the prompt; (2) AWM [ 15 ] , which distills successful episodes into persisted reusable workflows; (3) FIFO Memory , which prepends full episode transcripts under a first-in-first-out context budget; (4) EvoTest [ 3 ] , which evolves prompts, memory entries, and decoding hyperparameters across episodes; and (5) JitRL [ 4 ] , which converts returns of past trajectories into advantages that rescale candidate-action probabilities. EARN achieves the highest or tied Last-4 score in 14 of 15 Jericho game–budget settings (Table 1 ) and the best score in 14 of 15 ScienceWorld settings (Table 2 ), including ties. Crafter results evaluate its performance in multimodal interaction (Table ). Across these settings, the results support the value of selectively reusing accumulated experience for terminal performance, both when stored routes remain applicable and when decisions require adaptation to the current context. Larger interaction budgets do not uniformly yield higher scores, highlighting that how experience is used matters alongside how much is collected. When stored routes remain applicable, EARN combines reliable performance reproduction with fewer candidate-generation calls. At 50 × 50 50\times 50 on Jericho, Last-4 retention reaches 1.00 on every game, compared with five-game averages of 0.55 for EvoTest and 0.05 for JitRL (Figure 3 ). EARN therefore matches its run-best scores throughout the final four episodes, rather than recovering only in the last episode. On Jericho, replay avoids 78.90–86.27% of candidate-generation calls in the final four episodes. These final-window replay steps correspond to whole-run call savings of 4.94–12.39%, averaged equally across games (Figure 4 ). Retention measures reproduction of each run’s own peak, not global optimality. The saved calls could support additional exploration under a fixed inference budget, although the performance benefit of such reallocation remains untested. Experience Transfer (Q3). In ScienceWorld, task variations change the environment across episodes, so stored routes may become inapplicable. EARN can still use retrieved experience and action-level evidence to adjust candidate preferences, while generating actions conditioned on the current observation. This separates experience reuse from fixed-route execution: changes in object locations or task requirements need not invalidate all previously acquired evidence. EARN achieves the best or tied Last-4 score in 14 of 15 task–budget settings (Table 2 ). EARN reuses experience without requiring an entire route to remain applicable: retrieved memory informs candidate generation, while supported action-level evidence adjusts preferences under the current observation. This enables selective reuse of prior experience even when environmental changes prevent direct replay. 5.3 Ablation Study and Method Analysis Ablation Studies. We remove block-wise credit, step-wise credit, and conditional replay individually on ZTUU and Zork III to assess their contributions to final-episode performance and examine whether the gains come solely from replay. All settings use 30 episodes and 30 steps each. As shown in Table 5 , removing either block-wise credit or conditional replay reduces Last-4 from 20.8 to 12.0 on ZTUU and from 4.0 to 3.0 on Zork III. The decline without block-wise credit, even with replay retained, supports contributions from both credit-based action adjustment and route reuse. The effect of step-wise credit varies across tasks: removing it hurts ZTUU, supporting the use of feedback from all trajectories to complement block-wise experience, but improves both Last-4 and Last on Zork III. Additional step-wise feedback therefore does not necessarily improve performance, motivating further analysis of its contribution strength. Sensitivity Analysis. Block-wise selection prioritizes high-performing trajectories, while step-wise updates retain feedback from every episode. Conditional route replay reuses applicable action sequences during the final episodes, bypassing LLM candidate generation and returning to memory-guided selection when replay ends. Across Jericho, ScienceWorld, and Crafter, EARN achieves the best or tied-best Last-4 score in 31 of 33 task–budget settings, with substantial candidate-generation call savings on Jericho. Appendix B Additional Related Work Attribution and Credit Assignment in Sequential Decision Making Credit assignment in sequential decision making aims to determine which historical actions led to delayed outcomes. RUDDER reallocates delayed rewards by decomposing returns, attributing them back to critical decision points; similarly, JitRL estimates the advantage of current candidate actions using returns from past trajectories [ 20 , 4 ] . Memory Retention Prior work studies example-level forgetting [ 21 ] and memorization dynamics in language models [ 22 ] . Continual learning methods mitigate forgetting through parameter regularization [ 23 ] , episodic-memory constraints [ 24 ] , and replay with small memory buffers [ 25 ] . Experience Replay for Continual Learning [ 26 ] uses replay to reduce forgetting, while subsequent work explores interference-based replay selection [ 27 ] and preservation of past model outputs [ 28 ] . Do Your Best and Get Enough Rest for Continual Learning [ 29 ] examines how replay intervals affect retention. Distributionally robust memory evolution dynamically adjusts the memory data distribution to reduce buffer overfitting and alleviate forgetting [ 30 ] . In our setting with a frozen model, we define late-stage memory retention as the fraction of final episodes attaining the run’s peak score. Continual Learning for Agentic Systems Continual learning requires an agent to retain previously acquired effective behaviors while assimilating new experiences. Voyager achieves long-term capability accumulation in open worlds through automatic curriculum design and an executable skills library, whereas studies like LifelongAgentBench highlight that simply saving and replaying all historical experiences are prone to interference from irrelevant information and context length limitations [ 16 , 31 ] . Our study addresses cross-episode adaptation under a fixed budget: the environment resets between episodes, but the memory persists. Execution contexts combine normalized observations with supplementary state information and an environment-provided state identifier when available. Actions are matched after normalizing case, whitespace, and surrounding punctuation. Retrieval from Similar Observations. In Jericho and ScienceWorld, semantic retrieval supplements insufficient exact matches with records from similar observations. For block-wise retrieval, estimates from similar contexts are combined using observation-similarity weights and a transfer coefficient. Step-wise retrieval instead pools additional records before estimating action values. For Crafter, we disable this observation-text retrieval mechanism in our multimodal configuration and use exact-context retrieval for action evidence. Similarity-based retrieval does not replace the context checks required for route replay. Memory-Based Probability Adjustment. We normalize the LLM-reported confidence scores. When memory candidates are added, we assign them initial weights and renormalize to obtain p 0 ​ ( a ∣ s ) p^{0}(a\mid s) . Fused advantages are standardized using the candidate mean and population standard deviation, then clipped to [ − ζ , ζ ] [-\zeta,\zeta] . If the standard deviation is below 10 − 8 10^{-8} , the divisor is replaced by the larger of one and the maximum absolute centered advantage. Base probabilities are floored at 10 − 12 10^{-12} before taking logarithms. We fix ζ = 3 \zeta=3 and clip standardized estimates to [ − 3 , 3 ] [-3,3] before probability adjustment, bounding the credit-dependent exponent to [ − 3 ​ β , 3 ​ β ] [-3\beta,3\beta] . For a final candidate set of at most five actions, population-standardized estimates lie within [ − 2 , 2 ] [-2,2] , so this bound leaves them unchanged. Route Storage and Replay. Route memory is updated after each episode, independently of block-wise credit attribution. Candidate routes are compared by score, with shorter routes preferred when scores tie. Before each replayed action, the agent checks context agreement, action availability when a valid-action list is supplied, and stored failure-based restrictions. A failed check or exhausted route ends replay for the current episode, and the agent resumes memory-guided candidate generation and probability adjustment. Case analysis. Figure 5 shows that the key to conditional replay is that it reuses a validated route prefix without locking the agent into the entire stored trajectory. Block-wise credit emphasizes progress in high-performing trajectories, while step-wise credit retains feedback from other episodes; when replay no longer applies, the agent can return to memory-guided action generation. ZTUU’s final episode illustrates this flexibility. The first 22 replayed steps bring the score to 12. After the replay diverges at step 22, the agent resumes generation, obtains further progress through take red lens and take coin , and recovers its previous best score of 22. Thus, replay preserves a reliable prefix while leaving the remaining steps open to adaptation, rather than forcing the agent to repeat an entire old route. The avoided LLM calls could potentially be reallocated to additional exploration under a fixed call budget, provided further environment interactions are available; this reallocation is not evaluated here. Appendix G Additional Controlled Comparisons G.1 Baselines with Their Native Implementations We run Reflexion, AWM, EvoTest, and JitRL following the protocols in their publicly available implementations. Each retains its method-specific decoding, memory, and retrieval settings. For FIFO Memory, we prepend full transcripts of previous episodes to the prompt and remove the oldest transcripts when the memory context budget is exceeded. Within each benchmark, all methods use the same frozen backbone, task instances, interaction budgets, and scoring protocol. This preserves their distinct decision mechanisms while controlling the model and evaluation conditions. G.2 EARN under the Matched Evaluation Protocol EARN uses the same backbone and evaluation protocol as the baselines, without parameter updates. For EARN, we set the action-generation temperature to 0.8 across all three benchmarks, five candidate actions, and all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embeddings for semantic retrieval.
