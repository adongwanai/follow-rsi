Tactile sensing is an indispensable modality for contact-rich dexterous manipulation, a capability that hints
at the ultimate frontiers of embodied intelligence. However, commercially available tactile sensors are still
at a relatively early stage of development, with each type of sensor offering irreplaceable strengths alongside
its own drawbacks. A key challenge is thus to free tactile understanding from sensor-specific physics, so
that embodied models can effectively leverage heterogeneous tactile data. To this end, we develop LingBot-
Touch, a tactile foundation model characterized by the following properties. (i) Our model yields a unified
representation across different tactile sensors (i.e., piezoresistive, magnetic, and visuotactile sensors), and is
easily extensible to new sensor types. (ii) The resulting tactile tokens are plug-and-play: they can be seamlessly
integrated into policy models across paradigms, from diffusion policy to vision-language-action (VLA) models
such as π0.5 and LingBot-VLA, consistently enhancing manipulation dexterity. (iii) Extensive experiments,
spanning simulation and real-robot deployments across multiple hardware platforms, demonstrate that our tactile
representation substantially benefits complex manipulation tasks while maintaining strong computational
efficiency. We believe the findings in this work demonstrate the potential of leveraging cross-sensor data to
scale tactile learning, and offer encouraging insights for the design of future hardware and embodied models.

Introduction
Dexterous manipulation with multi-fingered hands is among the most remarkable abilities of humans, and giving robots
the same ability is a central goal of physical intelligence. Much of that ability rests on touch: with fingertip sensation
blocked, people struggle with tasks as simple as striking a match or buttoning a shirt, even with vision intact [16].
Robots, by contrast, still manipulate objects almost entirely by sight [8, 10, 31], and sight cannot tell them how hard
they are pressing, whether a grasp is slipping, or what is happening under an occluding finger.
Why not touch? Not for lack of sensors. Piezoresistive, magnetic, and visuotactile sensors are all commercially
available (Fig. 1, top), but touch remains an early-stage modality that lacks the three ingredients that let vision and
language scale. There is no common input format: each sensor type defines its own channels, layout, resolution, and
sampling rate [7, 37], so a reading from one sensor means nothing to a model trained on another. There is no large
curated corpus, because data from one sensor cannot be pooled with data from the next and every hour requires a
sensorized hand touching the world. And there is no established recipe: evidence that touch helps robot learning [14, 37]
is scattered across sensor-specific studies and individual tasks, with no shared representation to compare them on.
The three gaps share a root: tactile data are tied to the physics of the sensor that produced them. Contact itself is
not. A fingertip pressing on an edge is the same event whether a magnetometer, a resistive grid, or a camera behind
a gel records it, so a representation of contact can be shared once sensor-specific physics is removed from the input.

In this paper, we present LingBot-Touch, a tactile foundation model that learns such a representation from the three
dominant sensing principles, piezoresistive, magnetic, and optical, with one encoder, and extends to new sensors without
retraining. Three design decisions make this possible. We map every sensor’s taxels into a canonical fingertip space,
so that a model sees positions on a finger rather than indices in a sensor-specific array (Sec. 3.2). We train with a
joint-embedding predictive architecture [1, 4] that predicts representations of masked taxels in latent space, which suits
signals that are sparse and carry no labels (Sec. 3.2). And we assemble a 5,000-hour corpus from human tactile gloves
and teleoperated robot hands, the first at a scale where representation learning for touch becomes possible (Sec. 3.1).
Trained once on this corpus, LingBot-Touch behaves the way a foundation model should (Fig. 1, bottom). Its
frozen features recover contact force, location, extent, and short-term dynamics from fingertip signals and retrieve the
matching video frame from touch alone, on an optical hand seen in pretraining and on a magnetic hand whose sensor
model was not; on the latter it outperforms FTP-1 [34] fitted with a sensor-specific adapter on all five probing tasks
(Secs. 4.2, 4.3). Its tokens plug into existing policies without changing them: on 26 real-world dual-arm tasks, adding
the frozen 5.46M-parameter encoder raises the success rate of Diffusion Policy [10], π0.5 [8], LingBot-VLA [31], and
T-Rex [21] by 14 to 26 percentage points, while feeding the same policies raw tactile signals yields at most about half
that gain (Sec. 5). In simulation, reinforcement learning on LingBot-Touch features succeeds on 69% of attempts
across twelve dexterous tasks, against 46% with features from a task-specific CNN and 21% without touch, and with
far lower variance across seeds (Sec. 5.3). The representation also scales: the Small model improves monotonically
from 1K to 5K hours of pretraining on every probing task and both hands (Sec. 4.3), data from one sensor improves the
representation of another (Sec. 4.3), and latent prediction outperforms signal reconstruction by a wide margin, with a

Our contributions are as follows.
• A tactile corpus at scale. 5,000 hours of recordings, 464K episodes, and 1.33 billion frames across piezoresistive,
magnetic, and optical sensing, collected from human tactile gloves and teleoperated robot hands over 24 task categories.
• A tactile foundation model. LingBot-Touch combines a canonical fingertip space, modality-specific tokenizers, and
a shared Haptic Transformer, trained with Tactile-JEPA, a latent-prediction objective adapted to sparse, unlabeled
tactile signals through a mask warm-up and layout augmentation. One encoder serves all three sensing principles and
transfers to an unseen sensor model without retraining.
• Evidence across the stack. Frozen-encoder probing of contact physics on two hands, 26 real-world manipulation tasks
with four policy families, and twelve reinforcement-learning tasks in simulation, together with scaling, multi-source,
and objective analyses that explain where the gains come from.
• An open release. The inference code, a small set of sample tactile recordings, and checkpoints for the Tiny and Small
variants pretrained on approximately 5,000 hours of tactile data will be released. These resources will help researchers
integrate the tactile encoder into downstream robot learning systems.

Related Work
2.1
Tactile Sensing
Tactile sensors capture contact geometry, forces, and interaction dynamics through diverse transduction mechanisms.
Magnetic sensors, such as ReSkin [5] and AnySkin [6], sense contact through deformation-induced changes in
magnetic fields; PaXini’s commercial systems also employ Hall-effect sensing [23]. Optical tactile sensors, including
GelSight [35], DIGIT [17], and the fingertip sensors integrated into Sharpa Wave [26], use internal imaging to capture
contact-induced deformation. Resistive and capacitive sensors encode contact through changes in electrical resistance
or capacitance, exemplified by the piezoresistive Scalable Tactile Glove (STAG) [28] and the conformable capacitive
electronic skin DexSkin [30].
These technologies observe shared properties of physical contact through different measurement mechanisms,
producing signals that vary in channel semantics, spatial resolution, sampling rate, and sensing layout. Similar
interactions can therefore yield substantially different observations across sensors. LingBot-Touch addresses this
heterogeneity through a common interface that accommodates diverse measurements while preserving contact geometry
and temporal structure, enabling unified spatiotemporal contact representations across sensing modalities.
2.2
Tactile Representation Learning
Self-supervised representation learning has driven recent advances in large language and multimodal models, contributing
to the emergence and development of world models, vision-language-action (VLA) models, and world-action models.
Relevant approaches can be broadly grouped into joint-embedding architectures, such as DINO [9], CLIP [24],
SigLIP [36], and VideoCLIP [32]; generative architectures, such as MAE [13] and VideoMAE [29]; and joint-embedding
predictive architectures, including I-JEPA [1], V-JEPA [4], V-JEPA 2 [2], LeJEPA [3], and V-JEPA 2.1 [20].
Tactile representation learning adapts these approaches to tactile observations. Sparsh [14] investigates reconstruction,
self-distillation, and joint-embedding prediction for optical tactile sensing. T3 [37] combines sensor-specific encoders
with a shared Transformer backbone to enable transfer across optical tactile sensors and tasks. UniTouch [33] connects
tactile representations to pretrained multimodal embeddings, while AnyTouch [11] combines masked modeling,
multimodal alignment, and cross-sensor matching to learn static and dynamic tactile features. AnyTouch 2 [12]
further emphasizes force perception and temporal dynamics across optical tactile sensors. Sparsh-skin [25] learns
contextual representations from magnetic tactile histories and sensor geometry, whereas Sparsh-X [15] integrates image,
audio, motion, and pressure signals from Digit 360 [18]. HTT [7] addresses heterogeneous optical and array-based
sensors through modality-specific reconstruction and representation alignment between paired sensors. FTP-1 [34] maps
heterogeneous observations into morphology-aware tokens for foundation-policy pretraining and supports adaptation to
new hardware through sensor-specific encoders.

3
LingBot-Touch
LingBot-Touch learns actionable tactile representations for dexterous manipulation policies from data. Two challenges
stand in the way. First, tactile data are scarce. Unlike vision and language, no tactile corpus exists today at the
scale that representation learning requires. We address this directly by collecting 5,000 hours of tactile recordings
(Sec. 3.1). Because this effort spans human tactile gloves and robot hands with different sensors, the resulting data
are heterogeneous in transduction principle, taxel layout, resolution, and sampling rate, which we handle through a
canonical geometric interface and modality-specific tokenizers that feed a shared Haptic Transformer (Sec. 3.2). Second,
these recordings come with at most coarse task-level descriptions, and nothing that labels the contact physics inside a
clip, such as the forces exerted, where and how widely contact occurs, or when it begins and ends. The model must
therefore discover the structure of contact on its own. We address this with a self-supervised objective that predicts
contact in latent space, together with a mask warm-up that keeps training stable on sparse signals (Sec. 3.2), and with
layout augmentation that lets the learned representation transfer across sensors (Sec. 3.3).
Problem formulation.
We represent a normalized tactile clip as X ∈RT ×N×3, where T is the number of frames
and N is the total number of valid taxels across fingers. Each taxel is associated with a three-dimensional position
and a finger identity. Sensor layouts may differ in taxel count and arrangement, while single-channel observations are
zero-padded to the common three-channel interface. We seek a foundational tactile encoder that maps these observations
into a shared representation space, Z = E(g)
θ (X; A), where g selects the sensing-modality branch and A denotes the
canonical five-dimensional token addresses.

3.1
Touch at Scale: 5,000 Hours from Tactile Gloves and Robot Hands
As summarized in Fig. 2, our multi-source tactile dataset comprises 4,983 hours of recordings, 464,431 interaction
episodes, and approximately 1.33 billion frames from piezoresistive, magnetic, and visuotactile tactile sensors. Reaching
this scale is hard because every hour requires a sensorized hand physically interacting with the world. We do so by
combining two ways of producing such data. Human operators wearing tactile gloves generate the bulk of the corpus. A
glove needs no robot, so one operator can record hours of everyday manipulation per day in ordinary environments, and
the resulting egocentric recordings cover a breadth of objects and behaviors that would be slow to script for a robot. We
use piezoresistive and magnetic gloves for these recordings. Teleoperation contributes the remainder: a dual-arm system
with Sharpa Wave dexterous hands, whose fingertips carry visuotactile sensors, records the same families of tasks from
the hand we later deploy on. Gloves account for two thirds of the corpus and teleoperation for the remaining third.
The two sources are matched on behavior, not on hardware. Every episode is assigned to one of 24 task categories,
from opening, closing, and assembly to wiping, pouring, and tool use (Fig. 2), and most categories are covered by more
than one source. The sensors, by contrast, differ in transduction principle, taxels per finger, sampling rate, and fingertip
geometry (Tab. 4). Comparable interactions observed through incomparable sensors are what allow a shared encoder to
learn regularities of contact that belong to no single sensor, which Sec. 4.3 tests directly.
Annotations stop at the episode-level category. No clip carries labels of force, contact location, or contact timing.
During pretraining, we sample clips from the piezoresistive, magnetic, and visuotactile sources with equal sampling
probabilities, and all supervision comes from the latent prediction objective in Sec. 3.2.

3.3
Layout-Agnostic Augmentation
The canonical fingertip space of Sec. 3.2 gives every sensor’s taxels comparable coordinates, but it does not by itself
make the encoder indifferent to layout. Pretraining presents exactly three layouts, one per sensor, and RoPE hands the
backbone their exact coordinates, so nothing prevents it from memorizing three point sets. A hand it has never seen is a
fourth. We therefore treat the layout as a nuisance variable and randomize it during pretraining in two ways: the density
at which a fingertip is sampled, and the precise position of each taxel. Sec. 4.3 tests the result on a sensor absent from
pretraining, and Sec. 4.5 ablates the augmentation.
Multiple sampling densities.
We expose the model to the same interaction at multiple spatial resolutions. For magnetic
sensing, we construct views with 24, 32, 48, 64, 76, and 96 taxels per finger from native layouts containing 116 taxels
on the thumb and 96 on each remaining finger. The 96-taxel view uses a fixed subset of the thumb taxels and retains
7

all taxels on the remaining fingers; lower-resolution views are obtained by resampling the native measurements. For
visuotactile sensing, we aggregate deformation-map responses into 32, 96, or 200 virtual taxels per finger. Resampling
uses local Gaussian weights based on the 3D distances between source and target positions and operates on the observed
response channels. For each training clip, we uniformly sample one view from the resolutions available for its source.
We retain the native layouts of the piezoresistive sources. In particular, the Juqiao and Xspark fabric gloves provide
coarse fingertip arrays of 3 × 3 and 4 × 4 taxels, respectively, making further downsampling undesirable because it
would discard already limited spatial detail. Across the multi-resolution views, the underlying interaction remains shared
while spatial sampling varies, encouraging representations that generalize across resolutions.

4
Experiments: Probing the Tactile Representation
This section asks whether LingBot-Touch learns what it was built to learn, and why. Throughout, the pretrained
encoder is frozen and read out by lightweight probes, so every number measures the representation rather than a
task-specific model. We ask three questions. Does the representation expose the physics of contact, and does it do so
on a sensor the model never saw (Sec. 4.2)? How does it change with more hours, more sensor types, and a larger
model (Sec. 4.3)? Which design decisions matter, from the latent-prediction objective to masking and augmentation
(Secs. 4.4 and 4.5)? We close by looking at the latent space itself (Sec. 4.6). Sec. 5 then asks whether the same frozen
representation helps robots act.
4.1
Setup
Platforms.
All probing experiments are conducted on two dexterous hands. Sharpa Wave uses the visuotactile fingertip
sensors that provided the robotic portion of the pretraining dataset, so its sensor is seen during pretraining. Each
fingertip produces a deformation map at a native resolution of 240 × 240 and a sampling rate of 30 Hz, which we
spatially downsample to 96 taxels for model input. XHand1 uses magnetic fingertip sensors of a model absent from
the pretraining dataset, differing from the pretraining gloves in spatial resolution, sampling rate, and taxel placement.
Each fingertip has 120 native taxels sampled at 30 Hz. In our experiments, we spatially downsample these observations
to 96 taxels per finger, although the model can also directly process all 120 native taxels. XHand1 inputs follow the
same processing pipeline as other magnetic sources: taxel positions are mapped into the canonical fingertip space using
the sensor geometry (Sec. 3.2), and the readings are processed by the magnetic tokenizer. No tokenizer or encoder
parameters are updated to accommodate the new sensor.
Models.
We pretrain two sizes of the Haptic Transformer: LingBot-Touch-Tiny and LingBot-Touch-Small, with
5.46M and 21.76M parameters, respectively. Both use 12 Transformer layers, with embedding dimensions of 192 and
384, respectively. Parameter counts include the tokenizers and shared HaT backbone, excluding the pretraining predictor
and downstream task heads. Unless stated otherwise, results in this section use LingBot-Touch-Small pretrained on
the full dataset.

resultant force vectors over each pair of consecutive frames and then computing their magnitudes. Contact centers are computed as
contact-strength-weighted centroids of valid taxels above the activation threshold, while contact coverage is the fraction of valid
taxels that are active. Future force labels represent per-finger changes in force magnitude at each of the next four frames relative to
the final history frame. Contact centers are supervised only when contact is present.
Protocol.
Following the V-JEPA evaluation protocol [4], we freeze the EMA target tokenizer and target encoder, read
out the encoder’s concatenated multi-level features, and train one attentive probe per task: a cross-attention pooling
layer followed by a linear head. The tokenizer and encoder receive no task supervision. Tab. 5 in Appendix lists dataset
sizes, inputs, outputs, and metrics for every task, while Tab. 6 in Appendix provides the detailed probe training budgets
and hyperparameters.
Tasks.
Five tasks test four aspects of contact (Fig. 5). For contact intensity, fingertip force recovery regresses the
resultant contact force in newtons from fingertip signals. For spatial structure, contact center estimation localizes the
center of pressure, scored by distance error in millimeters, and contact area estimation predicts the fraction of the
fingertip in contact, scored by coverage error in percentage points. For temporal dynamics, future force prediction
forecasts the resultant force over the next four frames from the tactile history and the future finger joint states. For
cross-modal correspondence, tactile-to-video retrieval finds the video frame recorded at the same instant as a tactile clip,
scored by top-5 recall. This retrieval task tests whether touch alone identifies the visual context of an interaction.
4.2
Contact Physics from a Frozen Encoder
Fig. 6 reports all five tasks on both hands and compares against FTP-1 [34], a foundation tactile policy that maps
heterogeneous sensors into a morphology-aware token space through sensor-specific encoders. FTP-1 is the closest prior
encoder in scope, since it is built for heterogeneous sensors and includes a magnetic branch. For FTP-1, we freeze the
released shared tactile backbone and train a separate input adapter for each sensor and probe. The Sharpa Wave adapter
encodes 96 deformation values per finger, replacing the released image tokenizer, while the XHand1 adapter encodes
120 three-channel taxel measurements per finger. Each adapter is jointly trained with its probe head for 100 epochs
on the corresponding downstream training split. For LingBot-Touch, we freeze the target encoder and train only the
probe head, without an additional input adapter.
On the sensor seen in pretraining.
On Sharpa Wave, LingBot-Touch recovers fingertip force to within 0.81 N on
sensors with a 0 to 30 N range, locates the contact center to within 0.12 mm, and estimates coverage to within 0.66
percentage points, against 2.31 N, 2.19 mm, and 2.90 points for FTP-1. Future force error falls from 1.67 to 1.04 N. The
widest gap is in retrieval, where top-5 recall rises from 16.5% to 72.6%: the representation carries information about the
interaction as a whole, not only about the local contact patch. These margins do not depend on the full corpus. With
1,000 hours of pretraining, force and future-force errors are already below 2 N, contact-center error is below 1 mm, and
coverage error is below 2 points (Fig. 7).

4.4
Latent Prediction versus Signal Reconstruction
Sec. 3.2 motivates latent-space prediction for sparse tactile signals. We evaluate this choice by pretraining three models
on identical multi-source tactile data with matched hyperparameters: a variational autoencoder (VAE) and a masked
autoencoder (MAE), both of which reconstruct signals, and LingBot-Touch. Each model is pretrained at two data
scales, 1K and 4K hours, and evaluated on the five probing tasks (Fig. 9). To ensure comparable model capacity, VAE and
MAE each use 5.46M parameters, matching LingBot-Touch with the HaT-Tiny backbone. MAE and LingBot-Touch
also use the same masking ratio of 80% in this comparison.
Training dynamics.
The VAE loss drops to a low plateau within a few hundred steps and stays there; the MAE loss
converges to a small reconstruction error. Neither optimization signal corresponds to a good representation. LingBot-
Touch shows a different trajectory: the loss falls, then rises toward a nonzero plateau as the EMA targets evolve, the
expected signature of a target that keeps moving. Because the three losses live in different spaces, we compare the
representations only through probing.
Probing.
LingBot-Touch is best or tied-best on every task at both scales. At 1K hours it cuts the contact-center error
of either reconstruction model to less than a third (0.90 mm against 3.03 for the VAE and 3.07 for the MAE) and reaches
32.1% retrieval recall against 4.4% and 5.6%. At 4K hours it improves on all five tasks, to 0.32 mm and 49.3% recall.
The reconstruction objectives do not scale the same way. The VAE gets worse with more data on four of five tasks, its
contact-center error rising from 3.03 to 4.08 mm and its recall falling from 4.4% to 3.1%; the MAE improves on four
tasks but its recall stays below 9%. Reconstructing a signal that is mostly zeros rewards modeling the background, and
more background does not help. Predicting in latent space never sees the background as a target.

Ablation Study
Tab. 1 varies one design choice at a time and probes the frozen encoder on the five Sharpa Wave tasks. All variants use a
Tiny encoder pretrained for 100 epochs on the 1K-hour subset, so absolute numbers sit below those of the full models

Masking.
An 80% spatial mask is best on all five metrics; against 90% it lowers force error from 2.28 to 1.69 N

same tension that motivates the mask warm-up of Sec. 3.2. Extending the mask across the whole clip beats shorter
temporal spans on four of five metrics, confirming that the predictor should not be allowed to copy a masked taxel from
a neighboring time step.

4.6
What the Latent Space Encodes
We close by looking at how LingBot-Touch organizes short tactile histories, using the phases of robot manipulation as
the lens.
Protocol.
We use the Tiny variant of LingBot-Touch pretrained on the full pretraining dataset. Features are extracted
from the frozen target encoder using 16-frame windows with a stride of two frames. We average token features within
each finger over time and taxels, then concatenate the five 768-dimensional finger features into a 3,840-dimensional
representation per window. These representations are projected into two dimensions using UMAP. Each point is colored
according to the annotated interaction phase of its corresponding window: non-contact, contact onset, increasing contact
force, decreasing contact force, or contact release.
Observations.
In every example of Fig. 10, windows without contact collapse into one tight cluster away from
everything else. That part is expected, since their inputs are near zero; what matters is the structure within contact.
Contact windows form smooth, extended curves ordered in time: as finger responses strengthen, weaken, and redistribute
across fingers, consecutive windows occupy neighboring positions along the curve, and distinct phases of the same
interaction occupy distinct segments of it. The representation therefore preserves continuous variation within a contact
episode rather than only the contact versus no-contact distinction, which is what a policy needs in order to time its
actions.

5
Experiments: Robot Learning with Frozen Tactile Tokens
Sec. 4 showed that the representation exposes contact physics to a probe. This section asks whether a robot can use it.
Throughout, LingBot-Touch is a drop-in module: the encoder stays frozen, its tokens are appended to whatever the
policy already observes, and nothing else about the policy or its training changes. We ask three questions. Does adding
the tokens improve imitation-learning and VLA policies on real hardware, and by how much more than adding the raw
tactile signal (Sec. 5.2)? Does it improve reinforcement learning in simulation, in final performance and in the stability
of training (Sec. 5.3)? And does representation quality, as measured by probing, carry over to the policy when we vary
the pretraining data or swap in another tactile encoder (Sec. 5.4)?
5.1
Setup
Platforms.
The same two hands as in Sec. 4, each mounted on a dual-arm system: Sharpa Wave, whose visuotactile
sensors are seen in pretraining, and XHand1, whose magnetic sensors are not. Both hands are also simulated for the
reinforcement-learning experiments, with tactile signals rendered as deformation maps from geometric penetration
depth (Sec. C).
Integration.
On the real robots a frozen LingBot-Touch-Tiny encoder, 5.46M parameters, encodes 16-frame tactile
windows sampled at 30 Hz for each active hand. Trainable cross-attention readouts aggregate its outputs into 40 tokens
per hand, which are linearly projected to each policy’s conditioning dimension and enter the policy as an additional
conditioning input beside vision and proprioception. The encoder adds 0.16%, 0.15%, and 0.13% to the parameter counts
of T-Rex, π0.5, and LingBot-VLA. In simulation, the same frozen encoder processes eight-frame tactile windows, and
trainable cross-attention readouts compress its tokens into features concatenated with non-tactile observations as policy
inputs (Sec. C.3).
Real-world protocol.
We post-train four policies, Diffusion Policy as an imitation-learning baseline and π0.5, LingBot-
VLA, and T-Rex as VLA baselines [8, 10, 21, 31], in three configurations each: the original policy without tactile input,
the policy with the raw tactile signal added, and the policy with LingBot-Touch tokens added. All three see the same
demonstrations, 200 episodes per task and 500 for tasks that need both hands, and each is evaluated over 20 consecutive
trials per task. Sec. B describes every task and its success criterion.
Simulation protocol.
Twelve dexterous tasks, eight on Sharpa Wave and four on XHand1, follow prior tactile
reinforcement-learning suites [19, 22]: Baoding rotation, in-hand rotation, nut assembly, plate spinning, pen spinning,
blind grasping, grasp stabilization, and ball balancing on Sharpa Wave, and Baoding rotation, ball balancing, blind
grasping, and grasp stabilization on XHand1. Policies are trained with PPO over three seeds and compared on success
rate and return under five tactile observations: none, a binary contact indicator per taxel, the raw signal, features from a
CNN trained with the policy, and frozen LingBot-Touch features.
5.2
Real-World Manipulation with Imitation Learning and VLA Policies
Tasks.
The 26 tasks, 20 on Sharpa Wave and 6 on XHand1, are chosen so that touch matters. They include fine
manipulation (SD card ejection, pen uncapping, straw insertion), contact-rich actions (cap twisting, spray trigger
actuation, zipper closing), fragile and deformable objects (egg transfer, deformable cup transport, bag extraction), tactile
discrimination (stiffness sorting, mahjong sorting), and bimanual coordination (box handover, book extraction). Tab. 2
lists every task and Fig. 11 the means.
Across policies.
Averaged over the 26 tasks, LingBot-Touch tokens raise the success rate of Diffusion Policy from
21.6% to 35.6%, of π0.5 from 48.1% to 69.9%, of LingBot-VLA from 48.3% to 73.1%, and of T-Rex from 45.4% to
68.6%: gains of 13.9, 21.8, 24.8, and 23.2 percentage points for well under a fifth of a percent in parameters. The gain is
not confined to a few tasks. LingBot-Touch is the best of the three configurations in 89 of the 104 task-policy pairs
and at or above the original policy in 99 of them.

Representation, not modality.
The raw-tactile configuration is the control that separates having touch from understand-
ing it. Adding the raw signal helps about half as much at best, 7.0, 6.6, 8.2, and 11.2 points for the four policies, and it
hurts in 22 of the 104 pairs, in some cases badly: LingBot-VLA falls from 28.8% to 5.0% on mahjong sorting and
from 65.0% to 28.8% on egg transfer, and π0.5 from 39.0% to 15.0% on zipper closing. Given 200 demonstrations,
a policy cannot learn to read a sparse, high-dimensional sensor from scratch, and the attempt can distract it from the
signals it already reads. The frozen tokens arrive already meaning something.
On the hand the encoder never saw.
XHand1 makes the point sharply. On its six tasks the raw signal is nearly useless
to the policies, changing the mean success of Diffusion Policy, π0.5, LingBot-VLA, and T-Rex by +4.6, −1.7, 0.0, and
+2.0 points, while LingBot-Touch tokens computed from the same signal, with no sensor-specific training, add 16.3,
24.5, 17.8, and 13.8 points. Whatever makes an unfamiliar sensor hard for a policy to read, the canonical representation
of Sec. 3.2 removes it.
Where the gains come from.
The largest per-task gains sit at the step of a task that vision cannot resolve: whether the
zipper slider is engaged (LingBot-VLA, 11% to 55%), whether the trigger has been pulled far enough (LingBot-VLA,
42.5% to 87.5%), whether the card has released (T-Rex, 32% to 92%), whether the cap has broken loose (T-Rex, 56% to
90%), and whether the top cup has separated from the stack (π0.5 on XHand1, 32.5% to 72.5%). In the recorded runs
the tactile-conditioned policies adjust grip and timing at exactly those moments instead of replaying the demonstrated
trajectory. LingBot-Touch is not uniformly better: it falls below the raw-signal configuration in 9 of the 104 pairs, five
of them for Diffusion Policy, and below the original policy in 5. Sec. B analyzes the behaviors task by task.

Stability.
The standard deviation across seeds tells the other half of the story. For LingBot-Touch it never exceeds

return. A frozen representation that already separates contact states lowers the burden on exploration, and policies that
start from it converge both faster and more reliably.

Conclusion and Discussion
We presented LingBot-Touch, a tactile foundation model that learns a shared representation of contact from piezoresis-
tive, magnetic, and visuotactile sensors. A canonical fingertip space gives heterogeneous sensors one input interface,
and Tactile-JEPA trains a shared Haptic Transformer by predicting contact in latent space. Pretrained once on 5,000
hours of recordings from tactile gloves and teleoperated robot hands, the encoder is frozen and used as is.
Three findings follow. The representation belongs to contact rather than to a transducer: without any parameter
update, the encoder recovers contact physics on a magnetic hand absent from pretraining and outperforms FTP-1 fitted
with a sensor-specific adapter. The gain for robot learning comes from the representation, not the modality: frozen
tokens improve imitation learning, VLA, and reinforcement learning policies across 26 real-world and twelve simulated
tasks, while the raw signal helps at most half as much and often hurts. And the representation keeps improving with
hours, sensor families, and model size, where signal reconstruction stalls.
These results suggest how to scale touch. No single sensor has thousands of hours, and it does not need them:
intensity and dynamics transfer across sensing principles, so data can be collected with whatever sensor is easiest to
record and spent on whatever hand is deployed, at some cost in spatial precision. We hope this informs the design of
future tactile hardware and of embodied models that treat touch as a first-class input.
Limitations.
First, real-world and simulation experiments need to be conducted using a wider variety of hand
embodiments. Second, sensor coverage can be extended further. Capacitive sensors are currently absent from
pretraining because the capacitive recordings available to us did not meet our quality requirements. Finally, the
canonical geometry and pretraining layouts focus on the fingertips, leaving contacts on the palm and elsewhere along the
fingers underrepresented. Extending layout coverage from the fingertips to the whole hand is an important next step.

C
Simulation-Based Evaluation of Tactile Representations
C.1
Simulation Probing
Since LingBot-Touch is pretrained on multi-source real-world tactile data, its ability to generalize to simulated tactile
observations remains to be evaluated. Before conducting reinforcement learning experiments, we perform simulation-
based probing to assess whether the pretrained representations preserve contact-related physical information in the
unseen simulation domain. We collect 7,200 seconds of simulated single-hand grasping data on the Sharpa Wave
platform, covering tactile interactions with 47 objects across diverse grasp poses. We conduct four probing tasks:
fingertip force recovery, contact center estimation, contact area estimation, and future force prediction. We use the same
training hyperparameters and evaluation metrics as in the real-world probing experiments.
As shown in Fig. 16, the contact center and contact area prediction results indicate that the pretrained LingBot-
Touch representations retain decodable spatial contact information in simulation. With the encoder frozen, the probe
heads recover contact locations, spatial distributions, and coverage. Across the complete test set, contact-positive
regions achieve a mean Dice score of 0.708 and an area MAE of 1.089 percentage points. These results suggest
that LingBot-Touch representations support the recovery of simulated contact geometry despite the distribution shift
between simulated tactile observations and real-world sensor measurements.
The fingertip force recovery and future force prediction probes further assess force-magnitude and temporal
information. The current-force estimates capture loading, holding, and unloading trends, although deviations in absolute
magnitude remain, with an overall MAE of 3.264 N. Future force-change prediction achieves an MAE of 0.459 N,
indicating that historical tactile features contain information useful for predicting short-term force changes; the absolute
future forces shown in the figure are reconstructed by adding the predicted changes to the observed current force.
Differences in response mechanisms and contact dynamics between Isaac Sim’s deformation-based tactile observations
and real-world tactile measurements contribute to the absolute-force estimation errors in simulation. Overall, the
four probing experiments indicate that frozen LingBot-Touch representations retain useful spatial contact, force-
magnitude, and short-term temporal information in the simulation domain, providing a basis for using LingBot-Touch
representations in subsequent simulation-based reinforcement learning experiments.

C.3
Reinforcement Learning Experimental Settings
Simulation-based Tactile Generation. We generate simulated tactile observations following the geometry-based
penetration depth method of TacMap [27]. Specifically, sensing points and their corresponding surface normals are
predefined on the curved fingertip surfaces. During simulation, GPU-parallelized ray queries are performed against the
object meshes to estimate local penetration depths along the surface normals. These measurements are then assembled
into spatial deformation maps that represent the contact distribution and local fingertip deformation.
Tactile Observation Processing. To accommodate different fingertip geometries, we construct tactile observations
according to the sensing layouts of Sharpa Wave and XHand1. Specifically, Sharpa Wave uses 96 sensing points per
finger, while XHand1 uses 120 sensing points per finger. The simulated deformation measurements are normalized, and
8 consecutive frames are stacked to capture temporal contact dynamics.
Tactile Observation Configurations. To evaluate the contribution of different tactile representations to reinforcement
learning, we consider five observation configurations: No Tactile, Binary Tactile, Raw Tactile History, Tactile CNN, and
the pretrained LingBot-Touch representation. The No Tactile baseline uses only non-tactile observations, including
proprioceptive information. Binary Tactile provides thresholded contact indicators derived from the deformation
measurements, while Raw Tactile History directly uses the temporal sequence of deformation observations. Tactile CNN
employs a trainable tactile encoder optimized jointly with the policy. For LingBot-Touch, the simulated deformation
observations are processed by the frozen pretrained tactile encoder, followed by a trainable readout module that produces
features for downstream control. For the pretrained encoder, the scalar deformation measurements are placed in the
third channel of the three-dimensional tactile input, while the first two channels are set to zero. The resulting tactile
features are concatenated with the corresponding non-tactile observations and provided to the policy network.
Reinforcement Learning Protocol. Within each manipulation task, all observation configurations are evaluated under
the same reinforcement learning framework, with consistent action spaces, reward functions, environment randomization,
and task-specific training settings. The primary difference between configurations lies in how tactile information is
represented and processed before being incorporated into the policy. This controlled comparison allows us to assess the
influence of tactile representations on policy learning efficiency, training stability, and final manipulation performance.
