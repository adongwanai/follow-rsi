action values at discrete control steps and leave the continuity of physical motion to be learned implicitly. We
propose ODE policy, which learns the dynamics of the action rather than the action itself. A neural ordinary
differential equation (ODE) learns the vector field of the action by matching the integrated actions to the targets
over short action chunks in IL and single control steps in RL. A lightweight diversity head captures multi-modal
behavior by randomizing the direction of the field. At deployment, each action is integrated from the previous one,
so the executed trajectory is continuous by construction. We evaluate it as a visuomotor policy, as the action head
of vision-language-action (VLA) and world action models (WAMs), and as the actor of model-based RL against 14
baselines on unimanual, bimanual, dexterous, locomotion, and loco-manipulation tasks. In IL, against flow matching, it
reaches 5.9 × \times the success, 5.4 × \times the generalization, and 3.9 × \times the
mode balance after one epoch, and generates actions 10.8 × \times faster. It mostly moves more smoothly than human demonstrations. On 14 real-world tasks across single-arm, dual-arm, dexterous, and humanoid robots, it achieves the highest
success rate. In RL, it further speeds up SuperMimic for humanoid motion tracking and smooths its actions.

the previous one. In RL, each chunk holds a single action. Robot learning has advanced rapidly in the past few years. In imitation learning (IL), visuomotor
policies ( Chi et al., 2025 ; Zhao et al., 2023 ) , vision-language-action (VLA)
models ( Zitkovich et al., 2023 ; Kim et al., 2025 ; Black et al., 2024 ; Physical Intelligence et al., 2025 ; Bjorck et al., 2025 ) , and world action models (WAMs) ( Zhang et al., 2026 ; An et al., 2026 ; Hou et al., 2026 ) now perform a wide range of manipulation tasks.
In reinforcement learning (RL), actors trained in massively parallel simulation let legged and humanoid robots walk
and track human motions ( Rudin et al., 2022 ; Mittal et al., 2023 ; Liao et al., 2026b ) . Despite their differences,
most of these policies follow the same paradigm. They encode the history of observations and the proprioceptive
state, and map it directly to future actions at discrete time steps. This paradigm has limits in both settings. In IL, it relies on large-scale data and compute to generalize. Yet robot
data are heterogeneous in embodiment, quality, and control interface, and cannot be collected at the scale of
internet data. Training large foundation policies is also costly. Generative policies ( Chi et al., 2025 ; Black et al., 2024 ) further need multiple network evaluations for each chunk. They react to new observations only after the whole chunk
is produced, which limits real-time execution ( Black et al., 2025 ; Bai et al., 2026 ; Jia et al., 2026c ) . In RL, the policy
must gather experience from a large number of interactions ( Rudin et al., 2022 ) . Without explicit
regularization, its actions often oscillate at high frequency. This wastes control effort, wears the actuators, and
hurts the transfer from simulation to reality ( Mysore et al., 2021 ; Raffin et al., 2022 ; Peng et al., 2020 ) . A novel robot policy paradigm that is efficient in learning, data, parameters, and execution, and especially amenable to scaling, is therefore needed. A robot is a macroscopic classical system that moves continuously in time. The actions that command it, e.g., those
teleoperated by humans, are largely continuous as well. A demonstration is therefore largely a sampling of a
continuous action trajectory at the control frequency. Direct-action policies must learn this continuity implicitly
from data. At chunk boundaries, it is not even guaranteed, and the commands jump ( Black et al., 2025 ) . An ordinary
differential equation (ODE) ( Chen et al., 2018 ) naturally describes such continuous change. Applied to the
action, an ODE can model its dynamics, a law of motion shared by every step. Integrating it yields a continuous action
trajectory by construction. Notably, animal motor cortex is thought to generate movement through such dynamics
rather than by encoding its target ( Churchland et al., 2012 ; Shenoy et al., 2013 ) . ODEs have long described robot
motion ( Ijspeert et al., 2013 ; Bahl et al., 2020 ) . However, they fix the form of the vector field or force it to converge to an attractor. Each model is thus limited to a few motions. In this work, we propose ODE policy . It learns the dynamics of the action rather than the action itself,
i.e., it models the policy directly as an ODE (Fig. 2 ). Concretely, a neural
ODE ( Chen et al., 2018 ) learns the vector field of the action, conditioned on the observation. The action is then
integrated over the physical time of the robot. Integrating over a whole demonstration accumulates drift and requires
the costly adjoint method. In IL, we therefore integrate over action chunks ( Chi et al., 2025 ; Zhao et al., 2023 ) , a
key to current direct-action policies ( Pan et al., 2026 ; Lazzati et al., 2026 ) , and supervise the integrated path without any
derivative of the actions. A contractive perturbation shifts the start of each training chunk and teaches the field
to return, which improves robustness. In RL, we integrate over single control steps. At inference, each action is
integrated from the previous one, across chunk boundaries in IL and across control steps in RL.
Direct-action policies, in contrast, often jump or pause at chunk boundaries in IL and oscillate between steps in RL. Finally, a lightweight diversity head adds a sampled offset to the vector
field to retain multi-modal behavior ( Chi et al., 2025 ; Jia et al., 2026a ) . We validate ODE policy across the mainstream IL models and in RL (Fig. 1 ). We instantiate it as a
visuomotor policy, as the action expert of the π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} VLA ( Physical Intelligence et al., 2025 ) , as the action head
of the ImageWAM ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) WAM, and as the actor of SuperMimic ( Liao et al., 2026a ) for
whole-body motion tracking of a humanoid. We further deploy it on a real single-arm robot, a dual-arm robot, a robot
with dexterous hands, and a humanoid, where it replaces the action head of GR00T N1.7 ( Bjorck et al., 2025 ) . The
real-robot tasks include high-precision, long-horizon, deformable, multi-modal, dexterous, and loco-manipulation tasks. We compare with
14 state-of-the-art baselines, including generative and regression-based IL methods. Within each setting, all
methods share the same backbone, data, and training schedule. Experiments show that ODE policy learns remarkably fast . After one epoch, it succeeds in 79.2% of the
LIBERO episodes, against at most 43.2% for the baselines. As the action head of π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} , it succeeds in 96.0%
against 75.5% for its flow matching expert. As that of ImageWAM, it succeeds in 82.6% against 24.6% for the
original head. It matches flow matching with less than half of the demonstrations. As the actor of SuperMimic, it reaches a higher return early in training. On 14 tasks across single-arm, dual-arm, dexterous, and humanoid
robots, it achieves the highest success rate on every task. It also generates actions fast and smoothly .
A chunk takes 10.8 times less time than with flow matching in simulation, and 3.5 to 27 times less than with the
diffusion and flow matching baselines on real robots. On real robots, its motion is smoother than the human demonstrations in most cases. Moreover, it generalizes robustly to unseen perturbations in simulation, and it transfers
dexterous skills learned from public data of another lab to our real robot. It also keeps multi-modal
behavior , both on a navigation task and on a real bimanual task, where it uses both arm assignments. Finally, the
learned field supports data without action labels and arbitrary control rates. 2 Related Work 2.1 ODE in Robot Policies Action dynamics as ODEs. Representing robot motion as a dynamical system has a long history, from
dynamical movement primitives ( Ijspeert et al., 2013 ) and stable dynamical
systems ( Khansari-Zadeh and Billard, 2011 ) to neural vector fields with contraction or stability
guarantees ( Beik-Mohammadi et al., 2024 ; Sochopoulos et al., 2024 ) . Neural dynamic
policies ( Bahl et al., 2020 ; Totsila et al., 2023 ) further predict the parameters of such systems from
images. However, the fixed form or the stability constraint limits each system to a few motions
toward an attractor, mostly on single tasks. ODE policy instead places no structural constraint on
the vector field and learns one field for large-scale tasks. State dynamics as ODEs. A conventional paradigm in robot learning is to learn the
state dynamics of the robot or its environment and then design a policy or analytical controller upon it. Learned
dynamics, often in the form of neural ODEs, are used for model-based reinforcement learning ( Yildiz et al., 2021 ) and for feedback controllers that compensate for model
uncertainty ( Jia et al., 2025b ; Jia et al., 2025a ; Jia et al., 2026b ) . Continuous-time
networks ( Hasani et al., 2022 ) likewise evolve the hidden state of a policy by an ODE. In these
works, the ODE describes the state of the system or of the network, and the action is still
produced by a separate controller or output layer. ODE policy instead learns the ODE of the action
itself. Generative processes as ODEs. Diffusion and flow matching policies ( Chi et al., 2025 ; Black et al., 2024 ) integrate over a generation time without physical meaning. Streaming flow
policy ( Jiang et al., 2025 ) aligns the flow time with the execution time, but it is still
trained by regressing velocities derived from the demonstrations and regenerates each chunk over a
rescaled time interval. Chronos policy ( Wang et al., 2026 ) encodes the physical times of observations and
actions in a diffusion transformer, but time remains an input, and actions are still generated at a fixed rate.
In contrast, ODE policy integrates over physical time itself, is trained through the integrated path without
velocity labels, and continues each chunk from the previous one. 2.2 Action Chunking Predicting a chunk of actions per observation was popularized by action chunking transformer (ACT) ( Zhao et al., 2023 ) and
diffusion policy ( Chi et al., 2025 ) , and is now standard in large foundation
models ( Black et al., 2024 ; Physical Intelligence et al., 2025 ; Bjorck et al., 2025 ) . Recent analyses show that
chunking is a key factor in the performance of current IL policies ( Lazzati et al., 2026 ; Pan et al., 2026 ) .
Executing a chunk open-loop often outperforms updating the action at every observation ( Park, 2026 ) ,
which contrasts with the classical control experience that a higher closed-loop rate is better.
However, independently predicted chunks may be inconsistent at their boundaries, which is mitigated
by temporal ensembling ( Zhao et al., 2023 ) or by conditioning each chunk on the committed
actions of the previous one ( Black et al., 2025 ) . ODE policy also builds on chunking for IL, but uses the chunk as the unit of integration. During training, it
integrates the vector field over one chunk, so the gradient passes through a few solver steps without the adjoint method. At inference, it integrates each action from the previous one, which keeps the actions
continuous across chunks and executes each action without waiting for a whole chunk. Some policies predict actions
relative to the current pose ( Chi et al., 2024 ) , but still output each action of a chunk as a separate value.
In ODE policy, all actions of a chunk follow from one vector field evaluated along the path. 2.3 Efficient Policies Efficient IL policies. Generative policies require multiple network evaluations per
chunk. Consistency models ( Song et al., 2023 ; Zhang et al., 2025 ) , self-distillation ( Li et al., 2026 ) ,
and mean flows ( Geng et al., 2025 ; Fang et al., 2025 ) reduce the sampling to one or a few steps, and
informed source distributions shorten the transport
path ( Scholz and Turner, 2025 ; Dai et al., 2026 ) . VITA ( Gao et al., 2026 ) , action-to-action flow matching (A2A) ( Jia et al., 2026c ; Zuo et al., 2026 ) , and
FLASH ( Bai et al., 2026 ) further replace the flow source with image observations or historical
actions, which improves both learning efficiency and inference speed. These methods improve the efficiency of IL
within the conventional direct-action paradigm. ODE policy instead changes the paradigm to an ODE, which
exploits the inherent continuity of physical motion to improve efficiency. Efficient RL policies. In RL, sample efficiency is mostly improved on the side of the
critic and the update, e.g., by high update-to-data ratios with ensembled or regularized
critics ( Chen et al., 2021b ; Hiraoka et al., 2022 ; Bhatt et al., 2024 ) , scaled
networks ( Lee et al., 2025 ; Kim et al., 2026 ) , or learned world models ( Hansen et al., 2024 ) .
SuperMimic ( Liao et al., 2026a ) learns a differentiable world model and trains the actor by backpropagating
analytic gradients through short imagined rollouts. It surpasses a state-of-the-art motion tracker after 3 hours
on one GPU with more than 1000 times fewer GPU hours. These methods keep the actor as a direct map from states to
actions. ODE policy instead changes the actor itself, and further speeds up SuperMimic as a drop-in replacement of
its actor. 3 Preliminaries This section first formalizes the direct-action policies that ODE policy replaces, in both IL and RL, and then

so that each action is integrated from the previous one. In IL, it learns the vector field by
integrating over short action chunks, seamless chunking joins successive chunks without jumps, and the
contractive perturbation keeps the policy close to the demonstrations. On LIBERO, ODE policy reaches 79.2% after one epoch and 93.8% at
epoch 100, matches FM-DiT with less than half of the demonstrations, and generates a chunk of 8 actions in
5.7 ms. As the action head of the π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} VLA, it reaches 96.0% after one
epoch against 75.5% for its flow matching expert with about 3% of its parameters, and as the action head
of the WAM ImageWAM, 82.6% against 24.6% for the original head, generating a chunk 4.1 times faster.
On 14 real-world tasks across single-arm, dual-arm, dexterous, and humanoid robots, it achieves the highest success
rate on every task, and its motion is smoother than the human demonstrations in most cases.
As the actor of SuperMimic, it reaches a higher return early in training and still 0.885 against 0.845 at the end
of training. These results suggest that building the continuity of physical motion into the policy, rather than learning
it from data, makes robot policies more efficient in learning, data, parameters, and execution, and that this gain scales from
small visuomotor policies to large pretrained models and carries over from IL to RL. Beyond the results, viewing a policy as a vector field in the physical time of the robot opens uses
that direct-action policies do not naturally offer. We share three aspects with preliminary experiments, as
starting points rather than finished solutions, and close with a broader view of what the field may represent. Figure 19 : Two potential uses of the continuous-time field. integration step is a choice made at deployment rather than a part of the model. We take the model
trained with one RK4 step per control step at 20 Hz and change only the integration at deployment
(Fig. 19 (C)). Integrating each control step with 2 or 4 substeps moves the integrated
path by at most 1 mm, against about 10 mm between consecutive actions, and the success rate changes
from 94.8% to 92.8%. Reducing the step to 1 / 2 1/2 or 1 / 4 1/4 and sending every intermediate point runs
the same model at 40 or 80 Hz, with 16 or 32 actions per chunk, at 93.3% and 92.5% success and
the same completion time. In contrast, a conventional policy outputs actions at the fixed step of its
training data and must interpolate them to run at a higher rate, and even policies conditioned on physical
time keep a fixed action rate ( Wang et al., 2026 ) . One model could therefore serve
low-level controllers of different frequencies without retraining, and the integration step might even be
adapted online, e.g., refined near contact where precise control matters most. 9.3 Discrete Action Signals An ODE describes signals that change continuously. Contact tasks nevertheless do not hurt ODE policy, e.g., 96.2% vs. 78.8% for FM-DiT at epoch 10 on eight contact-rich LIBERO tasks. The gripper command, in contrast, is
binary on some robots, e.g., in LIBERO, while it is continuous on our real robots. The LIBERO experiments above
predict the binary command with a separate classification head. This simple head is effective and leaves the
smooth motion of the arm to the field. To probe the ODE itself, we instead integrate the
gripper as one more state of the field and send its sign, with each chunk starting the gripper state at
the last command. Without any further
design, the success rate drops from 79.2%, 95.0%, and 92.8% to 74.5%, 91.8%, and 92.2% at epochs 1, 5, and
10, mostly on Long, which requires repeated grasping and releasing. Even so, it still exceeds all baselines after
one epoch (74.5% vs. at most 43.2%) and at epoch 10 (92.2% vs. at most 86.9%).
Neural ODEs built for discontinuous dynamics, such as learned event functions ( Chen et al., 2021a ) and jump
terms ( Jia and Benson, 2019 ) , might further empower ODE policy. Actions learned without demonstrations need not be continuous either. In our early experiments with model-free RL, the learned actions often jumped between control steps, while the compliant PD controller still kept
the motion smooth. There, ODE policy brought little gain and sometimes even hurt, likely because its continuity
also smooths the exploration. Such jumps are, however, usually treated as a flaw rather than a need, since they
load the motors and widen the gap between simulation and reality, and RL for robots commonly penalizes the change of
actions ( Mysore et al., 2021 ; Rudin et al., 2022 ) . ODE policy thus suits best where the commands themselves should be
smooth, e.g., when imitating demonstrations or deploying on hardware. 9.4 Actions as Unfolding Intentions In physics, differential equations describe how a state changes. The action of a robot is different. It is a
command, an intention of where to go next, so it is not obvious that it should follow such a law. Neuroscience
suggests that it may. When a monkey reaches, its motor cortex does not simply encode the target. Its activity
evolves like a dynamical system from a preparatory state, and the movement unfolds from
it ( Churchland et al., 2012 ; Shenoy et al., 2013 ) . Human reaching is also smooth, close to minimum
jerk ( Flash and Hogan, 1985 ) . ODE policy works in a similar way. At the start of each chunk, it makes a
discrete choice, e.g., which arm places the apple, and the vector field then unfolds the motion. A policy might
thus only need to decide at a few key points of a task, e.g., where its modes branch, and leave the rest to the
learned dynamics. LIBERO has four suites (Object, Spatial, Goal, and Long) with 10 tasks each and 50
demonstrations per task. One model is trained on all 40 tasks and evaluated on each suite separately.
LIBERO-plus perturbs the camera, the initial robot pose, the object layout, the background, the lighting, and the
sensor noise. π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} and ImageWAM are tested on LIBERO and on a shared set of 2008 LIBERO-plus instances, which also perturb the language
instruction. Single-arm robot tasks. All four tasks on the Franka Research 3 arm use multiple cameras, and the actions
are joint angles. In the box task, the arm puts a mango into a box and then closes the lid. Its
40 demonstrations are collected with a SpaceMouse through the HIL-SERL teleoperation
framework ( Luo et al., 2025 ) . In the key task, the arm grasps a key and hangs it on a hook of a wooden key
holder, learned from 40 demonstrations collected in the same way. In the sock task, the arm takes a sock from the
top of a small cabinet, folds it on the table, puts it into the open drawer, and closes the drawer, learned from
40 demonstrations. In the insertion task, the arm grasps a blue block and inserts it into a matching hole of a black
board, learned from 40 demonstrations. Inference times on this robot are
measured on one RTX 5080. Dual-arm robot tasks. All tasks on the dual-arm and dexterous-hand robots use joint angles as actions at
15 Hz. In the flower insertion task, one arm of the Galaxea R1 Lite grasps a long-stemmed poppy or a short-stemmed
dahlia from a glass vase and inserts its thin stem into the narrow opening of another vase, so a small error of
the gripper pose makes the stem miss the opening. One model is trained on both flowers from 95 demonstrations
(38 poppy and 57 dahlia), with a head and a wrist camera. In the microwave task, one arm grasps a bowl of corn,
places it in the microwave, and pushes it in, and the other arm then closes the door. It is learned from 34
demonstrations, with a head and two wrist cameras. In the two-flower task, one arm inserts two flowers one after the other into the
same vase, learned from 95 demonstrations. In the drawer task, one arm puts an apple from the
top of a cabinet into its open drawer, and the other arm closes the drawer. The demonstrations contain both
assignments of the arms, 25 with the left arm and 25 with the right arm placing the apple, with a head and two wrist cameras. Dexterous-hand tasks. The policy runs at 15 Hz, and each predicted chunk is linearly interpolated to
60 Hz joint position targets for the arm and the 22 joints of each hand. In the can task, the Sharpa North robot
picks up a can with its Sharpa hand and places it on a plate, learned from 50 demonstrations. In the cup task, the hand picks up a fallen cup,
reorients it, and places it upright. Its 50 demonstrations are drawn at random from the reorient_cup_upright task of
the public SharpaDex dataset ( Sharpa Robotics, 2026 ) , recorded by Sharpa in another lab, and the policies are tested in
our lab with other cups and another background. In the pill task, the two hands pour pills from one cup into
another and put both cups back. Its 50 demonstrations are those of one recording session of the
transfer_pills_between_cups task of SharpaDex closest to our placement of the cups, and the policies are tested
with cups of other colors. In the stacking task, the hand stacks a red cube on a green cube. Its 50 demonstrations
come from the stack_cube task of SharpaDex, and the policies are tested in our lab with colored cubes instead of the wooden cubes
of the demonstrations. Humanoid tasks. The Unitree G1 humanoid with two Inspire DFX dexterous hands is controlled through
GR00T N1.7 ( Bjorck et al., 2025 ) . As in ImageWAM, ODE policy reads the condition from GR00T once per chunk.
A vector field of about 5M parameters, fine-tuned on top of GR00T, integrates chunks of H = 16 H=16 steps with Euler , with the diversity head as in all IL experiments. The baseline is the original action head of
GR00T N1.7. Visuomotor policies. All methods share the same observation encoder, a ResNet-18 ( He et al., 2016 ) with group normalization ( Wu and He, 2018 ) and random cropping that encodes the last 8 image frames, and a CLIP text
embedding ( Radford et al., 2021 ) of the instruction. Except in the
model-size study, the parameters outside the encoder are matched to about 24M (ODE-MLP 24.40M, ODE-DiT
24.15M, FM-DiT 23.93M, and the others within 5%). All methods are trained with AdamW ( Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019 ) ,
learning rate 10 − 4 10^{-4} , batch size 32, and a cosine schedule defined over 100 epochs, and shorter runs stop
early under the same schedule. The model-size study changes the width of the action head, with
4 layers for both methods. For the 1.5M ODE policy, the network that maps the observation features to the
condition is also narrowed (condition width 136 instead of 512), since it alone has 4.2M parameters at the default
width. Each visuomotor experiment is run with three random seeds, except ODE-DiT with one seed. The data-efficiency study changes only the number of demonstrations per task,
so fewer demonstrations also mean fewer gradient steps per epoch. ODE policy. The vector field is a 4-layer FiLM-MLP of width 1732 that takes the action state and the proprioception as input and is modulated by the condition. It corresponds to the conditional residual block of the 1D UNet of FM-UNet and DDPM-UNet without the temporal convolution, since the field takes a single state rather than a sequence of actions, and we therefore call it ODE-MLP in the tables. ODE-DiT replaces it with AdaLN Transformer
blocks of width 512 and 4 layers (Appendix B.8 ). The state is the desired action of the LIBERO operational space controller, i.e., the current pose plus 0.05 m and 0.5 rad times the normalized command,
so that a unit tracking gain reproduces the demonstrated command exactly. Each chunk of 8 steps is
integrated with RK4 at 20 Hz. Half of the training segments are perturbed by about 1 cm and 0.03 rad with ρ = 0.7 \rho=0.7 . The diversity head has K = 12 K=12 components. LIBERO uses the component means and the navigation task
samples them. The binary gripper is predicted by a separate head. Baselines. FM-DiT, FM-UNet, DDPM-DiT, and DDPM-UNet predict chunks of 8 actions with the DiT or the
1D UNet head of the corresponding generative model. A2A-DiT starts its flow from the history of actions,
perturbed by Gaussian noise of standard deviation 0.5. ACT uses its CVAE Transformer decoder.
FM-DiT and FM-UNet integrate their flow with 10 midpoint steps, i.e., 20 network calls per chunk, DDPM-DiT and
DDPM-UNet denoise in 100 steps, and A2A-DiT generates a chunk in one flow step. As for ODE policy, every
baseline reads the last 8 image frames, predicts a chunk of 8 actions, and executes all of them before the next
observation. Evaluation and metrics. Each LIBERO checkpoint runs 20 episodes for each of the 10 tasks of each
suite from the fixed initial states of the benchmark. Execution quality is measured on the successful
episodes. The jerk is the RMS jerk of the measured end-effector position. The in-chunk and cross-chunk
smoothness are the mean change of the command between consecutive steps inside a chunk and at the first
step of a new chunk, and their quotient is the boundary ratio. The completion time is the number of control
steps. Inference times in the main text are measured during these evaluations on shared GPUs, and times on one idle RTX 4090 are reported in Table S1 . LIBERO-plus samples 50 instances per perturbation type

Each iteration steps 4096
simulated environments once and stores the transitions, trains the world model on them, and updates the actor
by backpropagating the discounted return over 32 steps imagined in the frozen world model, plus the value of the
critic at the last step. The task is whole-body tracking of the 40 complete LAFAN1 clips (2.45 hours at 50 Hz,
100 to 300 s each) with a Unitree G1 humanoid with 29 actuated joints. The action is the offset of the
joint-position targets from the default pose, tracked by a PD controller. The actor encodes the target motion into
a latent 𝐳 t ∈ ℝ 32 \mathbf{z}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{32} and decodes it with 10 frames of proprioception into the action, with
8.91M parameters. We add the anchor-relative pose of BeyondMimic ( Liao et al., 2026b ) to the goal for both
actors, since with proprioception alone the heading drifts over such long clips and the return saturates at about
0.58. The ODE actor keeps this network and its initialization and only reads the decoder output as the velocity in
Eq. ( 11 ), where the previous action is the noise-free output of the actor, so exploration noise does
not accumulate along the integration. Without bounds, the integration turned an early error of the world model
into a drift of the action and a collapse of the return in one of the runs. We therefore bound the change per step
by 1.62 and each joint of the action by its own limit. Both bounds are set slightly above the 99th and 99.9th
percentiles of the actions of converged policies of both actors, so they are rarely active. Everything else, including the world model, the critic, the rewards, the training noise, and the hyperparameters,
follows the reproduction. Each run trains for 3 hours on one NVIDIA H200 GPU, about 33k iterations. Following
SuperMimic, every clip is played once from its first frame with the deterministic policy and without noise or
randomization, and an episode ends when the pelvis deviates from the reference beyond a threshold. We report the
reward per step normalized by its maximum, averaged over all steps of each clip with the steps after a fall counted
as zero and then over the 40 clips, and the mean squared second difference of the executed actions. PPO ( Schulman et al., 2017 ) uses the same environment, observations, rewards, termination conditions, domain
randomization, and clips as SuperMimic, but no world model. It samples its own exploration actions, so the
execution noise of the environment is turned off. Its hyperparameters follow the G1 tracking configuration of
BeyondMimic ( Liao et al., 2026b ) , with 8192 parallel environments and the network size of SuperMimic. Each
run also trains for 3 hours on one H200 GPU. Since an iteration of PPO differs from one of SuperMimic, all curves are
plotted over wall-clock time. Fig. S4 compares the returns of the ODE actor and PPO. The training return
of PPO includes its own exploration noise and is shown only for reference. Figure S4 : Returns in reinforcement learning. The ODE actor of SuperMimic vs. PPO on whole-body
