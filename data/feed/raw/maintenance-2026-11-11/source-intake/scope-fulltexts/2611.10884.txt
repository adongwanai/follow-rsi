Jianwei Yin Affiliation: Zhejiang University, China Abstract Much of the automation handed to language-model agents is procedural: each step needs little intelligence, but a long unattended run must follow a skill or SOP stated once at the start. We show that agents drift from such procedures over long runs, and that restating the instructions or resuming a stopped agent only moves the drift to another shortcut. We study an executable finite-state machine (FSM) that keeps the workflow state outside the model’s context and enforces it at three points: tool calls, state transitions and the end of the agent’s turn. In a pre-registered experiment on 200-account SOP-Bench batches, Claude Haiku 4.5 with the FSM decided on average 139 accounts correctly and by the procedure, against 48 for the strongest baseline (95% CI of the difference [46, 130]), and kept this rate to the end; across three models, the gain tracked how much the plain agent drifted. Because the runtime, not the model, supplies this long-horizon reliability, the FSM can let small models carry procedural work: Haiku 4.5 with it averaged more such decisions than plain Claude Sonnet 4.6 at 38% of the cost. Analyses on SWE-bench Verified, τ 2 \tau^{2} -bench, and staged and cross-context workflows further suggest that reliable long-horizon automation can come from stronger execution control, not only stronger models. Figure 1: Four conditions on one 200-account batch. 1 Introduction Language-model agents now run for hours without supervision, triaging cases, reviewing accounts and working through codebases under a procedure, a skill or standard operating procedure (SOP), stated once as advisory text in the context ( Dong et al., 2026 ) . Much of this work needs little intelligence per step but a long run that keeps to the procedure: long-horizon reliability, not per-step capability, is the bottleneck, and if the runtime can supply it, smaller and cheaper models can do the work. Yet models use the middle of long inputs less reliably and lose track of instructions over many turns (Section 2 ), and agent runtimes add automatic compaction, which summarizes the very context that holds the instructions. The resulting drift is severe, and it is not a lack of knowledge. Figure 1 shows one unattended batch of 200 accounts from SOP-Bench ( Nandi et al., 2026 ) , processed by Claude Haiku 4.5 in Claude Code under four conditions (top to bottom: Plain , the SOP given once; Remind , a reminder of the SOP with every account; Remind-loop , the reminder plus automatic resumption whenever the agent stops; and our FSM ) on seed 8, whose FSM − - Remind-loop difference is closest to the eight-seed mean; each sliver is one account, colored by whether its decision followed the SOP and was correct; quotes are verbatim from the logs, marked where written. The agent follows the procedure for a few dozen accounts and then, citing its token budget, takes whichever shortcut is still open: it batches accounts (Plain), stops with most of the work undone (Remind), or, when resumed, interleaves accounts and finally decides without looking at their data (Remind-loop). Restating the SOP leaves the shortcuts in place, and resuming the agent removes one, after which it takes another; we call these shortcuts escape routes : while one stays open, the drift moves there. Only the FSM run keeps the procedure to the last account: 139 decisions faithful and correct against 29, 37 and 53 (8-seed means 139 against 9, 15 and 48). We study an executable finite-state machine (FSM) that closes them: it turns the procedure into a workflow whose state lives outside the model’s context, where compaction cannot touch it, and that the runtime enforces at three points of the agent loop (Figure 2 ): a tool-call gate admits only the tools the current state permits, a transition guard runs checks before the state may advance, and a stop gate refuses to let the agent end its turn before the workflow is complete. In our main experiment the guards read only which tools were called, for which account and in which order: the FSM enforces how the agent works, not what it decides. In a pre-registered experiment with six non-FSM baselines and two ablations, Haiku 4.5 with the FSM decided 139 of 200 accounts correctly and by the procedure, against 48 for the strongest baseline, and held this rate to the last account. Across three models the gain tracked how much the plain agent drifted: large for Haiku 4.5 and for Claude Sonnet 4.6 sessions that batched accounts, small for Qwen3.8-flash, which mostly held the procedure; and with the FSM the small Haiku 4.5 averaged more such decisions than plain Sonnet 4.6, at 38% of the cost. Three analyses extend this to debugging on SWE-bench Verified ( Jimenez et al., 2024 ) , customer service in τ 2 \tau^{2} -bench ( Barres et al., 2026 ) and improvement loops across fresh contexts. Our contributions are (i) the finding that drift moves between escape routes rather than disappearing, from a design that separates information (where the instructions appear) from liveness (whether the agent is resumed); (ii) closing all routes at once with the three enforcement points of an executable FSM, with a formal account of what this guarantees and ablations showing that each point closes one route; and (iii) evidence across three models and three further settings on when enforcement pays off, including a small model with the FSM averages more faithful and correct decisions than a larger plain model at 38% of its cost. 2 Related Work Instruction following over long horizons. Instruction following is scored per response against constraints or a system message ( Zhou et al., 2023a ; Jiang et al., 2024 ; Qin et al., 2025 ) ; over long inputs and many turns, models use the middle of the context less reliably ( Liu et al., 2024a ) , reason worse ( Levy et al., 2024 ; Hsieh et al., 2024 ) and drift from their instructions ( Li et al., 2024a ; Laban et al., 2026 ) . Agent benchmarks span web, computer and application environments and planning ( Zhou et al., 2024 ; Liu et al., 2024b ; Xie et al., 2024b ; Drouin et al., 2024 ; Trivedi et al., 2024 ; Mialon et al., 2024 ; Xie et al., 2024a ) , and settings where a policy must be obeyed: customer service ( Yao et al., 2025 ; Barres et al., 2026 ) , web-agent safety ( Levy et al., 2026 ) , industrial procedures ( Nandi et al., 2026 ) and long terminal tasks ( Li et al., 2026 ) . Scaffolds, workflows and memory. ReAct interleaves reasoning and actions ( Yao et al., 2023 ) , Reflexion stores verbal feedback ( Shinn et al., 2023 ) , Generative Agents and MemGPT page memories ( Park et al., 2023 ; Packer et al., 2023 ) , and recursive summarization compresses long dialogs ( Wang et al., 2025a ) . SOP-driven frameworks encode procedures as roles and stages ( Hong et al., 2024 ; Zhou et al., 2023b ) , workflow search and memory derive them from experience ( Zhang et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2025c ) , programming models compile prompts into pipelines ( Khattab et al., 2024 ; Beurer-Kellner et al., 2023 ) , and software agents add interfaces, pipelines, program analysis and platforms ( Yang et al., 2024 ; Xia et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2025b ) . All keep the agent better informed or structured, but the procedure stays advisory; our baselines Sys and Remind show that better-placed instructions do not stop the drift. Skills, state machines and executable procedures. Recent work makes skills stateful or executable for efficiency and accuracy: Formal Skill moves skill procedures from prompt text into executable state machines and hook policies with skill-local runtime state ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) , constant-context skill learning replaces the history with a state block rendered by a deterministic tracker ( Xie et al., 2026 ) , and SKILL.state keeps an explicit, mutable execution state ( Badhe et al., 2026 ) . StateFlow drives task solving with state-specific prompts ( Wu et al., 2024 ) , and constrained decoding enforces an automaton on the token stream ( Willard and Louf, 2023 ; Geng et al., 2023 ) or an agent’s plan ( Li et al., 2024b ) . We do not claim the mechanism: where Formal Skill and constant-context skills optimize how efficiently and accurately a skill executes, we hold the procedure fixed and ask whether a long unattended run stays faithful to it, which escape routes the drift takes and when enforcement pays off, in a pre-registered study with ablations. Runtime enforcement. Our enforcement points form an execution monitor in the sense of security automata ( Schneider, 2000 ) and runtime verification ( Leucker and Schallhart, 2009 ) ; the workflow is a flat statechart ( Harel, 1987 ) . For LLM agents, guards and permission systems check actions against declared rules: input–output classifiers ( Inan et al., 2023 ) , guard agents ( Xiang et al., 2025 ) , dialogue rails ( Rebedea et al., 2023 ) , trigger–predicate–enforcement rules ( Wang et al., 2026 ) and least-privilege tool policies ( Shi et al., 2025 ) . Our tool-call gate is of this kind; the FSM additionally advances only through guarded transitions and refuses to let the agent stop early. 3 Executable FSM Workflow and runtime. A workflow is a finite-state machine W = ( S , s 0 , s exit , P , Δ ) W=(S,s_{0},s_{\mathrm{exit}},P,\Delta) with states S S , an initial state s 0 s_{0} , an exit state s exit s_{\mathrm{exit}} , a permission function P P giving for each state the set P ⁡ ( s ) P(s) of admitted tool calls (allow and deny rules over the tool’s name and arguments), and guarded transitions Δ \Delta ; g s → s ′ g_{s\to s^{\prime}} denotes the guard of the transition from s s to s ′ s^{\prime} (false if there is none), a predicate over the observable history h h , the log of tool calls so far, implemented as shell commands that must exit successfully (hard checks) and optionally a review by an independent model (soft check). Each state also carries instructions for the agent, and the workflow does not change during a run. A daemon outside the agent’s context keeps a stack of frames per agent: entering a workflow pushes a frame, which records the current state, its history and variables and is persisted to disk; exiting pops it and is admitted only in s exit s_{\mathrm{exit}} . The task instructions remain in the agent’s context, which compaction can paraphrase or drop; the frame lives outside it, so compaction cannot erase it and a fresh context can read it back. We use the referral-abuse-detection task of SOP-Bench (v2, 200 accounts) without modification. For each account, the SOP requires the agent to retrieve five data sets, score them in a four-step analysis (STEP 1–4) and submit one of seven enforcement actions; labels are skewed (134 of 200 are No Action ), so guessing is often right. One unattended claude -p session (Claude Code 2.1.292 with default settings, including automatic compaction) receives all 200 accounts and the SOP in its first message, which asks it to process the accounts one at a time; nobody replies to the agent, and it never sees a budget. Each seed fixes an account order shared by all conditions. Conditions. The information factor varies where the SOP appears: in the first message only ( Plain ), plus a copy in the system prompt that compaction cannot remove ( Sys ), or plus a reminder of the SOP’s decision section attached to the first data result of every account ( Remind ). The liveness factor varies whether the agent is resumed: the -loop variants resume the session, up to 50 times, whenever it ends its turn with accounts left. Remind-loop, with both, was designated the strongest baseline before any run. FSM adds our FSM to Remind; two ablations isolate its parts: Stop-only has only the stop gate, Gates-only the tool-call gate and transition guard with external resumption instead. Models. Claude Haiku 4.5 is the main model, with all seven conditions over eight seeds and the two ablations over three. To see how the effect depends on drift, we also run Plain, Remind-loop and FSM with Claude Sonnet 4.6 (three seeds) and Qwen3.8-flash (eight seeds) on the same task and account orders, under a rule fixed before these runs: a model drifts if Remind-loop’s compliance is below 80% and does not drift if it is at least 95%; we predicted a gain of at least 30 over Remind-loop for drifting models and a difference within ± \pm 10 otherwise. With Claude Haiku 4.5, FSM averages 139.2 faithful and correct decisions (FC) out of 200 (Table 1 ), with 31.9–37.6 in every window of 50 accounts. Every baseline loses the procedure early: after the first 50 accounts none exceeds 12 per window, and Remind-loop falls from 29.9 to 5.4. FSM exceeds Remind-loop in 7 of 8 seeds, by 90.9 on average (95% CI [46.2, 130.2]; Wilcoxon p = 0.023 p=0.023 , sign test p = 0.070 p=0.070 ), and the best auto-continue baseline of each seed by 70.5 [27.4, 114.5]. The one negative seed is a session in which Remind-loop itself held the procedure. Strict accuracy does not suffer: FSM is correct on 146.5 accounts and Remind-loop on 138.9 ( + 7.6 +7.6 , CI [ − 16.1 -16.1 , 29.8]), although many baseline decisions were made without looking at the data, which the skewed labels often reward. A model that does not drift. Qwen3.8-flash does not drift by the rule (Remind-loop compliance 98.1%), yet FSM exceeds Remind-loop by 12.9 (CI [2.6, 28.5]; 7 of 8 seeds; Wilcoxon p = 0.047 p=0.047 ), beyond the ± \pm 10 we predicted, with higher strict accuracy ( + 10.5 +10.5 , CI [1.2, 23.4]). Claude Sonnet 4.6 retrieves many accounts’ data before deciding any, against the one-at-a-time instruction, while staying accurate. This batching took compliance to nearly zero in one of three Plain sessions and one of three Remind-loop sessions (the other four were fully compliant); FSM was fully compliant in all three. The FSM helped where the plain agent would otherwise abandon part of its instructions, under token pressure, by batching, by handing off a customer, by losing the record of a loop; where the agent largely held the procedure (Qwen3.8-flash once reminded and resumed, Sonnet 4.6 on easy SWE-bench tasks), the gain was small or absent and cost time and tokens. How much a plain agent drifts is therefore a practical test of whether enforcement will pay off. Close every route, and specify them carefully. Because drift moves to whichever route remains open, enforcement has to cover every route; the one our static permissions left open was found in three of eight sessions, and item-bound permissions would close it. Enforcement is also only as good as its specification: over-strict checks cost Retail tasks, and a tight turn budget left the month-end FSM agent no room for its checks. A procedure that matters over a long run, skill or SOP, should therefore come with gates on tool calls, transitions and turn endings, not only text; writing such a workflow is mostly transcription, and the guards are the real work (Appendix E ). 7 Conclusion Agents that receive their procedure once drift from it over long unattended runs, and restated instructions do not stop them. An executable FSM that holds the workflow state outside the context and enforces it at tool calls, transitions and turn endings kept agents on procedure, with gains tracking the plain agent’s drift, and extended to debugging, customer service and improvement loops at 1.2 to 3.5 times the plain agent’s time or cost where both were measured; because the runtime, not the model, supplies this reliability, small models gain a path to long-horizon procedural work. Limitations Evaluation Scope. Our evaluation demonstrates that executable state enforcement can improve long-horizon procedural fidelity, but several limitations remain.
The main experiment focuses on a single SOP task, one agent runtime, and a limited set of models.
Although we observe consistent improvements across seeds and additional domains, broader evaluation over diverse procedures, runtimes, and agent architectures is needed to better characterize the general applicability of the approach. Decision Quality. The FSM in our framework enforces how an agent executes a procedure rather than improving the model’s underlying reasoning or decision quality.
Therefore, stronger procedural compliance does not necessarily translate into better final decisions when the model lacks domain knowledge or makes incorrect judgments within an allowed state.
Future work could explore combining executable enforcement with mechanisms that improve decision-making capability. Workflow Specification. Executable workflows require explicit specifications and carefully designed guards.
Poorly specified constraints may introduce unnecessary restrictions or fail to capture important edge cases.
Developing methods for automatically generating, verifying, and refining workflow specifications remains an important direction. Execution Overhead. Runtime enforcement introduces additional computational overhead due to state tracking and action verification.
While this overhead enables stronger reliability guarantees for long unattended tasks, future work should investigate more efficient enforcement strategies that preserve procedural guarantees with lower execution cost. External limits, enforced outside the model, were a spending cap per session ($25 for Claude Haiku 4.5, $150 for Claude Sonnet 4.6 and a higher cap for Qwen3.8-flash), 6,000 turns per call, termination after 20 minutes without a new decision, at most 50 consecutive stop-gate blocks (after which the runtime would let the turn end; this never happened) or 50 resumes per session, termination after 10 resumes without a new decision, and a time limit of 6 hours (12 hours for Sonnet 4.6 and Qwen3.8-flash). No analyzed session reached a spending or time limit; the most expensive Haiku 4.5 session cost $13.87 and the longest took 115 minutes. Conditions. The reminder of conditions Remind, Remind-loop, FSM, Stop-only and Gates-only consists of the SOP’s decision section (5.7) and its list of critical rules (6.1), copied verbatim, and a paragraph stating that the run is unattended, that accounts are processed one at a time, and that there is no need to stop, summarize, pause or rush. It is attached once per account, to the result of the first successful data-tool call for that account. The resume message of the -loop conditions and of Gates-only and the stop-gate message of FSM and Stop-only are produced by the same function: the number of accounts left, the next undecided account, the per-account procedure and the statement that ending the turn saves neither tokens nor context. For FSM only, the stop-gate message ends with one sentence naming the current workflow state, since the other conditions have no state to name. The first message of FSM adds a paragraph that tells the agent to enter the workflow and how to move between states. Stop-only uses a single-state workflow with the stop gate only; Gates-only uses the FSM workflow without the stop gate and is resumed like Remind-loop. Scoring. An account’s decision is its first submitted decision. Investigated : all five data tools succeeded for the account before its decision. Recorded : the last STEP 1–4 record for the account before its decision follows all five retrievals and names the submitted action. Focused : no other account’s data was retrieved between the first of these retrievals and the decision. Compliant = investigated ∧ \wedge recorded ∧ \wedge focused ( inv \mathrm{inv} , rec \mathrm{rec} and foc \mathrm{foc} in Section 4.1 ); strict correctness is SOP-Bench’s exact label match, and FC counts compliant and strictly correct decisions. Compliance rates are compliant / decided per session, averaged over sessions. Pre-registration, freeze and deviations. The plan fixed conditions, prompts, metric, predictions, statistics and limits; seed 1 was used for development, and the code, workflow and analysis scripts were committed before any formal run, and no run could start if any of these files had changed since. Every later change was recorded before it took effect. The main ones: (i) the diagnostic count of decisions submitted for another account than the last record, added before the formal runs once the permission gap was found; (ii) a correction to the count of stop-gate blocks, which had also counted quotations of the block message inside compaction summaries, applied after the pre-registered analysis had been run and changing no other number; (iii) one session (Stop-only, seed 4) that ended when the model fetched 160 accounts in one turn and overflowed its context, which we classified as a model outcome, not an infrastructure failure, and did not rerun; (iv) for the extension models, written before their runs, the regime rule and predictions (Appendix B ), a check that every response was served by the requested model, and a classification of errors into quota, infrastructure and context overflow. Infrastructure failures and seeds. Models were accessed through a commercial API relay, and every response in every analyzed session was served by the requested model. A session counted as an infrastructure failure when it ended with an API, quota or server error or a crash of the FSM daemon rather than with an action of the model; this was decided from the error returned, never from the session’s decisions. Under the pre-registered rule, such a failure voided every session of that seed and a replacement seed was added, numbered from 10 upward; under an amendment written before the seeds it applied to were run, only the interrupted session was rerun from scratch. No voided seed was rerun or reused, including sessions that had completed. The relay account’s credit was exhausted twice, which returned a quota error to every running session and voided Qwen3.8-flash seeds 7–12, Claude Sonnet 4.6 seeds 2–4 and 10–12, and the first Haiku 4.5 runs for the comparison with Sonnet (seeds 10–12); the analyzed seeds are therefore 2–9 for Claude Haiku 4.5, 2–6 and 13–15 for Qwen3.8-flash, and 13–15 for Sonnet 4.6. Sonnet sessions were then run one at a time, so the sessions of a seed ran hours apart; two attempts of Sonnet’s FSM session on seed 15 were ended by a relay error and by a congestion rule written before it was applied, and rerun from scratch. For the comparison with Sonnet, Haiku 4.5 was run on seeds 13–15 with the same code; these seeds do not enter Table 1 . No voided or interrupted session enters any analysis. Appendix D Details of the analyses D.1 Analysis 1: SWE-bench Verified Table 3 gives resolved tasks, patches that add a regression test, and median wall-clock time and mean cost per task, which were recorded for Easy-100 only; one run per task and agent. Agents. Both agents run Claude Code with the same repository, model, budget and time limit per task. The plain prompt lists four steps (investigate; root cause, with “do not modify source code yet”; fix; verify) and asks for investigation.md and root_cause.md to be committed. The FSM agent’s prompt invokes a debugging skill whose workflow has states Investigate , RootCause , Fix and Exit ; its transitions require a clean working tree, the corresponding markdown file, and no change to Python sources before Fix . The prompt wording thus differs between the two agents, while the procedure is the same. Neither agent is asked to write tests. Task sets and runs. Hard-100 combines two 50-task samples drawn by the same heuristic, which scores task metadata (moderate difficulty, issue and gold-patch length, one to six failing tests, root-cause keywords) and balances tasks across repositories; two tasks were carried over from an earlier pilot in which the FSM had been useful on them, and in Hard-100 one was resolved by both agents and the other by neither, so they contribute nothing to the difference. The selection was not pre-registered. It was run in June 2026 through Claude Code’s sonnet alias, which referred to Claude Sonnet 4.6 at the time, with a wall-clock limit per task; no record of that run names the exact model version, and time and cost were not preserved. Easy-100 is a random sample (seed 20260918) of 100 tasks annotated as “ < < 15 min fix” that had not been used before. It was run in September 2026 with Claude Sonnet 4.6, a $5 budget and a 1,200 s limit per task; sessions interrupted by relay quota errors were resumed, the retry being decided by the error alone. It was planned as a non-inferiority test with a 5-point margin; with 100 tasks the interval is too wide to establish non-inferiority at that margin, and seven tasks that neither agent resolved may have environment problems, which affects the absolute counts rather than the comparison. We report one run per task and agent, and every patch was scored with the official harness on a clean image. Process measures. “Procedure followed” requires the investigate, root-cause and fix phases to have been completed in order according to the phase log derived from the commit history; we evaluate it on the 98 plain and 99 FSM Easy-100 attempts that produced a patch. “New tests” counts patches that add a function named test_* ; SWE-bench replaces test files with the gold tests when scoring, so this measure does not affect resolution. On Easy-100, the plain and FSM agents had 5 and 5 timeouts, 2 and 1 empty patches, and 37.9 and 48.6 turns per task on average over the tasks that finished within the time limit, over which mean cost is also computed. Setup. Both agents used the unmodified benchmark (v1.0.1), gpt-5.4-mini at medium reasoning effort for the agent and the user simulator, the official policy, tools, step limits and seeds, and the same static checklist. The FSM agent also runs our FSM runtime through a Python adapter. In Telecom its workflow has four states (scope, diagnose, retest, close). The runtime binds the customer’s phone line and original symptom, keeps a dependency-ordered ledger of unresolved faults, issues one repair at a time, opens a fresh test after every repair, and denies transfers to a human while faults remain, unless public evidence shows an irreversible condition such as a locked SIM. The workflow contains no task identifiers, hidden state, expected actions or rewards. Because the adapter emits many steps itself (62% of the FSM agent’s turns, against 10% for the control agent, whose only scripted turns are the benchmark’s opening greetings), the result measures the FSM-driven controller as a whole. Telecom development used only the training split. D.3 Analysis 3: Staged protocols Table 5 summarizes the three protocols; Table 10 gives the per-campaign, per-period and per-pair detail. AutoResearch. The task tunes a scikit-learn classifier on the digits dataset from a weak SGD baseline, using Claude Code’s default Sonnet model (the version was not recorded). Agents may edit only the training script, preferably just its configuration block. The protocol requires 25 trials over eight stages (baseline 1, scheduler 3, optimizer and learning rate 4, regularization 4, feature augmentation 4, throughput 3, combination 3, confirmation 3), each recorded in a results file and a ledger row. A row is valid if it has the required fields, a decision label in {keep, discard, crash}, a known stage, a matching axis, a numeric metric, only the allowed file changed, and a recorded candidate commit. The FSM agent’s stage transitions check the ledger before the next stage begins. The FSM run of one campaign timed out at 22/25 rows and was resumed once with a confirm-only prompt; without that campaign the totals are 41/75 vs. 75/75 valid rows and 1/3 vs. 3/3 complete protocols. In a later version in which both agents were given helper scripts that write rows in the required format, both kept valid ledgers and the completion gap narrowed (FSM 7/10, plain 6/10 over ten campaigns): tooling that makes the compliant action the default does part of the enforcement. Month-end close. The synthetic world is a small trading company with three currencies, two warehouses, about 40 SKUs and injected policy events. Each monthly period is a fresh claude -p session (Claude Sonnet 4.6) sharing only workspace files. The FSM workflow’s states follow the six steps of the written close procedure, and transitions are gated by a check script that never sees the answers. A period is scored by the fraction of 11 report fields within tolerance. Each run had an $8 budget, checked before each period starts, so a run could end slightly above it. The plain agent’s misses were periods that ended by time-out or turn cap without a final report; timed-out periods record no cost, so its cost is underestimated. A later, unmatched continuation of the FSM run of seed 1 over further periods is not used anywhere in this paper. DuckDB optimizer. The Long-Horizon-Terminal-Bench DuckDB optimizer task provides the DuckDB 1.1.0 source tree, a benchmark command that rebuilds DuckDB and times the 22 TPC-H queries (with a capped number of rebuilds), a plan profiler and a correctness check; the official verifier gives zero reward if any query result changes. Both agents used Claude Sonnet 4.6 with thinking disabled, ten fresh contexts of at most five turns, $0.80 per context and $8 in total, the same prompt, the same public optimization guidance, a deterministic data cache and the unchanged verifier. The FSM workflow has four states (implementation, measurement, verification, terminal). It persists the agent’s own evidence, a handoff note and the hash of the current patch across contexts, runs the shared measurement once per new patch and the shared correctness check after the speedup gate, and, when a measurement fails, binds the failure to the patch hash and instructs the next context to change the patch. The pre-registered endpoint was the mean reward difference over three pairs, with confirmation requiring all three pairs valid, at least two FSM wins, a mean difference of at least 0.05 and at least two full successes; the fourth pair was declared before it ran as a no-retry replication. Three caveats apply. The design was developed over many earlier versions, each changed after its results were seen, before the pre-registered version was frozen; within that version, the first pair served as a go/no-go check before the other two were run. The FSM agent stops once it reaches the terminal state, while the plain agent always uses all ten contexts, which affects cost and turn comparisons. The verifier gives about 0.77 to a correct patch without speedup, so mean reward mixes such patches with real successes; restricted to pairs 2–4, the mean difference is again + 0.41 +0.41 with two full successes. On other tasks of the benchmark (a data-validation audit, grammar fuzzing, Sokoban and others), earlier FSM variants did not outperform their controls; the largest of these studies, a pre-registered ten-pair confirmation on the data-validation audit task, gave a reward difference of − 0.009 -0.009 against a checklist control. Each workflow mirrors the written procedure state by state (the per-account loop of the SOP, the four debugging steps, the six close steps, the eight tuning stages), its instructions repeat what the procedure says, and its permissions name the tools of that step; writing the YAML was therefore mostly transcription, 61–160 lines of which most is instruction text, and the main experiment’s file is generated from the SOP. The guards are the work, and the useful ones check the process , never the answer: tool names and call order in the main experiment, git state and the existence of notes in debugging, required fields and arithmetic consistency of the agent’s own ledgers in the close and tuning tasks. Two workflows mark the other end of the range: the Telecom YAML has no hard checks, and the fault ledger and goal binding live in a Python adapter and controller of about 1,300 lines, which is why Section 5.2 credits the FSM-driven controller as a whole; in DuckDB the guards are modest but the context-boundary hook that carries evidence, the patch hash and failed measurements across fresh contexts is 1,214 lines, the compound part of that treatment. The failures we hit were specification errors, not runtime errors: over-strict guards (the first Retail workflow rejected order changes that policy allowed and lost 15 against 9 tasks), a turn budget too tight for the checks (24 turns per period in the month-end close), and a permission model that cannot refer to the current item (the open route of the main experiment). When a workflow was wrong, it was wrong in a guard that encoded an assumption about the task instead of a property of the procedure, and the fix was to check less, not more.
