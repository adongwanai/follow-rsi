Affiliation: The University of Tokyo Abstract World models enable agents to plan and learn from imagined experience, but in an unfamiliar environment they must learn its mechanics from limited interaction.
A mechanic is a rule for how an action or an interaction changes part of the environment.
Each occurrence of a mechanic that the model observes is one exposure.
We ask how many exposures a world model needs to predict the effects of a new environment’s mechanics.
We address this question on ARC-AGI-3, a benchmark of interactive games defined by their programs.
We introduce MechEval, which labels 69 recurring mechanics across 20 games from these programs.
It scores each prediction on the cells the mechanic changes at each exposure.
As a test bed, we build WARC (World model for ARC-AGI-3), a 35M-parameter vision transformer that is pretrained on other games and updates all of its parameters after each transition.
On held-out games, WARC learns to predict four in five mechanics with at least 80% accuracy on the cells they change and keeps that accuracy to the end of the recordings.
Half of them reach that accuracy within about 80 exposures, in a single play of the game, and without updates only one in seven does at all.
In closed-loop play on the public games, WARC-Agent, which pairs WARC with a fixed graph explorer, triples the explorer’s official efficiency score from 0.15 to 0.46, mostly through test-time training.
On the hidden games of the Kaggle leaderboard, our entry scores 0.20. Figure 1 : WARC and MechEval. Left: WARC is pretrained on recordings of many games (top). In a new game (bottom), it predicts the next frame from the last three frames and actions, then updates its weights. Right: For each mechanic that fires, MechEval scores the prediction made before the update on the cells it changes, as given by the game program. The accuracies shown are illustrative. 1 Introduction An agent that enters a new environment must learn how it works from the little experience it gathers there.
How efficiently it learns is central to intelligence, which has been defined as the efficiency of acquiring new skills [ 15 ] .
ARC-AGI-3 measures this efficiency directly, comparing agents and humans by the actions they need to clear each level of interactive games they have never seen [ 4 , 16 ] .
To clear a level, an agent must discover the rules of the game, explore it, and find its goal.
Exploring and finding the goal both depend on predicting what its actions will do.
A world model provides these predictions [ 32 , 33 ] , but it is usually learned from 10 4 10^{4} to 10 8 10^{8} interactions with the environment it models [ 33 ] .
In a new game, as in any new environment, it must instead learn the rules from a few interactions and predict their effects in situations it has not seen [ 62 ] . Two gaps make this regime hard to study.
First, methods differ in what they predict and how they use new evidence, from an inferred context to gradient steps on latent dynamics [ 62 , 92 ] , and have not been compared under a common protocol.
Second, there is no established way to measure the adaptation itself. Ying et al. [96] call for assessing world models by how efficiently they adapt, but the usual measures do not isolate it. An agent’s return confounds model learning with exploration and planning [ 33 , 50 ] . We propose to measure adaptation by how efficiently a world model learns the mechanics of the new environment.
A mechanic is a conditional rule for how an action or an interaction changes part of the environment. For example, a tile that rotates whatever is placed on it does so wherever it appears.
A model that has learned the rule can predict its effect in new places, so a few transitions inform many predictions [ 45 , 67 , 85 ] .
Each occurrence of a mechanic adds evidence for one rule, so we count experience in exposures , the occurrences observed so far.
Exposure does not depend on the model, so methods can be compared at equal exposure, and accuracy on occurrences in new configurations separates learning the rule from memorizing transitions.
We ask: How many exposures to a new mechanic does a world model need to predict its effects? Each game of ARC-AGI-3 is a program, so the rules that act at each step can be read from it. MechEval , an annotator built into the game programs and shown in How Many Exposures Does a World Model Need? Learning Unseen Game Mechanics at Test Time , applies this measure to ARC-AGI-3. For any transition, it reports which mechanics fire and which cells each one changes. Each prediction is scored on those cells and tracked against the mechanic’s exposure. With this protocol, we study what makes a world model learn fast.
Our test bed is WARC (World model for ARC-AGI-3), a 35M-parameter vision transformer, pretrained on other games, that predicts the next frame in pixel space and updates all of its parameters after each transition.
With WARC, we compare two design choices: gradient updates versus a recurrent state, and full-parameter updates versus LoRA [ 38 ] .
Finally, WARC-Agent , WARC inside a fixed graph explorer that ranks actions by the novelty of its predicted outcomes, tests whether faster learning carries over to closed-loop play.
Our contributions are: • MechEval : an annotator built into the game programs that measures per-mechanic prediction accuracy for 69 mechanics in 20 games. • WARC : a simple vision transformer that learns to predict mechanics reliably in fewer exposures than the baselines. Four in five mechanics reach criterion, half of them within about 80 exposures. • WARC-Agent : a simplified graph explorer [ 73 ] guided by WARC that triples the explorer’s official score, Relative Human Action Efficiency (RHAE), from 0.15 to 0.46. 2 Related Work Adapting a world model to a new environment. A world model is usually learned from the environment it models.
Model-based agents such as DreamerV3 [ 33 ] learn a world model from 10 4 10^{4} to 10 8 10^{8} interactions with each game [ 60 , 98 , 37 ] .
Pretrained world models transfer across games and tasks but are still finetuned on thousands of transitions or more of each new one [ 94 , 14 , 35 , 34 ] . AdaWorld adapts from few interactions by pretraining with latent actions [ 29 ] .
Test-time adaptation of world models has been studied for changes in a known quantity, such as mass, appearance, or layout.
In-context methods infer a context from recent transitions [ 62 , 52 , 68 , 74 ] , amortize the update into a hypernetwork or a mixture of experts [ 64 , 40 ] , or learn from a synthetic prior [ 24 ] .
Gradient-based methods update the model at test time. AdaJEPA does so inside the planning loop [ 92 ] , others on explored transitions or in residual modules [ 87 , 81 ] , and CAVIA only on a context vector [ 100 ] .
On static ARC puzzles, test-time training on each puzzle’s demonstrations is the standard recipe [ 1 , 39 ] , and WARC applies it along the stream of a game.
We compare both forms on the mechanics of unseen games and score the world model’s predictions rather than an agent’s return. Learning the rules of a game. Game description languages such as VGDL [ 76 ] and Autumn [ 19 ] write a game as a set of rules, like our mechanics.
Theory-based agents infer such a rule set from play as a program [ 85 ] , and language-model agents, including several published agents on ARC-AGI-3, write and repair it as code [ 84 , 71 , 79 ] .
These methods recover the rules explicitly, whereas we ask how many exposures a neural world model needs to predict them. Evaluating world models. Evaluation of world models has been extended from visual fidelity to physical understanding [ 61 ] and the consistency of game mechanics [ 55 ] .
ScratchWorld credits a prediction only when it computes the fields that an action changes [ 54 ] , and AutumnBench evaluates prediction and planning after a reward-free interaction phase [ 93 ] . Ying et al. [96] propose new games under a trial budget to assess how efficiently a world model adapts.
In contrast, MechEval scores each mechanic at each exposure while the model learns, with ground truth from the game programs. Appendix J covers other work. 3 Preliminaries Games. Each ARC-AGI-3 game is a turn-based environment governed by a deterministic program with a hidden state s t s_{t} [ 4 ] .
At every turn, the agent observes a frame o t o_{t} and takes an action a t a_{t} .
A frame is a 64 × 64 64{\times}64 grid of cells in 16 colors, and an action is one of up to five keys or a click on a cell, as in How Many Exposures Does a World Model Need? Learning Unseen Game Mechanics at Test Time .
The state changes only when the agent acts, s t + 1 = T ⁡ ( s t , a t ) s_{t+1}=T(s_{t},a_{t}) , where T T is the game’s transition function.
The frame is rendered from the state, o t = ρ ⁡ ( s t ) o_{t}=\rho(s_{t}) .
Each game consists of a sequence of levels.
A level ends once its hidden goal is reached, and the next level applies the same dynamics to a new layout.
The setting is discrete and deterministic, and since the frame shows the whole board, it is nearly fully observable. Mechanics. As in game description languages [ 76 , 19 , 85 ] , a mechanic m m pairs a binary condition c m c_{m} on the state and action with a change e m e_{m} to the objects it acts on.
It fires whenever c m = 1 c_{m}=1 .
The transition function is then the combined effect of the mechanics that fire, T ⁡ ( s t , a t ) = s t ⊕ ⋃ m ∈ M t e m ​ ( s t , a t ) , T(s_{t},a_{t})=s_{t}\oplus\bigcup_{m\in M_{t}}e_{m}(s_{t},a_{t}), (1) where M t = { m : c m ​ ( s t , a t ) = 1 } M_{t}=\{m:c_{m}(s_{t},a_{t})=1\} is the set of mechanics that fire and ⊕ \oplus applies their changes to the state in the order the program defines.
Learning a game’s dynamics therefore means learning its mechanics.
A mechanic is a rule as the program implements it, and we measure learning per mechanic.
Each mechanic changes only a few cells, so most of the frame stays the same.
Mechanics also recur during play, at other positions and in later levels. World models and test-time training. A world model with parameters θ \theta predicts the next frame from the last h h frames and actions, o ^ t + 1 = f θ ( o t − h + 1 : t , a t − h + 1 : t ) \hat{o}_{t+1}=f_{\theta}(o_{t-h+1:t},\,a_{t-h+1:t}) .
Such a model is usually trained on the environment it models and then kept fixed.
Test-time training instead updates θ \theta with a self-supervised loss on the test input before predicting [ 82 ] . VARC. VARC [ 39 ] applies test-time training to the static puzzles of ARC-AGI-1 and ARC-AGI-2, and WARC builds on it ( Sec. 5 ).
It treats a puzzle as an image-to-image task, in which a vision transformer predicts the color of each cell of the output grid from the input grid.
Each puzzle has its own learned task token that conditions the network.
For a new puzzle, VARC trains the network and a new task token on the two to four demonstration pairs and their rotated, flipped, and recolored copies, then predicts the test output.
A puzzle is static, with all its demonstrations given at once. Learning along a stream. In a game, by contrast, the input arrives as a stream.
A model pretrained on other games observes the transitions of an unseen game one at a time and can learn from each transition only after predicting it [ 20 ] , θ t + 1 = θ t − η ∇ θ ℒ ( θ t ; o t − h + 1 : t + 1 , a t − h + 1 : t ) , \theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta\,\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathcal{L}\big(\theta_{t};\,o_{t-h+1:t+1},\,a_{t-h+1:t}\big), (2) where ℒ \mathcal{L} is the next-frame loss and η \eta the learning rate.
The parameters persist along the stream, as in online test-time training [ 82 , 90 ] .
A model can instead keep θ \theta fixed and condition on a context of recent transitions.
In both cases, the model carries what it has learned along the stream. 4 MechEval Our question is how many exposures a model needs before it predicts the effect of a mechanic in a new game.
Accuracy over all cells of the frame cannot answer this question for two reasons.
First, copying the last frame already predicts 98.8% of the cells correctly, because most cells do not change.
Second, an average over a game is dominated by its most frequent mechanics.
MechEval therefore follows each mechanic separately along the stream of Sec. 3 .
It scores every occurrence of a mechanic, a step at which the mechanic fires, on the cells it changes.
It reports this accuracy against the model’s exposures to the mechanic, as in How Many Exposures Does a World Model Need? Learning Unseen Game Mechanics at Test Time . Annotation. MechEval covers 20 of the 25 public games, with 69 mechanics and 279 human recordings [ 43 ] , each truncated when level 2 is first completed.
Each mechanic produces one type of change, such as the rotation of the key piece when the player steps on a tile, or a counter that decreases with every move.
We first described the mechanics of each game in natural language, from the game code and the screen transitions, in a codebook of 107 entries.
We checked every entry against the game programs and the recordings, and instrumented the programs to log which mechanics fire at every step and on which objects.
Occurrences and exposures are thus read from the program rather than inferred from the frames ( Appendix B ).
For every step of every recording, the ground truth gives the mechanics M t M_{t} that fire and, for each, the cells of its objects that change color, with rules for cells that two mechanics share.
These are all that MechEval needs from an environment.
Other environments can be scored in the same way if their simulator can log them.
A change that happens only once or twice, such as picking up the only key of a level, leaves no later occurrence to predict.
We therefore keep a mechanic only if it fires at least three times in the median recording that contains it. Protocol. A model follows the recorded actions and, at every step, predicts the next frame o ^ t + 1 \hat{o}_{t+1} before any update on that transition ( Eq. 2 ).
Every model observes the same occurrences in the same order, so dynamics learning is measured separately from exploration and goal inference. Accuracy by exposure. The exposure of mechanic m m at step t t is the number of earlier steps at which it fired, k m ​ ( t ) = | { u < t : m ∈ M u } | , k_{m}(t)=\big|\{u<t:\ m\in M_{u}\}\big|, (3) including those whose change is not visible, which have no cells to score.
It does not depend on the model, so methods are compared at equal k k .
For an occurrence of m m at step t t , C m ​ ( t ) C_{m}(t) denotes the cells of its objects that change color, as given by the ground truth.
The occurrence is scored by its mechanic accuracy , acc m ( t ) = 1 | C m ​ ( t ) | ∑ g ∈ C m ​ ( t ) 𝟏 [ o ^ t + 1 ( g ) = o t + 1 ( g ) ] , \mathrm{acc}_{m}(t)=\frac{1}{|C_{m}(t)|}\sum_{g\in C_{m}(t)}\mathbf{1}\big[\hat{o}_{t+1}(g)=o_{t+1}(g)\big], (4) and its average over a mechanic’s occurrences with the same k k gives its learning curve .
In How Many Exposures Does a World Model Need? Learning Unseen Game Mechanics at Test Time (right), the player and the steps bar are predicted with at least 80% accuracy at step t t , and the piece rotation is not.
A mechanic is fully learned only when the model also predicts its effect in a position, object, or level it has not observed. Exposures to criterion. To summarize each learning curve by one number, we follow trials to criterion from studies of human and animal learning [ 78 ] : the number of trials a learner needs before its responses meet a criterion that the study sets, such as ten correct answers in a row.
It marks when the predictions of a mechanic become reliable rather than how accurate they are on average.
We count exposures instead of trials.
We define the exposures to criterion (EtC) of a mechanic as the first k k from which its learning curve stays at or above 80% for every later exposure.
A mechanic whose accuracy is below 80% at its last exposure has not reached the criterion (n.r.), and its EtC is that last exposure. 5 WARC WARC, the base model of our study, builds on VARC.
It keeps the vision transformer that predicts the color of each cell, and it learns each new task at test time, as VARC does.
The main change is in how it learns.
VARC trains on a few demonstrations and then answers, whereas WARC predicts each transition of a game and then trains on it ( Sec. 5.2 ).
WARC also has no task token, so everything it learns about a new game is held in its weights.
The other changes adapt the model and its data to games ( Sec. 5.1 ).
It takes the last h h frames and actions instead of one grid, patchifies the frames jointly, and is pretrained on human recordings of other games. The distribution factorizes over cells, each with a distribution over the 16 colors, and the predicted frame o ^ t + 1 \hat{o}_{t+1} takes the most probable color at each cell.

The h = 3 h=3 frames are patchified jointly into 3D patches of 2 × 2 2{\times}2 cells and three frames, so each token covers the same cells across time.
The actions condition every block.
No token identifies the game, since a new game comes with no identity and the frames show which game it is. 5.2 Learning on the Stream The input of WARC covers only the last h h frames and actions, so a transition outside this window affects a prediction only through the updates it has caused.
The weights thus play the role of a recurrent state, as the weights of the inner model do in test-time training layers [ 83 ] . Section 6.4 compares these updates with a recurrent state that reads the same transitions.
To learn a new mechanic from its first few exposures, WARC updates after every transition.
To keep the mechanics it has already learned, it also replays earlier transitions.
After each transition, it takes 20 gradient steps of Eq. 2 with the loss of Eq. 5 , on minibatches of eight drawn from the ten most recent transitions, twenty older ones sampled at random, and their augmented copies.
When a mechanic fires for the ( k + 1 ) (k{+}1) -th time, WARC has already trained on all k k earlier occurrences.
All parameters are updated, so a new mechanic can change any part of the model.
Each transition is augmented with its three rotations, with the click positions rotated with the frames and the four movement keys relabeled, which assumes that these keys move in the four directions.
The updates use the pretraining optimizer, Muon with AdamW, at a constant learning rate, keeping its state across updates. Appendix C gives the architecture and hyperparameters. 6 Experiments on MechEval We address three questions on MechEval.
How quickly does WARC learn the mechanics of an unseen game, and how do the alternatives compare ( Sec. 6.2 )?
Does it learn the mechanics or memorize the transitions it has seen ( Sec. 6.3 )?
Which design choices in the model and the update determine its speed ( Sec. 6.4 )? 6.1 Setup Evaluation. Learning curves are computed on the 20 games that MechEval covers.
Each recording is processed independently.
The model starts from its pretrained checkpoint, learns along the recording, and is then discarded.
The 25 public games are split into three folds, and each is evaluated with the model pretrained on the human recordings of the other two folds, with augmented copies ( Appendix C ). Metrics. We report EtC with the fraction of mechanics not reached (n.r., Sec. 4 ).
The false-edit rate (FE) is the fraction of unchanged cells predicted to change, including steps where a move is blocked ( Appendix F ). Kinds of mechanics. To break down the results, we label each mechanic by how it follows from an action ( Appendix B ).
A direct mechanic is the effect of a key press (15 mechanics) or a click (16) on the object it acts on.
A linked mechanic follows another change of the state, such as a marker that moves to the selected piece (10).
A contact mechanic happens only when the action brings two objects together, such as a block that slides when pushed (6).
A counter is a step or budget bar (21), and one autonomous mechanic is an agent that moves on its own. Model selection. The test-time recipe was selected on the five public games not covered by MechEval, so that no evaluated game influenced the choice. Comparisons. All methods receive the same frames and actions and are scored identically.
Those that learn by gradient descent also receive WARC’s update budget ( Sec. 5.2 ).

We therefore place WARC in a fixed explorer, which proposes actions that WARC ranks by the novelty of their predicted outcomes, and call this agent WARC-Agent.

The explorer, a simplified version of a published graph explorer [ 73 ] , hashes frames into states, records the actions tried in each state, and returns along known paths to states with untried actions.
Its actions are the available keys, a click on each connected component of the frame, and a grid of 64 clicks, tried in priority groups.
Without a model, the explorer picks an untried action of the current group at random.
With WARC, it ranks the untried actions of the current group, as in model-based exploration [ 77 ] .
WARC predicts the next frame for each of these actions and extends the most novel ones by a second step.
The explorer takes the action whose predicted frame differs most from the frames seen in the level. Protocol. Each agent plays each of the 25 public games with seeds 0 to 9, and every measure is read from its first 1,000 actions.

A world model that learns faster could therefore still raise the score.
Our Kaggle entry, WARC-Agent, scores 0.20 on the public leaderboard ( Appendix A ). 8 Limitations MechEval covers levels 1 and 2 of the 20 public games whose changes can be attributed to single objects.
It scores a mechanic only in the situations that the human recordings reach, so reaching the criterion is weaker than learning the rule in general.
This work does not model the goal of a game, which changes once per level and so falls outside the recurring mechanics we evaluate.
In closed-loop play, the action selector is hand-designed and shared across conditions, so our claims concern the world model rather than the agent.
Even with an oracle world model, the explorer clears no level in five games.
All results come from one benchmark of deterministic, discrete games on a 64 × 64 64{\times}64 grid.
Whether updating the weights after each transition works as well with continuous or stochastic dynamics is untested. 9 Conclusion We asked how many exposures a world model needs to predict the mechanics of a new game.
Within a single play of an unseen game, WARC brings four in five mechanics to criterion, half of them within about 80 exposures, and without updates only one in seven reaches it.
One play has far fewer interactions than the 10 4 10^{4} to 10 8 10^{8} from which world models usually learn their environment.
What WARC learns comes from each mechanic’s own transitions and carries over to other recordings of the game, including moves it has not seen.
The speed comes from pretraining on other games and from a gradient update after each transition.
The recurrent state of WARC (GRU), which reads the same transitions, does not replace these updates at this pretraining scale.
With the action selector fixed, WARC-Agent triples the efficiency score of the explorer alone, mostly through test-time training, and an oracle world model triples it again.
Higher scores need both a world model that learns faster and an action selector that aims at the goal of each game.
Unlike the score of a level, MechEval shows which mechanics a model learns and after how many exposures.
It can score any model that predicts the next frame, including the programs that language-model agents write to simulate a game [ 71 , 17 , 53 ] .
These agents could in turn use a world model learned in play as a simulator, to test the actions they propose or to roll out plans toward a goal.
Other environments, such as robotics simulators, can be scored in the same way if they log which mechanics fire and what each one changes, which we leave to future work. Parts that always change together form one object, and a change to another object is a separate mechanic.

A 3D convolution with kernel and stride 3 × 2 × 2 3{\times}2{\times}2 turns the three embedded frames into 32 × 32 32{\times}32 tokens with rotary position embeddings, followed by the transformer of VARC ( Tab. 6 ).
The current action is embedded by its type and, for a click, by its x x and y y coordinates, and conditions every block through adaptive layer normalization initialized at zero [ 66 ] .
A click also adds a learned vector to the token of the clicked patch, one for each of the four cells in the patch.
The two earlier actions are embedded in the same way and added to every token through a linear layer initialized at zero, and the output head predicts the 2 × 2 2{\times}2 cells of each token. In both stages, Muon [ 41 ] updates the two-dimensional weights of the transformer blocks except the modulation layers, with momentum 0.95, and AdamW [ 59 ] updates the remaining parameters. Pretraining warms up the learning rate linearly over six epochs and then decays it with a cosine schedule [ 58 ] . The GPUs are RTX PRO 6000, and test-time training runs on one. Pretraining. The 25 public games are split into three folds at random.
Each fold holds two of the six games without clicks, and the five games outside MechEval are spread over the folds as two, two, and one.
We train each fold model on the human recordings of its 16 or 17 training games, 90k to 123k transitions after removing resets.
For each training game, one recording that reaches the last level is held out to select the checkpoint by exact-frame accuracy, the fraction of next frames predicted with every cell correct.
Each recording is augmented into 51 copies, the original and the 50 combinations of five rotations and flips with the original colors or one of nine color permutations, with the actions transformed with the frames. Test-time training. A transition is valid unless it crosses a reset, a level change, or the end of an episode, and the history window restarts at these boundaries.
After every valid transition, the model takes 20 gradient steps on the ten most recent valid transitions and twenty older ones drawn at random, with a new draw at every update.
Each drawn transition enters the minibatches as the original and its three rotations, each rotation repeated ten times (31 copies).
The optimizer state persists across updates, and every parameter is updated. Tab. 7 lists the optimization settings of both stages. Choosing the recipe. We chose the recipe on the five public games outside MechEval (61 recordings), each with the fold model that holds it out, by a rule fixed before any run.
The score is the accuracy on the changed cells of every valid transition that changes the frame, predicted before the update on that transition.
Candidates whose false-edit rate exceeds twice the lowest are discarded.
Starting from AdamW at 10 − 4 10^{-4} with five recent and ten older transitions and rotations with color permutations, we changed one setting at a time.
This gave Muon at 3 × 10 − 4 3{\times}10^{-4} , ten recent and twenty older transitions, and rotations only.
The final recipe reaches 82.8% over three seeds, against 82.0% for the starting point. Appendix D Baselines The learned baselines, DreamerV3 and WARC (GRU), are pretrained on the same folds as WARC, and when they are updated, they take 20 gradient steps on minibatches of eight after every valid transition. Episodic retrieval. Episodic retrieval stores every valid transition of the recording so far and has no parameters.
For each cell, it predicts the most frequent next color among earlier cells with the same 3 × 3 3{\times}3 neighborhood under the same action type, breaking ties by recency.
For a click, the earlier cell must also lie at the same offset from the clicked cell, up to two cells away.
A cell without a match is predicted unchanged.
A 5 × 5 5{\times}5 neighborhood scores 34.5% against 32.9%, and copying the most similar earlier transition scores at most 19.1%. DreamerV3. We use a PyTorch port of the DreamerV3 world model [ 33 ] that matches the official JAX implementation.
We take its 50M configuration with a CNN depth of 64 (45.9M parameters).
A categorical decoder over the 16 colors lets it be scored cell by cell like WARC, and the action is factored into its type and click coordinates.
It is pretrained with only the world-model loss for 50 epochs on batches of 16 sequences of 64 steps, with the depth and the learning rate chosen on fold 1.
We keep the checkpoint with the best accuracy over all cells of the next frame on held-out recordings.
At test time, it predicts from a state recomputed over the last 64 frames.
Every part of the model is updated, and each transition is trained as the last step of a sequence of up to nine frames.
Its update was tuned on the five games outside MechEval by the rule of Appendix C .
The tuning gave AdamW at a constant 3 × 10 − 4 3{\times}10^{-4} , five recent and ten older transitions, and rotations only.
Muon was not tried, since the model was not pretrained with it. WARC (GRU). WARC (GRU) keeps the blocks, the click vectors, and the head of WARC and replaces the three-frame window by a convolutional GRU over the 32 × 32 32{\times}32 patch grid.
Each transition is encoded into a 64-channel map from its frames, its action, and a reset flag, and the GRU reads these maps in order from a zero state at the start of the recording, ending with the current frame.
The state, mapped to the token width, forms the input tokens, so the model sees the frames only through the state.
The action also enters as a prefix token and through adaptive layer normalization.
The model has 32.2M parameters.
We pretrain one model per fold, reading each recording in chunks of 64 transitions with the state carried across chunks.
In half of the recordings, the five keys are permuted in the action labels, so that what a key does must be read from the state.

It keeps a graph of the states reached in the current level and the actions tried in each.
A state is keyed by a hash of the frame, with the status bars masked as in the first frame of the level, and by the set of available actions.
The actions are the available keys, one click at the center of each connected component of the frame, and a fixed grid of 64 clicks.
They are tried in priority order, first the keys and the components of at most 1,024 cells, then the grid, then the larger components.
In a state with untried actions, the explorer chooses among the untried actions of the highest open priority group, uniformly when it has no world model.
Otherwise it moves along known edges to the nearest untried action, by priority and then by distance.
If none is reachable, it retries the least-tried actions of the current state.
The published explorer adds hand-set rules on the frame.
It detects status bars by their shape and position, sorts the clicks into five groups by color and size, with segments of non-gray colors and medium size first, and opens the groups one at a time.
Our explorer instead orders the clicks by size and masks only thin bars along the top and bottom edges.
It clicks at the center of each component and on a fixed grid rather than at a random cell of each segment, adds the available actions to the state key, and never rebuilds its graph within a level. A cell is action-independent if every candidate action leads to the same predicted color for it and this color differs from the current frame, as for a timer or a step counter.
The mask extends to the connected components that contain such cells, excluding components of more than 1,024 cells and cells on which the predictions disagree.
It is recomputed at every step, skipped when all actions lead to the same predicted frame, and applies only to the novelty score, not to the state keys. I.2 Protocol and Measures Protocol. Each run plays one of the 25 public games with environment seed 0 and agent seeds 0 to 9, and every measure is read from its first 1,000 actions, including resets.
Random, which picks a uniformly random available action, and the from-scratch CNN play 4,000 actions under a limit of 600 s per run.
Each of these runs uses up its actions within 246 s, so the time limit never binds.
Graph plays 1,000 actions on a CPU with no time limit, in 98 s on average.
Runs with a world model also play 1,000 actions with no time limit.
Every run with these seeds is included. RHAE. The official score, Relative Human Action Efficiency [ 3 ] , rates each cleared level by the squared ratio of the human players’ actions to the agent’s, weights later levels more heavily, and normalizes over all levels of a game on a 0–100 scale.

Appendix J Additional Related Work Video world models. Trained on large video and interaction datasets, video world models simulate real-world interaction [ 95 ] , physical scenes [ 63 ] , and navigation [ 8 ] .
In games, GameGAN [ 47 ] , Genie [ 11 ] , GameNGen [ 86 ] , DIAMOND [ 2 ] , Muse [ 44 ] , and Matrix-Game [ 36 ] simulate the games they were trained on.
Latent world models such as DINO-WM [ 99 ] and V-JEPA 2 [ 6 ] predict in the feature space of a pretrained encoder and plan with them.
These models are finetuned for a new domain or applied to it without updates.
We instead study how a world model adapts online to an unseen game. Test-time training and continual learning. Test-time training updates a model on each test input with a self-supervised loss [ 82 ] , and dynamic evaluation adapts a sequence model to the sequence it is predicting [ 49 ] .
TENT updates only the normalization parameters on unlabeled test data [ 88 ] .
Test-time training layers make the hidden state of a sequence model the weights of an inner model, updated by a gradient step on each token [ 83 ] .
LaCT updates them on large chunks [ 97 ] .
Such layers also generate minute-long videos [ 18 ] .
On a video stream, a model can also be trained online on each frame [ 91 ] .
Over a stream of updates, continual learning limits forgetting by replaying earlier data [ 72 ] or by protecting important weights [ 48 ] .
Continual test-time adaptation restores part of the source weights [ 90 ] .
Plasticity can also be lost as training continues [ 21 ] .
WARC replays earlier transitions in every update ( Sec. 5.2 ). Exploration. Curiosity-driven agents reward transitions that their own model predicts poorly [ 65 , 12 ] , and Plan2Explore plans toward states where an ensemble of world models disagrees [ 77 ] .
Go-Explore keeps an archive of visited states and returns to promising ones before exploring from them [ 23 ] .
Our agent combines a graph of visited states with a ranking of untried actions by the novelty of their predicted outcomes ( Appendix I ). ARC puzzles. Vision transformers such as VARC [ 39 ] , RoSeViT [ 57 ] , and TraceViT [ 56 ] cast a puzzle as image-to-image translation.
Loop-OWM instead learns a composable, object-centric world model of a puzzle’s rule [ 28 ] . Agents on ARC-AGI-3. Besides the agents cited in Sec. 2 , published agents have a frontier language model write and repair a programmatic world model [ 70 ] , as in OPINE-World [ 17 ] and Tycho [ 53 ] .
Others run a language model within a harness with memory, feedback, or a curriculum [ 25 , 46 , 26 , 13 , 75 , 5 ] .
Agents without a language model explore with a graph over states [ 73 ] or train a predictor from scratch on each game, like StochasticGoose [ 80 , 42 ] .
We compare with both in closed-loop play ( Sec. 7 ). Rule changes and rule discovery. Boult et al. [10] formalize novelty, and NovGrid [ 7 ] , NovelGridworlds [ 30 ] , NovelGym [ 31 ] , and NovPhy [ 27 ] inject controlled rule changes into grid worlds and physics puzzles.
DiG-bench makes rule discovery the task itself [ 9 ] .
