Dataset Code Webpage Abstract World action models jointly predict video and actions conditioned on language.
Existing studies focus primarily on architectures and training mechanisms, typically treating language–video alignment acquired through video pretraining as an established foundation.
However, introducing action learning may impair this alignment.
Principled guidance on data and supervision design for maintaining language–video alignment during action learning remains limited.
To investigate this, we curate a pool of approximately 47,000 hours of general videos, egocentric videos, UMI demonstrations, and robot demonstrations.
Building on this, we systematically study how language–video supervision, language–video–action supervision, and the joint use of these two forms affect WAM performance.
In particular, we examine how language annotation quality affects pretrained language–video capabilities, and how action supervision from UMI and robot demonstrations extends these capabilities to language–video–action modeling.
To better scale the model, we further conduct detailed studies on joint training recipe and model architecture.
Under a fixed training budget, mixing egocentric videos and UMI demonstrations improves control generalization.
Model architecture affects how effectively these experiences are used, while scaling pretraining under the selected configuration brings further gains.
The resulting recipe, post-trained on RoboTwin Clean demonstrations only, achieves success rates of 88.10% and 61.56% under Easy (Clean-to-Clean) and Hard (Clean-to-Random) conditions, respectively. Our model achieves 40.00% SR across three real-world tasks, compared with 23.33% for the strongest baseline, indicating better generalization under unseen conditions.

Contents 1 Introduction Pretrained video models learn associations between language descriptions, visual content, and visual dynamics, acquiring visual and dynamics priors across scenes, objects, and interactions [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] . These experiences provide a foundation for robots to understand manipulation goals and predict environmental changes [ 5 , 6 ] . World action models (WAMs) jointly predict video and actions conditioned on language, connecting visual changes to robot actions and providing a route from video pretraining to robot control [ 7 , 8 ] . This paper studies how to better use these priors to extend language-conditioned video modeling into generalizable action capabilities. When we introduce action prediction, the language–video capabilities acquired in video pretraining should be preserved.
This requires further alignment between language, video, and action.
It bridges the gap between language-described targets, visual observation, and executed actions.
Existing work on WAMs largely focused on model architectures and training mechanisms [ 9 , 10 ] .
Methods on video pretraining often treat the language–video alignment as an established foundation [ 7 , 8 ] .
However, introducing action learning raises a basic question: how to preserve established language–video prior and further solve language–video–action misalignment?
The roles of different experiences and forms of supervision in this process, particularly for control generalization, remain unclear [ 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 ] . Figure 2: Multisource data and model overview. (a) Composition of the 47k-hour pretraining data pool. (b) Our world action model architecture, with video and action branches connected through shared masked attention. To study these questions, we first construct a multisource data pool with approximately 47,000 hours, comprising approximately 11,000 hours of general videos, 11,000 hours of egocentric videos, 15,000 hours of in-house UMI demonstrations, and 10,000 hours of robot demonstrations, as shown in Figure 2 (a) .
These sources provide different forms of cross-modal supervision.
General and egocentric videos primarily provide language–video supervision, covering general visual dynamics and human interactions, respectively.
UMI demonstrations record video and action trajectories of humans manipulating objects with handheld grippers, while robot demonstrations provide observations, states, and actions during robot task execution.
Both include language descriptions corresponding to the manipulation process, supporting language–video–action learning in addition to language–video supervision.
UMI demonstrations provide scalable human manipulation experience, whereas robot demonstrations provide direct but more limited robot control experience [ 13 , 14 , 15 ] . The sources differ in scene and task coverage and in the completeness of language and action supervision. Multisource pretraining therefore requires combining these experiences appropriately: language–video supervision preserves video pretraining capabilities, while human and robot action supervision establishes video–action correspondences [ 16 , 17 , 18 ] . Building on the constructed multisource experience, we conduct systematic studies at three levels: language–video alignment, language–video–action alignment, and joint learning.
Specifically, at the language–video level, we examine how to preserve the visual prior in pretrained video generation with the design of language supervision.
At the language–video–action level, we investigate how to utilize action supervision from UMI and robot demonstrations to help action prediction and robot control.
In joint training, we study the data recipe and model architecture for better cross-modality modeling.
The data recipe determines the coverage of the visual and interaction experience in tasks, scenes, and the proportions of different experiences.
The model architecture answers how to conduct cross-modal supervision through modality interactions and language conditioning pathways, as shown in Figure 2 (b) .
We further examine the benefits of scaling training with the resulting data and supervision recipe. Our study shows that preserving language–video capabilities and extending language–video–action capabilities jointly support control generalization in WAMs.
Detailed manipulation descriptions and environmental cues improve language–video supervision, which maintains language–video alignment and thereby improves control generalization.
And UMI demonstrations improve robot control in simulation and real scenes, proving the value of human manipulation experience with action supervision for robot learning.
In joint learning, the benefits of multisource experience depend both on data and model supervision recipe.
Experiments show that increasing training investment under the selected data and model recipe further improves control generalization.
These findings provide empirical guidance for data collection and organization, supervision design, and joint training in WAMs. The pretraining recipe derived from these findings achieves success rates of 88.10% and 61.56% under the RoboTwin Easy (Clean-to-Clean) and Hard (Clean-to-Random) settings, respectively. Ours achieves 40.00% SR under the Hard setting across three real-world tasks, exceeding OpenWAM’s 23.33%. This result indicates better generalization to unseen objects and backgrounds. 2 Related Work World Action Models. World action models aim to connect visual observations with executable robot actions [ 7 ] . Early work treated video as a transferable visual plan or dynamic representation. UniPi first generates a language-conditioned visual plan and then recovers actions with an inverse-dynamics module [ 19 ] . VPP conditions a policy on predictive representations from a video model [ 6 ] . Motus, DreamZero, and LingBot-VA place visual dynamics and action prediction within unified or interactive architectures, exploring discrete tokens, latent actions, joint flow matching, and causal video–action modeling [ 20 , 7 , 8 ] . Fast-WAM and InternW0- Δ \Delta further distinguish future supervision during training from action generation at inference, showing that the prediction target and the deployment interface can be separated [ 21 , 17 ] . VPP2 and LingBot-VA adapt video pretraining to robot manipulation by studying how manipulation data organization, temporal and video representation modeling, and action-branch adaptation can preserve video prediction capabilities while supporting action learning [ 18 , 8 ] . We build on this foundation and focus on how language–video capabilities from video pretraining can transfer to language–video–action learning and action control. Multisource Data for Robot Learning. Robot learning increasingly draws on cross-embodiment trajectories, general videos, egocentric videos, and human manipulation demonstrations rather than a single source of robot data. Open X-Embodiment, Octo, and OpenVLA aggregate and filter multi-robot data to study shared representations, observation adaptation, and action-space alignment, showing that cross-embodiment learning requires source normalization and interface alignment [ 22 , 23 , 24 ] . General and egocentric videos expand coverage of scenes, objects, and interaction dynamics, but usually lack action labels directly corresponding to robot execution [ 25 , 26 ] . EgoMimic and EgoScale study how such human experience can be transferred through view and embodiment alignment, including settings with limited robot data [ 27 , 28 ] . UMI complements these sources by using portable interfaces to record manipulation trajectories in an end-effector-like interaction format, thereby providing action supervision associated with the manipulation process [ 13 , 14 , 15 ] . EgoWAM, Ego4WAM, and Motus further demonstrate the complementary roles of video, action trajectories, semantic descriptions, and world-prediction targets [ 29 , 10 , 20 ] . The central issue in multisource learning is therefore not simply adding sources, but understanding differences in scene coverage, interaction dynamics, embodiment interfaces, and available supervision. We curate approximately 47,000 hours of multisource data. We use this pool to systematically examine how experience combinations and supervision design affect WAM pretraining. 3 Method This section presents the world action model and its multimodal supervision. We first present the problem formulation, model architecture, and training objectives, then describe multisource experience curation and supervision construction. Pretraining uses four types of experience: general videos, egocentric videos, UMI demonstrations, and robot demonstrations. These sources differ in scene and task distributions, data quality, manipulation diversity, and available action supervision. General and egocentric videos provide language–video supervision, while UMI and robot demonstrations additionally provide state and action signals where available and valid. Given the visual observations acquired so far, a language instruction, and its current proprioceptive state, the robot predicts actions over a future interval. Let n n be a prediction start point sampled from a trajectory, K > 0 K>0 a fixed prediction horizon, V 0 : n V_{0:n} the visual history including the current observation, P P the language condition, and S n S_{n} the current state. Control learning primarily supervised by observation–action pairs must jointly address scene understanding, interaction dynamics, and action generation. We adopt a world action model (WAM) based on a pretrained video model, using the associations between language descriptions and visual dynamics learned during video pretraining and learning correspondences between manipulation processes and executed actions through action supervision. The model jointly predicts video changes and actions over the same interval. The video sequence V n : n + K V_{n:n+K} describes observable scene evolution during manipulation, while the action sequence A n : n + K A_{n:n+K} describes the corresponding manipulation behavior. The two sequences span the same physical time interval but may use different sampling rates. The final equality follows from our modeling choice that the video branch is not conditioned on the proprioceptive state. The first term learns language-conditioned video prediction, corresponding to language–video learning. The second term predicts actions from language, video information, and the current state. Together, the two terms constitute joint language–video–action learning. Starting from a pretrained video model, we train a unified WAM end to end with a joint prediction objective. The action branch uses visual dynamics and language conditioning information supplied by the video branch. Training retains the video prediction objective while introducing action supervision, aiming to continue using existing language–video modeling capabilities and to learn correspondences between manipulation processes in video and executed actions. Multisource experience supplies different parts of the supervision for joint prediction. General and egocentric videos provide language–video correspondences for learning language-conditioned visual dynamics. UMI and robot demonstrations additionally provide actions temporally associated with video, enabling action prediction alongside video learning. All samples participate in video learning. Samples with valid action annotations additionally participate in action learning. Missing or invalid action targets contribute no action supervision. Experiences with different supervision coverage can therefore participate in unified WAM pretraining. 3.1.2 Model Architecture Figure 3: World action model architecture and attention masks. For chunk t t , let V t ∈ ℝ T v × H × W × 3 V_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{v}\times H\times W\times 3} denote the corresponding video, S t ∈ ℝ D s S_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{s}} the available state at the chunk start, and A t ∈ ℝ H a × D a A_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{H_{a}\times D_{a}} the normalized target action sequence. A VAE encodes video into latents x t x_{t} . Separate encoders map actions and states to tokens. A text encoder maps the language instruction to condition p p . We illustrate the model architecture and the attention masks used during training and inference in Figure 3 . The model uses a MoT architecture [ 30 ] , with the video branch initialized from Wan [ 2 ] and the action branch randomly initialized. The action DiT branch comprises action and state encoders, transformer blocks, and an action decoder. Video and action maintain separate residual streams, with joint attention passing information unidirectionally from video to action. The video token stream passes through the video branch and causally attends to current and past video tokens. The action token stream passes through the action branch and attends to video tokens from past and current chunks, as well as state and action tokens within the current chunk. This unidirectional interaction allows samples with video supervision alone to continue training the video branch without directly updating the action branch through the video objective, supporting the use of videos without action supervision. The video branch receives encoded language conditions through cross-attention. The action branch uses language information only through attention interactions with the video branch and has no direct text cross-attention. This conditioning pathway aims to reuse the language–video correspondences established during video pretraining, allowing action prediction to use semantic information associated with the manipulation process. The video branch predicts flow velocities in video latent space, while the action branch decodes its output tokens into flow velocities in action space. 3.1.3 Training Objectives For chunk t t , x t x_{t} and A t A_{t} denote the clean video latents and action targets, respectively. We perform quality checks, standardize the video records, segment long videos into subtasks where applicable, and generate multi-level captions before mixing the resulting data for pretraining. Captions include action descriptions at three levels of detail, along with scene and lighting information. General videos. We use OpenVid [ 32 ] and Panda70M [ 33 ] , totaling 10,909.6 hours. These open-source video generation datasets contain videos and text descriptions covering diverse scenes, objects, and visual dynamics. We retain their videos and original descriptions to provide language–video experience beyond manipulation demonstrations. Egocentric videos. We use Ego4D [ 25 ] , EgoVerse [ 26 ] , and EgoSuite [ 34 ] , totaling 371,212 trajectories and 10,986.3 hours. Ego4D primarily records daily activities across real-life settings. EgoVerse covers more diverse scenes, adding egocentric visual experience across environments. EgoSuite is currently the largest open-source egocentric manipulation video dataset. We retain the videos and language descriptions from these sources. Data preparation. We package all sources in LeRobot 2.1 format. We retain only samples whose videos, language descriptions, and metadata can be linked. We exclude samples with unassociated video, language, or metadata, videos with encoding or decoding failures, and known low-quality samples. Video records retain their corresponding language descriptions and metadata. The inventory counts the recorded episodes and videos at their source durations. Visual content filtering. For general video datasets such as OpenVid and Panda70M, we use original annotations and VLM-based content review to remove animations, static scenes, television programs, and other unsuitable content. For egocentric videos, we use a VLM to assess motion blur, brightness, occlusion, and whether a clip contains meaningful actions, reducing the impact of low-quality imagery and uninformative behavior on training. The UMI and robot demonstrations described below use similar filtering rules. Video model adaptation on general videos. Our WAM modifies
Wan’s video modeling design to support robot control, which may disrupt
pretrained visual and dynamics priors. To ease this adaptation, we first train
the video branch on general videos, whose distribution is close to that of
Wan’s pretraining data. During this stage, we use the same input frame count,
spatial resolution, and causal attention scheme as subsequent WAM training.
This stage aims to preserve these priors during adaptation, before introducing
embodied data. 3.2.2 Language Annotation Language annotations describe manipulated objects and executed actions and may additionally include interaction relationships, manipulation processes, and environmental information. For WAM pretraining, what semantic information helps translate video experience into control capabilities? Can these annotations help preserve language–video alignment and improve downstream generalization? We organize language supervision along three dimensions: the level of detail in manipulation descriptions, environmental information, and temporal granularity. Level of detail. Task descriptions cover executed actions, interaction objects, and their attributes during manipulation. We define three levels of descriptive detail, Brief, Medium, and Detailed, with requirements and examples in Table 3 . These levels define controlled language-annotation variants for evaluating how description detail affects the transfer of pretrained video capabilities to robot control. Table 3: Language annotation requirements and example descriptions at three levels of detail. All examples describe the same manipulation segment. Detail level Annotation requirements Example language annotation Brief Summarize the main action and core object with a verb–object phrase. Pick up the snack. Medium Summarize the manipulation in one sentence, adding object attributes, spatial relations, or visible outcomes. Pick up the snack in the light-blue package. Detailed Describe the manipulation chronologically, including discernible hand actions, object interactions, and necessary adjustments. The right hand first touches and grasps the snack in the light-blue package, then lifts it from the table, while the left hand does not participate. The three descriptions convey the same manipulation. The Brief level specifies the main action and object, the Medium level adds the object’s appearance, and the Detailed level further specifies hand involvement and action order. Scene information. We use Qwen 3.8 Max [ 35 ] and Seed 2.1 Pro [ 36 ] for subtask segmentation and caption annotation. Scene annotations supplement task descriptions with the working environment, spatial layout, and initial state. Lighting annotations describe brightness, exposure, illumination uniformity, and shadows. For example, the scene annotation for one clip is: “The manipulation takes place on a table covered with a black-and-white striped tablecloth, with various packaged snacks scattered across the surface.” Its lighting annotation is: “Indoor lighting is bright and uniform, with no obvious shadows or overexposed regions. All snacks and the manipulation area are clearly visible.” Temporal granularity. Episode descriptions cover an entire trajectory, while Subtask descriptions correspond to local manipulation segments with start and end times. A VLM segments continuous video into subtasks and outputs each segment’s timestamps and description. Descriptive detail and temporal granularity are independent annotation dimensions: the same subtask can have descriptions at different detail levels, and an entire trajectory can also have a brief or detailed description. During training, the Episode setting uses the description of the full trajectory, while the Subtask setting uses the annotation of the corresponding time interval as the language condition. 3.3 Language–Video–Action Alignment UMI and robot demonstrations follow the video curation and filtering procedures in Section 3.2.1 and use the language annotations described in Section 3.2.2 . Building on these language–video correspondences, we align video segments with state and action records to construct language–video–action supervision. We describe the collection and curation of these demonstrations below. UMI demonstrations. We use 411,754 in-house UMI trajectories totaling 15,284.9 hours. We collect human manipulation demonstrations using RoboPocket [ 15 ] . The system integrates a smartphone with a handheld adaptive gripper into a portable collection device. Operators interact with objects by moving, opening, and closing the gripper. Its structure and interaction mechanism resemble robot end effectors, reducing interface differences between human demonstrations and robot execution. Our collection tasks include mouse placement, block sorting, seasoning pouring, towel folding, bimanual snack bagging, and other everyday manipulation tasks. They involve pick-and-place, long-horizon manipulation, large wrist rotations, deformable object manipulation, and bimanual coordination. Collection environments include indoor and outdoor scenes with different lighting and background textures. During collection, head and bilateral wrist views record the overall manipulation scene and local interaction details, respectively. Smartphone visual–inertial tracking provides action trajectories, while external encoders record gripper opening and closing. A shared coordinate system and time reference establish spatial and temporal correspondences among the videos of both hands, motion trajectories, and gripper states. The collection procedure includes equipment checks and synchronization verification before recording, tracking stability and kinematic feasibility checks during recording, and video and AR trajectory playback afterward. These checks identify anomalies such as localization drift and trajectory jumps and verify task completion. Robot demonstrations. We curate data from AgiBot World Alpha/Beta/2026 [ 37 ] , RoboCOIN [ 38 ] , RoboMIND [ 39 ] , InternData-A1 [ 40 ] , Galaxea Open-World [ 41 ] , and ABC-130K [ 42 ] . These sources span different robot embodiments, camera configurations, and manipulation environments. We retain only demonstration subsets collected with bimanual robots that include head and bilateral wrist views and meet our quality requirements, maintaining consistent data interfaces and observation semantics. We exclude subsets with missing views, inconsistent view semantics, insufficient image resolution, overly simple tasks, single-arm robot embodiments, or other issues. After applying the configured episode exclusion lists, we retain 768,356 trajectories totaling 9,836.0 hours. State–action representation. UMI and robot demonstrations use a shared 16-dimensional bimanual end-effector representation. Each hand has a three-dimensional position, a four-dimensional rotation quaternion in wxyz order, and a one-dimensional gripper value. State and action records are associated with their corresponding video segments. Each source is normalized using its own q1 and q99 statistics. Trajectory quality. For our in-house UMI demonstrations, we construct action-supervision masks from the state and action validity annotations provided by the collection pipeline. Videos participate in video learning, while valid action labels additionally provide action supervision and invalid labels are masked. For the collected robot demonstrations, we first check action dimensions, missing values, NaNs, non-finite values, and quaternion validity. For subsets with the required joint and end-effector state fields, we further exclude trajectories with abnormal joint angles, excessive changes in joint angles or end-effector poses between adjacent frames, or no movement throughout the trajectory. Trajectories that pass these quality checks are used for action training. 4 Experiments We aim to use multisource experience to preserve the language–video

and motion from large-scale language–video data. During WAM pretraining, we provide informative language supervision
that accurately matches video content. We aim to help the model retain
pretrained priors and reduce forgetting of existing language–video
capabilities when adapting to robot manipulation data. Across all settings, we pretrain on a mixture of egocentric videos and UMI
demonstrations for 20k steps, examining description detail, scene information,

For description detail, we compare three levels, Brief, Medium, and Detailed.
We also compare settings with and without scene descriptions covering
backgrounds, spatial layouts, and lighting. For temporal granularity,
we compare Episode descriptions covering complete trajectories with

Fruit placement and rainbow stack demonstrate generalization to new objects, containers, and scene appearances. 5 Discussion and Conclusion In this work, we studied how to extend pretrained language–video
capabilities to generalizable robot control with multisource experience.
Using approximately 47,000 hours
of experience, we systematically investigated language–video alignment,
language–video–action alignment, and their joint learning.
We found detailed and accurate language descriptions preserve pretrained language–video capabilities.
Meanwhile, action supervision from UMI demonstrations further supports robot control, demonstrating the value of human manipulation experience for robot learning.
Our findings prove the importance of experience organization and supervision usage.
The resulting training recipe demonstrates generalization to unseen conditions in simulation and real-world evaluations, with further gains from continued pretraining.
We release the data, code, and model weights to support future research. Upon the pretrained video model, the proposed method offers limited support for complex reasoning and explicit multi-stage planning, despite outperforming VLA models in our control generalization evaluations.
The limited historical context may also hinder the execution of long-range tasks.

demonstrations, and robotic demonstrations, we construct manipulation annotations
using Qwen 3.8 Max primarily for subtask segmentation and Seed 2.1 Pro for
caption annotation. The procedure consists of the following steps. Subtask Segmentation. We use Qwen 3.8 Max to divide
egocentric videos and UMI recordings into temporally ordered, non-overlapping
subtasks according to action intent, recording each subtask’s start and end
time. Robotic demonstrations are short, so each complete episode is treated as
one subtask without further segmentation. Caption Annotation. We use Seed 2.1 Pro to annotate each
egocentric or UMI subtask and each robotic episode. The model identifies
whether the segment contains manipulation and whether the visual evidence is
sufficiently clear, and describes the scene, lighting, and actions at multiple
levels of detail. Segments that contain no manipulation or whose visual evidence is insufficient are discarded entirely. Action Quality Filtering. For UMI and robotic data, we further screen the associated state and action trajectories. Samples that fail one or more of the following checks are removed from action training: • Missing or non-finite values, including NaNs, invalid dimensions, or invalid quaternions. • Excessive absolute joint values, with any | q | > 20 ​ rad \lvert q\rvert>20\,\mathrm{rad} . • Adjacent-frame joint jumps, with any | q t + 1 − q t | > 1 ​ rad \lvert q_{t+1}-q_{t}\rvert>1\,\mathrm{rad} . • End-effector position jumps, with the Euclidean distance between adjacent 3D positions exceeding 0.2 ​ m 0.2\,\mathrm{m} . • End-effector orientation jumps, with a rotation-angle difference exceeding 90 ∘ 90^{\circ} for either arm between adjacent frames. • Persistent abnormal jerk in the joint or end-effector trajectories. Easy settings retain the scene conditions and physical objects used in post-training, including their shapes and colors, while varying their initial configurations across layouts.
Hard settings vary across scene and object attributes, including background, tabletop material, object instance, shape, color, size, and texture.
The evaluation layouts are illustrated in Figure 9 .
All models use synchronous inference and execute actions at 30 Hz. Layouts and trials. A layout specifies the initial object configurations, target positions, and scene appearance.
For each task and setting, ten layouts are fixed before evaluation and shared across all models.
We run three trials per layout to account for variability in object placement and model sampling, yielding 30 trials per task and setting.
To reproduce a layout, we align the fixed live head-camera view with a reference photograph using an image overlay. Scoring and termination. Each task is divided into predefined, equally weighted subtasks, scored by reviewing execution videos.
The protocol permits up to five additional autonomous replanning attempts after an initial failed attempt, retaining the current environment state and execution history without human assistance.
All models follow the same retry and termination rules.
A trial is marked as a failure if the cup tower collapses or an object leaves the workspace. Metrics. We report success rate (SR) and partial success rate (PSR).
For a task with N = 30 N=30 trials and K K subtasks, let s i ​ k ∈ { 0 , 1 } s_{ik}\in\{0,1\} indicate whether subtask k k succeeds in trial i i .
Complete-task success requires all subtasks to succeed, so its indicator is z i = ∏ k = 1 K s i ​ k z_{i}=\prod_{k=1}^{K}s_{ik} .
The two metrics are SR = 1 N ​ ∑ i = 1 N z i , PSR = 1 N ​ K ​ ∑ i = 1 N ∑ k = 1 K s i ​ k . \mathrm{SR}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i},\qquad\mathrm{PSR}=\frac{1}{NK}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}s_{ik}. (6) SR measures complete-task success. PSR measures progress at the subtask level.
The three-task score is the equally weighted mean of the task-level rates, computed separately for Easy and Hard and expressed as a percentage. Task details. Figure 10 shows the three real-world tasks and their corresponding ordered subtask decompositions.
