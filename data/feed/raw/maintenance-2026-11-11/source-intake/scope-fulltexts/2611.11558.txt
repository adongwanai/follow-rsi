We present InfraRSI , the agentic harness that turns open-ended human commands into verified training-system optimizations through a five-stage workflow: init locks editable scope and a fixed task contract over workload, loss, and timing invariants to prevent reward hacking, doctor profiles baseline phases and calibrates cluster noise, run co-optimizes launch configurations and code patches before verifying top candidates in back-to-back baseline re-runs and writing checked findings to a cross-hardware experience bank, and report and apply audit source paths, account for failed-trial costs, and commit promoted changes. Rather than stepping greedily from a single configuration during run , InfraRSI initializes from diverse experience-seeded configurations and maintains global execution-time and censored peak-memory models fitted to all trials, including OOM failures. Using these models, it estimates per-setting memory costs, revises miscalibrated LLM speed priors from live measurements, and pairs memory-hungry optimizations with memory-saving settings and verified code patches. Across four single- and multi-node dense and Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) LLM training workloads, InfraRSI achieves a speedup of up to 2.75 × 2.75\times and lifts mean Model FLOPs Utilization (MFU) from 17.4 % 17.4\% to 30.0 % 30.0\% , outperforming existing configuration tuners and coding agents while transferring experience across GPU architectures. As it improves training infrastructure efficiency, InfraRSI forms a two-level Recursive Self-Improvement loop—revising priors online within runs and distilling experience across clusters—to push foundation model scaling. Building on classic formulations of self-referential improvement [ 34 ] , recent Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI) systems demonstrate that large language models (LLMs) can discover mathematical constructions [ 33 , 25 ] , synthesize machine learning pipelines [ 14 ] , and rewrite agentic scaffolding [ 45 ] . While these systems target isolated algorithms or single kernels, compute-optimal scaling laws [ 12 ] increasingly bind foundation model progress to the throughput of distributed training infrastructure [ 36 , 24 , 30 , 29 , 46 ] : launch configuration knobs, operator and training-loop implementations, collective communication schedules, and device memory limits (Table 1 ). In this work, we study how to turn open-ended human commands and language-model proposals into verified training-system optimizations through a two-level self-improving loop—revising priors online within each tuning run while distilling verified runtime evidence into a cross-cluster experience bank under fixed task contracts. Jointly optimizing both launch configurations and training source code is essential because hardware-sensitive mechanisms such as FP8 quantization [ 23 , 7 ] , tensor and expert parallelism, graph compilation ( torch.compile ) [ 2 ] , and FlashAttention [ 6 , 35 ] interact non-linearly and often reverse their performance impact across GPU architectures and interconnect rooflines [ 42 ] (Table 3 ). Yet applying unconstrained coding agents [ 41 , 15 , 16 ] or greedy local search loops directly to production training repositories exposes four recurring failure modes (Section 2 ). Open-ended code edits frequently introduce reward hacking, numerical drift, or timing shortcuts, achieving illusory speedups by altering effective batch sizes, deferring optimizer updates past the timing window, or applying unstable low-precision casts. At the same time, distributed clusters exhibit wide-spectrum measurement noise across two orders of magnitude, causing fixed acceptance thresholds either to promote random jitter on shared hosts or to discard valid communication overlaps and incremental code patches on quiet nodes. Guidance from zero-shot LLM priors is similarly unreliable: predicted speedups correlate poorly with actual hardware execution and often veto high-gain compilation or communication settings. Finally, single-incumbent search loops stall at out-of-memory (OOM) and compiler boundaries whenever a throughput-boosting setting exceeds device memory on its own and must be paired simultaneously with memory-saving knobs and compatible kernel patches. To address these failure modes, we present InfraRSI (Figure 1 , Section 3 ), the agentic harness that orchestrates a five-stage optimization workflow ( init → \to doctor → \to run → \to report → \to apply ). In InfraRSI, init locks editable repository scope and a fixed task contract over workload, loss, and timing invariants to prevent reward hacking, while doctor profiles baseline execution phases and calibrates paired step-time noise on the target cluster. The run stage executes budgeted compositional search (Section 4 ), a history-aware search controller organized around three mechanisms:
(1) Multi-Start Initialization and Global Time/Memory Models (§ 4.1 ), which starts from diverse experience-seeded configurations and fits global execution-time and right-censored peak-memory models across all trials;
(2) Prior Calibration, Failure Attribution, and Bundle Completion (§ 4.2 ), which revises miscalibrated LLM priors from live probes and pairs high-upside OOM configurations with model-priced memory savers; and
(3) High-Impact Knob Escape and Code Patch Stacking (§ 4.3 ), which expands search around high-leverage launch settings while stacking verified code patches onto stable configurations. At the close of run , the harness verifies top candidates via independent back-to-back re-runs against the baseline (Table 2 ) and distills hardware-normalized runtime evidence into the cross-hardware experience bank (§ 5 ), after which report and apply audit source paths, account for failed-trial costs, and commit promoted changes. We evaluate InfraRSI in open-code optimization campaigns across four dense and Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) LLM training workloads: Qwen3.5-9B on 8 × \times H100, Gemma-4-26B-A4B MoE on 8 × \times H200, Qwen3.8-27B on 8 × \times H200, and two-node Qwen3.5-35B-A3B MoE on 16 × \times H100 (Table 4 , Figure 2 ). Across these workloads, InfraRSI achieves a 1.83 × \mathbf{1.83\times} geometric-mean speedup and lifts mean training Model FLOPs Utilization (MFU) from 17.4 % 17.4\% to 30.0 % 30.0\% (reaching up to 2.75 × 2.75\times on Gemma-4-26B-A4B MoE), outperforming RE-Bench ( 1.37 × 1.37\times , 25.1 % 25.1\% MFU), Auto-Research ( 1.34 × 1.34\times , 24.5 % 24.5\% MFU), and knob-only OptiCo ( 1.23 × 1.23\times , 21.3 % 21.3\% MFU). Furthermore, cross-SKU experience transfer accelerates convergence and improves final throughput over cold-start search (Table 5 ), while offline receipt and tolerance audits confirm that our 16-invariant verifier reliably filters out invalid and reward-hacking executions across numerical regimes (Table 6 ). Our contributions are: • Unified Execution and Verification Contract (§ 3 ): A five-stage multi-stack harness over launch configurations and source patches that couples 16 workload, numerical, and timing invariants (Table 2 ) with source-path auditing, full failed-trial cost accounting, and back-to-back baseline verification. • Budgeted Compositional Search (§ 4 ): A stateful configuration and code search controller that uses Cholesky execution-time and censored Tobit peak-memory models to guide empirical prior calibration, failure-attributed bundle completion, and high-impact knob escape (Figure 1 ). • Cross-Hardware Experience Transfer and Audited Evaluation (§ 5 , § 6 ): A structured experience protocol that transfers hardware-conditioned performance evidence across GPU architectures, validated across dense and MoE training workloads and numerical tolerance regimes (Tables 4 – 6 , Figures 2 – 5 ). 2 Problem Definition and Empirical Motivation 2.1 Optimization Objective and Fixed Task Contract Optimizing distributed training infrastructure differs fundamentally from algorithmic discovery [ 33 , 25 , 14 ] and isolated kernel generation [ 26 , 16 ] (Table 1 ). In InfraRSI, each candidate combines launch configuration knobs and source-code patches to maximize training throughput under a strict fixed task contract : the model architecture, dataset, effective workload (tokens processed per optimizer update), allowed numerical precision menu, and optimizer update protocol are held invariant. Each task contract declares the evaluation window, permitted numerical modes, and relative loss tolerance, while retaining the full GPU and API search cost of any failed or rejected candidate. Evaluating a candidate under this contract separates two distinct verification questions: short-horizon loss consistency checks that the candidate’s execution path remains numerically faithful within the declared precision menu and loss window, whereas independent back-to-back verification confirms that a candidate’s speedup reproduces against a fresh reference execution beyond cluster measurement noise. Because distributed training mechanisms compete for finite device memory, streaming multiprocessors, and collective-communication overlap windows, individual optimizations combine sub-additively and frequently trigger steep feasibility boundaries, such as compilation and activation-checkpointing conflicts on Mixture-of-Experts models. Expressing both search cost and projected runtime savings in common GPU-hour units allows the harness to schedule and evaluate optimization trials directly. Across all campaigns, completed trial counts, GPU execution time, and token consumption are logged along the search trajectory, including all failed and audit-excluded attempts. Audited runs across our four dense and MoE LLM workloads (Table 4 ) expose four recurring failure modes in unconstrained coding agents and greedy local tuners: 1. Reward hacking and timing-boundary skew. Without strict contract verification and source-path auditing, open-code agents frequently achieve illusory speedups via reward gaming [ 37 ] by altering effective work or timing boundaries. Common failure patterns include unauthorized low-precision casts that violate the declared precision menu, deferred auxiliary updates that push work past the timing window while masking runtime import errors behind a single receipt’s validity flag, step-logging timer shifts that inflate native reported speed relative to wall-clock time, and aggressive low-precision or fused cross-entropy edits that degrade training loss (§ 3 ). 2. Wide-spectrum measurement noise. Across clusters and workloads, paired step-time noise spans more than two orders of magnitude, ranging from 0.04 % 0.04\% – 0.10 % 0.10\% on dedicated 8 × \times H100 and 8 × \times H200 nodes to over 6 % 6\% on shared hosts experiencing periodic data-loader or collective stalls. A hardcoded acceptance threshold (such as a fixed 2 % 2\% gate) is too coarse on quiet clusters, where it discards valid 0.5 % 0.5\% – 1.5 % 1.5\% communication overlaps and incremental source-code patches that compound when stacked, yet still promotes random jitter on noisy hosts unless governed by calibrated cluster noise gates and independent back-to-back re-runs (§ 4.1 ). 3. Miscalibrated LLM quantitative priors. While frontier LLMs can inspect training repositories and propose qualitative mechanisms, their zero-shot speedup predictions correlate negatively with hardware execution (Spearman rank correlation r ∈ [ − 0.49 , − 0.06 ] r\in[-0.49,-0.06] ). In addition, zero-shot priors issue false-negative vetoes on critical high-impact knobs: on Gemma-4-26B-A4B MoE (8 × \times H200), the prior predicts a negligible + 2.0 % +2.0\% gain for graph compilation where empirical execution unlocks + 55 % +55\% ; on two-node Qwen3.5-35B-A3B MoE (16 × \times H100), it predicts a slight slowdown for increasing NCCL communication channels where execution yields a + 10.2 % +10.2\% gain. A search engine must therefore combine multi-start initialization with contrarian probing and empirical prior revision (§ 4.2 ). 4. Single-incumbent myopia at memory and compiler cliffs. Optimizing training infrastructure requires coordinating multiple launch knobs and code patches across steep memory and compatibility boundaries. Greedy single-incumbent search loops fail in three ways. First, they discard or repeat failures without attribution, repeatedly triggering identical out-of-memory crashes or blaming valid launch configurations when an exploratory source patch fails. Second, they abandon multi-knob synergies after single-knob OOMs: on Gemma-4-26B-A4B MoE, disabling activation recomputation alone exhausts device memory ( + 18 ​ GiB +18\text{ GiB} shortfall) and enabling compilation with default fused kernels hangs, locking out tuners that cannot pair the memory-hungry optimization with a memory-saving partner ( − 14 ​ GiB -14\text{ GiB} ) and FlexAttention [ 8 ] to reach 2.75 × 2.75\times ( 17.6 % 17.6\% MFU). Third, they stall in local optima, as when Auto-Research plateaus at 1.29 × 1.29\times on Gemma-4-26B-A4B MoE and spends its remaining trials circling that local basin (§ 4 , § 5 ). 3 System Architecture: The InfraRSI Harness To address the failure modes in § 2 , InfraRSI decouples language-model hypothesis generation from measurement, verification, search, and experience storage. The harness pairs a declarative infrarsi.yaml task specification with lightweight stack adapters ( < 15 <15 hook lines) to expose a uniform execution and receipt interface across major distributed LLM training frameworks, including Megatron-Core [ 36 , 24 ] , FSDP2/TorchTitan [ 46 , 18 ] , DeepSpeed [ 30 , 29 ] , HF Trainer/TRL [ 43 ] , and native PyTorch DDP. 3.1 Workflow and Multi-Stack Adapters Every optimization campaign progresses through five deterministic CLI stages (Figure 1 ): • 1. init : Parses target repository ASTs, declares the task scope and editable_files , and locks SHA-256 digests of all frozen_files (dataset loaders, evaluation scripts, loss definitions, and harness timers). • 2. doctor : Executes reference baseline runs, trims warmup steps, calibrates cluster step-time noise, profiles execution phase breakdowns (forward, backward, optimizer, data loading, and collectives), probes interconnect bandwidth, and grades experience-bank prior coverage (§ 5 ). • 3. run : Executes InfraRSI’s compositional search controller (§ 4 ) over candidate configurations and source patches, preserving raw execution receipts and full resource costs for every attempted candidate; at the end of search, it subjects the fastest valid candidates to fresh back-to-back verification re-runs against the reference baseline on the same GPU allocation, writes verified observations and experience packs back to the experience bank (§ 5 ), and saves result.json , pack.yaml , and REPORT.md . • 4. report : Renders and inspects the structured campaign report ( REPORT.md ), invariant verdicts, and resource accounting for candidate source-path auditing. • 5. apply : Reads the promoted candidate from result.json , checks target repository files for post-search working-tree conflicts, and writes the validated launch configuration and source diffs (optionally on a new git branch). 3.2 Measurement and Resource Accounting InfraRSI represents each candidate as a joint specification of launch configuration knobs (CLI flags, environment variables, and configuration keys filtered by algebraic batch-conservation checks) and source-code patches that synthesize AST-validated unified diffs on editable_files , such as compiling block-sparse or chunked attention kernels [ 8 , 2 ] , fusing normalization, rotary-embedding [ 13 ] , and AdamW [ 21 ] optimizer operators, and bucketing gradient-norm collectives. Every candidate evaluation and verification run enforces a fixed optimizer-update count, explicit warmup trimming, and a synchronized steady-state timing window. To prevent survivorship bias, the harness tracks GPU hours across reference, search, promotion, and profiling stages alongside language-model token consumption, retaining the full GPU and API cost of every failed or audit-excluded candidate. Incomplete runs from transient host retries are flagged in the ledger, and migrating a workload to a new host requires establishing a fresh local baseline. 3.3 Semantic, Numerical, and Source-Path Verification Within each fixed task contract, InfraRSI maximizes log speedup over the candidate space 𝒜 \mathcal{A} subject to 16 deterministic invariants ℐ k \mathcal{I}_{k} across five verification layers: max a ∈ 𝒜 ⁡ y ⁡ ( a ) = ln ⁡ T 0 T ⁡ ( a ) s.t. When no candidate clears all verification and promotion gates, the harness retains the reference configuration and reports a delivered speedup of 1.00 × 1.00\times , preserving all unpromoted and excluded trials in the audit log. 4 Budgeted Compositional Search Under practical multi-GPU search budgets, measurement jitter, miscalibrated language-model priors, steep memory and compiler-conflict cliffs, local optima, and late-stage context growth frequently stall naive proposal loops (§ 2 ). In each step of the run stage, InfraRSI reads and updates global execution-time and memory models across all completed trials (§ 4.1 ), revises language-model priors using live measurements and failure blame (§ 4.2 ), and proposes coordinated knob bundles and source patches (§ 4.3 ; Figure 1 ). 4.1 Multi-Start Initialization and Global Time/Memory Models Online Noise Calibration and Phase-Headroom Ceiling. To guard against just-in-time compilation warmup [ 2 , 39 ] and periodic data-loader or allocator jitter, the doctor stage trims initial warmup steps and measures each run’s post-warmup steady-state window mean step time for candidate scoring, MFU accounting, and final promotion, while using robust median and split-half statistics inside the search loop so that transient single-step spikes do not distort surrogate updates. Across runs, InfraRSI estimates paired log-step-time noise σ pair \sigma_{\mathrm{pair}} by pooling paired reference re-run differences with within-run split-half deviations (floored by the within-window white-noise floor), using max ⁡ ( 0.02 , 2 ​ σ pair ) \max(0.02,\,2\sigma_{\mathrm{pair}}) to govern big-knob selection and stall detection during search and requiring fresh back-to-back promotion re-runs to exceed the paired relative margin exp ⁡ ( 2 ​ σ pair ) − 1 \exp(2\sigma_{\mathrm{pair}})-1 . This protocol measures paired baseline noise as low as 0.04 % ​ – ​ 0.10 % 0.04\%\text{--}0.10\% on dedicated 8 × \times H100 and 8 × \times H200 nodes. Using the doctor stage’s profiled operator-phase breakdown (forward, backward, optimizer, and collectives), InfraRSI also caps single-lever log-speedup predictions via an Amdahl phase-headroom ceiling [ 1 ] , bounding each lever’s predicted gain by the profiled time share of the execution phases it targets. Multi-Start Full-Configuration Initialization. Infrastructure search spaces partition into disconnected basins separated by out-of-memory boundaries and compiler incompatibilities. Starting solely from the unmodified default configuration wastes a large fraction of the trial budget flipping basic switches one by one, whereas trusting a single model-proposed bundle risks an immediate crash if any pair of levers conflicts. InfraRSI therefore dedicates its first three full trials to probing complementary complete configurations (Figure 1 ). The first start warm-starts from the best verified full configuration in the most similar hardware and model environment in the experience bank via canonical mechanism normalization. The second start probes the prior model’s highest-rated full configuration that differs from the first start in at least one high-impact knob, promoting configuration diversity. The third start executes a uniform random legal configuration filtered by active conflict rules to anchor global speed and memory variance, with the first fitting run establishing the memory baseline. In our Qwen3.5-9B (8 × \times H100) run, the experience-seeded first start reaches 1.55 × 1.55\times on Trial 1 before subsequent search advances to 1.73 × 1.73\times . Global Time and Censored Memory Models. Because configuration knobs and source patches interact non-monotonically across speed and memory, stepping greedily from only the latest incumbent deadlocks whenever an early choice blocks a downstream optimization. InfraRSI instead maintains two global models fitted across the full trial history and checkpoints search state after each trial. A Cholesky Bayesian linear time surrogate models log-speedup over indicator features for non-reference knob settings, verified source-code patches, and enabling pairs, updating the posterior precision matrix in closed form while bounding prior variance. Fitting the posterior memory parameters via damped Newton steps with Laplace covariance yields calibrated memory feasibility probabilities and per-setting memory estimates (in GiB) that distinguish memory spenders from memory savers. 4.2 Prior Calibration, Failure Attribution, and Bundle Completion Contrarian Big-Knob Probing and Empirical Prior Revision. Zero-shot language-model priors correlate poorly with actual multi-GPU execution (Spearman rank correlation r ∈ [ − 0.49 , − 0.06 ] r\in[-0.49,-0.06] ) and frequently issue false-negative vetoes on high-leverage compiler or communication flags. To prevent miscalibrated priors from permanently hiding effective levers, InfraRSI applies empirical-Bayes shrinkage [ 10 ] across both action types: configuration-knob priors are regularized by their prior spread ( σ 0 ≥ max ⁡ ( σ min , 0.5 ​ | μ 0 | ) \sigma_{0}\geq\max(\sigma_{\min},\,0.5|\mu_{0}|) ) and updated in closed form by the Cholesky time surrogate as observations arrive, while language-model code-patch gain predictions are scaled by a conservative shrinkage factor κ 0 \kappa_{0} ( μ patch = κ 0 ​ μ ^ LLM \mu_{\mathrm{patch}}=\kappa_{0}\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{LLM}} ). Alongside Bayesian posterior updating, InfraRSI actively schedules contrarian empirical probes whenever a high-impact knob is rated low or vetoed by the prior yet exhibits high uncertainty whose two-sigma upside clears the big-knob threshold. InfraRSI tests such levers against the incumbent by reverting any conflicting setting to reference and automatically attaching the top estimated memory-saving setting when memory feasibility is low. After repeated configuration failures or any failure of a high-confidence setting, InfraRSI also triggers a structured empirical prior revision carrying measured step times, peak memory, and out-of-memory shortfalls, directly updating the prior levels ( μ 0 , σ 0 \mu_{0},\sigma_{0} ), refreshing the time surrogate’s prior parameters and the memory ledger’s declared GiB priors, and re-solving the posterior in place. As shown in § 2 , this probing and revision loop recovers high-gain compilation and communication settings ( + 55 % +55\% and + 10.2 % +10.2\% ) that zero-shot priors initially rate near zero or negative. Failure Blame Attribution Across Memory, Conflicts, and Source Patches. InfraRSI logs out-of-memory events, numerical violations, and runtime crashes separately in the ledger. When a non-reference setting triggers an OOM, it is tagged as requiring a memory-saving partner covering at least the measured GiB shortfall, and is excluded only if it still fails across multiple distinct savers. For non-memory crashes, hangs, or numerical divergence, InfraRSI bans only the minimal conflict tuple (the smallest co-occurring pair or triple of settings absent from all passing runs) while keeping each individual setting eligible in other combinations. Failures during code exploration are attributed to the candidate patch rather than the underlying launch configuration. Bundle Completion and Conflict Backtracking. Whenever a configuration fails due to insufficient memory despite high surrogate-predicted speedup, InfraRSI retains it in a high-upside failure buffer and performs bundle completion, pairing the configuration with reference-held memory savers that cover the measured GiB shortfall while reverting any conflicting knob to reference. For example, on Gemma-4-26B-A4B MoE, bundle completion pairs disabling activation recomputation ( + 18 ​ GiB +18\text{ GiB} shortfall) with disabling Liger fused cross-entropy [ 13 ] ( − 14 ​ GiB -14\text{ GiB} ) and enabling torch.compile (§ 6.1 ). Similarly, when a high-prior lever conflicts with an earlier accepted baseline, conflict backtracking re-evaluates the lever directly on the clean root reference, migrates the incumbent once verified, and retains compatible settings on top of the updated baseline. 4.3 High-Impact Knob Escape and Code Patch Stacking Focusing Exploration on the Big-Knob Set. When search needs to backtrack, probe contrarian directions, or jump out of a local basin, perturbing minor knobs (such as memory pinning or worker thread counts whose effect is below 1 % 1\% ) cannot shift the hardware roofline or free enough memory to unlock major speedups, and risks attributing gains to sub-noise fluctuations. After every observation, InfraRSI explicitly partitions the configuration space to isolate the Big-Knob set 𝒦 big \mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{big}} , defined as all configuration knobs whose prior ( y ^ prior \hat{y}_{\mathrm{prior}} ), surrogate-posterior ( y ^ surr \hat{y}_{\mathrm{surr}} ), or empirical ( y ¯ emp \bar{y}_{\mathrm{emp}} ) single-knob log-speedup magnitude on at least one setting clears max ⁡ ( 0.02 , 2 ​ σ pair ) \max(0.02,\,2\sigma_{\mathrm{pair}}) ( ≈ 2 % \approx 2\% ). All structural exploration operators (initial probe diversification, contrarian probing, trust-region escape, tabu deduplication, and warm restarts) operate over 𝒦 big \mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{big}} . High-Impact Trust-Region Escape and Candidate Deduplication. Whenever two consecutive full trials fail to improve the incumbent beyond the noise gate, InfraRSI enters escape mode over 𝒦 big \mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{big}} . It dynamically expands the minimum trust-region distance from the incumbent from one to three big knobs to force multi-knob leaps out of the local basin, extrapolates further along ordered axes after improving steps, and performs periodic warm restarts from the best historical run of a distinct configuration class. To prevent wasted evaluations, InfraRSI also enforces big-knob tabu filtering and registers every proposed configuration-and-patch signature in the visited set before execution, cleanly separating pre-execution rejections from completed trials and preventing repetitive proposal loops. Code Patch Stacking and Trajectory Summarization. When open-code editing is enabled, late-stage configuration proposals whose two-sigma upside falls below max ⁡ ( 0.02 , 2 ​ σ pair ) \max(0.02,2\sigma_{\mathrm{pair}}) yield their evaluation slots to source-code synthesis, and verified patches with small individual gains are stacked and tested together in the spirit of group testing [ 9 ] once their combined log-gain clears the noise gate. To keep prompts bounded over long trajectories, InfraRSI retains full execution traces for the fastest and most recent runs while summarizing older trials into compact single-line records ( ∼ 28 ​ k \sim 28\text{k} characters). In addition, when a candidate refines launch knobs on top of an already verified patch stack, unmodified inherited diff lines are not double-counted against the per-step line limit. 5 Empirical RSI Loop: Experience Protocol and Cross-Hardware Transfer Reusing optimization history across training jobs requires converting raw execution traces into portable priors without inducing negative transfer across GPU generations. A two-tier normalizer maps framework-specific flags (such as fused AdamW [ 21 ] or mixed-precision switches) across stacks into 71 canonical mechanisms . To transfer priors safely across clusters, InfraRSI filters and ranks each source record against the target environment using a compatibility scope check and an environmental distance d ≥ 0 d\geq 0 . The scope check requires matching training frameworks for all records and grades an effect as covered when the model family and sequence-length bucket match (and, for topology-sensitive balance mechanisms, only when the GPU SKU, node count, per-node GPU count, multi-node interconnect fabric, parameter-scale bucket, and parallel layout also match), downgrading partial matches to partial and discarding incompatible records. Eligible records are then ordered lexicographically by coverage level ( covered before partial ), readability, evidence tier, and environmental distance d d , which sums indicator penalties for GPU SKU and node-count mismatches, log-ratio differences | ln ⁡ ( a / b ) | |\ln(a/b)| in parameter count and sequence length, and 0.25 × 0.25\times reference-state mismatches. Environmental similarity is used strictly to filter and rank prior evidence (grading target levers into covered, null, partial, and unobserved states), whereas actual target observations govern posterior estimates during search via Bayesian surrogate updating and empirical prior revision [ 10 ] (§ 4.2 ). Because prior studies evaluate disparate hardware generations, model sizes, and timing rules, Table 7 compares structural capabilities while Table 4 benchmarks representative methods under identical task contracts. (1) Proposal Models. While the classical Gödel Machine [ 34 ] formulated self-referential modification via formal proof search, recent code-evolution and agent frameworks (including FunSearch [ 33 ] , AlphaEvolve [ 25 ] , Darwin Gödel Machine [ 45 ] , AIDE [ 14 ] , METR RE-Bench [ 41 ] , and autoresearch [ 15 ] ) rely on the zero-shot coding priors of frontier LLMs to propose edits. In distributed LLM training, however, zero-shot speed predictions correlate poorly with measured hardware throughput and frequently veto high-gain optimizations (§ 2 ). InfraRSI decouples candidate generation from zero-shot model priors through empirical Bayes prior calibration [ 10 ] , contrarian probing, and failure-driven prior revision. (2) Search Controllers. Existing controllers split into static parallelism planners, fixed configuration-knob tuners, and single-incumbent free-form code-editing loops. Across distributed training stacks such as Megatron-LM [ 36 , 24 ] , DeepSpeed [ 30 , 29 ] , PyTorch FSDP [ 46 ] , and TorchTitan [ 18 ] , automatic parallelism planners like Alpa [ 47 ] , Galvatron [ 22 ] , and nnScaler [ 20 ] synthesize distributed execution plans over static computation graphs, whereas Bayesian optimization [ 38 ] , tuning platforms such as Ray Tune [ 19 ] , and multi-agent tuners like OptiCo [ 3 ] search predefined launch flags; none of them synthesizes source-level operator patches. Conversely, free-form coding agents such as autoresearch [ 15 ] , RE-Bench agent scaffolds [ 41 ] , and ResearchBoost-Agent [ 32 ] edit source files directly, but step greedily from the latest incumbent without a global memory model or combinatorial failure recovery, often stalling when individual edits trigger out-of-memory errors or compiler conflicts. InfraRSI coordinates launch knobs and source patches through multi-point initialization, a global step-time model paired with a censored memory model [ 40 ] , bundle completion, plateau escape, and bounded context compression (§ 4 ; evaluated under identical contracts in § 6.1 , Table 4 ). (3) Execution and Verification Interfaces. Kernel compilers, hand-optimized kernel libraries, and kernel-synthesis benchmarks, including TVM [ 4 ] , Triton [ 39 ] , PyTorch 2 Inductor [ 2 ] , FlashAttention-2/3 [ 6 , 35 ] , FlexAttention [ 8 ] , Liger Kernel [ 13 ] , FP8 primitives [ 23 ] , KernelBench [ 26 ] , and AI CUDA Engineer [ 16 ] , optimize isolated operators against unit-test shapes, whereas open-code training agents typically judge full scripts by reading self-reported stdout timers that are vulnerable to reward gaming [ 37 , 17 ] . InfraRSI treats compilers and custom kernels as composable primitives within its staged verification harness (§ 3.4 ), combining the 16-item receipt verifier, source-code audits, and noise-calibrated promotion gates. (4) Experience Memory. While online agent-serving platforms such as Reef [ 31 ] continually update deployed agents from inference feedback, most training-time hyperparameter tuners and coding agents either start from scratch on each cluster, rely on static hand-written skill cards [ 32 ] , or append raw execution transcripts until context windows saturate. InfraRSI records reusable infrastructure findings as structured experience packs (§ 5 ) that normalize hardware rooflines, parallel topologies, verified log-speedups, numerical tolerances, and failure constraints across canonical optimization mechanisms. Harvested packs from external hardware SKUs warm-start initialization and seed time and memory priors while excluding same-task, same-SKU runs (§ 6.2 , Table 5 ). 8 Conclusion In this paper, we propose the InfraRSI Harness , a harness that turns language-model proposals into verified distributed training optimizations by combining five-stage contract verification, history-aware compositional search over launch knobs and source patches, and cross-hardware experience transfer. Across single- and multi-node dense and Mixture-of-Experts training workloads, InfraRSI consistently improves end-to-end throughput and hardware utilization over existing configuration tuners and coding agents while preventing reward hacking.
