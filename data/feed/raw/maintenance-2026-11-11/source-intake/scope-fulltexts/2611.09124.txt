We introduce A 2 -MEAS, an embodied multi-agent framework built around code as a scaling interface . Here, a correction code is a learned representation of corrective language, grounded spatial evidence, execution history, and an action proposal. MetaBrain supplies corrective supervision during training and grounded guidance during execution. The correction encoder and adapter learn to turn this guidance into actions, absorbing corrective knowledge into trainable action-policy weights. At deployment, fresh codes adjust behavior while all weights remain fixed. This couples learning from foundation models with using their reasoning when new failures arise. The underlying action model, A 2 -WLA, augments a vision-language-action policy with motion-aware history and a latent world model. Surprisingly, even before online correction, this simple architecture with action pretraining and task specialization reaches 0.5755 Q-score on the 50-task BEHAVIOR 2026 public subset, approximately 2.21 × 2.21\times the 2025 winner’s reported public score of 0.2605 across different benchmark editions and training configurations. On the full 100-task BEHAVIOR 2026 public set, the complete system combines MetaBrain, diagnostic agents, and learned correction to achieve 0.6411 Q-score and 45.40% complete-task success. The same action design also supports short-horizon bimanual manipulation, achieving 93.98% average success on RoboTwin 2.0, or 95.05% with the complete system. Real-robot evaluation spans 12 tabletop tasks across five household settings, where A 2 -WLA achieves 57.92% average success compared with 26.67% for π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} . Long-horizon pour-over coffee preparation tests recovery during sustained execution. At two filter-handling steps, a history-conditioned Adapter raises post-failure completion from 14.00% to 94.00% and from 8.00% to 82.00%, relative to retries by A 2 -WLA without the Adapter. To support development at this scale, our training infrastructure reaches 427.1 samples per second, approximately 4.03 × 4.03\times the throughput of a π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} reference implementation on matched hardware. We will release the system, models, training scripts, and infrastructure as an efficient, usable framework for embodied learning. Recent evaluations show that general-purpose models can generate robot actions without task-specific parameter updates. On the 42-task RoboDojo evaluation, GPT-6 Astra ranks above the 40 public policies included in the comparison, although precision and dynamic control remain limitations [ 1 ] . Specialized action models can benefit from this progress by absorbing foundation-model knowledge into their weights and consulting these models during execution. Learning recurring decisions into a compact policy offers efficient control, while access to a capable foundation model can help address unfamiliar failures. The challenge is to combine these routes to sustain coherent behavior throughout an activity. A robot must do more than choose a plausible next action. Each interaction changes the conditions for what follows. A recovered grasp may secure an object in an orientation that prevents its later placement, and a successful local correction may leave the original goal unfinished. Long-horizon autonomy therefore requires reasoning about both the immediate physical state and the consequences of changing it. This motivates our focus on how learned control, task reasoning, and diagnostic evidence cooperate across complete activities, including failures and the attempts to recover from them. Recovery exposes the need to connect task reasoning directly to action generation. Language-guided skill selection and feedback-driven replanning help a robot decide what to attempt next [ 2 , 3 , 4 ] . After a failed grasp, however, choosing to retry does not determine how the next attempt should differ. The controller must adjust its approach, target alignment, or gripper timing using evidence from the failed interaction. Language-conditioned robot policies and vision-language-action (VLA) models learn to map observations and instructions to control [ 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 ] . Predictive approaches, from GR-1 to world-action models (WAMs), also connect action generation with future visual dynamics [ 11 , 12 , 13 ] . The interface must make newly inferred corrections usable within this control process. Spatial planning, joint reasoning-and-action models, and learned recovery offer ways to connect decisions to execution [ 14 , 15 , 16 ] . We focus on a learned interface that carries corrective intent together with its spatial and temporal evidence into the action policy, so that a diagnosis can change the next trajectory while preserving the task goal. We introduce A 2 -MEAS, an embodied multi-agent framework that coordinates reasoning, diagnostic grounding, and continuous control through a shared execution record. Its action model, A 2 -WLA, learns from long-horizon demonstrations and uses recent visual history and predicted scene features to generate actions. A vision-language coordinator, MetaBrain, maintains the task goal and examines unexpected outcomes. Diagnostic agents provide perceptual, spatiotemporal, and interaction evidence, using object localization, segmentation, tracking, available depth, and robot-state signals. These are complementary diagnostic functions rather than a fixed list of mandatory perception models. MetaBrain combines their findings with executed action history to formulate a correction. The action policy then attempts the correction, and the observed result becomes evidence for the next decision. Our central principle is code as a scaling interface . The code is a learned conditioning representation that brings together a recovery instruction, grounded spatial evidence, executed history, and a fresh action proposal. Language expresses the intended change, while the other inputs specify the physical situation in which it must be made. A residual adapter uses the resulting code to modify the action expert’s hidden states throughout trajectory generation. This gives heterogeneous agents a common route for influencing continuous control without requiring them to share an architecture. During training, MetaBrain-generated instructions and expert continuations teach the correction encoder and adapter how corrective reasoning should change actions. This absorbs corrective knowledge into trainable action-policy parameters while leaving the A 2 -WLA backbone frozen. During deployment, new guidance changes the code and the generated behavior, with all parameters fixed. The framework therefore links knowledge learned into the policy with reasoning supplied when execution needs it. A strong action model is the starting point for this cooperation. A 2 -WLA augments a vision-language-action policy with motion-aware history and a latent world model that predicts future visual features. Surprisingly, this simple architecture, together with action pretraining and task specialization, already yields strong long-horizon performance before adding online correction. On the 50-task public subset of BEHAVIOR 2026, one shared A 2 -WLA model reaches 0.4522 Q-score, and task-specialized policies reach 0.5755. The latter is approximately 2.21 × 2.21\times RLC’s public score of 0.2605 in the 2025 competition [ 17 ] . RLC, the 2025 winner, combines learned control with explicit stage-transition logic, a gripper-reopening heuristic, and a radio-specific rollback rule [ 18 ] . The strong result from our comparatively simple action architecture highlights how much competence can be learned within the policy before adding agent-guided intervention. We then ask what grounded intervention adds to that competence. Across all 100 BEHAVIOR 2026 tasks, the multi-model A 2 -WLA baseline obtains 0.6040 Q-score and 40.60% complete-task success. Adding MetaBrain, diagnostic evidence and learned correction raises these public-set results to 0.6411 and 45.40%, with at least one successful episode on 88 tasks. Fixed-policy Adapter ablations and a disjoint set of task instances test the correction mechanism separately from model selection. We also test the action model beyond long-horizon whole-body control. On RoboTwin 2.0, A 2 -WLA achieves 93.98% average success across Clean and Randomized conditions, and the complete system reaches 95.05%. Real-robot experiments span 12 tabletop tasks across five household settings, with 20 trials per task and configuration. Without high-level correction, the full A 2 -WLA recipe reaches 57.92% macro success, compared with 26.67% for π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} [ 9 ] . These tasks cover storage, retrieval, food preparation, pushing and tool use. Long-horizon pour-over coffee preparation takes approximately four minutes per run. At two filter-handling steps, a lightweight history-conditioned Adapter completes 94/100 and 82/100 post-failure trials, compared with 14/100 and 8/100 for retries by the same frozen A 2 -WLA policy with the Adapter disabled. These real-robot recovery tests complement the full correction-interface experiments in simulation. Long-horizon experiments also make iteration expensive. We therefore develop training and evaluation infrastructure alongside the models. Our implementation processes 427.1 demonstration samples per second on eight GPUs, approximately 4.03 × 4.03\times the throughput of a π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} reference implementation on the same hardware. The reference uses matching image resolution and action horizon, with each implementation’s batch size chosen for resource utilization. For evaluation, ideal linear scaling of the estimated 1,275-hour serial runtime gives a naive eight-GPU baseline of approximately 159.4 hours. Our eight-Pro6000 simulation and policy worker pool completes the 1,000-episode suite in 18.1 hours, approximately 8.8 × 8.8\times faster than this projected baseline, with the reasoning service hosted separately. We will progressively release the system code, models, training scripts and optimized infrastructure at https://github.com/AutoLab-SAI-SJTU/A2MEAS . The release is intended to give researchers and developers an efficient, usable framework for training, evaluating and deploying embodied agents across large-scale simulation platforms and real-world tasks. Over a long activity, a robot must remember what remains to be done while responding to changes in objects, contacts, and its own configuration. A successful movement is useful only if the resulting state supports continued progress. A 2 -MEAS addresses this problem by coupling learned action generation with task-level reasoning and evidence-based diagnosis. The framework contains three cooperating groups of agents ( Figure 1 ). A 2 -WLA generates continuous actions from the task instruction and observed history. MetaBrain monitors execution in relation to the full goal and determines when the next attempt should change. Diagnostic agents investigate the physical questions needed to make that decision. All three use a shared record of actions and observations. When execution requires intervention, a learned correction interface converts their instruction and evidence into changes inside the action generator. The local loop repeatedly generates action chunks from the task instruction and recent observations. The task-level loop uses the resulting evidence to decide whether execution can continue, whether the goal has been achieved, or whether recovery is needed. A shared execution record stores the goal, dispatched controls, and observed outcomes. It gives all agents a common account of what has actually happened without requiring them to share a backbone or run at the same frequency. The execution layer dispatches only a prefix of each predicted chunk, then obtains fresh observations before the next policy call. Thus, control is closed-loop across chunks even though an uninterrupted prefix executes without a new policy prediction. A 2 -WLA handles ordinary changes through this repeated observation–action cycle. When monitoring detects an abnormal outcome, the runtime pauses pending controls and asks MetaBrain to review the evidence. Verified recovery returns execution to the nominal loop, and verified completion ends the episode. Diagnostic agents are invoked when a decision depends on an unresolved physical condition. Table 1 groups their functions into perceptual grounding, spatiotemporal grounding, and interaction assessment. Perception locates objects and regions. Temporal analysis tracks how they move relative to one another. Interaction assessment combines those observations with available robot-state, contact, and geometric evidence. For example, seeing an object between the gripper fingers does not establish a secure grasp. Tracking whether it moves with the hand, together with gripper-state evidence, supports a more informative assessment. Depth or reconstructed geometry can supply additional evidence when available. Each finding retains its observation source and timestamp so that MetaBrain can distinguish a supported conclusion from a missing observation. The result determines what happens next. Evidence that supports the required condition permits execution to continue. Evidence of a mismatch prompts a correction. Insufficient evidence prompts another observation within the sensing budget. A negative answer and an unknown answer therefore lead to different decisions. Conditional tool invocation keeps ordinary control inexpensive while allowing several diagnostic capabilities to contribute when a correction needs them. 1.2 Action model and correction interface Let g g denote the complete task instruction, retained throughout nominal execution. The policy input x t x_{t} aggregates synchronized multiview visual observations, a finite temporal window of past visual frames, and the current proprioceptive state s t s_{t} comprising joint positions and end-effector configurations. A 2 -WLA combines three sources of action context. A vision-language backbone encodes the task instruction and visual observations. Its motion-aware history encoder represents changes across recent frames. A latent world model predicts future visual features from a transition representation inferred by the backbone. The action expert uses current context, predicted future context, and proprioception to generate controls. Future observations supervise training but are unavailable at deployment. MetaBrain uses this feedback to decide what evidence or corrective instruction is needed next. 1.3 From failure diagnosis to corrected action The correction interface becomes useful when observing the changed scene is not enough to change an ineffective behavior. Figure 2 illustrates a filter-placement example. The support ring should rest on the cup rim, with the filter hanging inside the opening. If one edge slips below the rim, continuing downward may wedge the ring or disturb the cup. Recovery instead calls for lifting the ring, aligning it above the opening, and lowering it again. After detecting an anomaly, the framework pauses pending controls and establishes a recovery state. MetaBrain examines the observations and executed controls between the last verified nominal state and the detected deviation. It asks the diagnostic agents to assess contact stability and the spatial conditions required for another placement attempt. Perceptual tools identify the filter and cup regions, while temporal tracking helps bound the interval in which support was lost. Available robot-state and contact evidence informs the recovery assessment. Geometric and kinematic checks assess whether the proposed lifting motion is feasible. The recovery instruction is finalized once its required premises have been resolved. If occlusion leaves a necessary condition unknown, the system requests another observation within its sensing budget. The instruction specifies the adjustment and reference frame, while A 2 -WLA generates the continuous trajectory. At the recovery state, the base policy first generates an uncorrected proposal. This proposal shows what the policy would attempt without intervention. The condition encoder combines it with the instruction, spatial evidence, and executed history. In the filter example, “realign the filter” is therefore interpreted in relation to a particular ring orientation, cup opening, and attempted movement. The adapter can then apply different residuals to different action positions, allowing a chunk to lift first and approach again later. These are learned adjustments rather than a prescribed geometric trajectory. Before dispatch, the runtime checks that the evidence remains current and that the candidate satisfies the available joint-limit and obstacle-clearance checks. It executes a prefix, records the outcome, and checks whether the support condition has been restored. Successful recovery returns the robot to the unfinished task. Otherwise, the new observations and failed attempt inform another recovery pass. The reference configuration allows at most three physical retries and 60 seconds per anomaly, with separate sensing and reasoning limits specified in Section 2.6 . The methodological contribution is code as a scaling interface , a learned connection between grounded recovery context and the action policy’s internal representation. MetaBrain can express what should change, diagnostic tools can establish where and under what conditions, and the action model can translate that context into movement. During training, the condition encoder and adapter learn from MetaBrain-generated guidance, offline-mined candidate deviations, and expert continuations while the original A 2 -WLA checkpoint remains frozen ( Section 2.5 ). At deployment, fresh guidance and evidence change the correction code, which shapes action generation with all model parameters fixed. The interface thus connects corrective knowledge learned during training with the physical context encountered during execution. 2 Cooperation through grounded correction codes A diagnosis is useful to a robot only if it changes what the robot does next. Our organizing principle, code as a scaling interface , makes the connection through a learned representation that carries reasoning and diagnostic evidence into action generation. MetaBrain describes the intended adjustment, diagnostic agents ground it in the observed scene, and the execution record identifies what has already been tried. A condition encoder combines this context with the current action proposal into a correction code. The adapter uses the code inside A 2 -WLA to shape the next movement. Language is one input to this interface, together with the spatial and temporal evidence needed to make the instruction actionable. Different agents can contribute through the same context schema while retaining their own architectures. 2.1 MetaBrain diagnosis and recovery instructions MetaBrain formulates a recovery instruction u t u_{t} from the task goal g g and feedback b t b_{t} , including relevant executed history and diagnostic findings. The public-set system uses Qwen3.8-27B [ 19 ] for this role, separately from the 2B vision-language backbone inside A 2 -WLA. It compares the observed outcome with the intended operation and requests further evidence when a condition is unresolved. The instruction describes how the next attempt should change while preserving the original goal. The memory-limited hidden-set deployment uses a smaller coordinator, as described in Section 5.2.3 . A visible failure may occur several controls after the action that caused it. An unstable grasp, for example, may become apparent only when the object slips during transport. MetaBrain therefore examines the interval from the last verified nominal state to the detected anomaly, including the controls actually executed. This history helps distinguish errors during approach, grasp closure, and transport. When u t u_{t} requests a spatial adjustment, it also identifies the reference frame, such as the camera, robot base, or object. MetaBrain specifies the intended change, while A 2 -WLA determines the continuous controls. 2.2 Diagnostic agents and evidence verification Before a recovery instruction is executed, the framework checks the physical conditions on which it depends. Ordinary deployment uses observations and robot-state readings, without ground-truth object poses or privileged simulator segmentation. The simulator-assisted scheduling controls in Section 6.4.2 are a separate diagnostic experiment. 2.3 Correction context and code construction An instruction such as “realign toward the opening” leaves several questions unanswered. The policy must know which opening is intended, where it is in the current observation, and what the previous attempt did. Feasibility assessments inform both instruction construction and the decision to dispatch a candidate. This division lets different tools contribute through a common schema while preserving the distinction between an observation, an inferred relation, and a proposed action. After detecting an anomaly, the runtime pauses pending controls and saves the observations, robot state, and executed history. Recovery preparation may include controlled stopping, posture adjustment, or contact release when required. The runtime then reacquires observations and robot state. Returning the robot to an earlier pose does not imply that the object or environment has been restored. MetaBrain and the required diagnostic agents use this updated evidence to prepare the recovery instruction. Spatial evidence, the relevant observation history, the base proposal, and the corrected candidate must all correspond to the current recovery state. Before dispatch, the runtime checks evidence freshness, joint limits, and obstacle clearance. If the state changes during candidate generation, it refreshes the affected evidence and regenerates the candidate before execution. After each corrective prefix, the runtime checks whether the required physical condition has been restored. If so, nominal execution resumes. Otherwise, the failed attempt and updated observations inform another recovery query. Each anomaly allows at most two additional viewpoints, each with a five-second allowance, and three physical retries. A MetaBrain request times out after ten seconds, and the entire recovery event is limited to 60 seconds. Resubmitting a query does not reset these counters. The runtime aborts recovery when a required condition cannot be verified or an applicable budget is exhausted. MetaBrain determines how the intended operation should change, grounding agents assess the physical conditions needed for that change, and A 2 -WLA generates the continuous controls. This division of labor allows reasoning and diagnostic capabilities to contribute through the shared CorrectionContext . It contains the task instruction, robot state, synchronized multiview history, and a target action chunk. Recorded future frames and phase annotations provide additional targets when available. Input histories end at the anchor step. Future observations and actions appear only as supervised targets or offline annotation evidence. For the BEHAVIOR 2026 configuration described here, training uses the official corpus of 20,000 teleoperated demonstrations, totaling approximately 1,950 hours across 100 household tasks and seven scenes [ 25 ] . Demonstrations are collected through the JoyLo whole-body interface, which coordinates the mobile base, torso, arms, and grippers. The average trajectory lasts 5.86 minutes, and the annotations cover 31 skill primitives, including grasping, pouring, wiping, and articulated-object manipulation [ 26 ] . These extended trajectories require the policy to handle object-state changes and dependencies between successive operations. Recorded timestamps align multiview images, proprioceptive states, and robot controls. Task instructions and skill intervals supply the corresponding language and phase annotations. Official head-camera streams recorded at 720 × 720 720\times 720 resolution and dual wrist-camera streams recorded at 480 × 480 480\times 480 resolution are bilinearly downsampled to 224 × 224 224\times 224 for policy training, matched with continuous robot states and controls at 30 Hz. Self-supervised world model pretraining ingests visual inputs at 256 × 256 256\times 256 spatial resolution. Robot states and action dimensions use quantile normalization computed from the training split. The embodiment adapter instantiates 23-dimensional absolute continuous commands encompassing whole-body actuation channels. Training windows retain the complete task instruction, while skill annotations define the temporal boundaries used for auxiliary progress supervision and transition sampling. When skill intervals overlap, the earlier-starting skill determines the phase label. Action supervision continues across skill boundaries wherever trajectory steps are valid. Separate masks identify valid phase labels, progress targets, and control dimensions, so a missing auxiliary label does not discard otherwise valid action supervision. History sampling schedule. We sample one positive look-back offset from each of the five intervals bounded by [ 30 , 84 , 138 , 192 , 246 , 300 ] [30,84,138,192,246,300] , using a log-uniform distribution within each interval. Deployment uses fixed relative offsets [ − 256 , − 205 , − 154 , − 60 , − 30 , 0 ] [-256,-205,-154,-60,-30,0] . At 30 Hz, training look-back offsets span one to ten seconds, and the oldest deployment frame is 8.53 seconds old. The boundary-sampling configuration increases sample weights fivefold within 60 frames on either side of a primitive transition. Continuous action optimization via flow matching constitutes the primary computational bottleneck within the multi-stage training pipeline.
In the baseline single-device implementation, each optimization step requires approximately 3.03 seconds, with input processing alone accounting for 1.30 seconds.
This delay stems from redundant visual decoding, where identical temporal video frames are repeatedly decompressed across overlapping observation windows.
To eliminate this bottleneck, we consolidate frame extraction through batch JPEG decompression, map demonstration datasets into Apache Arrow shared-memory caches, and streamline inter-dataset indexing.
These adjustments reduce per-step input preparation time from 1.30 seconds to 28 milliseconds.
Together with asynchronous background data prefetching, single-GPU step latency drops to 1.13 seconds, representing a 2.7 × 2.7\times training acceleration.
Furthermore, expanding dataset diversity from ten to 100 manipulation tasks incurs only a 3% throughput penalty, confirming that data routing scales with task coverage without introducing proportional preprocessing overhead. Figure 8 summarizes separate measurements of data preparation, step latency, throughput, and startup time. ▶ \blacktriangleright Distributed Startup and Synchronization. Distributed training across multi-GPU clusters frequently suffers from slow initial startup and hidden communication stalls.
During job initialization, dozens of worker processes often attempt to parse the same raw dataset files simultaneously, causing severe storage contention and prolonged idle waiting.
We parallelize dataset preparation, share sampler weights from rank zero, and delay parameter initialization on non-primary ranks to reduce repeated loading.
During active training steps, device synchronization is further streamlined by locking the backward communication order into a fixed static schedule.
This configuration groups scattered parameter gradients into contiguous memory buffers for unified all-reduce synchronization, while eliminating redundant broadcast transfers for static model buffers that do not change during training. ▶ \blacktriangleright Prefix Computation Sharing. A substantial computational redundancy in continuous flow matching arises from sampling multiple noise-time pairs ( τ , ϵ ) (\tau,\epsilon) for each demonstration trajectory.
Although these stochastic pairs evaluate distinct flow targets, they share identical visual histories, language instructions, and proprioceptive states.
Rather than evaluating the complete model repeatedly, our implementation computes the multimodal prefix once through the vision-language backbone.
The resulting representations are shared across the action expert’s noise-time evaluations, which are batched instead of run serially. The reference configuration uses four evaluations per demonstration on average, while the adaptive schedule varies the number by sample.
Each flow sample processes its own noisy continuous action and time coordinate, whereas resulting backpropagation gradients accumulate into the shared prefix representation.
For the same samples and masks, shared-prefix execution preserves the per-demonstration reduction in Equation ( 22 ) . Gradients from the action branches accumulate into the shared prefix. This equivalence concerns the shared-prefix computation and does not establish equivalence across complete training trajectories.
Together, shared-prefix execution and Arrow caching achieve 261.9 samples per second on eight GPUs. ▶ \blacktriangleright Kernel Stabilization and Optimizer Sharding. Additional latency in deep transformer training arises from dynamic sequence lengths and kernel launch overhead.
We mitigate these delays by caching static tokenizer outputs, rotary position embeddings, and task metadata whenever input sequences permit reuse.
Stable sequence-length buckets and padding upper bounds that increase only when necessary reduce repeated Triton compilation and autotuning.
These optimizations reduce static host-side overhead from 33 milliseconds to 0.8 milliseconds and forward latency from 243.7 to 210.7 milliseconds.
At the optimization boundary, we coordinate AdamW with the matrix-oriented Local Muon optimizer.
We distribute 126 independent matrix updates across eight local ranks according to their computational cost. Each update uses the configured Newton–Schulz iterations, and the results are gathered in BF16.
Sharded Local Muon reduces optimizer latency from 67.0 to 19.7 milliseconds. Bitwise equivalence was verified for the compared sharded optimizer updates. The final SDPA-based implementation reaches 427.1 demonstration samples per second on eight GPUs with global batch size 256. Multiple flow samples from one demonstration count as one demonstration sample.
The π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} reference reaches 106 demonstration samples per second on the same GPU hardware with matching image resolution and action horizon. Each implementation uses a batch size chosen to utilize the available resources. The resulting 4.03 × 4.03\times ratio compares implementation throughput under these conditions.

We next examine what task specialization reveals about the design of generalist action models. 6.2 Task Specialization and Model Design A generalist action model must share knowledge across activities while retaining the control precision needed for specific interactions. In our development experiments, a model trained specifically on a 12-task subset outperformed the shared model trained on all 100 tasks when evaluated on that subset. This observation raises a model-design question about how to preserve the benefits of broad training without diluting the representations needed for particular scene layouts, object interactions, and action sequences. We examine one form of specialization using a bank of A 2 -WLA policies trained on task subsets. Prior evaluations determine the task-to-model mapping and execution configuration, both fixed before the reported evaluation. Each episode uses one selected policy conditioned on the complete task instruction. Across all 100 tasks, this configuration reaches 0.6040 Q-score and 40.60% success, compared with 0.4597 and 27.20% for the shared model ( Section 5 ). The gains combine specialization, checkpoint selection, and execution configuration. These results motivate architectures that combine shared representations with specialized control capacity. One direction is to share the visual, temporal, and world-model backbone while allowing smaller action experts to adapt to different interaction patterns. A learned router conditioned on observations and task language could choose the appropriate action expert without a fixed mapping from benchmark task IDs. Another direction is to distill the specialized policies into one model, retaining their complementary skills while simplifying deployment. These possibilities remain to be tested under matched parameter and compute budgets. We examine execution cases to assess how this capability supports local recovery and whether recovery enables the robot to resume and complete the original task. 6.5.1 Emergent Skill Transfer in the Base Policy Figure 18 presents three representative cases. The boxing-glove washing task provides the clearest example. While carrying a glove toward the washing machine, the robot accidentally drops it, bends down to retrieve it, and resumes the washing sequence, ultimately completing the task. Although the demonstrations do not include recovery from such a drop, the policy adapts learned object-retrieval and transport skills to the unexpected state and incorporates the recovery into the remaining sequence. Similar adaptations occur in the other two tasks. During water fetching, the robot adjusts its grasp on an inverted bottle and completes a handover. During vegetable sorting, it regrasps a fallen ear of corn and resumes transport. These cases suggest that manipulation skills acquired through joint training can be reused and adapted across tasks, enabling autonomous recovery in states not covered by the demonstrations. However, this qualitative evidence does not identify the specific training tasks from which these skills originate. Recovery capability alone does not ensure efficient correction. Without targeted guidance, the base policy may spend extended periods on repeated attempts. In the water-fetching and vegetable-sorting episodes, prolonged retries exhaust the available time budget, resulting in timeout failures despite the observed adaptive behaviors. These cases motivate targeted guidance during recovery. Within A 2 -MEAS, MetaBrain combines foundation-model reasoning with current observations and execution history to generate corrective instructions. The Adapter translates this guidance into action adjustments, aiming to reduce unproductive retries and help the policy resume progress toward the original goal within the remaining episode budget. 6.5.2 Physical State Changes and Embodiment Constraints Failures can lead to prolonged retries and directly disrupt the scene conditions required for task execution ( Figure 19 ). Examples include a cart tipping over, a lamp falling into a gap between furniture, and food pieces scattering into the sink. The robot must then re-establish suitable grasping, support, or contact conditions. However, hardware and workspace constraints may place the required recovery actions beyond the robot’s reach, joint range of motion, or gripper capabilities. Such scene disruptions can therefore prevent task continuation when recovery demands exceed the robot’s physical capabilities. These constraints define the scope of reasoning, diagnosis, and action correction in A 2 -MEAS. Reliable long-horizon embodied autonomy requires learned policies, system orchestration, motion control, and hardware design to be developed and validated as an integrated system. Scheduling comparisons and demonstration statistics expose the demands of task transitions. The execution cases illustrate both local recovery by the base policy and its limits. Together, these studies distinguish improvements in action generation, intervention, and continued progress toward the task goal. 7 Discussion and Conclusion We treat long-horizon execution as a capability to be learned by the action model, with the agent system providing verification and correction at critical points. The experiments in Sections 5 and 6 examine this division of roles through task performance, action-model design, and grounded correction. 7.1 Simple Action Models and Room for Improvement A striking result is how far a relatively simple action architecture can go. A 2 -WLA augments a vision-language-action policy with motion-aware history and a latent world model that predicts future visual features. Before adding agent-driven correction, its task-specialized configuration reaches 0.5755 Q-score on the 50-task BEHAVIOR 2026 public subset, approximately 2.21 × 2.21\times the 0.2605 public score of RLC, the 2025 competition winner ( Table 8 ). Even the shared A 2 -WLA policy reaches 0.4522. RLC also uses explicit stage-transition logic and recovery heuristics [ 18 ] . The strong performance of A 2 -WLA before agent-driven correction therefore motivates learning more of the required behavior within the action model itself, while retaining the comparison’s limits. The useful lesson is to strengthen a small set of complementary capabilities by retaining recent interaction history, predicting how the scene will change, and translating that context into continuous control. The component study on Selected12 supports this direction. Adding the latent world model to the action-pretrained baseline raises Q-score from 0.4846 to 0.5183, while motion-aware history and the subsequent data improvements bring the full recipe to 0.5905. The remaining gap between partial progress and complete-task success indicates considerable room to improve temporal representations, predictive objectives, demonstration coverage, and training efficiency within this compact design. Better action policies would also give the multi-agent framework a stronger foundation for targeted correction and reliable task continuation. 7.2 Learning Long-Horizon Capability in the Action Model Long-horizon tasks involve nonlinear composition because the feasibility of a later operation depends on the physical state and interaction history produced by earlier operations. A bottle grasp that completes retrieval may be unsuitable for pouring, and retrieving one object can change hand availability for the next operation. As analyzed in Section 6.4.3 , independently successful subtasks do not automatically compose into a successful activity. Their ordering and transition conditions must be represented by the policy, the scheduler, or both. This motivates training the action policy directly on long-horizon task trajectories, rather than relying on a scheduler to assemble independently learned subtasks. The policy can learn recurring dependencies between operations from these trajectories. Its online decisions still depend on a finite observation window, so long-horizon training does not give it complete memory of the episode. The shared execution record and task-level reasoning supply complementary context. The sparse coverage of preceding contexts in the demonstration analysis further emphasizes the need for training data that preserve cross-step dependencies and cover different ways of reaching a task transition. Stronger foundation models offer another source of knowledge for action-policy learning. In our implementation, MetaBrain generates corrective instructions that train the correction encoder and adapter, while the original A 2 -WLA parameters remain frozen. The current results evaluate the learned correction interface. A further direction is to distill broader task reasoning and successful corrected executions into the main action-policy weights, which could reduce the need for repeated deliberation. The agent system supports this learned capability through verification and correction at critical execution points. It checks progress and physical feasibility, diagnoses deviations, and supplies grounded corrective context through the Adapter. The action model continues to generate the actions needed for the remaining activity. The fixed-policy Adapter experiments demonstrate the value of this complementary role. System-level feedback adjusts execution while the underlying model weights remain unchanged. Recovery is useful when the corrected state allows the policy to continue the unfinished activity. The 100-task analysis identifies where this division of roles must improve reliability. The system succeeds at least once on 88 tasks, but only 11 tasks succeed in all ten evaluated instances. High Q-scores also coexist with low complete-task success, as in food storage. Further development therefore requires both broader long-horizon competence in the action model and effective verification and correction at points where execution departs from the task goal. 7.3 Limitations and Future Directions The current correction process pauses execution for diagnosis, which limits its use for events that demand immediate reactions. We prioritize scaling and diversifying interaction data so that the action model learns how contact states evolve and how to respond to deviations from visual observations and execution history. Training trajectories should cover varied object properties, contact configurations, and both successful and failed attempts, including recovery and subsequent task continuation. Together with faster interaction between reasoning and control, this data-driven approach aims to strengthen timely responses within the learned policy. Correction training uses deviations generated at demonstration states, whereas deployed policies encounter failures that alter object arrangements and grasp configurations. Collecting these execution states and their subsequent outcomes would extend supervision to the situations in which correction is actually needed. This calls for a data pipeline that links failure detection, corrective instructions, and the success or failure of the remaining task. Current quantitative evaluations focus on household simulation. Additional Adapter tests examine cross-task generalization, while real-robot studies cover short-horizon manipulation and sequential coffee preparation. Future work will expand coverage to more task families, robot embodiments, and physical environments, and assess the consistency of performance gains across independent training and evaluation runs. Evaluation will continue to report both goal satisfaction and complete-task success to assess how improvements in individual operations contribute to reliable end-to-end execution. 7.4 Conclusion We present A 2 -MEAS, an embodied multi-agent framework that connects MetaBrain, grounding agents, and A 2 -WLA through code as a scaling interface . The learned correction encoder and adapter absorb MetaBrain-generated guidance during training and convert fresh corrective intent and spatial evidence into action adjustments while keeping model parameters fixed during deployment. On 100 BEHAVIOR 2026 tasks, the system achieves a Q-score of 0.6411 and a complete-task success rate of 45.40%, with at least one successful episode on 88 tasks. The accompanying training and evaluation infrastructure supports iteration across this broad task set, and coffee preparation tests a history-conditioned recovery variant on a real robot. Our approach combines long-horizon capability learned within the action model with system-level verification and correction at critical points. Further progress depends on strengthening both the learned continuity of execution and the effectiveness of targeted intervention. We will release models, training scripts, and evaluation tools to support research on this approach.
