We argue that the reusable content of a skill lies not in its surface wording but in its procedural transitions: which operation to execute under a given state and which observable outcome to produce.
Based on this observation, we propose State–Move–Outcome (SMO), a compilation-like framework that decomposes raw skills into reusable procedural bricks and organizes admitted bricks as an externalized symbolic structure.
When compiling a skill, SMO first retrieves and composes existing bricks, dynamically connects them through Outcome-to-State conditions, and creates a candidate brick only for an uncovered procedure; the candidate becomes reusable only after validation and admission.
Experiments on SkillsBench v1.1 demonstrate that SMO represents 232 rawSkills from 87 underlying tasks with 95 shared bricks, reducing the corresponding skill representations from 366,472 to 149,154 tokens—a 59.3% reduction and 2.46 × \mathbf{2.46}\times compression .
Across 232 skill-conditioned evaluation instances, SMO raises the average task score from 0.6379 to 0.7478 and increases 5/5 Pass instances from 138 to 164 , while matching or outperforming rawSkill on 208 of 232 instances .
It also reduces average runtime token consumption by 38.38% .
These results show that compiling natural-language skills into shared procedural units can jointly improve skill reuse, representation efficiency, and downstream execution. Xiamen University Introduction Large language model (LLM) agents increasingly rely on procedural knowledge stored outside model parameters.
Prior work has shown that external language structures can be treated not merely as static prompts, but as programs that can be compiled and optimized against downstream objectives ( Khattab et al. 2023 ) .
Recent agent systems further induce reusable workflows from interaction histories, distill reasoning strategies from successful and failed trajectories, dynamically organize linked memories, and manage growing skill ecosystems ( Wang et al. 2024 ; Ouyang et al. 2025 ; Xu et al. 2026 ; Li et al. 2026a ) .
Benchmarks have accordingly begun to evaluate skills as first-class agent artifacts rather than incidental prompts ( Li et al. 2026b ) .
Agent skills are therefore becoming persistent procedural infrastructure that must be represented and maintained at corpus scale. Most of this infrastructure is still authored and distributed as natural-language instructions or semi-structured SKILL.md packages.
SkillsBench, for example, defines a skill package around a natural-language SKILL.md file and associated resources; SkillOpt treats such a document as trainable external state; and AgentSkillOS studies ecosystems containing up to hundreds of thousands of complete skills ( Li et al. 2026b ; Yang et al. 2026 ; Li et al. 2026a ) .
This document-centric representation is convenient for authors, but it obscures reusable procedures.
As illustrated in Figure 1 , several rawSkills may express the same operation in substantially different prose, requiring the agent to interpret redundant content repeatedly.
A single document may also mix reusable operations with task-specific entities, paths, formats, and explanations.
The resulting corpus is difficult to compare, deduplicate, compose, and update systematically. Figure 1: One procedure, many descriptions.
Multiple rawSkills may encode the same operation using different natural-language expressions, causing repeated interpretation.
SMO compiles them into smoSkills that reference one shared admitted brick, reducing duplicated procedural context while retaining task-specific composition. Existing approaches address adjacent aspects of this problem rather than the corpus-level problem as a whole. (i) Experience-learning methods distill workflows, rules, memories, or hierarchical skills from agent trajectories ( Zhao et al. 2024 ; Peng et al. 2024 ; Wang et al. 2024 ; Chen et al. 2024 ; Ouyang et al. 2025 ; Xu et al. 2026 ; Xia et al. 2026 ; Ding et al. 2026 ) . (ii) Compilation and automated-design methods optimize declarative LM programs, function-call plans, agent modules, workflows, or individual skills ( Khattab et al. 2023 ; Kim et al. 2023 ; Hu et al. 2025 ; Zhang et al. 2025 ; Shang et al. 2025 ; Yang et al. 2026 ; Liang et al. 2026 ; Chen et al. 2026 ) . (iii) Ecosystem-level systems retrieve and orchestrate complete skills, while graph-based tool selectors structure transitions among predefined tools ( Li et al. 2026a ; Jia and Li 2026 ) .
These directions improve acquisition, optimization, discovery, or runtime execution, but they do not establish a shared procedural basis that can reconstruct many independently authored skill documents. Challenge 1: discovering reusable procedures across heterogeneous descriptions. Surface similarity is insufficient because distinct wording may implement the same transition, while similar wording may hide different preconditions, operations, or outcomes.
A corpus-level representation must therefore identify equivalence at the level of executable State–Move–Outcome behavior, while separating task-specific values from genuinely new operations. Challenge 2: reconstructing skills through valid composition. Decomposition alone does not guarantee that the resulting units can be reassembled.
A reusable unit must expose when it applies, what it does, and what it establishes; adjacent units can be connected only when the preceding Outcome satisfies the successor State.
Small residual differences must remain bounded, and uncovered procedures must not be silently approximated by incompatible units. Challenge 3: reducing redundancy without sacrificing utility. Shorter text is not necessarily a better skill representation.
Corpus compression must preserve the procedures required for downstream tasks, prevent unvalidated units from contaminating the shared basis, and reduce the context repeatedly loaded during execution.
Representation size, task utility, and runtime cost must consequently be evaluated together. We address these challenges with State–Move–Outcome (SMO), a compilation-like framework for natural-language skill corpora.
SMO atomizes each rawSkill into procedural requirements, reconstructs them through L1 brick matching, L2 instance binding, and L3 bounded-delta patching, and assembles the matched bricks into a graph through Outcome-to-State gates.
Compilation gaps are classified as bindings, bounded deltas, new-brick candidates, or out-of-family cases; admitted candidates and retained deltas are incorporated into the next frozen symbolic-basis snapshot.
On 232 SkillsBench v1.1 skill–task instances, SMO achieves 2.46 × \mathbf{2.46}\times representation compression , raises the average task score from 0.6379 to 0.7478 , and reduces average runtime token consumption by 38.38% . Our contributions are: 1. We introduce a State–Move–Outcome representation that separates reusable procedural transitions from task-specific bindings and bounded differences. 2. We present a corpus-level compilation procedure that prioritizes brick reuse, constructs skill graphs through Outcome-to-State gates, and admits uncovered procedures only through controlled basis evolution. 3. We demonstrate that shared procedural compilation can improve downstream task utility while reducing both skill-representation size and runtime token consumption. Related Work Agent Skill Learning and Management Recent agents convert interaction histories into reusable external knowledge.
ExpeL allows agents to extract transferable insights from experience, while Hypothesis, Verification, and Induction develops skills through self-driven interaction and verification ( Zhao et al. 2024 ; Peng et al. 2024 ) .
Agent Workflow Memory induces recurring workflows, ReUseIt synthesizes workflows from successful and failed executions, and ReasoningBank distills generalizable reasoning strategies ( Wang et al. 2024 ; Liu et al. 2026 ; Ouyang et al. 2025 ) .
AutoManual maintains structured rules before formulating an instruction manual, A-MEM dynamically links and evolves memory notes, and SkillGen induces an action-centric graph of step-wise skills ( Chen et al. 2024 ; Xu et al. 2026 ; Ding et al. 2026 ) .
SkillRL further constructs a hierarchical skill library that co-evolves with the policy ( Xia et al. 2026 ) .
These methods primarily learn reusable artifacts from interaction trajectories.
SMO instead starts from an existing corpus of independently authored skills and identifies procedural transitions shared across documents. Other work operates at the skill-document or ecosystem level.
SkillOpt optimizes individual natural-language skills, SkillsBench evaluates modular skill packages, and Anything2Skill turns heterogeneous resources into complete structured skill contracts organized in a SkillBank ( Yang et al. 2026 ; Li et al. 2026b ; Pan et al. 2026 ) .
AgentSkillOS organizes complete skills through a capability tree and DAG pipelines ( Li et al. 2026a ) .
These systems establish the value of skill optimization, structuring, evaluation, and orchestration, but retain the complete skill as their principal unit.
SMO instead decomposes many skills into a shared procedural basis and represents each skill through bindings and brick composition. Compilation and Structured Skill Representation DSPy establishes a compilation paradigm in which declarative LM programs are optimized against task-level objectives rather than executed as fixed prompts ( Khattab et al. 2023 ) .
At the agent-system level, ADAS searches over code-defined designs ( Hu et al. 2025 ) .
AgentSquare searches modular combinations ( Shang et al. 2025 ) , while AFlow searches workflow graphs ( Zhang et al. 2025 ) .
SMO applies this optimization perspective at a different representation level: it compiles independently authored natural-language skills into reusable State–Move–Outcome transitions shared across skills. Recent work also compiles or restructures individual agent programs and skills.
LLMCompiler constructs dependency-aware plans for parallel function calling; the Scheduling–Structural–Logical representation converts one skill document into a typed graph; and SkVM compiles skills for portable execution across models and harnesses ( Kim et al. 2023 ; Liang et al. 2026 ; Chen et al. 2026 ) .
AutoTool constructs a directed graph over predefined tools to reduce repeated model inference during tool selection ( Jia and Li 2026 ) .
These systems structure one program, skill package, or tool set at a time.
SMO instead discovers shared procedural units across a skill corpus and composes them only when their Outcome and State conditions are compatible. Compression of External Agent Knowledge Compression of external knowledge must preserve downstream utility rather than merely shorten its surface form.
Context-aware Prompt Compression removes less relevant sentences while retaining answer-critical context, CMT compresses documents into memories for continual knowledge learning, and SkillRL abstracts experience into hierarchical skills ( Liskavets et al. 2025 ; Li et al. 2025 ; Xia et al. 2026 ) .
SMO instead compiles an entire skill corpus: repeated procedures are stored once as admitted bricks, task-specific differences remain in bindings or bounded deltas, and evaluation jointly measures representation tokens, task performance, and runtime consumption. Derivations are deferred to the supplement. Experiments We evaluate whether SMO can compile natural-language skills into a compact shared representation while improving task performance and reducing inference cost.
The main study covers 87 SkillsBench tasks and 232 associated skills; a paired diagnostic corpus separately controls for generic structuring.
We examine representation compression, reuse, downstream performance, inference tokens, and ablations of structure and sharing. Experimental Setup Dataset and raw skills. We use SkillsBench v1.1 ( Li et al. 2026b ) at commit a8eefb4 : 87 tasks with isolated environments and 232 official SKILL.md documents.
We compile every document into one smoSkill while preserving its task mapping, yielding 232 skill-conditioned instances over 87 distinct tasks. Controlled fixture corpus. To separate generic structuring from brick sharing, we construct an internal engineering-diagnostic corpus with 20 source clusters and 60 primary units.
A source cluster groups units derived from the same diagnostic source; a primary unit is the content-preserving item transformed across conditions.
Each unit is converted one-to-one into three representations without changing its content or order, yielding 60 paired units per condition and 180 test units in total.
The paired design varies the external representation while holding unit content and ordering fixed.
These fixtures are used only for this representation-level control and are not drawn from SkillsBench, TerminalWorld, or Anything2Skill’s official data. Baselines and ablations. On SkillsBench, No Skill uses only the task instruction, rawSkill loads the official document, and SMO loads its compiled counterpart.
Length-Matched Compression controls for text length, while SMO w/o Sharing assigns private bricks to control for cross-skill reuse.
All use identical task inputs, executor, and environment. On the fixture corpus, Raw Control preserves the unstructured source; Structured Contract uses GPT-5.5 to render the same content as a complete standalone contract inspired by Anything2Skill ( Pan et al. 2026 ) ; and Full SMO uses its already compiled brick decomposition.
A local deterministic renderer serializes the SMO objects; it neither discovers nor rewrites bricks and makes no LLM call.
Structured Contract controls for generic structuring; it is not a reproduction of Anything2Skill’s taxonomy, SkillBank, retrieval pipeline, or datasets. Implementation details. Task execution uses the configured model identifier openai/gpt-5.5 with the Codex-ACP agent and reasoning_effort=high .
We use BenchFlow 0.4.0 (commit 7a479e9 ) in isolated Docker environments.
Each skill-conditioned instance is executed independently five times, with identical decoding settings across the compared methods.
We retain the task-specific time limits declared by the benchmark configurations rather than imposing a single global timeout. Representation length is computed from the serialized representation with the same tokenizer implementation pinned in the evaluation code for every condition.
It includes only the rawSkill or smoSkill representation, excluding task inputs and execution traces.
Runtime token consumption is obtained from execution logs.
We first average the five runs of each instance and then report a macro-average over the relevant instances.
This metric measures task-execution cost after deployment and excludes the one-time offline cost of compiling skills with SMO. Evaluation metrics. For each run, an independent GPT-5.5 judge scores the complete execution trace in [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] under a fixed rubric.
The judge provides semantic scores; deterministic programs check format and constraints and aggregate results.
On the fixture corpus, a unit is accepted only when its semantic score meets the pre-specified criterion and every deterministic check succeeds; the criterion is fixed across conditions.
We average five run-level scores per skill-conditioned instance and then macro-average over 232 instances. 5/5 Pass requires all five runs to be fully successful and is therefore not pass@5.
Better/Tie/Worse compares instance-level averages. Representation efficiency is reported through the number of shared bricks, total skill-representation tokens, percentage token reduction, and compression ratio.
Token reduction is the percentage decrease from the rawSkill representation to the corresponding smoSkill representation, and the compression ratio divides the rawSkill token count by the smoSkill token count.
Runtime efficiency is summarized by the average execution tokens per instance and their percentage decrease relative to rawSkill.

Controlled structured baseline. The fixture verifier accepts 36/60 Raw Control units and 60/60 units under both structured conditions.
Holding acceptance fixed, Table 1 (b) shows that Full SMO reduces the context-token index by 46.63% and serialized bytes by 53.54% relative to Structured Contract, giving 1.87 × 1.87\times token compression.
Unique-brick reuse is the fraction of evaluated bricks referenced by at least two distinct source clusters; the 95.0% rate therefore measures cross-cluster sharing. The 36/60 versus 60/60 acceptance contrast is not used as a downstream task-performance claim; it verifies that the two structured conditions preserve the fixture contracts before their representation sizes are compared.
Raw Control is therefore omitted from Table 1 (b): comparing its size with accepted representations would confound compression with missing contract content.
Token and byte indices are computed from the exact serialized outputs of each condition.
The token index measures model-context load, whereas the byte index checks that the observed reduction is not specific to one tokenizer. The reuse metric is inventory-based rather than invocation-weighted: each distinct brick counts once, so one frequently used brick cannot dominate the result.
Requiring references from at least two source clusters also excludes repeated use confined to one source.
The metric therefore targets the cross-source structural sharing that distinguishes a brick inventory from a collection of independent contracts. Because the 60 primary units are transformed one-to-one, every Full SMO item has a content-matched Structured Contract counterpart.
The 46.63% token reduction is therefore measured over paired representations rather than unrelated corpora or different task mixtures.
We treat this result as a controlled representation comparison after both conditions meet the same acceptance rule, not as an end-to-end reproduction or task-accuracy comparison with Anything2Skill. Together with the SkillsBench ablations, the controls isolate three alternatives.
Length-Matched Compression tests whether fewer tokens alone suffice; Structured Contract tests whether a complete standalone schema suffices; and SMO without sharing tests whether private structured units suffice.

Ablation Study Table 3 isolates representation length, SMO structure, and cross-skill reuse.
Despite matching full SMO’s 149,154 tokens, Length-Matched Compression scores 0.6025 with 129 5/5 Pass instances, below rawSkill.
Text shortening alone therefore cannot explain SMO’s gains. The fixture comparison adds a stronger structural control: after both Structured Contract and Full SMO reach 60/60 verifier acceptance, shared bricks still reduce context tokens by 46.63%.
This separates SMO’s sharing benefit from generic contract structuring. Disabling cross-skill sharing increases the representation to 379 private bricks and 228,312 tokens, reducing representation savings from 59.3% to 37.7% and runtime savings from 38.38% to 8.17%.
Yet SMO without sharing and full SMO score 0.7494 and 0.7478, respectively, and both attain 5/5 Pass on 164 instances.
Brick sharing therefore primarily improves representation and runtime efficiency without an evident aggregate-quality loss. When a task requires multiple smoSkills, a shared brick is injected once; private copies re-enter the context through each skill.
Taken together, the controls show that shorter text alone cannot account for the gains: SMO structure preserves task utility, while cross-skill sharing reduces representation and runtime context. The near-equal aggregate scores of full SMO and the no-sharing variant support this division of roles, but do not imply strict causal independence: changing context length or ordering may still alter individual executions.
These ablations evaluate deployed representations and runtime traces; the separate one-time compilation cost is not included. Limitations Our evaluation has two main limitations.
First, it uses a fixed SkillsBench collection, one executor configuration (GPT-5.5 with Codex-ACP), and a GPT-5.5 judge under a fixed rubric.
The Structured Contract comparison uses an internal diagnostic fixture corpus rather than Anything2Skill’s official data or complete system.
Results may not transfer directly to other models, harnesses, or skill collections. Second, SMO does not improve every instance: 24 instances regress from 5/5 Pass to non-5/5, suggesting that compilation may occasionally omit useful details or alter how instructions are interpreted.
Moreover, the reported runtime tokens exclude the one-time offline compilation cost, so the overall benefit depends on how frequently compiled skills are reused. Conclusion We introduced State–Move–Outcome (SMO), a compilation-like framework that transforms natural-language skills into reusable procedural bricks and dynamically assembles them into executable skill graphs.
On SkillsBench v1.1, SMO reduced representation tokens by 59.3%, improved the average task score from 0.6379 to 0.7478, and reduced average runtime token consumption by 38.38%.
Ablations indicate that these gains arise from the SMO structure and cross-skill reuse rather than text shortening or standalone structuring alone.
