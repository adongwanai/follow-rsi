Affiliation: StepFun Affiliation: FinStep Email: baizuo@stepfun.com Abstract Large language model (LLM) agents are extending agent-mediated commerce on the Web from shopping for consumers to operating businesses for sellers.
In online-to-offline (O2O) commerce, decisions are issued through Web-based platforms but realized through costly physical execution.
Evaluating such agents requires going beyond individual transactions to ask whether decisions made online turn into sustained economic returns.
We introduce AutoVend, a benchmark in which one LLM agent manages 20 vending machines across five site types through a Web-based operating interface for 180 simulated days.
Its data-grounded simulator combines a shared warehouse, procurement lead times, paid replenishment, inventory expiration, and recurring operating costs.
We evaluate five LLM agents against a rule-based inventory-control baseline and diagnose their site coordination, execution, and performance over time.
Four of five evaluated LLM agents fall short of this baseline, and returns per dollar of inference cost rank models differently from returns alone.
Operational analyses show that gains in individual metrics do not consistently yield stronger economic growth, as inventory and execution costs erode these gains.
Trajectory analysis further reveals difficulties in grounding feasible actions, managing inventory commitments, and converting feedback into effective policy revisions. 1 Introduction Agent-mediated commerce is emerging as a Web application domain for large language model (LLM) agents, with buyer-side research spanning product discovery Yao et al. (2022) , personalized shopping Kim et al. (2026) , and customer support Wang et al. (2025) .
Seller-side autonomy requires repeated decisions about assortment, pricing, procurement, and inventory whose consequences extend beyond an individual interaction Shi et al. (2026) ; Fan et al. (2026) .
In online-to-offline (O2O) commerce, decisions are made on Web-based management platforms, while their economic returns depend on the costs and constraints of physical execution.
For agents managing distributed retail through such platforms, this requires coordinating digital decisions with physical execution over time.
Smart vending is a compact instance, as connected machines are increasingly run through cloud management platforms Fortune Business Insights (2026) while every restock requires a paid site visit.
It also retains the dependencies among assortment, inventory, execution, and cash management.
For Web economics, AutoVend thus provides a controlled testbed for seller-side agent-mediated commerce that measures both the returns an agent generates through a Web-based interface and the inference cost of generating them. Figure 1: AutoVend connects Web-based agent decisions to distributed physical retail through shared inventory, procurement lead times, and paid on-site replenishment. Sustaining performance in this setting requires three interdependent capabilities. (1) Cross-site Resource Coordination. The agent must match heterogeneous local demand while allocating shared resources and monitoring conditions across the network Eppen (1979) .
An allocation to one site changes the resources available to others, so local decisions must account for consequences across the network. (2) Physical Execution Planning. Stock in a shared warehouse cannot meet local demand until it is placed on machine shelves.
Procurement requires advance ordering to account for delivery lead times, while on-site replenishment incurs visit costs.
The agent must coordinate advance orders and site visits to maintain product availability while controlling execution expenses, storage costs, and expiration losses Coelho et al. (2014) ; Nahmias (1982) . (3) Continual Strategy Improvement. Demand is initially unknown, and observed sales depend on the agent’s own assortment, prices, and stock availability.
The agent must use accumulated feedback to refine its demand estimates and revise its decision rules so that subsequent decisions become more effective Zhang et al. (2026) ; Shi et al. (2026) . Existing benchmarks provide important foundations for evaluating long-horizon business agents.
Vending-Bench studies the operation of a single vending machine and simulates physical tasks through a separate execution agent Backlund and Petersson (2025) .
RetailBench evaluates supermarket operation with inventory aging, delayed deliveries, and cash-flow constraints Zhang et al. (2026) .
MerchantBench examines seller-side e-commerce under mixed-latency feedback Shi et al. (2026) , while E-Commerce Bench extends business evaluation to multiple online stores with pooled inventory, fulfillment costs, and delayed settlements Fan et al. (2026) .
These evaluations do not establish how effectively agents can coordinate shared inventory across heterogeneous physical retail sites when on-site replenishment itself incurs costs and delays.
Assessing this capability requires an environment that brings these constraints together and exposes their cumulative economic consequences. We introduce AutoVend , a benchmark for evaluating LLM agents on long-horizon autonomous vending operations.
A single agent manages 20 vending machines spanning factory, campus, residential, commercial, and office locations, with heterogeneous demand and a shared warehouse.
The agent selects products, sets prices, places procurement orders, and arranges replenishment through tool calls that mirror such a platform.
The simulator models procurement lead times, paid on-site stocking, product expiration, and storage charges on warehouse stock.
These mechanisms connect decisions made through the interface to inventory availability and subsequent economic outcomes. AutoVend combines terminal net assets with operational metrics and trajectory diagnostics.
We examine site coordination, the cost and availability of paid replenishment, and changes in operating performance over time.
Our contributions are threefold. • We introduce AutoVend, a benchmark for long-horizon seller-side agent-mediated commerce, with a unified task, operating interface, and evaluation protocol for managing a distributed retail network. • We develop a simulator grounded in real vending records that models heterogeneous demand, procurement lead times, inventory aging, and paid site visits, and validate its demand behavior against observed sales patterns. • We evaluate five LLM agents and find that four of five fall short of a rule-based inventory-control baseline.
Operational diagnostics and trajectory analyses reveal difficulties in coordinating inventory commitments, execution costs, and policy revision. 2 Related Work Agent Evaluation in Commerce. Commerce is a prominent domain for evaluating LLM agents on the Web, with benchmarks for online shopping Yao et al. (2022) ; Peeters et al. (2026) ; Kim et al. (2026) , customer service Yao et al. (2025) ; Wang et al. (2025) , and multi-agent markets Bansal et al. (2025) ; Zheng et al. (2026) .
Their evaluations typically end with a completed task, dialogue, or transaction, whereas a seller’s decisions affect inventory and cash long after they are made.
Seller-side benchmarks instead evaluate the sustained operation of a business mainly by its accumulated economic outcome.
MerchantBench and StoreBench each cover a single online store Shi et al. (2026) ; Raghuvanshi et al. (2026) , Business Arena a single cross-border shop Pan et al. (2026) , and E-Commerce Bench up to four online stores that share a warehouse Fan et al. (2026) .
In physical retail, RetailBench covers a single supermarket Zhang et al. (2026) and Vending-Bench a single vending machine Backlund and Petersson (2025) , a setting that Vending-Bench 2 extends to a simulated year and its Arena to several competing agents with one machine each Andon Labs (2025) .

Long-horizon benchmarks commonly lengthen the work toward a defined goal, as in workflows on websites and computers Zhou et al. (2024) ; Yuan et al. (2026) , tasks in a simulated company Xu et al. (2025) , and exploration of partially observable environments Luo et al. (2026) .
Sustained-operation benchmarks instead fix a span of simulated calendar time in which costs accrue and the environment evolves whether or not the agent acts, so performance is an accumulated economic result rather than goal completion Backlund and Petersson (2025) ; He et al. (2026) .
Because terminal outcomes alone reveal little about why agents succeed or fail, evaluations add progress rates Ma et al. (2024) , capability profiles across operating dimensions Fan et al. (2026) , and attribution of gains and losses to actions Pan et al. (2026) .
Continual-learning benchmarks further ask whether agents turn accumulated experience into reusable capabilities Zheng et al. (2025) ; Cheng et al. (2026) .

The network comprises six worksite, two campus, four residential, six commercial, and two office machines, with differences in product preference, price sensitivity, and holiday response.
Each machine holds at most 200 units and is replenished from one shared warehouse.
Procurement is paid at placement.
Orders arrive in the warehouse three days after placement.
Staff are available for on-site visits on working days, and multiple visits can be made within a day.
Replenishment and removal of shelf stock require physical execution through an on-site visit, which costs 35 CNY per machine per day regardless of the number of operations. Inventory aging and costs. Batches expire 30 or 90 days after warehouse arrival.
Selling expired products incurs a fine of 1,000 CNY per unit sold.
Warehouse rent is 0.03 CNY per unit per day, and machine rent is 4 CNY per machine per day.
The agent must balance stock availability against storage costs and expiration risk, since excess inventory incurs recurring charges and may expire before it is sold. Customer demand. Demand is generated separately for each machine, with temporal dependence introduced by persistent daily shocks.

AutoVend exposes the remote operations available to a networked-vending operator as tools over the Model Context Protocol (MCP) Anthropic (2024) , with physical tools standing in for dispatching staff on site.
Each day begins with due order arrivals and charges for warehouse rent and fixed operating fees, followed by a daily observation for the agent.
During the action window, the agent queries operating records and executes actions, receiving query results or execution feedback after each tool call.
Valid actions update the operating state immediately.
When the agent closes the action window, the simulator realizes demand using the resulting shelf inventory and prices, settles sales and on-site visit costs, and proceeds to the next day. Prompts. A fixed system prompt specifies the objective, starting conditions, goods flow, operating costs, inventory and pricing constraints, and qualitative demand mechanisms without prescribing an operating strategy.
A daily user prompt updates the date and supplies a compact report of cash and net assets, recent profit and loss, warehouse and in-transit stock, upcoming holidays, and each machine’s inventory, sales, and expiry status.
Product-level details are retrieved through queries rather than included in the daily prompt.
Appendices A and B provide the complete prompts and tool specifications. Tools and memory. AutoVend provides 18 tools organized into three functional groups covering remote operations, physical execution, and persistent memory. • Remote operational tools. These support procurement, machine-specific pricing, warehouse disposal, and closing the daily action window.
Queries provide product attributes and costs, warehouse and shelf inventory, product-level sales within and across machines, and financial records.
Read-only SQL supports custom analysis of operating records, while calendar and weather queries provide published holidays and near-term weather information. • Physical execution tools. These transfer warehouse stock onto machine shelves and return specified quantities or all remaining units of a product to the warehouse.
Execution requires paid on-site visits, charged once per machine per day regardless of the number of operations. • Memory tools. These allow the agent to read and overwrite a persistent notebook containing selected observations, plans, and findings.
Conversation history continues across days, while the notebook survives context compaction. 4 Experiments 4.1 Experimental Setup Baseline. We evaluate Claude Sonnet 5.5 Anthropic (2026) , GPT-6.1 Sol OpenAI (2026) , DeepSeek V4.1 Flash DeepSeek-AI (2026) , Kimi K3 Kimi Team (2026) , and GLM-5.3-Flash Z.ai (2026) .
We use the Pi harness Zechner (2026) and configure each model with its highest supported reasoning effort.
Each agent operates the same 20-machine network for 180 simulated days, starting with 40,000 CNY and using identical prompts, tools, and daily interaction budgets.
We report means and the standard deviation of terminal net assets over three runs per model, all in one frozen world with the same random seed.
Variation across LLM runs thus reflects stochastic agent decisions and the demand draws they induce.
The deterministic rule-based baseline attains 208.73 ± 2.26 208.73\pm 2.26 thousand CNY (CV 1.1%) across seeds 1–3, and we report seed 1. The rule-based baseline combines the newsvendor critical ratio Arrow et al. (1951) with periodic order-up-to replenishment Silver and Robb (2008) .
It sets inventory targets from rolling 30-day demand and variability estimates, balancing lost sales against holding and spoilage costs, and replenishes sites weekly.
Procurement accounts for delivery lead times, available stock, and outstanding orders.
Assortments follow observed sales, while prices are adjusted through local gross-profit trials.
All decisions use observable information. Metrics. Our primary outcome, Net Assets , is terminal cash plus unexpired inventory valued at cost across shelves, the warehouse, and transit. Assortment compares expected gross profit with a heuristic reference mix under common prices, display volume, and environmental conditions. Waste Rate is inventory loss divided by valid sales cost plus loss, counting expiry and early disposal once. Pricing Regret is the expected gross-profit shortfall from a price-search reference at a fixed site, assortment, and inventory. Shelf Utilization measures the mean fraction of capacity occupied by unexpired stock before sales across sites and days.
We also report the share of site-days with empty shelves. Capital Efficiency is mean daily gross profit divided by mean inventory value at cost. Tool Calls counts tool invocations over the episode. API Efficiency averages per-run net asset growth per USD of estimated API expenditure, using standard reference rates for recorded input, output, cache, and compaction usage. 4.2 Main Results Table 2: Main experimental results on AutoVend.

No single model leads across all five dimensions, and stronger profitability does not consistently coincide with better inventory control, pricing, or capital efficiency.
We examine these differences below to characterize how each agent combines product selection, inventory management, and resource use during long-horizon operations. Profitability. Kimi K3 achieves the lowest return at 91.52 thousand CNY. Assortment. GPT-6.1 Sol leads the LLM agents in assortment quality, scoring 0.992.
GLM-5.3-Flash follows at 0.817, while Claude Sonnet 5.5 has the lowest score at 0.500. Inventory. Claude Sonnet 5.5 and GLM-5.3-Flash record the lowest waste rates at 0.16% and 0.27%, respectively.

All 15 LLM executions finish above the initial capital, whereas only GPT-6.1 Sol exceeds the rule-based baseline in all three executions.
The trajectories in Figure 5 (a) also distinguish sustained growth from early gains followed by stagnation, particularly for Kimi K3. 5.3 RQ2: Can agents coordinate resources across sites? Site coordination remains incomplete even for the best-performing agent.
We evaluate this through the best reassignment of existing assortments across sites at six 30-day checkpoints, using the standardization of the assortment metric.
GPT’s actual assignment falls 7.05% below this restricted optimum, compared with 2.61% for the baseline in Figure 5 (b).
Its strong aggregate return therefore coexists with opportunities to improve where its selected products are offered. Because this diagnostic introduces no new products, the gap identifies unrealized value in matching existing offerings to heterogeneous sites.
Conversely, a small reassignment gap does not establish strong coordination when the underlying assortments are weak.
DeepSeek and Kimi illustrate this distinction through their limited reassignment gains and low assortment scores.
Coordination must therefore be assessed jointly with product quality, since efficient placement cannot compensate for unsuitable offerings. 5.4 RQ3: Can agents plan physical execution? Agents do not consistently balance local availability with economical execution.
Over days 16–180, GPT and DeepSeek both avoid completely empty shelves, but GPT achieves approximately twice DeepSeek’s visit-cycle contribution per site-day in Figure 5 (c).
Sonnet and Kimi instead exhibit persistent empty-site exposure despite holding corresponding products in the warehouse. These outcomes reflect two separate requirements of physical execution.
Central inventory becomes available to customers only after a paid on-site transfer, so procurement alone cannot maintain local supply.
Conversely, visits that preserve availability can absorb a substantial share of gross profit when they are too costly relative to the sales they support.
Effective execution requires coordinating replenishment timing and visit frequency with local demand.
Cycle returns, measured as sales gross profit minus visit fees divided by cycle duration, must therefore be interpreted alongside availability and the costs excluded from this measure. 5.5 RQ4: Can agents improve through feedback? Figure 6: Early- and late-period operating outcomes.
Markers show mean LLM outcomes and rule-based baseline outcomes. The trajectories provide limited evidence of consistent economic improvement as feedback accumulates.
Comparing days 16–45 with days 136–165 in Figure 6 , daily asset growth decreases for all five model means and in 13 of the 15 executions.
Meanwhile, several agents improve individual operating metrics.
Better waste control or pricing therefore does not consistently translate into a stronger overall operating policy. An accounting decomposition helps explain this disconnect.
DeepSeek generates more gross profit, but additional warehouse and visit expenses offset most of that gain.
These patterns suggest that local corrections can coexist with increasingly costly inventory and execution commitments.
However, changing environmental conditions and inventory states prevent this temporal comparison from causally identifying whether feedback itself improves the agents’ decisions. 5.6 Failure Modes Trajectory inspection identifies four observed failures in action feasibility, inventory cost planning, physical execution, and policy revision.
These cases illustrate behavior without establishing prevalence or causal effects. Incomplete Grounding of Action Plans. In one Kimi K3 run, all 49 allocations in a day 170 restocking batch were rejected because the requested products lacked sufficient warehouse stock.
The agent had observed 4,701 warehouse units in aggregate and attempted replenishment across seven sites while an order remained in transit.
After rejection, it ended the day and waited for delivery.
The episode shows why allocation plans require product-level availability checks. Insufficient Planning for Inventory Costs. In another Kimi run, six purchases between days 136 and 169 repeated an identical bundle of 21 products.
Although core products required replenishment, the bundle also included products with no recorded sales during this interval.
For one such product, 2,158 unexpired units were already in the warehouse before day 169, yet the agent ordered another 80 units.
Procurement thus continued to expand inventory that was not being deployed or consumed. Fragmented Physical Execution. In one DeepSeek V4.1 Flash run, a campus machine generated only 5.14 CNY of gross profit between visits on days 125 and 126, against a 35 CNY visit fee.
The agent returned on day 126 to add just four units, incurring another visit fee.
This sequence illustrates how frequent responses to small stock changes can create interventions whose costs are poorly matched to site-level turnover. Ineffective Use of Operating Feedback. GLM-5.3-Flash illustrates the difference between immediate error recovery and persistent policy revision.
On day 163, four allocations requesting four units each were rejected because only three units of the product remained in the warehouse.
The agent then successfully allocated those three units to one site.
However, the same four infeasible allocations reappeared on day 168, followed by the same local repair.
The feedback supported correction within each interaction but did not prevent the allocation pattern from recurring. 6 Conclusion We introduced AutoVend, a benchmark for long-horizon autonomous business management across 20 heterogeneous vending sites with shared inventory, procurement lead times, and paid physical execution.
Evaluation of five LLM agents over 180 simulated days shows that four of five underperform a rule-based inventory-control baseline.
Operational diagnostics reveal that strong aggregate returns can coexist with coordination gaps, while improvements in individual metrics need not produce stronger economic growth.
The trajectory cases further expose difficulties in verifying feasible allocations, adapting procurement, consolidating visits, and retaining corrections across decisions.
Future work should test these findings, drawn from three runs per model in one environment, across varied operating conditions and controlled interventions such as explicit state verification and persistent policy updates. Appendix A Prompts A.1 System Prompt ⬇ You operate a network of 20 smart vending machines for 180 days , starting with 40000 in cash . Each day is one turn : you receive an observation , may call tools , then call end_day . ## Objective Maximize net assets at the end of the run . Net assets = cash + the cost value of every unexpired unit you hold , wherever it sits : on a shelf , in the warehouse , or in transit . - The only thing that increases it is selling above unit cost . - The things that decrease it : warehouse rent , labor , the daily fee , expired - sale fines , and stock that expires or is discarded unsold . - Buying stock moves value from cash into inventory ; it is not by itself a gain or a loss . ## Starting state On day 1 every shelf and the warehouse are empty , no order is in transit , and there is no sales history . The catalog is the only thing that exists : 1372 SKUs . Nothing sells until you order stock , wait for it to arrive , and restock a device on site . ## Goods flow order -> in transit -> warehouse -> restock ( on site ) -> shelf -> sale - Ordered units are debited from cash immediately . - The supplier promises 3 days ; actual delivery may be earlier or later . Units in transit cannot be shelved and cannot serve a customer . Arrivals by day and any delayed orders are in the daily observation . - Units land in the warehouse . The warehouse is the only pool a restock can draw from . - Reducing or delisting returns units from a shelf to the warehouse ; they are not destroyed . - A unit leaves your system only when a customer buys it or when you discard it . ## Prices Prices are per ( device , SKU ), and each of the 20 machines is configured independently . A price may be set anywhere from 0.50 x to 2.00 x the SKU ’ s suggested price ; out - of - band prices are rejected . ## Costs Warehouse rent is 0.03 per unit of volume per day . Every unit of every SKU takes exactly one unit of volume , so rent is charged per unit held and a machine ’ s capacity is a count of units . On top of rent you pay a fixed fee of 80 per day whether or not you act . On - site actions ( restock , reduce , delist ) cost 35 per device per day touched — a device touched any number of times in one day is charged once . Ordering , repricing , discarding and all queries are remote and cost nothing . Each tool description says which kind it is . ## Shelf life Each SKU has a ‘ shelf_life_days ‘ value in the catalog , counted from the day a unit arrives in the warehouse . Restocking , reducing and delisting do not reset that clock . Expired units do not disappear , and they are worth nothing in net assets . - Expired units on a shelf still sell , and every expired unit sold costs an extra 1000. Delisting sends them back to the warehouse , which stops the fine exposure but does not make them sellable again . - Expired units in the warehouse keep taking up volume and incurring rent . They cannot be restocked into anything but more expired stock ; discarding is the only way to remove them . ## Funding Cash can go negative . Rent , the daily fee , visits and purchases keep being charged , no interest is added , and the run does not end . A negative balance counts against net assets one for one , so buying on credit does not change net assets by itself — it moves cash into inventory at cost . What it does cost is rent for every day the stock sits , and 3 days before it can be sold . ## Environment Shanghai , CN ( Asia / Shanghai ). All amounts are in CNY . Temperature , rain and public holidays affect demand , differently by product category and by site . ## Demand - Fill level : a fuller device sells faster . Free volume depresses demand for every item on that device , not only for the missing ones . - Depth : an item ’ s sales stop increasing beyond a few units on the shelf ; further units only accrue rent . - Noise : daily sales are random around that item ’ s underlying rate , and that rate is what a longer window of observations estimates . - Demand rate , price response and product fit all vary with location type . A product that sells at one site can be dead stock at another , and the same price change can help at one site and hurt at another . ## Memory Saved notes are never trimmed or shortened . They are limited to 6,000 characters . ## Operating loop There is no human user : no one will prompt you , approve a plan , or flag a problem . When the day ’ s work is done , call end_day .
