Traditional similarity-based schema matching methods fail to resolve semantic ambiguities and conflicts
in complex, domain-specific mappings due to a lack of commonsense and domain-specific knowledge. In
parallel, large language models (LLMs) without sufficient background knowledge tend to hallucinate, further
complicating schema matching. To overcome these limitations, we introduce KG-RAG4SM, a Knowledge
Graph-based Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Schema Matching. This method integrates novel retrieval
mechanisms, including LLM-based, vector-based, graph traversal-based, and hybrid techniques with advanced
reranking schemes to extract the most relevant subgraphs from large external KGs. Our approach achieves
superior results without requiring re-training, as evidenced by improvements of 39.79% in precision and 30.56%
in F1 score over LLM-based state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods (e.g., Jellyfish-8B) on the MIMIC dataset, and
enhancements of 69.20% in precision and 21.99% in F1 score over pre-trained language model (PLM)-based
SOTA methods (e.g., SMAT) on the Synthea dataset. Additionally, KG-RAG4SM scales effectively across KGs
from commonsense KGs to domain-specific ones, while its instruction-tuning knowledge exclusion component
ensures truthful performance in end-to-end schema matching tasks despite the suboptimal relevance of KG
context. Our empirical analysis and case studies from real-world scenarios show that the hallucination problem
in LLMs for schema matching is well mitigated by our solution.

Introduction
Schema matching reconciles multiple database schemas by identifying semantic correspondences
between source and target schema elements through mapping techniques [19]. In conventional
approaches, similarity scores are computed for each pair of attribute names and accepted if they
exceed a predefined threshold. However, these methods often lack the depth to recognize im-
plicit semantic relationships, such as synonyms, abbreviations, and different terminologies from
heterogeneous schemas, due to their inherent reliance on surface-level similarity. This seman-
tic heterogeneity frequently necessitates human intervention and domain expertise to validate
mapping decisions [54]. Given the vast number of entities and attributes across heterogeneous
databases, such manual oversight is impractical. For example, disparate management information
systems (MIS), including electronic health records (EHR), must often be integrated into a stan-
dardized database to ensure efficient data access and management. Here is an example of schema
matching between the e-MedSolution data model [40] and the OMOP CDM data model [23] from
the real-world scenario. As shown in Figure 1, the ATTEND_DOCTOR from hun_case_i(o) table is
a semantically similar attribute with the DOCTOR of hun_case_diag table in the source schema,
and both of them should be matched with the provider_id from CONDITION_OCCURRENCE table in
the target schema. Traditional schema matching approaches struggle with such complex scenarios
due to semantic heterogeneity and ambiguity, as they do not effectively capture the semantic
information and the meaning of the tables [16].
In recent years, language models have demonstrated superior capabilities in schema matching
and heterogeneous data integration. For instance, past works propose to integrate the textual
description of entities and attributes with large language models (LLMs) [51], e.g., fine-tuning
LLMs [63, 83], prompting LLMs [15], and augmenting LLMs [60] to generate the top-𝑘mappings.
Other works employ pre-trained language models (PLMs) to classify the potential mappings [68, 88].

Knowledge Graphs (KGs) are one of the widely used knowledge bases, where the rich semantic
knowledge and fact-based relations are structured in the form of ⟨subject, predicate, object⟩
triples [10]. They store large-scale, real-world facts using graph structures in a highly curated
manner [86], where a node (subject or object) represents an entity, and an edge (predicate) denotes
a relationship between two entities. The structured semantic knowledge in external commonsense
KGs makes it possible to enhance the language models with knowledge inference by incorporating
commonsense knowledge, factual knowledge, and domain knowledge, thereby resolving complex
matching cases without any re-training of LLMs. In particular, commonsense, fact-based, and
domain-specific KGs can be introduced to augment the LLMs and better handle the above complex
schema matching case. This is mainly facilitated by the commonsense and fact-based KGs, such
as Wikidata [69], which contain several common knowledge facts for taxonomic and hierarchical
relationships, acronyms, abbreviations, relatedness, etc., which are always accessible and retrievable.

Given that the rich fact-based semantic knowledge inherent in KGs has demonstrated remarkable
performance in knowledge reasoning and semantic disambiguation [56, 75], KGs can be exploited
to augment the capability of LLMs for the aforementioned complex schema matching. Unlike
mainstream Graph RAG and KG-RAG for knowledge graph question answering (KGQA), where
local, small-scale KGs can be built from benchmark datasets or text chunks [22, 32, 57, 58], there
are no internal KGs within databases that can be directly leveraged to augment LLMs for complex
schema matching because the required semantic knowledge exists in external and large KGs. It
is not technically feasible to augment the LLMs with these large-scale KGs directly, because the
contexts of LLMs have a finite length [76]. Besides, context-poisoning issues may occur if too
much irrelevant knowledge is utilized to augment the prompt. As a result, the performance of
the LLM-based schema matching will decrease [51]. Therefore, the key technical challenge of
augmenting LLMs with external KGs lies in how to effectively and efficiently retrieve the relevant
subgraphs from the large-scale external KGs, for instance, Wikidata includes billions of triples [69].
To address the above challenges, we propose a Knowledge Graph-based Retrieval Augmented
Generation for Schema Matching (KG-RAG4SM). Our method integrates vector-based, LLM-based,
and graph traversal-based retrievals to identify the relevant subgraphs from large and external
KGs and further prunes the irrelevant knowledge by using a ranking scheme. Then, it leverages
the retrieved subgraphs to augment the LLMs and prompts for generating the final results for the
complex schema-matching task.

To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to augment the LLMs and prompts for schema matching
tasks with the retrieved relevant subgraphs from large and external KGs. Unlike the emerging Graph
RAG [22, 58, 72] and KG-RAG [32, 57] that retrieve local neighbors or subgraphs from small, self-
constructed graphs out of text chunks, or the inherent KGs derived from the knowledge graph
question answering (KGQA) dataset, our KG-RAG4SM retrieves relevant subgraphs directly from
large external commonsense KGs. This enables the system to leverage rich factual knowledge to
resolve complex cross-domain schema matching tasks. Mainstream graph reasoning methods [5,
38, 65, 90] generate multi-step paths through a KG, typically via beam search or chain-of-thought
prompting, which introduces significant computational overhead on large graphs and complex
queries. KG-RAG4SM takes a different route: it uses hybrid retrieval, knowledge refinement, and
exclusion mechanisms to extract a compact, task-relevant factual subgraph from large external
KGs, enabling one-shot grounding rather than iterative path construction. By grounding the LLM
in retrieved precise facts instead of internalizing graph thinking patterns, KG-RAG4SM delivers
more efficient and accurate cross-domain schema matching performance. Our contributions are
outlined as follows.
• We present a knowledge graph-based retrieval-augmented generation for schema matching that
retrieves and integrates the relevant subgraphs from large external KGs into LLM-based schema
matching via in-context learning.

Problem Statement
We formally define the problem of knowledge graph-based retrieval-augmented generation for
schema matching. Given a large language model (LLM), an external and large-scale knowledge
graph KG, a retrieved subgraph G, and a schema matching task 𝜃, formally, the KG-RAG4SM is a
transformation F :
LLM′ := F (LLM, G)
s.t.
𝑎LLM′ (𝜃) > 𝑎LLM(𝜃)
where LLM′ denotes the LLMs augmented with the retrieved subgraph G (G ⊂KG) from large-size
KG. The constraint 𝑎LLM′ (𝜃) > 𝑎LLM(𝜃) describes that the overall correctness of the generated
answers for schema matching by LLMs augmented with retrieved subgraphs is better than the
answers generated by LLMs without retrieved subgraphs from the KG for schema matching.
In our KG-RAG4SM, KG-RAG is introduced to augment the generation phase of LLMs for complex
tasks by integrating LLM-based, vector-based, and graph traversal-based, as well as hybrid retrievals
to retrieve the relevant subgraphs from large KGs. Technically, KG-RAG augments the question
using the top-𝑘subgraphs from KG based on vector similarity and ranking.

Methodology
We provide an overview and technical details of the proposed solutions. Our key technical contri-
butions include a thorough investigation of the novel KG-RAG paradigm for the schema matching
problem by designing efficient subgraph retrieval methods via LLM-based, vector-based, and graph
traversal-based retrievals, as well as a hybrid approach that integrates them after analyzing their
trade-offs, followed by effective ranking schemes to provide precise knowledge for augmenting the
LLMs in schema matching.

3.2
Augmenting LLM with Knowledge Graphs
We empirically find that the attribute names and their textual descriptions alone are not sufficient
in the LLM schema matching pipeline for complex matching scenarios that require semantic
contextual information. To extract semantic similarity effectively and efficiently in the form of
relevant subgraphs from external knowledge graphs (KGs), we develop a number of retrieval
methods as described below. They generally follow three main steps: (1) retrieval of relevant
entities and relations from the KG w.r.t. the question; (2) then identifying relevant subgraphs
induced by the relevant entities from the KG; and finally (3) pruning and ranking of the relevant
subgraphs to provide more precise semantic contextual information in LLM prompts.
3.2.1
Retrieving Relevant Entities from the KG. Before retrieving the subgraph from KG, we need to
retrieve the entities from large-size KGs that are relevant to the given question of schema matching.
We follow one of the two developed paradigms for this purpose.
LLM-based Entity Retrieval. Given a schema-matching question, in this approach, we ask an
LLM (e.g., GPT-4o-mini) to retrieve a few relevant entities from the KG. Our prompts for this
purpose, including a few-shot examples with their correct answers and explanations, are illustrated
in Appendix A.2. The placeholders {question} are replaced with the real questions from our selected
dataset. Nevertheless, LLMs can hallucinate; thus, they may report some entities that do not, in
fact, exist in the commonsense KG. Therefore, we need to verify the retrieved entities by LLMs
before retrieving the subgraphs among these entities. It is worth mentioning that LLM-based
entity retrieval only works well for commonsense and fact-based KGs (e.g., Wikidata) rather than
domain-specific KGs, since the former KGs or their open text data forms (e.g., Wikipedia) might
have already been utilized to pre-train the LLMs. As a result, these KGs are well-known to LLMs,
while the domain-specific KGs may not be well-known to LLMs.
Vector-similarity based Entity Retrieval. This method leverages vector similarity search to
retrieve the relevant entities and relations from the KG that are relevant to the schema matching
questions. By representing both the questions and the knowledge graph components as dense vector
embeddings, the vector search techniques are used to identify the top-𝑘relevant entities based on
efficient computation of similarity scores. This approach ensures high-quality and scalable retrieval
of knowledge graph components, making it well-suited for applications in schema matching and
semantic search.
Offline KG embedding and vector storage. The embeddings for KG entities and relations are gener-
ated in an offline manner using pre-trained transformer-based language models such as RoBERTa [36],
as it demonstrated strong results for vector search in terms of both effectiveness and computa-
tional efficiency [46]. These embeddings, along with associated metadata (e.g., entity or relation
identifiers), are stored in a vector database such as ChromaDB [50]. The storage process includes
indexing within the vector database to facilitate efficient retrieval of relevant embeddings during
online querying.
Online question embedding. The online component starts by generating embeddings for each ques-
tion. In particular, question embeddings are generated using Sentence-BERT [55], which employs

the same pre-trained model, RoBERTa, and is specifically designed for sentence embeddings be-
cause it is good at capturing the semantic meaning of sentences. This approach works for all KGs,
including domain-specific ones, because sentence transformers, such as RoBERTa, are pre-trained
on diverse datasets, enabling them to capture general semantic patterns and relationships. By
embedding entities and relationships into a universal vector space, they ensure that semantic
similarity is preserved across domains.
Online vector search. To retrieve the top-𝑘most similar entities and relationships, we employ cosine
similarity as the distance metric. Cosine similarity is chosen after extensive experimentation against
alternative distance metrics such as Euclidean and Manhattan distances. Although Euclidean and
Manhattan distances are effective for capturing absolute differences in vector space, cosine similarity
is superior in our context as it measures the angular alignment between vectors, disregarding
their magnitude. We experimentally observe that cosine similarity consistently outperforms other
metrics in our vector-based search and retrieval. Cosine similarity measures the cosine of the angle
between two vectors, and it is defined as:
Cosine Similarity(𝐴, 𝐵) =
𝐴· 𝐵
∥𝐴∥∥𝐵∥
Here, 𝐴and 𝐵represent the embeddings of the question and a KG entity or relationship, respectively,
· denotes the dot product, and ∥∥represents the magnitude of a vector.
Additionally, we use the HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World) indexes implemented
in the vector databases for efficient vector retrieval [44]. The HNSW index constructs a graph-
based structure where nodes represent data points, and edges connect neighboring points based
on similarity, from which the search starts at a high-level entry point and navigates through
progressively denser graph layers, effectively narrowing down to the most similar neighbors. These
optimizations ensure fast and scalable ANN searches, even for large datasets, which are necessary
for scaling the system over large knowledge graphs such as Wikidata, where there are billions of
entities and triples.
3.2.2
Retrieving Relevant Subgraphs from the KG. This module aims to retrieve relevant subgraphs,
paths, or triples from large KGs that contain concise knowledge to augment the generation capability
of LLMs for knowledge-intensive tasks. To fully leverage the rich semantic knowledge of KGs for
augmenting LLMs, we retrieve relevant subgraphs from large KGs by using one of the following
techniques. Among them, LLM-based subgraph retrieval, vector-based KG triple retrieval, and
hybrid subgraph retrieval aim to directly identify the relevant subgraphs from the KG. Only
BFS-based subgraph retrieval requires relevant entities retrieved from the previous phase (§3.2.1).
LLM-based Subgraph Retrieval. In this method, we call the LLMs to directly retrieve the relevant
subgraphs or paths that are relevant to answer the given question, without the previous step of
identifying relevant entities first. The prompts in Appendix A.3, including few-shot examples with
expected responses, are given to guide LLMs towards directly retrieving the relevant subgraphs
from large-scale KGs. Similar to previous cases, there is a placeholder {question} that will be replaced
with the real questions from our selected dataset in the prompt.
Due to the fact that LLMs hallucinate and may also not know the KGs well, especially domain-
specific KGs, the quality of the subgraphs retrieved by LLMs can often be poor. Similar to the
LLM-based entity retrieval, the quality of the subgraphs retrieved by LLMs largely depends on the
extent to which LLMs know the KGs. Since the commonsense and fact-based KGs or their open
textual data forms might have already been utilized to pre-train LLMs, LLM-based subgraph retrieval
works well for commonsense and fact-based KGs, while it might not work for domain-specific KGs.
Vector-based KG Triple Retrieval. In this method, we focus on generating, storing, and retrieving
embeddings for triples in large-scale knowledge graphs to facilitate vector similarity-based KG

triple retrieval. First, the embeddings for KG entities and relationships are created by processing
their labels and descriptions, fetched through the Wikidata API. These embeddings are generated
using a pre-trained language model, e.g., RoBERTa. The embeddings are then normalized to unit
vectors to enhance the efficiency of cosine similarity computations. The embeddings, along with
metadata such as entity and relationship identifiers, are stored in a vector database. KG triple
retrieval is performed by computing cosine similarity between the question embedding and the KG
triple embeddings, identifying the top-𝑘most relevant triples.
BFS-based Subgraph Retrieval. After retrieving the relevant entities from large-scale KGs by
leveraging either LLM-based entity retrieval or vector similarity-based entity retrieval, we explore
the relevant subgraphs induced by these entities with the Breadth-First Search (BFS) algorithm.
Given a set of retrieved entities 𝐸= {𝑒1,𝑒2, . . . ,𝑒𝑛} from KG, the retrieval strategy depends on 𝑛.
When 𝑛= 1, local BFS expands the neighborhood of the single seed entity up to three hops. When
𝑛≥2, we form entity pairs 𝑒𝑝= {𝑒𝑠,𝑒𝑒} ∈EP by combining the entities in 𝐸, where 𝑒𝑠is the start
entity and 𝑒𝑒is the end entity, and retrieve the subgraphs of up to three hops connecting 𝑒𝑠and 𝑒𝑒(if
any) via BFS. The three-hop search is a trade-off solution between the number of retrieved subgraphs
for subgraph refinement and the time complexity and the quality of the retrieved subgraphs based
on our empirical results. The BFS-based subgraph retrieval supports two BFS pipelines:(1) local
BFS in memory, and (2) remote BFS via SPARQL queries.
The remote pipeline forms entity pairs in the same way as the local one. For each pair, it sends
a SPARQL query to Wikidata that searches for paths of up to three hops between 𝑒𝑠and 𝑒𝑒. The
queries are generated from predefined templates (see Appendix A.6): the start and end entity
identifiers are substituted into the template, intermediate entities and relations are left as variables,
and UNION clauses cover both edge directions. The retrieved subgraphs are then refined with the
same ranking scheme as in local BFS.
3.2.3
Ranking-based Retrieval Refinement. Our methodology incorporates several ranking schemes
to prioritize and rank the already retrieved paths from a knowledge graph. These schemes are
designed to select the most relevant paths, ensuring that only the best candidates are integrated.
This facilitates effective integration with LLMs by evaluating the retrieved paths based on the
ranking of relevance and structural significance.
One ranking scheme is frequency-based ranking, which prioritizes those paths that also contain
several of the top-𝑘retrieved relations (§3.2.1: Vector-similarity-based Entity Retrieval). Recall that
the top-𝑘retrieved relations are identified based on the similarity scores calculated between the
question embedding and the relation embedding. To further enhance the relevance of retrieved
paths, we employ a second ranking strategy that normalizes frequency-based scoring with respect
to path length. This normalization ensures that longer paths, which inherently consist of more
relations, do not disproportionately dominate the rankings. This strategy ensures that paths are
ranked based on both the presence of relevant relations and structural conciseness. Additionally, for
§3.2.2: Vector-based KG Triple Retrieval, we directly use the cosine similarity between the question
embedding and the triple embeddings to rank the retrieved triples.
Based on our ranking scheme, we select the top-1, top-2, or all retrieved paths/triples to augment
the large language model. Our experimental results show that using the top-2 or top-1 retrieved
paths/triples is generally more effective and efficient in terms of LLMs’ retrieval-augmented gener-
ation compared to using all retrieved paths/triples.
3.2.4
Enhancing Prompts with Retrieved Subgraphs. After retrieving the relevant subgraphs from
external KGs, we leverage them as the semantic knowledge context to enhance the prompts of LLMs
for augmenting the generation of the final responses for schema matching tasks. To make LLMs
fully understand the retrieved subgraph, there is an option to convert the retrieved subgraph to

full text by introducing the verbalizer [78]. Namely, the LLMs can serve as the verbalizer, in which
case the retrieved subgraphs will be converted to text by calling the LLMs again. However, this
option is not necessary and not the optimal solution either, since the precondition of calling LLMs
to convert the subgraph to text is that the LLMs can fully understand it. Besides, some structured
knowledge of the retrieved subgraphs will be lost during the conversion from triples to text.
In our case, each retrieved subgraph is a semi-structured text with some special characters,
such as arrows and operators, to represent multi-hop relations and separate multiple paths, which
can still be understood and fully leveraged by LLMs. We feed the retrieved subgraphs within
the prompts to LLMs directly and highlight the roles of the provided KG context with few-shot
examples. The prompts, including the few-shot examples and the retrieved subgraphs from KGs to
guide and augment LLMs in making the correct decisions, are demonstrated in the Appendix A.4.
As shown in the prompt, we provide examples with explanations to guide LLMs to fully leverage
the provided KG context when making decisions and generating final answers for schema-matching
questions in our given examples. There are two placeholders, {question} and {paths}, that will be
replaced with the real questions and the retrieved subgraphs from the selected dataset. Other
placeholders [d1] and [d2] are descriptions for attribute1 and attribute2, respectively. It is worth
mentioning that the KG context given in example 1 within the prompts provides extra knowledge
about the attributes. Namely, the date of death is the opposite of the date of birth, which
indicates that date of death and date of birth are not semantically equivalent even if they
are syntactically similar. This precise knowledge provides the exact relationships between two
matching attribute pairs, which is helpful for LLMs to generate the correct answers for the given
schema matching questions.
3.2.5
Poorly-relevant Subgraphs Exclusion. One challenge in augmenting LLMs with retrieved
subgraphs from KG lies in the inclusion of irrelevant subgraphs that can mislead the model and
result in erroneous outputs. Although the ranking-based retrieval refinement designed in §3.2.3
can filter out the irrelevant subgraphs based on the vector similarity score, some poorly relevant
subgraphs may still exist in the refined results. The previous study [67] reveals that irrelevant
context significantly hampers RAG performance in knowledge-intensive tasks, highlighting the
critical importance of retrieving relevant and useful context for optimal results.
Inspired by this, we introduce a rule-based instruction tuning strategy to further exclude poorly
relevant subgraphs from the previously retrieved and ranked subgraphs and mitigate the LLM’s
hallucinations. As shown in the prompt, we design an instruction to prompt (detailed in Appen-
dix A.5) LLMs with examples in the manner of few-shot learning, which aims to guide LLMs to
analyze the given schema matching question step by step and alleviate hallucinations by excluding
the poorly relevant subgraph when making the decision.
In particular, we instruct the LLM to assign a relevance score between 0 and 10 to each given
schema matching question and the provided retrieved subgraph from the external KG, based on
few-shot examples with the assigned relevance score and explanation for scoring. We prompt
and guide LLMs to only consider the provided and retrieved knowledge graph context if the
relevance score is greater than or equal to 7, thereby filtering out distracting, poorly relevant
subgraphs and ensuring that only high-value triples augment the model for schema matching.
It is worth noting that the rule-based instruction tuning for poorly relevant subgraph exclusion
in § 3.2.5 is by default disabled in KG-RAG4SM in the main evaluation, unless otherwise stated,
because it was intentionally designed for KG-RAG4SM with a domain-specific KG. In §4.4.2, we
provide experimental results on how instruction-tuning performs for improving the truthfulness of
KG-RAG4SM even in the presence of retrieved poorly relevant subgraphs.

Experimental Results
We empirically evaluate the performance of the proposed KG-RAG4SM method by comparing it
against the state-of-the-art schema matching approaches from two domains: pre-trained language
models (PLMs) and large language models (LLMs). We conduct experiments using three widely used
schema matching benchmark datasets and a self-created dataset from the real-world healthcare
database. To evaluate the cross-domain transferability of KG-RAG4SM, we additionally include
two non-healthcare datasets, Bank and IMSA. Our code and datasets are available at Github1.
We aim to investigate the following research questions.
• Exp-1: Effectiveness. How does our method compare with the state-of-the-art (SOTA) models
for schema matching in terms of effectiveness?
• Exp-2: Efficiency. How does our method compare with the SOTA models for schema matching
in terms of efficiency?
• Abl-1: Subgraph Retrieval. Which subgraph retrieval techniques work better for KG-RAG4SM
in terms of both effectiveness and efficiency to generate high-quality results for schema matching?
• Abl-2: Backbone LLMs. Which LLMs perform better in KG-RAG4SM, e.g., do fewer-parameter
LLMs work better than large-size LLMs for KG-RAG4SM?
• Ext-1: Scalability across KG Domains. Which KGs work better in KG-RAG4SM, e.g., do
domain-specific KGs work better than commonsense KGs for KG-RAG4SM?
• Ext-2: Truthfulness of KG-RAG4SM. Does the rule-based relevance scoring and instruction-
tuning work for improving the truthfulness of KG-RAG4SM?
• Ext-3: Ablation Study on Subgraph Refinement in KG-RAG4SM and Comparison with
SOTA GraphRAG. We investigate the following two research questions: (Ext-3a) What is the
isolated contribution of the similarity ranking-based subgraph refinement mechanism to the
performance of KG-RAG4SM? (Ext-3b) How does the complete KG-RAG4SM framework perform
relative to the state-of-the-art GraphRAG baseline?
• Case: Hallucinations. How does KG-RAG4SM mitigate the hallucinations of LLMs for complex
schema matching in a real-world scenario?

4.4.1
Ext-1: Scalability across KG Domains. Which KGs work better in KG-RAG4SM, e.g., do
domain-specific KGs work better than commonsense KGs for KG-RAG4SM?
Evaluation Setting. To demonstrate the scalability across KG domains of KG-RAG4SM, we extend
the domain of KGs from commonsense KGs (Wikidata) to domain-specific KGs (Medical KGs) and
evaluate the effectiveness of medical KGs in KG-RAG4SM. In view of our schema matching dataset
originating from the healthcare databases, we retrieve and rank the relevant entities from the UMLS
KG based on vector similarity and then expand each entity with retrieved relations from the UMLS
KG by using the UMLS API20 to form the triples. Specifically, we leverage UMLS-BERT [45] to
encode the given schema matching question and then query the relevant entities from UMLS, and
next retrieve the relation for each entity by using the caching mechanism to accelerate relation
retrieval from UMLS API endpoints. For each entity, we expand it with queried relations as triples
and then rank the triples based on the similarity score between the question and the entity. We
present results of KG-RAG4SM with GPT-4o-mini and Jellyfish-8B as backbone LLMs and the
retrieved & ranked triples from UMLS KG in comparison to the triples from Wikidata by using
vector-based KG triple retrieval on the Synthea dataset in Table 11.
Results Analysis. We find that KG-RAG4SM with UMLS KG achieves comparable yet worse re-
sults in comparison with the results of KG-RAG4SM having triples from Wikidata KG across
the metrics on the Synthea dataset, both with GPT-4o-mini and Jellyfish-8B as backbone LLMs.
The reasons are twofold: (1) Relevance and Quality of KG: Although all of our schema matching
datasets originate from healthcare databases, the questions of schema matching pairs, includ-
ing table name (admission, patient, visit, insurance, service, etc.) and attribute name
(admission_location, patient_weight, visit_date, insurance_id, service_provider,
etc.) from source and target data models, are more relevant to the entities and taxonomic
relations (e.g., patient, instance of, human, etc.) from commonsense KGs than medical entities
and relations (e.g., diabetes status: Prid: Pt: ˆ Patient: Nom, has_supersystem, patient,
etc.) from medical KGs. Most of the triples that exist in UMLS KG are one-hop relations, as the UMLS
maintains the entities and their associated one-hop relations, while the multiple-hop relations are

from the UMLS KG in our schema matching case. For instance, the KG triples from the UMLS KG

rectly infer that the reason for stopping medication is associated with drug exposure. However,
the retrieved connections explicitly mention that stopping medication (120234) is a synonym of
Medication discontinued (274512008), which is associated with Drug therapy (416608005),
but Drug therapy (416608005) is a subclass of Drug or medicament (410942007) rather than
a semantically equivalent relation. Since LLMs are prone to generating false positive answers based
on the found connections from the provided KG context, this further induces decreased performance
of KG-RAG4SM in this scenario. Notice that we have not employed rule-based relevance scoring and
instruction tuning (§3.2.5) in this experiment, and we demonstrate this module’s impact separately
later in §4.4.2.
4.4.2
Ext-2: Truthfulness of KG-RAG4SM. Does the rule-based relevance scoring and instruction-
tuning work for improving the truthfulness of KG-RAG4SM?
Evaluation Setting. To mitigate the truthfulness issue of KG-RAG4SM with poorly relevant and
lower-quality KG triples from the UMLS KG, we further introduce the rule-based relevance scoring
with instruction tuning (§3.2.5). Before feeding the retrieved triples from the KG to LLMs, we
call LLMs to assign a relevance score between the provided KG context and the given schema
matching questions with few-shot examples and rule-based instructions. According to the rule-
based instruction, LLMs can generate the answer based on their own knowledge when the relevance
score between the provided KG context and the given schema-matching questions is lower than 7,
or the KG context is unavailable. We evaluate the effectiveness of rule-based relevance scoring with
instruction tuning in KG-RAG4SM using Jellyfish-8B as the backbone LLM on the Synthea dataset.
Results Analysis. Table 12 shows the results of KG-RAG4SM with rule-based relevance scoring and
instruction-tuning in comparison to KG-RAG4SM without instruction-tuning, with the settings of
leveraging Jellyfish-8B as the backbone LLM and UMLS KGs as domain-specific KGs on the Synthea
dataset. We observe that KG-RAG4SM with rule-based relevance scoring and instruction tuning
contributes significant improvements across metrics on the Synthea dataset with Jellyfish-8B as
the backbone LLM in comparison to KG-RAG4SM without instruction tuning. In particular, the
KG-RAG4SM with rule-based relevance scoring and instruction tuning and top-1 ranked triples

with the designed rule-based relevance scoring and instruction-tuning in mitigating false-positive-
prone answers and hallucinations of RAG systems with poorly relevant retrieved KG context.

Related Work
Schema Matching and Data Integration. Schema matching is a process of establishing semantic
associations between different schemata, in which the semantic correspondences between the source
and target schemas are usually identified [54]. In contrast to schema matching, schema mapping is
a process of generating the assertions and mappings from the identified semantic correspondence,
by which the source schema can be mapped onto the target schema to provide a common interface
for accessing and querying heterogeneous data [17]. In general, the correspondences between
the source and target schemas can be identified manually by domain experts, which requires a
lot of human effort and experience [41]. In the case of manual mapping, mismatches and errors
are also inevitable, as the intensive and repeated work might distract humans. To improve the
matching efficiency and to minimize mismatches, an auto-match system is designed to support
automatic schema matching based on machine learning [2]. In this method, probability knowledge
is obtained from domain experts based on Bayesian learning, which provides an attribute dictionary
to find optimal matching pairs. Considering the strengths of similarity-based matching and machine
learning-based matching, a hybrid schema matching model is proposed by combining lexical and
semantic similarity with machine learning [6].
To free human hands from the tedious schema matching task, a new semi-automatic schema
matching approach is proposed [13]. In this approach, the domain ontologies and sample instances
are reused to identify and determine the semantic correspondences between schemata. Similarly,
background ontologies are utilized to provide the semantic reference for schema matching based on
ontology alignment [48]. To minimize mismatches and errors, the medical knowledge bases from the
medical encyclopedia are utilized to learn better representations of medical concepts and properties.
Thereby, a medical schema matching method is proposed based on the relation alignment between

the description embedding of an extracted medical entity and learned heterogeneous knowledge
graph embedding [18]. However, these methods follow supervised learning, in which large labeled
data are required for model training.
Recently, several schema matching approaches based on LLMs have been proposed to address
the above limitations, such as fine-tuned LLMs Magneto [37], retrieval-enhanced LLMs [60], and
reasoning-guided LLMs [79]. To improve the performance of LLMs for data preprocessing, an
instruction-tuning LLM [83] is proposed, which enables optional knowledge injection during
inference through instruction tuning with reasoning based on domain knowledge. Since the inherent
knowledge might be useful to enhance LLMs for schema matching, RAG-based approaches are
proposed to augment LLMs by retrieving the relevant knowledge from text chunks constructed
based on the textual descriptions of tables and attributes, e.g., a retrieval-enhanced large language
model for schema matching [60] that retrieves the top-𝑘documents from the textual documents
based on semantic relevance. Inspired by RAG on LLMs, a compositional language model for
schema matching [59] enhances and refines the results based on semantic retrieval from multi-
vector documents and confidence scoring by multiple calls of LLMs. Similarly, an LLM-based
knowledge-compliant schema and entity-matching framework [79] is proposed that guides LLM
reasoning based on pseudo-code-based task decomposition and knowledge retrieval from domain
knowledge sets by calling LLMs to query the relevant keywords.
The prevailing methods of schema matching could be classified into five categories: similarity-
based matching [2, 6, 34], graph-based matching [13], pre-training language model-based match-
ing [18, 68, 88], LLMs-based matching [83], and RAG-based matching [59, 60, 79]. Although there
are some recent works, such as ReMatch [60] and Matchmaker [59], they leverage RAG over LLMs
to address schema matching, but only the knowledge from the textual documents of the descriptions
for the table and attribute is retrieved for augmentation. KcMF [79] retrieves knowledge from the
self-built knowledge set, but the knowledge from the large commonsense and fact-based KGs in the
real world has not been adapted and fully leveraged to augment the LLMs for schema matching. To
some extent, the aforementioned methods achieve (semi-) automatic schema matching, but they
are still incapable of resolving the semantic ambiguities and conflicts in some complex mapping
cases because of the missing evidence and factual knowledge.
Besides schema matching and mapping, the field of data preparation/integration deals with several
other critical challenges, e.g., data acquisition, extraction, cleaning, augmentation, transformation,
and annotation; entity matching; ontology alignment; and data fusion, among others [3, 8, 12].
Recent advances in deep learning and foundation models have also achieved promising results
on many such tasks. Notable works include deep learning embedding-based data integration over
relational databases [7, 27], and ontology matching [26], pre-trained language models (PLMs)
for entity matching [33, 66], and column type annotation [64], large language models (LLMs)-
powered entity resolution [14, 30, 52] and other data wrangling tasks [47, 89], etc. The focus of
our work is schema matching, which is different from the aforementioned problems. We do not
consider cell values (nor rows/entities) in the table; instead, we directly aim at finding semantic
correspondence among attribute names (i.e., column names) in tables from the source and target
schema. Additionally, none of these works employ external and large-scale knowledge graph-based
retrieval augmentation paradigms to enhance the LLM’s performance in data integration tasks.
Knowledge-augmented LLMs and RAG. There are several ways in which external knowledge
from KGs can be integrated with LLMs [42]. To leverage the strengths and capabilities of hierarchical
and structured knowledge in complex reasoning, the KGs and text are aligned and interact to
jointly train the language models [82, 87] for knowledge-intensive tasks. In the phase of joint
learning, the text and KG pair are aligned via entity linking and then fed into the cross-model
encoder to bidirectionally fuse text and KG, thereby performing joint reasoning on text and KG. In

30
Chuangtao Ma, Sriom Chakrabarti, Arijit Khan, and Bálint Molnár
addition to joint learning, knowledge-based instruction tuning integrates graph representations
into language generation by aligning LLM inference with the graph’s topological structure, enabling
more effective use of graph knowledge for complex reasoning [5]. However, the resulting model
must be re-fine-tuned whenever the KG is updated or when adapting to a new domain, because the
method embeds only static KG information into the model parameters.
Graph reasoning approaches such as RoG [38] and ToG [65] guide LLM inference using KG-
derived reasoning paths generated through BFS or beam search, rather than relying solely on static
knowledge fusion. KG-CoT [90] extends this idea by introducing chain-of-thought reasoning over
graphs, producing explicit reasoning paths and confidence scores based on transition matrices.
Agent-based methods have also been explored, exemplified by KG-Agent [24], which treats the
LLM as an agent that autonomously selects KG-related tools and updates memory for iterative
reasoning. Despite their strengths, these graph reasoning methods repeatedly search paths at each
step of beam search or CoT, resulting in substantial computational overhead on large-scale KGs.
Different from the aforementioned techniques, RAG aims to retrieve relevant knowledge from
specific data sources, such as text chunks, tabular data, graph data, etc., to augment the capability and
reliability of LLMs for knowledge-intensive NLP tasks [29]. However, the mainstream RAG methods
retrieve the relevant knowledge from the textual chunks, which ignore the structured information
and inter-relations of these textual chunks. To mitigate this limitation, Graph RAG [22, 80] and
KG-RAG [57] are proposed. Instead of retrieving knowledge from textual chunks, Graph RAG
directly retrieves the relevant paths from the graph data. GraphRAG [22] retrieves subgraphs from
the textual graph that is indexed based on PLMs and then integrates the retrieved and pruned
subgraphs with the query by aggregating and aligning the graph embeddings with text vectors
based on GNNs. Similarly, KG-based RAG aims to augment LLMs with KGs by leveraging the
reasoning capability of knowledge from various knowledge sources for knowledge-intensive tasks.
KG-RAG approaches retrieve the subgraph from the self-constructed KG based on unstructured text
or textual chunks [57] or from the inherent KGs derived from the KGQA dataset [32] to augment
the reasoning capability of LLMs for complex QA.
Recent research works have demonstrated the remarkable performance of KG-RAG on several
knowledge-intensive tasks such as text classification [71], sentiment analysis [31], entity linking [61],
KGQA [32, 42, 57], recommendation [9], etc. These mainstream Graph RAG and KG-RAG approaches
usually retrieve knowledge from local and small-sized graphs built from text chunks or the inherent
KGs derived from the KGQA dataset. Our method differs from the existing KG-RAG on the KGQA;
it leverages vector-based retrieval and graph traversal-based retrieval to retrieve the relevant
subgraphs from large external KGs instead of constructed KGs based on textual chunks or the
inherent KGs derived from the KGQA dataset. It mainly focuses on augmenting LLMs with KGs
for complex schema matching tasks rather than the commonly investigated natural language
processing and understanding tasks.

Conclusion
In this paper, we presented a novel knowledge graph-based retrieval-augmented generation for
schema-matching solution, KG-RAG4SM, to address the limitations that traditional similarity-based
and LLM-based schema-matching methods are incapable of resolving semantic ambiguities in
complex mapping cases. Our framework differs from the emerging graph RAG approaches that
retrieve knowledge from local and small-sized graphs built from text chunks. We proposed several
subgraph retrieval methods from large KGs, followed by ranking-based subgraph refinement.
Our empirical results confirmed that the vector-based KG triple retrieval and vector-based entity
retrieval + BFS graph traversal methods are the optimal subgraph retrieval strategies. The results of
the extensive experimental evaluation demonstrated that our KG-RAG4SM outperforms PLMs and

Knowledge Graph-based Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Schema Matching
31
LLM-based SOTA methods in terms of effectiveness and efficiency. We investigated the scalability
across KG domains from commonsense KG to domain-specific KG and the truthfulness of KG-
RAG4SM with poorly relevant retrieved KG context. Our case studies from real-world scenarios
showed that the hallucination of LLMs for schema matching is well mitigated by our method.
In this study, we augmented LLMs with relevant subgraphs retrieved from a large external KG via
few-shot learning. However, resolving knowledge conflicts between the internal knowledge of LLMs
and the retrieved KG, examining retrieval performance across KGs varying in scale and connectivity,
and developing more advanced graph traversal strategies to further optimize subgraph retrieval on
large KGs remain interesting directions for future work. Additionally, we shall investigate how our
KG-RAG paradigm can improve other LLM-based data integration and data fusion tasks.

Detailed Analysis of Baselines with a 0% or 100% Score
Detailed Analysis of Jellyfish with a 0% Score We present detailed results and a discussion of
the Jellyfish-8B baseline, as we observed that Jellyfish-8B achieved a 0% score on the EMED dataset.
Figure 6a shows the confusion matrix of Jellyfish-8B on the EMED baseline.
The reasons behind this are twofold: (1) the dataset is unseen, and (2) it is highly imbalanced.
Jellyfish-8b is a fine-tuned model based on Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct with several data preprocess-
ing datasets, including schema matching datasets, i.e., MIMIC and Synthea. The observed 0% score
arises from the fact that the fine-tuned Jellyfish-8B model fails to identify any true positive matches
(TP=0) on the EMED dataset, which is newly created and unseen for this model. In addition, EMED
is highly imbalanced and negative-dominant, with very few positive matching pairs in the ground
truth. This further highlights the limited robustness of the fine-tuned model when applied to an
unseen and imbalanced dataset.

C.4
LLM-based Subgraph Retrieval
In LLM-based KG retrieval, we treat the LLM as a natural language interface for the commonsense
KG. This is mainly based on the fact that commonsense KGs, such as Wikidata, are well known to
LLMs, as the commonsense KGs or their open textual data forms might have already been utilized
to pre-train LLMs. Accordingly, we designed an LLM-based entity retrieval + BFS and an LLM-based
subgraph retrieval.
In the LLM-based entity retrieval + BFS, the interaction pipeline is summarized as: (1) En-
tity/Relation ID Generation: The LLM is prompted to output the Wikidata entity identifier (QID)
and relation identifier (PID) that are relevant to the given schema matching question in context
learning; (2) ID fetcher and Verification: We verify and fetch the name of the entity and the relation
with the identifier from Wikidata via the Wikidata REST API; (3) Subgraph Search: We execute the
subgraph search via BFS with the verified entity and relation.
In LLM-based subgraph retrieval, the interaction pipeline is summarized as: (1) Path Generation:
The LLM is prompted to output the paths from Wikidata that are relevant to the given schema-
matching question; (2) ID fetcher and Verification: We verify the paths by fetching the name of the
entity and relation with identifiers from Wikidata via the Wikidata REST API.
Our empirical evaluation (Abl-1: subgraph retrieval) results reveal that the LLM-based sub-
graph retrieval method works on commonsense KGs because LLMs know the concepts and relations
of Wikidata, but sometimes it returns the entity and relation identifiers that actually do not exist in
Wikidata. However, LLM-based subgraph retrieval works for commonsense KGs, whereas it may
not work as effectively for domain-specific KGs, as LLMs are not familiar with them.

Complexity Analysis and Empirical Verification
We provide a time complexity analysis of vector retrieval and BFS graph traversal, along with
empirical verification of the computational feasibility of KG-RAG4SM on large-scale KGs.
Time Complexity Analysis. Let 𝐺= (𝑉, 𝐸) denote a KG, where 𝑉is a set of entities with 𝑛= |𝑉|
and 𝐸is a set of edges with 𝑚= |𝐸|. The average branching factor is the average out-degree of
a node in the KG: 𝑏= 𝑚/𝑛. For a given query 𝑞, the complexity is derived as follows: (1) Vector
Retrieval: For cosine similarity search over KG embeddings in a dimension 𝑑vector space, the time
complexity scales linearly with the KG size 𝑛: 𝑂(𝑛· 𝑑). In practice, Approximate Nearest Neighbor
(ANN) indexes such as HNSW can reduce query cost substantially, often exhibiting sublinear
empirical search behavior: 𝑂(log𝑛· 𝑑), although worst-case complexity depends on the index and
data distribution. (2) BFS Traversal: Unlike standard BFS that may visit all nodes 𝑂(|𝑉| + |𝐸|) for
each entity, our designed BFS search is constrained by the number of entity pairs formed based on
the number of seed entities 𝑉𝑆in the initial vector-based entity retrieval and the maximum BFS
search depth 𝐷𝑚𝑎𝑥. The BFS search executes for each entity pair, so the total time complexity for the
