Yet whether these additions exceed the native capability of the MLLM remains unexplored. We introduce SimplEvo to explore how far an MLLM can improve under direct control when it retains its execution experience in self-written text memories. It requires no weight updates, post-trained policies, or privileged information. The MLLM writes a coordinate-free task memory once from a single demonstration. Under a strict train–test protocol, it then creates a bounded working memory on training episodes only and refines it after each episode with lessons on procedure, completion, and recovery. Both memories stay fixed at test time. On EBench, SimplEvo lifts the success rate (SR) of GPT-6-Astra under direct control from 46.7% to 81.7%, and tops the RoboDojo leaderboard with 69.63% SR and a score of 75.32. Compared with memories that also store past visual frames, SimplEvo reaches a similar SR with text memory alone while using fewer tokens. With its memories fixed, SimplEvo also generalizes to unseen layouts and randomized scene configurations. Moreover, the working memory transfers across models: written by GPT-6-Astra, it can be reused as is by weaker MLLMs and improve their performance without any adaptation of their own. (d) SR on the 12 RoboDojo Generalization tasks with standard and randomized configurations. 1 Introduction Generalist robotic manipulation is a central problem of embodied intelligence. Vision-language-action models (VLAs) [ Kim et al., 2024 ; Black et al., 2025b ; Black et al., 2025a ] and world action models (WAMs) [ Ye et al., 2026 ; Yuan et al., 2026b ; Wang et al., 2026e ] align their manipulation skills through post-training on benchmark-specific trajectories [ Liu et al., 2023 ; Mu et al., 2025 ; Chen et al., 2025 ] . In contrast, recent studies show that frontier multimodal large language models (MLLMs) directly operate robots to complete tasks without parameter updates. They command the end effector from camera views and language instructions [ OpenAI, 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026b ; Liu et al., 2026 ; Jia et al., 2026 ; Lou et al., 2026 ; Hu et al., 2026a ] , explore and adapt their behaviors to the observed results [ Guo et al., 2026a ; Cheng et al., 2026b ] , and recover from errors within an episode [ Fu et al., 2026 ; Yao et al., 2026 ] . These findings have motivated a growing range of embodied agents built on frontier MLLMs. These agents fall into two lines of work. The first is code-as-policies , which began with language models writing robot code [ Liang et al., 2023 ] and composes perception modules and motion primitives into executable programs. In systems such as Code as Policies [ Liang et al., 2023 ] , COAP [ Hu et al., 2026b ] , and RAX on RoboDojo [ Huang et al., 2026 ] , the synthesized programs run at test time without the MLLM in the loop, so they cover only anticipated scene variations and failure modes. Recent works such as CaP-X [ Fu et al., 2026 ] and PyRUA-Lean [ Si et al., 2026 ] therefore execute code online with the MLLM in the loop. Even so, code-as-policies systems typically rely on privileged information beyond RGB views and proprioception, such as metric depth, camera parameters, and object sizes, or on estimates from calibrated depth sensors and vision foundation models [ Kirillov et al., 2023 ; Ravi et al., 2025 ; Yang et al., 2024a ; Yang et al., 2024b ] , to which the resulting programs are sensitive. Frontier MLLMs, by contrast, already perceive and reason about space from RGB views and proprioception, which raises the question of whether routing perception through intermediate models is necessary at all. The second is harness engineering , which extends the harness around the MLLM with post-trained policies or external improvement loops. Some systems build harnesses with policy that expose post-trained policies as callable tools [ Zhang et al., 2026c ; Wang et al., 2026c ] , so the MLLM plans while the policy executes low-level actions. Each delegated call returns only its final observation and status, so the actions of the policy never enter the context of the MLLM, and the harness can constrain the capability of the underlying MLLM. Other systems adapt self-evolving language agents into embodied recursive self-improvement (RSI) frameworks that revise context, tools, or control logic through revision loops around the agent [ Tian et al., 2026 ; MMLab & PhysicalRSI contributors, 2026 ; Lu et al., 2026 ; Cheng et al., 2026a ] . These frameworks iterate on external harnesses and rarely measure the base model under direct control first. Some self-evolving designs adapt on the test scenes themselves [ Wang et al., 2026d ] . Because the evaluator then returns feedback on the scenes used for evaluation, test-scene information can leak into the evolved system, and its gains no longer show whether the acquired experience generalizes to scenes the agent has never attempted. It thus remains unclear how far a frontier MLLM can go under direct control on its own, and hence how much of this added machinery is necessary. Both lines of work focus on either exploiting the coding ability of the MLLM or wrapping it with post-trained policies and improvement loops, thereby overlooking the rising capability of the MLLM itself to act directly as a general-purpose robot controller. We refer to this setting as direct control , where the MLLM commands the end effector directly using RGB views and proprioception, without external perception modules or a post-trained policy. Direct control returns the result of every executed command to the MLLM, so the closed-loop feedback and within-episode recovery are explicit. After a missed grasp or a failed insertion, the MLLM compares the commanded end-effector coordinates with the resulting camera views, diagnoses the error, and adjusts its next command [ Yao et al., 2026 ] . These adjustments and the experience of each trial, however, vanish when the episode ends, so each episode starts anew. Can a frontier MLLM unlock strong manipulation under direct control simply by inheriting and evolving the in-episode adjustments and trial experience across episodes, relying only on its native multimodal capabilities? To show that recording and revising its own memory is enough for a frontier MLLM to excel under direct control, we present SimplEvo, illustrated in Figure 2 . SimplEvo keeps the native direct-control loop unchanged and introduces only two compact text memories that the MLLM writes itself during adaptation on the training set. From one training demonstration, the MLLM formulates a coordinate-free task memory once, and after each training episode, it reflects on its own trajectory in one turn to rewrite a bounded working memory of transferable lessons. A strict train–test protocol confines adaptation and its outcome feedback to training scenes, none of which appears in the test set. Both memories are then fixed, and every test episode reads them without reflection turns or memory updates. Throughout adaptation and testing, the agent observes only RGB views and proprioception and requires no post-trained policies, weight updates, or privileged information. With its working memory fixed after adaptation, SimplEvo outperforms post-trained policies and other MLLM agents on the test episodes of EBench [ Gao et al., 2026b ] and the test layouts of RoboDojo [ Chen et al., 2026a ] . On EBench, SimplEvo raises GPT-6-Astra from 46.7% under direct control to a success rate (SR) of 81.7% across its 510 test scenes while reducing tokens per success, against up to 56.0% SR for harnesses with policy and 61.4% SR for the strongest post-trained policy. On RoboDojo, after adapting on 15 training layouts per task, SimplEvo leads the public leaderboard across 50 test layouts per task with 69.63% SR and a score of 75.32. This paper makes three contributions: • We propose SimplEvo, a two-stage memory framework for direct control, in which the MLLM derives a task memory without coordinates from a single training demonstration and rewrites a bounded working memory through its own reflection on every training episode, without post-trained policies or weight updates. • Under a strict evaluation protocol with train–test separation, native step limits, and no privileged information, SimplEvo ranks first on both the EBench and the RoboDojo leaderboards, ahead of post-trained VLAs, WAMs, and other MLLM agents, while using fewer tokens per success than direct control. • We show that SimplEvo performs well with simple text-based memories and, with these memories fixed, generalizes to unseen layouts and randomized scene configurations. Moreover, the working memory created by a frontier MLLM can be reused by weaker MLLMs to improve their performance. 2 Related Work Harnesses and self-improvement for embodied agents. Beyond the code-as-policies systems and harnesses with policy discussed in Section 1 , harnesses also coordinate post-trained policies through memory [ Xu et al., 2026 ] , and direct control can command end-effector targets [ Zhang et al., 2026b ; Jia et al., 2026 ; Lou et al., 2026 ] or semantic action units [ Chen et al., 2026b ] . Self-improving embodied systems differ in what they change. They revise tools, harnesses, or code policies during development and score the fixed result [ Tian et al., 2026 ; MMLab & PhysicalRSI contributors, 2026 ; Hu et al., 2026b ] , co-evolve guidance with harness modules [ Wang et al., 2026b ; Ding et al., 2026 ; Li et al., 2026b ; Tang et al., 2026 ; Wang et al., 2026a ] , or distill trajectories into policy weights [ Bousmalis et al., 2024 ; Physical Intelligence et al., 2025 ; Xiao et al., 2026 ] , and Zhou et al. [2026] classify such systems by which component a retained change updates and how broadly its gain holds. Controlled audits find that harness evolution does not consistently outperform test-time scaling at equal budget, that frequent updates can fail to improve held-out performance [ Wang et al., 2026d ; Zheng et al., 2026 ] , and that selection on small, noisy evaluations admits spurious gains [ Shawn, 2026 ; Gideoni et al., 2026 ] . SimplEvo keeps tools, harnesses, code, and model weights fixed. It acts under direct control and updates only the text working memory that the MLLM reads across episodes. Frontier MLLMs as robot policies. Frontier MLLMs now control robots without being trained on robot data. For example, GPT-6-Astra [ OpenAI, 2026 ] commands end effectors directly on RoboDojo [ Chen et al., 2026a ; Zhang et al., 2026b ] and learns from demonstration blocks in context [ Liu et al., 2026 ] , while other MLLM agents write robot commands from saved procedures [ Jia et al., 2026 ] or refine a revisable model of the robot body [ Lou et al., 2026 ] . Yao et al. [2026] profile such an agent on EBench [ Gao et al., 2026b ] across direct control and harnesses with policy, and they find that its failures concentrate in physical execution. This study measures how far an agent under direct control improves once its execution experience persists across episodes. Memory and experience evolution. Agent memory systems differ in how they represent and update experience across episodes. Language agents improve without weight updates by accumulating verbal reflections [ Shinn et al., 2023 ] , extracting insights from successful and failed trajectories [ Zhao et al., 2024 ] , or curating evolving playbooks [ Zhang et al., 2026a ] . In multimodal interactive environments, VISTA [ Han et al., 2026 ] preserves a lossless visual history of past observations, so carrying it across manipulation episodes lengthens the context with every executed command. Embodied agents store experience as executable skills [ Wang et al., 2024 ] , validated program repairs [ Lu et al., 2026 ] , policy repositories of prompts, tools, and control code [ Elmaaroufi et al., 2026 ] , per-task programs [ Wang et al., 2026c ] , or a knowledge package that GPT-6-Astra writes once from training demonstrations and reads unchanged in every test episode [ SenseNova Joint Research Team et al., 2026 ] . Unlike reflections, insights, and playbooks distilled from language-level actions, or knowledge written from demonstrations, the working memory of SimplEvo distills the low-level commands that the MLLM itself issued, is rewritten in full within a fixed size after each adaptation episode, and stays fixed at test time. 3 Method Figure 2: Overview of SimplEvo. During online adaptation (left), an annotator instance of GPT-6-Astra reviews one training demonstration and writes a coordinate-free task memory of the stages and the completed state, and after every training episode, GPT-6-Astra rewrites the working memory from the trajectory and outcome of that episode into lessons on procedure, completion, and recovery. Once fixed (right), both memories are read in new scenes, and other MLLMs read the working memory of GPT-6-Astra without modification. All panels show peg insertion on EBench. As shown in Figure 2 , SimplEvo equips direct control with two memories written by the MLLM, each overcoming a limitation of isolated episodes. The task memory supplies the order of stages and the final state of a task before the first attempt, and the working memory preserves the corrections that would otherwise vanish when an episode ends. 3.1 Direct Control under a Strict Information Protocol Agent under direct control. The agent pairs a fixed-weight MLLM core F F with an agent harness that specifies its observation and action space. Under direct control , the MLLM issues every robot command itself instead of calling a post-trained policy. At decision step t t of the k k -th episode on task τ \tau , the MLLM receives the observation o t = ( I t , q t ) o_{t}=(I_{t},q_{t}) , which consists of RGB images I t I_{t} from an overview camera and two wrist cameras, or from any subset of them that the MLLM requests, and the proprioceptive state q t q_{t} of the robot. Given the observations o ≤ t o_{\leq t} and the earlier actions a < t a_{<t} of the episode, the MLLM issues a t = F ⁡ ( ℓ τ , M task , M k − 1 work , o ≤ t , a < t ) , a_{t}=F\big(\ell_{\tau},\,M^{\mathrm{task}},\,M^{\mathrm{work}}_{k-1},\,o_{\leq t},\,a_{<t}\big), (1) where ℓ τ \ell_{\tau} is the task instruction with basic specifications of the robot embodiment, and M task M^{\mathrm{task}} and M k − 1 work M^{\mathrm{work}}_{k-1} are the task and working memories of Sections 3.2 and 3.3 . Each action a t a_{t} is a chunk of 1 to 6 absolute waypoints, each setting an end-effector pose and a gripper target for each arm and, on the mobile robot of EBench, a base pose. Inverse kinematics converts the end-effector poses into joint commands. An episode ends when the evaluator registers success, when the step limit of the task is reached, or earlier when the MLLM ends its turn. The observations and actions of episode k k form its trajectory ξ k = ( o 1 , a 1 , o 2 , a 2 , … ) \xi_{k}=(o_{1},a_{1},o_{2},a_{2},\dots) . By comparison, a harness with policy hands control to a post-trained policy for a bounded number of physics steps and receives only the end state, so ξ k \xi_{k} omits the actions that the policy executes. Information protocol. On both benchmarks, we restrict the agent to the information that a physical deployment provides. During execution, the agent observes the scene solely through RGB images and robot proprioception o t = ( I t , q t ) o_{t}=(I_{t},q_{t}) , matching the inputs of a post-trained VLA or WAM. Although recent evaluation guidelines for robot-use agents allow camera intrinsics [ OpenRUA Team, 2026 ] , we exclude privileged information such as depth, camera parameters, or object states used by prior systems [ Liang et al., 2023 ; Hu et al., 2026b ; Huang et al., 2026 ] . On a physical robot, such information would come from external estimation modules that are sensitive to perception errors. Restricting observations to RGB views and proprioception therefore reflects more faithfully how the agent adapts to the robot embodiment and refines its execution in the environment. Beyond o t o_{t} , the MLLM receives only the instruction ℓ τ \ell_{\tau} , the two memories, and the training demonstration D τ D_{\tau} of Section 3.2 . Finally, like post-trained policies, the agent acts within the native per-task step limits of each benchmark and receives no intermediate progress signals. 3.2 Task Memory from a Demonstration The task memory M task M^{\mathrm{task}} records the stage order and the visual completion criterion that a single demonstration conveys reliably. Before the first episode, an annotator instance of the same MLLM reviews the recorded actions and camera frames of the demonstration D τ D_{\tau} and writes M task M^{\mathrm{task}} once. M task M^{\mathrm{task}} lists the stages of the task in order, stating for each stage the operation and the arm that performs it, and ends with the visual state of the completed task. It contains no coordinates, so that it applies to layouts other than the demonstrated one. Because the final stage specifies the completed state, M task M^{\mathrm{task}} gives the MLLM an explicit stopping criterion before any execution experience exists, which we term terminal-state anchoring . The MLLM reads M task M^{\mathrm{task}} in every episode and can revisit D τ D_{\tau} during execution. Tasks without a demonstration, the eight Open tasks of RoboDojo, run without M task M^{\mathrm{task}} . Appendix A.2 provides the annotation prompt and the tools for reviewing the demonstration. 3.3 Working Memory via Trajectory Reflection The working memory M work M^{\mathrm{work}} carries execution experience across the episodes of a task through compact text rewrites. After episode k k , the same MLLM F F updates the working memory in a single reflection turn without robot actions: M k work = F ⁡ ( M k − 1 work , ξ k , r k ) , | M k work | ≤ B , M^{\mathrm{work}}_{k}=F\big(M^{\mathrm{work}}_{k-1},\,\xi_{k},\,r_{k}\big),\qquad|M^{\mathrm{work}}_{k}|\leq B, (2) where ξ k \xi_{k} is the executed trajectory and r k r_{k} is the terminal outcome, the success and score reported by the benchmark evaluator as an episode-level reward. In each update, the MLLM may replace M k − 1 work M^{\mathrm{work}}_{k-1} with a full rewrite within a size budget B B of 32 KB, so the MLLM itself decides what the bounded memory keeps. Replacing the memory in full allows the MLLM to revise or discard outdated content while keeping M k work M^{\mathrm{work}}_{k} far shorter than the cumulative history of the chain. By analyzing the executed commands and their observed consequences in ξ k \xi_{k} , particularly the causes of failures, the MLLM distills concise lessons on task procedure, completion criteria, and error recovery, accompanied by representative commands and outcomes from earlier attempts as supporting evidence. To discourage path dependence on the MLLM’s own exploration trajectories, the description of the memory-writing tool asks it to treat measured coordinates as scene-specific evidence instead of commands to replay in another scene. Appendix A.3 gives the reflection prompt and the memory tools, and Appendix B.2 presents excerpts of working memories. 3.4 Online Adaptation and Deployment During online adaptation, the agent executes the episodes of a task sequentially along an adaptation chain , starting with the working memory M 0 work M^{\mathrm{work}}_{0} empty. Under the train–test protocol , online adaptation runs exclusively on training scenes or layouts, after which the working memory M K work M^{\mathrm{work}}_{K} at the end of the chain is fixed. At deployment, M K work M^{\mathrm{work}}_{K} takes the place of M k − 1 work M^{\mathrm{work}}_{k-1} in ( 1 ) in every new scene without further updates, so no information from deployment scenes can leak into the memory. Appendix B.3 analyzes the dynamics of the adaptation chains. 4 Experiments We evaluate SimplEvo on two public manipulation benchmarks against their leaderboard entries and against GPT-6-Astra without working memory, with adaptation confined to training scenes. 4.1 Setup Benchmarks and generalization. The test episodes of EBench [ Gao et al., 2026b ] and RoboDojo [ Chen et al., 2026a ] evaluate the working memory under variations in scene conditions. EBench comprises 26 tasks of a simulated ARX Lift 2 dual-arm mobile robot in Isaac Sim [ Gao et al., 2025 ] , and its 510 test scenes perturb the manipulated objects, the background, the instruction, or several of them at once. Its seven single-layout tasks keep one object layout across training and test episodes while varying objects, backgrounds, and instructions. Its 19 multi-layout tasks also vary the layout, so a test layout may be unseen or already seen in training. Fifteen of these 19 tasks include both seen and unseen test layouts. RoboDojo comprises 42 dual-arm tabletop tasks in five capability dimensions, each tested on 50 layouts of initial objects and their poses. On its 12 Generalization tasks, half of the test layouts randomize the background, the lighting, the target objects, and the clutter. Following the official protocols, we report SR and the stage-completion score, both on a 0–100 scale. Train–test protocol. Per task, adaptation uses up to 15 EBench training episodes or 15 RoboDojo training episodes, then fixes the working memory for testing. On EBench, adaptation stops early after three consecutive successes, as Appendix B.3 details. On RoboDojo Generalization tasks, training includes both standard and randomized configurations. The task memory M task M^{\mathrm{task}} comes from one training demonstration per task. Agent and baselines. We use GPT-6-Astra [ OpenAI, 2026 ] at high reasoning effort as the MLLM core F F in all experiments except the cross-model transfer study. On EBench, we compare against the 12 post-trained VLAs and WAMs on the public leaderboard, as well as GPT-6-Astra under direct control and in harnesses with π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} [ Black et al., 2025a ] or InternW0- Δ \Delta [ Miao et al., 2026 ] following Yao et al. [2026] , all reading the task memory but no working memory. On RoboDojo, we compare against the leading post-trained policies and agents on the public leaderboard, the official zero-shot entry of GPT-6-Astra on test layouts, and task memory only on the training layouts used by SimplEvo. We also report tokens per success, the total tokens of an evaluation divided by its successes, and analyze cost in Appendix G . 4.2 Results on EBench With its working memory fixed, SimplEvo reaches 81.7% SR on the 510 test episodes of EBench and ranks first in every task group of Table 1 , using the leaderboard snapshot of 29 October 2026, ahead of PICA-WAM [ Zou et al., 2026 ] , the best post-trained policy, at 61.4% and the best harness with policy at 56.0%. The largest gains arise on high-precision, tabletop, and long-horizon tasks. On high-precision tasks, direct control without working memory succeeds in 11.3% of the episodes, and neither harness with policy exceeds 12.5%. With working memory, the same direct control reaches 82.5%, 2.3 times the 36.3% of PICA-WAM, which shows that the waypoints of the MLLM are precise enough for these tasks once the corrections of earlier attempts persist. On long-horizon tasks, direct control drops to 27.3% from 73.2% on short-horizon ones, while SimplEvo reaches 76.3%, above even the short-horizon SR of direct control. The task memory fixes the stage sequence, and the working memory adds to each stage the completion checks and recovery steps verified in earlier episodes. On bottle collection, SimplEvo reaches 45.0% SR, while none of the 12 leaderboard policies succeeds. It also reaches 90.0% on coffee-bean collection and 95.0% on shop checkout, where direct control without working memory has no successes. At test time, SimplEvo spends 2.76M tokens per success, compared with 8.12M for direct control, 3.46M for the harness with π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} , and 3.99M for the harness with InternW0- Δ \Delta , as Appendix G reports. On online chains of ten validation scenes for each of the four high-precision EBench tasks, the task memory stays fixed and only the working-memory representation varies. Each chain starts from an empty working memory, so its SR includes the first attempts of adaptation, unlike the fixed working memory of Table 1 . Under direct control, Table 4 shows that the text working memory more than triples the SR of task memory alone, from 15.0% to 47.5%. Adding a visual replay of earlier attempts, which archives their camera frames, to the text working memory leaves the SR unchanged, and the MLLM queries the replay in only 3 of the 40 episodes. Maintaining a single continuous conversation across the chain, following the visual history design of VISTA [ Han et al., 2026 ] , reaches a similar SR with a slightly higher score. The text working memory uses the fewest tokens per episode and the shortest control time, with an SR similar to that of the visual memory designs. Appendix E.1 details the protocol of this design study. Outcome feedback. The working memory retains most of its gain without terminal outcome feedback. Eight tasks test this signal: the four high-precision tasks and four multi-layout tasks, namely dishwasher loading, the fruit task, utensil placement, and dish placement. When the terminal outcome r k r_{k} is withheld during online adaptation along their 20-scene chains, the overall SR decreases from 73.8% to 65.0%. The high-precision chains keep 52.5% SR in both conditions. On the final five episodes of the four multi-layout chains, where task memory alone also runs on the same scenes, working memory without outcome feedback reaches 80.0% SR, compared with 35.0% for task memory alone and 95.0% with outcome feedback, thereby retaining three quarters of the improvement through self-assessment of ξ k \xi_{k} alone, as detailed in Appendix E.2 . On the 12 Generalization tasks of RoboDojo, SimplEvo keeps 52.00% SR in both configurations, whereas the zero-shot entry of the same MLLM drops from 32.67% to 28.33% and the two strongest post-trained policies lose 15.00 and 33.88 points. Matched comparisons that rerun task memory alone on the same test scenes isolate the contribution of the working memory. With the task memory M task M^{\mathrm{task}} held fixed, adding the fixed working memory raises the SR from 67.4% to 85.3% on the last five test episodes of each of the 19 multi-layout EBench tasks and from 40.83% to 66.11% on three test layouts of each of the 42 RoboDojo tasks, as Appendix E.5 details. The working memory pairs distilled lessons with evidence from earlier attempts, as in Figure 3 a, and the episode prompt of Appendix A presents these notes as hypotheses to verify against the current views. Insight 4. Text working memory carries the explicit execution lessons of a stronger MLLM to weaker MLLMs without weight updates, whereas low-level control habits that the memory leaves unstated do not transfer. 6 Conclusion Although embodied agents increasingly surround frontier MLLMs with perception pipelines, harnesses with policy, and critic loops, our results show that a frontier MLLM under direct control already achieves strong closed-loop manipulation once its within-episode corrections persist across episodes. SimplEvo retains these corrections in two self-written text memories: a coordinate-free task memory distilled once from one training demonstration, and a bounded working memory rewritten after each training episode. Under the strict train–test protocol, with RGB views and proprioception as its only observations, SimplEvo raises GPT-6-Astra from 46.7% to 81.7% SR on EBench and leads the RoboDojo leaderboard. Our analyses yield four takeaways for embodied agent design. (1) A bounded text memory distilled from the trajectories of the agent itself matches the SR of visual memory at lower cost and retains most of the improvement without outcome feedback. (2) The proposed memory mechanism improves direct control more than the compared harness with policy. (3) Lessons on task invariants, applied to the objects that the MLLM locates in the current views, hold on unseen layouts and under randomized configurations. (4) A text memory written by one MLLM also improves other MLLMs that read it without modification, though only for the behaviors that it states explicitly. Limitations and future directions. These findings point to three directions for future work. First, verified memory updates could validate each candidate rewrite before it enters the working memory and thus filter out spurious lessons. Second, object-centric, behavior-explicit memory could anchor recorded actions to visual features and state the low-level control habits that GPT-6-Astra leaves implicit, so that recorded evidence carries across workspace geometries and the memory serves weaker MLLMs. Third, both benchmarks pause the simulator during MLLM deliberation and end episodes automatically upon completion, and each episode costs millions of tokens. These conditions motivate asynchronous, token-efficient deployment on physical robots with autonomous completion detection [ OpenRUA Team, 2026 ] . Each design runs one chain of ten validation scenes per task in the same scene order, and the first episode of each chain reads the task memory alone. The third design adds visual replay to the text working memory. The replay archives the frames and robot states of earlier attempts for inspection during execution and reflection. Both agent harnesses run these three designs on the four high-precision tasks. In the harness with π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} , a fourth design, described in Appendix E.3 , adds the executed trajectory of every policy call to the text design on the four high-precision tasks. Under direct control, a continuous design adapted from VISTA [ Han et al., 2026 ] replaces the working memory with one conversation across the ten episodes of each chain, together with the task memory, a guide that the MLLM revises, and an archive of observed frames. E.3 Executed Trajectory of π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} As defined in Section 3.1 , a policy call reports only the state at its end, so the motion of π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} never enters the trajectory ξ k \xi_{k} on which the reflection operates. The π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} trajectory design in Table 5 places this motion into ξ k \xi_{k} . Recorded states. π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} predicts absolute joint and gripper targets and replans every 30 physics steps. At the end of each replanning interval, the harness reads the proprioception of the robot and records the end-effector pose of each arm, as a position and a unit quaternion in the planning frame of that arm, together with the gripper openings, the base pose, and the physics step. These fields and frames match the measured state that every end-effector command returns, so the MLLM can compare a state reached by π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} with its own waypoints and command that state directly. The last entry of each policy call records the state at the end of that call, and because a physical robot measures these quantities at every control step, the record satisfies the information protocol of Section 3.1 . Exposure to the MLLM. The record reaches the MLLM through three channels. First, the result of each policy call lists one entry per replanning interval next to the existing step count, state, and images. Second, the memory tools that list earlier calls and measured robot states return the same entries from the episode record, so the MLLM can query them during the episode and during reflection. Third, the reflection prompt after the episode includes the entries of every policy call, so the record remains available for reflection in long episodes. All other settings, including the tool descriptions, the size budget of the working memory, the step limits, and the π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} checkpoint, match the text design. Results. With the executed trajectory, the harness succeeds in 17.5% of the episodes, down from 25.0%, while the use of the harness stays nearly unchanged. Every policy call of this design returns its executed trajectory, yet π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} executes 41% of the commanded physics steps, against 42% with the text design, the final working memories store poses about as often, and the MLLM commands positions near the states that π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} reached about as often as with the text design. E.7 Failure Modes of the Working Memory Most failures with the fixed working memory follow two patterns, a stored recipe that reproduces failing executions as well as successful ones, and a lesson drawn from training successes whose cause the camera views leave ambiguous. Of the 93 failures among the 510 test episodes of the train–test protocol, 41 replay stored coordinates, with at least a quarter of their moving waypoints within 1 cm of a coordinate in the fixed working memory, and 27 of these 41 failures occur on single-layout tasks. On nut tightening, each of the six failures places more than half of its moving waypoints near a stored coordinate, as the 14 successes do. Pen placement shows the second case. In four of its seven training successes, the official success arrived during a late adjustment of a pen that already appeared to stand in the holder, and the working memory states that “a late small intervention can precede success without proving its exact mechanism” while still recommending such adjustments. With this working memory, pen placement succeeds in 5 of 20 test episodes, and each of its 15 failures replays at most 5% of its moving waypoints. Pen placement and utensil placement together account for 23 of the 52 failures without replay.
