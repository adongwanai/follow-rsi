However, these models lack a focusing mechanism during training, resulting in perceptual representations that are often ambiguous or overly similar.
In this work, we introduce two visual focusing loss functions designed to establish correspondence between the model’s attention and specific regions within images.
Specifically, we leverage the multi-head attention mechanism within the model to selectively steer the model’s focus, leading to the emergence of spatial attention head specialization, characterized by diverse and spatially focused perceptual capabilities without requiring annotations.
Through both qualitative and quantitative evaluations, we demonstrate that our method substantially increases the spatial selectivity and diversity of attention heads, improving both interpretability and performance on dense prediction tasks such as zero-shot image segmentation. (Images: Visualization of 16 attention heads in the last layer of trained ViT-L/16-Reg model) 1 Introduction Self-supervised learning has become a powerful paradigm in computer vision, enabling models to acquire rich and meaningful visual representations without the need for costly and time-consuming labeled data.
Among various architectures, ViT ( Dosovitskiy et al., 2021 ) have attracted considerable attention for their remarkable ability to capture long-range dependencies and complex visual patterns within images.
Methods such as DINO ( DINO ) ( Caron et al., 2021 ) have demonstrated that large-scale unlabeled image datasets can effectively train ViT to produce robust and transferable visual features.
Despite these advances, a fundamental challenge remains: unlike supervised approaches that rely on explicit guidance via labels or self-supervised approaches that incorporate prior knowledge to guide attention, self-supervised ViT models without such explicit guidance or prior knowledge typically lack a focusing mechanism during training.
Consequently, the learned perceptual representations often tend to be ambiguous or overly similar across attention heads, which limits their discriminative capacity and interpretability. Addressing this limitation is crucial for extending the applicability of self-supervised ViT ,
particularly in tasks that require dense representations such as object segmentation and interpretability analysis.
To this end, we propose two visual focusing loss functions that establish correspondence between the model’s attention
and specific regions within images during training.
We refer to this correspondence as region-to-focus correspondence (ReFoCo).
The first loss, the inside-focus loss , is designed to align the model’s focus on local regions within images with the cropped images of these corresponding regions.
The second loss is inspired by weakly-supervised learning with masked images as proposed in Kumar Singh and Jae Lee (2017) ,
where masking forces the model to focus not only on the most discriminative object parts, but also on other regions.
Our proposed second loss, the outside-focus loss , is designed to achieve a similar effect in a self-supervised manner by associating partially masked images with the model’s focus on regions outside the masked parts. Our approach leverages the multi-head attention mechanism in ViT to selectively steer the model’s focus,
and we find that diverse and spatially focused perceptual capabilities emerge in our trained model, as shown in Fig. 1 .
Our method also substantially improves the model’s interpretability. We conduct extensive qualitative and quantitative evaluations to validate the effectiveness of our approach.
The results demonstrate that incorporating our visual focusing losses substantially improves the model’s ability to localize
and differentiate between various image regions.
These improvements lead to higher zero-shot image segmentation performance, unsupervised object discovery performance, and multi-object image retrieval performance,
along with clearer and more interpretable attention maps, highlighting the practical advantages of our method. We note that while the proposed losses encourage the emergence of diverse and spatially focused perceptual capabilities,
we observe a slight reduction in classification performance.
Accordingly, our results should not be interpreted as demonstrating universally stronger global or dense representations.
Rather, our method aims to introduce focusing mechanisms into self-supervised ViT learning without requiring annotations,
encouraging attention heads to capture diverse and spatially focused visual information. Overall, our contributions are as follows: • We identify the ambiguity and similarity of perceptual representations present in current self-supervised ViT models, which limits their interpretability. • We present a new direction for introducing focusing mechanisms into self-supervised ViT learning without requiring annotations, using visual focusing loss functions that link representation learning more directly to visual understanding. • We evaluate the performance of models trained using our visual focusing loss functions and demonstrate that they achieve higher zero-shot image segmentation performance, unsupervised object discovery performance, and multi-object image retrieval performance, while also producing more interpretable and meaningful attention maps. 2 Related work Vision Transformers and self-supervised learning. The advent of ViT ( Dosovitskiy et al., 2021 ) has ushered in a paradigm shift in self-supervised learning.
Leveraging their global receptive fields and adaptive attention mechanisms, ViT effectively capture long-range dependencies that conventional convolutional neural networks ( Simonyan and Zisserman, 2015 ; He et al., 2016 ) typically struggle to model.
Techniques such as DINO ( Caron et al., 2021 ) and iBOT ( Zhou et al., 2022 ) have demonstrated that ViTs are effective at learning structured, semantically rich representations without the need for labeled data.
Remarkably, both DINO and its successor, DINOv2 ( Oquab et al., 2024 ) , shows that ViTs can generate attention maps closely aligned with object boundaries, suggesting an emergent capacity for dense visual recognition. Visual focusing mechanisms. However, existing self-supervised ViT methods often lack effective focusing mechanisms during training.
Consequently, multiple attention heads tend to converge toward similar activation patterns, producing ambiguous or redundant representations ( Bhojanapalli et al., 2021 ) .
Some approaches have attempted to mitigate this issue by promoting attention map diversification ( Ren et al., 2025 ) , employing block dropping ( Bhojanapalli et al., 2021 ) , or knowledge distillation ( Wu et al., 2022 ) techniques to reduce redundancy.
However, these strategies are typically indirect, relying on architectural heuristics rather than explicitly designed loss functions that encourage interpretability or spatial differentiation.
Post-hoc explanation methods, such as attention rollout ( Abnar and Zuidema, 2020 ; Jo et al., 2025 ) and gradient-based techniques ( Selvaraju et al., 2017 ) ,
can reveal where the model focuses after training but do not influence the training dynamics.
Other efforts have introduced architectural modifications ( Chen et al., 2023 ) , add-on modules ( Lee et al., 2021 ) , or prior knowledge-guidance ( Miao et al., 2022 ) to foster diversity across attention heads or enhance spatial localization.
Yet these methods often require supervision or additional annotations, limiting their applicability in fully self-supervised settings. In summary, although current methods successfully capture global structure and high-level semantics, they often lack mechanisms that promote diverse, meaningful, and discriminative attention across different image regions.
The proposed visual focusing losses offer a principled approach to encourage spatial specialization, enhancing interpretability within self-supervised ViT frameworks.
Integrating such focusing mechanisms that shape attention during training is a relatively unexplored direction with the potential to link self-supervised representation learning to transparent and spatially grounded visual reasoning. Visual prompt techniques. Inspired by prompt engineering in natural language processing ( Brown et al., 2020 ) , a variety of visual prompts have been studied in computer vision
to intuitively modify the behavior of models in a visual manner ( Kirillov et al., 2023 ; Yang et al., 2023 ; Shtedritski et al., 2023 ; Zhuang et al., 2024 ; Jia et al., 2022 ; Park and Byun, 2024 ; Yoo et al., 2023 ; Bahng et al., 2022 ; Nozawa et al., 2025 ) .
In this work, to selectively steer the model’s focus, we leverage a visual prompting technique called PHS ( PHS ) ( Nozawa et al., 2025 ) .
In PHS , the visual prompt is given as a set of binary values R R assigned to each pixel in the image, where each value indicates whether it is included in the ROI ( ROI ) or not.
This has the advantage that the shape of the ROI is not restricted. 3 Proposed method We consider self-supervised learning settings, such as DINO ( Caron et al., 2021 ) , where teacher and student models are used and training is performed by minimizing a loss function between the two models.

To determine the training method adopted in our method, we compare the attention maps of four different training methods:
DeiT III ( Touvron et al., 2022 ) , CLIP ( Radford et al., 2021 ) , DINOv2 ( Oquab et al., 2024 ) and DINOv2 with registers ( Darcet et al., 2024 ) , utilizing publicly available pretrained models.
DeiT III is a label-supervised training method for ViT .
CLIP is a text-supervised training method using contrastive learning on image-text pairs.
DINOv2 with and without registers are self-supervised training methods to learn robust visual representations.
DINOv2 with registers extends DINOv2 by introducing additional register tokens to get rid of high-norm tokens in attention maps, which is called artifacts ( Darcet et al., 2024 ) .
More details about the pretrained models are described in Sec. C.1 . Figure 3 shows the average of the attention maps and the head mean of them on the validation set of ImageNet-1k ( Russakovsky et al., 2015 ) .
Artifacts are observed in the maps produced by DeiT III, CLIP, and DINOv2, which is consistent with Darcet et al. (2024) , whereas the maps generated by DINOv2 with registers are clearer.
Based on this result and extended analysis presented in Sec. D.1 , we adopt DINOv2 with registers as the baseline training method. More details about the training conditions are described in Sec. C.1 . 4.3 Zero-shot image segmentation To validate the ability of ViT models to focus on various regions within images, we evaluate their zero-shot image segmentation performance.
We follow a standard protocol ( Chefer et al., 2021 ) for evaluating the effectiveness of their heat maps.
For each heat map, we compute the head mean of the attention maps, where we use the [CLS] tokens from each head in the last attention layer as the attention maps.
We then binarize the heat map into a foreground/background segmentation map, where the patch mean of the heat map is used for the threshold value.
We utilize two datasets, ImageNet-Segmentation ( Guillaumin et al., 2014 ) and PASCAL VOC ( Everingham et al., 2010 ) , for the evaluation following Chefer et al. (2021) and Helbling et al. (2025) .
ImageNet-Segmentation consists of 4,276 single-label images from the validation set of ImageNet-1k ( Russakovsky et al., 2015 ) with annotated segmentations from 445 categories.
For the evaluation on PASCAL VOC, we utilize PASCAL VOC 2012 benchmark ( Everingham et al., 2015 ) and evaluate on a multi-class split (1,449 images).
In our multi-class split experiment, all annotated objects are considered as a single foreground. Quantitative evaluation results. To investigate the impact of changes to the loss function, we quantitatively evaluate zero-shot image segmentation performance using three standard evaluation metrics: mIoU ( mIoU ), Acc ( Acc ), and mAP ( mAP ). To investigate which regions of the images the models focus on, we visualize the average of the attention maps across the entire set of evaluation dataset images. Figure 4 shows the average of the attention maps and the head mean of them used as the heat map on the validation set of ImageNet-1k.
As a result, we find that the visual loss functions qualitatively change the characteristics of attention maps. Emergent focusing ability: Spatial Attention Head Specialization. As shown in Fig. 4 (a), the attention maps of each head in the original DINOv2 model mainly concentrate their outputs around the center.
In contrast, as illustrated in Fig. 4 (b), the model with ℒ in \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{in}} shows substantial variety in the output locations across different heads.
This visualizes the emergence of the ability for each head to focus on distinct spatial positions, and their information is integrated to produce the comprehensive heat map.
Notably, the focus area of the heat map in this model is broader compared to that of the original model.
In the model with ℒ out \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}} , in addition to heads that primarily focus on the central region as seen in the original model,
there also emerge heads that tend to avoid the center and focus on other regions.
This behavior is considered to reflect the design of ℒ out \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}} , which encourages attention to objects other than the most discriminative objects.
Additionally, a large activation is observed in a single patch at the top-left corner, which is considered to be a type of artifact, as discussed in Darcet et al. (2024) .
As described above, by using ℒ in \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{in}} and ℒ out \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}} , various attention behaviors emerge across different heads.
In the model with both ℒ in \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{in}} and ℒ out \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}} , we observe the coexistence of the attention maps observed in the model with ℒ in \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{in}} alone or ℒ out \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}} alone, in addition to attention maps that focus on broader regions.
We note that the artifacts seen in the model with ℒ out \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}} alone are absent.
While standard DINOv2 already exhibits content-based head specialization, with individual heads capturing different semantic features within an image,
our method retains such content-based specialization while introducing an additional, complementary form of specialization across spatial regions.
We refer to this phenomenon as the emergence of spatial attention head specialization. Figure 4 : Comparison of the average of attention maps on ImageNet-1k from head 1 1 to head 16 16 and their head mean.

For k-NN classification, we observe that the accuracy drops by approximately 1 − 3 % 1-3\% when visual focusing loss functions are introduced compared to the original DINOv2 model.
Our possible explanation for the performance drop is as follows.
The classification task is formulated as a single-label problem, where images typically contain a dominant object near the center.
This task setting aligns with local-to-global correspondence for single objects in DINO ( Caron et al., 2021 ) .
In contrast, our proposed loss functions are designed to capture a wide range of regions and focus on various locations, and may therefore be less specialized for this setting.
For linear probing classification, the accuracy difference between our visual focusing models and the original DINOv2 model is smaller than that observed with k-NN classification.
This result suggests that training a linear classifier for classification purposes enables effective utilization of the frozen features that have been pretrained to focus. Image retrieval performance. We also evaluate our models using an image retrieval task called FOIR ( FOIR ) ( Nozawa et al., 2025 ) . FOIR task is a multi-object image retrieval task that evaluates whether models can retrieve appropriate images for each specified object in query images, where the query images may contain multiple objects.
We utilize COCO ( Lin et al., 2014 ) and PASCAL VOC ( Everingham et al., 2010 ) datasets for evaluation.
We use Mean Precision at k k ( MP ​ @ ​ k \mathrm{MP@}k ) as an evaluation metric to measure the relevance of the top- k k retrieved results. Table 4 presents MP ​ @ ​ 100 \mathrm{MP@}100 for each model and dataset.
We evaluate models using two retrieval schemes.
The first scheme, referred to as “Fixed per image”, utilizes a single retrieval result obtained by extracting one feature vector from each query image for image retrieval.
The second scheme, “Visual prompt per object”, applies PHS during inference and specifies a bounding box for each object as a visual prompt, thereby obtaining retrieval results that focus on individual objects.
First, the consistent performance gain of the visual prompt per object scheme over the fixed per image one across the models indicates that PHS is effective during inference.
In a comparison between the models, under the fixed per image scheme, the original DINOv2 model outperforms the model with ℒ in \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{in}} and ℒ out \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}} ;
however, under the visual prompt per object scheme, the trend reverses, with the model with ℒ in \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{in}} and ℒ out \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}} outperforming the original DINOv2 model.
This result suggests that the feature representations of DINOv2 effectively capture global image characteristics, resulting in averaged retrieval performance over objects,
while the model with ℒ in \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{in}} and ℒ out \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}} , consistent with its training objective, exhibits its strengths when focusing on individual objects. Our results highlight a distinction between conventional local-to-global correspondence ( Caron et al., 2021 ) and
region-to-focus correspondence introduced by our visual focusing losses.
The main DINOv2 objective is based on local-to-global correspondence and is well suited to images containing a large dominant object,
as it encourages correspondence between the global representation of the object and local representations of its parts.
In contrast, our method is designed for images containing multiple objects or semantic regions, a common setting in natural images.
To address this setting, in addition to establishing local-to-global correspondence,
our proposed loss functions encourage correspondence between regional representations obtained through masking or cropping and selective attention toward the corresponding regions.
As observed in our experiments, this encourages the emergence of spatial attention head specialization,
with performance gains in zero-shot image segmentation, unsupervised object discovery, and multi-object image retrieval. Practical implications. These properties may also have practical implications for interpretability and customizability.
Distinct and spatially specialized attention heads can provide a more structured view of the visual information
captured by the model, potentially facilitating the analysis of its behavior and failure cases.
Moreover, such specialization may offer a straightforward mechanism for feature manipulation during inference.
For example, the output features could be modified by dynamically disabling the signals from individual attention heads during inference,
thereby potentially providing a mechanism for on-the-fly model customization.
These possibilities could be useful in applications that require selective or dynamically varying visual focus,
such as robotic vision and autonomous driving.
However, we do not evaluate such applications in this work,
and their effectiveness remains an important direction for future investigation. 6 Conclusion Our work addresses a key limitation of self-supervised ViT models
by introducing visual focusing loss functions that establish correspondence between
the model’s attention and specific regions within images.
We find that diverse and spatially focused perceptual capabilities emerge in our trained model,
enabling the model to leverage different attention heads to focus on distinct and meaningful image regions.
This targeted focusing mechanism effectively mitigates the ambiguity and redundancy often observed in learned representations, thereby improving the interpretability of the model’s attention.
Through comprehensive experiments, we demonstrate that our approach improves performance in zero-shot image segmentation, unsupervised object discovery, and multi-object image retrieval, while also producing clearer and more informative attention maps that support better visual understanding.
Overall, these results highlight the potential of introducing focusing mechanisms into self-supervised representation learning without requiring annotations, providing a new direction for learning diverse and spatially focused visual information. Appendix A Limitations While our method can train the model to focus on diverse regions within an image,
it does not allow explicit control over where each attention head focuses,
as the regions of focus are determined implicitly during training.
As a result, it is not possible to know in advance which regions each attention head will focus on until after training.
Our method also increases the training time and introduces a trade-off between classification performance and performance improvements in
zero-shot segmentation, object discovery, and FOIR tasks.
Exploring other effective ways to utilize the visual focusing image representations
obtained by our method remains an important direction for future work. Appendix B Societal impacts In this study, we propose a self-supervised training method, and our trained models demonstrate high-performance segmentation across a wide range of areas within images.
Our method can identify both salient and subtle objects in images, and it has potential applications such as medical imaging, autonomous driving, and defect detection.
However, incorrect segmentation of subtle structures may lead to erroneous decisions, posing risks especially in scenarios where safety is critical.
The ability to extract subtle visible information also has a risk of misuse, such as applying the method to privacy-invasive surveillance.
To mitigate these concerns, we emphasize clear communication of limitations and intended use. Appendix C Details of training and evaluation setups C.1 Training Most of the training hyperparameters are identical to those used in Oquab et al. (2024) for training ViT-L/16 on ImageNet-1k.
Here, we describe the differences from that setting.

Correspondingly, the batch size per GPU is set to 128 for the base and large models, and 64 for the giant models.
In addition, for the models with registers, the number of registers is set to 4 4 . Data augmentation. For each training image, following DINOv2, we apply multi-crop data augmentation, where global and local crops are generated using random resized cropping,

When our loss functions ℒ in \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{in}} or ℒ out \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}} are introduced, a box b x b_{x} for the PHS method is obtained as a region sampled via random resized cropping from the image,

For ℒ out \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}} , we also introduce randomness by selecting either the top or bottom edge of the visual-prompt box with equal probability for each batch and aligning the selected edge at the same height across all images in the batch. learning rate in {0.0001, 0.0002, 0.0005, 0.001, 0.002, 0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5},

D.2 Continual learning in existing models In this subsection, we consider applying our training method to continual learning in existing models.
For the pretrained model, we utilize the publicly available backbone ViT-L/14-Reg weights pretrained by DINOv2 with registers ( Darcet et al., 2024 ) , which we evaluate in Sec. 4.1 and Sec. D.1 .
Since pretrained weights of the DINO head is not available, we first train the DINO head using DINOv2 with registers from scratch, while keeping the pretrained backbone frozen.
After training the DINO head, we then perform training the entire networks, using each loss function with fewer iterations than training from scratch.
We use ImageNet-1k as the training dataset, as in the main experiments.
Details of the training setup are described in Sec. C.1 . Table A2 presents a comparison of zero-shot image segmentation performance between the pretrained model and models further trained using four different loss functions.
On ImageNet-Segmentation dataset, the performance improves with all loss functions used for continual learning, with the DINOv2 model with ℒ in \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{in}} and ℒ out \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}} achieving the highest performance.
On PASCAL VOC dataset, the DINOv2 loss, DINOv2 loss with ℒ in \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{in}} , and DINOv2 loss with ℒ out \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}} all show performance degradation across all metrics.
The discrepancy between the results on ImageNet-Segmentation and PASCAL VOC can be attributed to the domain gap between these datasets.
While further training on ImageNet-1k improves performance on the in-domain dataset ImageNet-Segmentation, it results in degradation in the out-of-domain dataset PASCAL VOC.
Notably, the DINOv2 loss with both ℒ in \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{in}} and ℒ out \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}} improves performance on two out of three metrics, mIoU and mAP.
This indicates that combination of ℒ in \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{in}} and ℒ out \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}} exhibits stronger robustness to domain shifts and better generalization ability. Our ℒ in \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{in}} & ℒ out \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}} model exhibits lower mean per-head entropy than the original DINOv2 model but high entropy after averaging attention across heads,
indicating that individual heads produce spatially concentrated attention, while their aggregation covers a broad range of image regions.
This result provides quantitative evidence of spatial specialization and diversity across attention heads.
