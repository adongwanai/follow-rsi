Abstract
Real-time 3D human mesh recovery from a single image
on mobile devices is a challenging problem with many ap-
plications, including robotics, biomechanics, AR/VR, and
exergames. Current state-of-the-art models are either too
slow to run efficiently on mobile devices or sacrifice ac-
curacy for speed.
We present InstantHMR, a family of
open-source single-image regressors suited for mobile in-
ference, based on the Momentum Human Rig (MHR) para-
metric model and trained exclusively on publicly avail-
able annotations from the SAM 3D Body dataset.
Our
approach achieves remarkable performance on a Samsung
Galaxy S23 NPU, recovering a 6,890-vertex body mesh in
5.5 ms per person, and 0.98 ms on an NVIDIA RTX 4070
GPU. This speed surpasses all existing methods we eval-
uated. On mobile, it is 12× and 23× faster than NLF-S
and NLF-L, the only phone-compatible methods that out-
perform ours in accuracy, while achieving competitive ac-
curacy with 40.0 mm PA-MPJPE on 3DPW, 48.8 mm on
EMDB and 41.7 mm on RICH. To ensure fair comparisons,
we re-evaluate 11 state-of-the-art methods under a unified
protocol aligned with the closest MHR-based competitors,
including Meta’s official MHR-to-SMPL conversion, and
report their reproduction results. Additionally, we measure
latency across eight different targets, ranging from desk-
top TensorRT to two phone NPUs. Code and models are
available at https://github.com/Naturalpad-
studio/InstantHMR.
1. Introduction
Monocular real-time human mesh recovery (HMR) is a
long-standing goal in computer vision, with applications
in robotics [10], tele-rehabilitation [39], workplace er-
gonomics [13, 21], AR/VR [12, 27], exergames [45], and
3D animation [37]. Since 2023, most smartphones include a
neural processing unit (NPU), which is a formidable oppor-
tunity for local real-time inference, fostering privacy, low
energy consumption and ease of use.

We present InstantHMR, a family of single-image HMR
models designed for phones. InstantHMR regresses the pa-
rameters of the Momentum Human Rig (MHR) [7] with
an efficient convolutional backbone [46] and a small trans-
former decoder. It is trained on the publicly released an-
notations of SAM 3D Body’s training set. On a Samsung
Galaxy S23 NPU, InstantHMR-M recovers a 6,890-vertex
mesh in 5.5 ms per person, faster than every method we
measure and 12× and 23× faster than NLF-S and NLF-L,
the only phone-compatible methods that outperform ours
in accuracy (cf. Fig. 1 and Tab. 1). Published numbers on
EMDB [15] and 3DPW [40] are produced under different
person boxes, focal lengths, joint sets and conversions be-
tween body models, and these choices move a single model
by several millimetres (supplementary material), as much as
the gaps between methods. We therefore re-evaluate eleven
methods under one protocol, matched to the closest MHR-
based competitors, and report for each how closely we re-
produce its paper. In summary, our contributions are:
• InstantHMR, a family of four MHR regressors (nano to
medium) that trace one accuracy/latency curve, trained
only on released annotations; InstantHMR-M runs in real
time on a phone NPU at 48.8 mm PA-MPJPE on EMDB-

• A matched evaluation protocol with Meta’s official
MHR-to-SMPL conversion, under which eleven com-

against its own paper.
• Latency on eight targets, from desktop TensorRT to two
phone NPUs and two phone CPUs, measured the same
way for every method.

2. Related work
Parametric body models.
Most HMR methods do not
predict a mesh directly, but the parameters of a paramet-
ric body model that maps a few pose and shape coeffi-
cients to a skinned mesh. SMPL [25] is by far the most
used, with a 6,890-vertex mesh, 24 joints and a shape space
learned from 3D scans.
SMPL-H [32] adds articulated
hands, and SMPL-X [30] adds both hands and an expressive
face. More recently, Meta released the Momentum Human
Rig (MHR) [7], which decouples the skeleton from the body
shape. Unlike SMPL, Anny [3] does not learn its shape
space from 3D scans. Its shape is set by interpretable pa-
rameters such as age, height and weight, built on the open-
source MakeHuman software and calibrated on WHO pop-
ulation statistics, so that a single model spans bodies from
infants to elders. SOMA [33] goes one step further and
maps SMPL, SMPL-X, MHR and Anny to a single canon-
ical mesh and skeleton. These models differ in topology,
skeleton and units, so comparing methods that predict dif-
ferent models requires converting between them, which is a
key part of our protocol (Sec. 4). InstantHMR predicts in
MHR parametric space.
Human mesh recovery from a single image.
Histor-
ically, HMR [14] consists in regressing SMPL parame-
ters from a single image.
HMR2.0 [8] scales this for-
mulation with a ViT backbone and large pseudo-labelled
data, TokenHMR [6] predicts the pose as discrete tokens,
CLIFF [23] conditions the regressor on the location of the
person in the full frame, and CameraHMR [29] adds a
camera-aware head trained with estimated focal lengths.
HybrIK [19] recovers the rotations from 3D keypoints with
analytical inverse kinematics, and NLF [34] predicts any
body point as a continuous field, which allows training a
single model on annotations from different skeletons and
meshes.
SMPLer-X [4] and Multi-HMR [2] regress the
expressive SMPL-X model, for one person or for many
at once, and the authors of Anny show that a Multi-HMR
model trained on synthetic Anny images matches its scan-
based counterparts [3]. SAM 3D Body [44] moves to MHR
with a promptable DINOv3-based architecture trained on a
large human-annotated corpus, and Fast SAM 3D Body [43]
accelerates it by up to 10.9× without retraining. NVIDIA’s
GEM-X [28] also builds on DINOv3 and predicts SOMA. It
provides a single-image model, and a video model that we
discuss in the next paragraph. These recent models are re-
markably robust, but they are built for GPUs. InstantHMR
predicts in MHR parametric space, with an architecture tai-
lored to phone inference.
Human mesh recovery from video.
Video methods use
the temporal information to recover smoother and more ac-
curate motion, often in world coordinates. WHAM [38]
lifts sequences of 2D keypoints to 3D, fuses them with
the features of a pretrained single-image encoder, and es-
timates the global trajectory with recurrent networks that
only look at past frames, so it runs online. TRAM [41]
and GVHMR [36] recover the global trajectory and motion
of people from whole sequences, GEM [20] unifies mo-
tion estimation and generation in a single diffusion-based
model, and the video model of GEM-X [28] follows the mo-
tion over the sequence from the features of its single-image
model and 2D keypoints. FastHMR [26] accelerates a ViT
regressor by merging tokens and layers, then denoises the
pose over 243-frame clips with a diffusion decoder, reach-
ing 103–150 FPS on an RTX 3090.

Efficient and on-device recovery.
Many applications
such as AR/VR, tele-rehabilitation or the teleoperation of
robots, need a low latency and not only a high through-
put: the pose of a frame must be available while that frame
is captured. Most video methods induce latency, since an
output that depends on future frames has to wait for them:
FastHMR waits for up to 242 later frames, about 8 s at
30 FPS. Online methods such as WHAM avoid this wait,
but still need a per-frame image model: the 200 FPS re-
ported for WHAM exclude its ViTPose keypoint detector
and its pretrained image encoder [38]. This study there-
fore focuses on the single-image model, which estimates
the current frame from that frame alone and is the per-frame
building block of online video methods such as WHAM.
Among single-image methods, efficient HMR has mostly
targeted GPUs, e.g. Seeing Machines reports 5.5–15.8 ms
on an NVIDIA Jetson Thor robotics module [35].
On
phones, BlazePose GHUM Holistic [9] estimates 3D land-
marks in real time and lifts them to a body model, but is
not evaluated on a standard mesh benchmark. The closest
work to ours is MeshPose [18], which localises a low-poly
SMPL mesh with DensePose-style supervision and runs at
97–153 FPS on an iPhone 12, depending on the backbone;
we evaluate its released model in Sec. 5.
Evaluation protocols.
3DPW [40], EMDB [15] and
RICH [11] are the standard in-the-wild benchmarks, but pa-
pers evaluate on them in different ways. They differ in the
person boxes (ground truth or detections), the focal length
(calibrated or a fixed field of view), and, for methods that do
not output SMPL, the conversion to SMPL. For instance,
SAM 3D Body converts MHR to SMPL with Meta’s it-
erative fitter, Fast SAM 3D Body replaces that fitter with
a learned network, and GEM-X releases its own dataset-
specific projectors. Each of these choices moves a single
model by several millimetres, so we fix one protocol, the
one of the closest MHR-based competitors, and re-run ev-
ery method under it (Sec. 4).
3. InstantHMR
InstantHMR takes a square crop of the person, I
∈
R288×288×3, and the position of that crop in the full im-
age, c ∈R3 (defined in Sec. 3.1). It returns three outputs
(Fig. 2). The first is the person’s MHR parameters: the rig
parameters θ ∈R204, which hold the root translation and
rotation, 130 joint parameters and 68 bone scales, and the
identity coefficients β ∈R45, which shape the body sur-
face. The second is, for each of the 70 2D keypoints, two
probability distributions over 96 bins, one for its x and one
for its y coordinate, P ∈[0, 1]70×2×96 (SimCC [22]). Their
expectations give the 2D keypoints x ∈R70×2, in crop co-
ordinates. The third is the camera translation t ∈R3, which
places the body in the camera frame. The MHR rig M then
turns (θ, β) into the posed mesh V = M(θ, β) ∈R6,890×3
and its 70 3D keypoints J ∈R70×3, which are in camera
coordinates once translated by t. V is a farthest-point sub-

3.1. Network
HGNetV2 Backbone.
A convolutional HGNetV2 [46]
backbone E, pretrained on ImageNet, turns the 288 × 288
crop into a 9 × 9 grid of features. A linear layer projects
them to d = 512 channels, and a fixed 2D sine-cosine posi-
tional embedding is added, which gives the 81 image tokens
F = E(I) ∈R81×d. Convolutions, unlike the attention of a
ViT backbone, map directly onto the operators phone NPUs
accelerate. The four sizes of InstantHMR differ only in the
backbone (B0, B1, B2, B4 for nano, tiny, small, medium),
and have 20.0, 20.3, 24.4 and 32.2 M parameters. The de-
coder and the heads are the same in every size, and the de-
coder alone holds 16.8 M of these parameters.
CLIFF conditioning.
A crop alone does not determine
where the person is in 3D: the same crop could come
from the centre or the edge of a wide-angle image.
As
in CLIFF [23], the network therefore receives the posi-
tion of the crop in the full image, but expressed through
the focal length f rather than the image size, so that it
keeps one meaning across cameras.
For a box of cen-
tre (bx, by) and size bw × bh in an image of size W ×
H, c holds the two viewing angles of the box centre,
arctan((bx −W/2)/f) and arctan((by −H/2)/f), and
the box size max(bw, bh)/f. A two-layer MLP ϕ embeds
it into ϕ(c) ∈Rd. At deployment, without calibration, f
defaults to 1.05× the image diagonal.
Transformer Decoder.
71 learned queries Q ∈R71×d,
one for the body and one per 2D keypoint, receive the posi-
tion embedding ϕ(c), added to every query. A 4-layer pre-
norm transformer decoder [5] D (8 heads) then lets them
attend to each other and to the image tokens F, and outputs
one feature zi ∈Rd per query. The feature z0 comes from
the body query, and z1, . . . , z70 from the keypoint queries.
Decoder Heads.
Three heads read the decoder output.
The SimCC head h2D, a two-layer MLP shared by all
keypoints, turns each keypoint query into its two distribu-
tions, Pk = h2D(zk), whose expectations over the bin cen-
tres give the 2D keypoint xk. Classifying the coordinates
rather than regressing them keeps the image evidence of ev-
ery joint in the decoder. The camera head hcam, a linear
layer, predicts the camera translation from the body query,
t = hcam(z0). The MHR head hMHR predicts the body
from the same query, (θ, β) = hMHR(z0). It is a two-layer
head in SAM 3D Body’s continuous parameterisation: the
root rotation as a 6D vector [47], the body pose in its contin-
uous form, the identity coefficients β, 28 bone-scale prin-
cipal components and two hand blocks, which are decoded
into the rig parameters θ. The root translation is fixed to
zero (the person is placed by the camera translation), and
the bone-scale coefficients and the six limb-length parame-
ters are bounded to the range seen in training. Without these
bounds, the scales enter the rig’s forward kinematics mul-
tiplicatively along each limb, and a single large prediction
can produce a skeleton hundreds of metres tall that diverges
the training.
The 3D keypoints are not regressed: they are read from
the posed rig through its own forward kinematics, so 3D
keypoints and mesh can never disagree.
3.2. Training
Dataset.
InstantHMR is trained on the human-annotated
MHR fits that Meta released with SAM 3D Body [44], over
the images of five of its sources: SA-1B [17], AI Chal-
lenger [42], Harmony4D [16], COCO [24] and MPII [1],
4.2 M person crops in total, sampled at 43 / 26 / 14 / 13 / 4 %.
No 3DPW or EMDB images are used. The labels are the
dataset’s annotations, not the predictions of a teacher model,
so the student is not capped at a teacher’s accuracy.
Losses.
The training loss is a weighted sum of eleven
terms, which fall into three groups. The first group com-
pares the predicted MHR parameters with the annotated
ones: the joint parameters, the root rotation, the bone scales,
the identity coefficients and the camera translation. The
second group compares the geometry that the rig produces
from these parameters: the 70 3D keypoints, 595 vertices
spread over the mesh, and the 127 joints of the skeleton. As
in NLF [34], the keypoint and vertex errors are plain Eu-
clidean distances, not squared ones, so that a few bad sam-
ples do not dominate the gradient. The vertex term matters
for shape: the identity coefficients β move the mesh but not
the skeleton, so without it they receive no geometric gradi-
ent at all. The third group supervises the image side: the
SimCC cross-entropy against a Gaussian label centred on
each annotated keypoint, a distance on the 2D keypoints x,
and a reprojection term that projects the 3D keypoints with
the predicted translation t and the calibrated camera, and
compares them with the annotated 2D keypoints. The 40
finger keypoints weigh 0.2 in the 3D and reprojection terms,
and the terms that go through the rig are ramped up over the
first 2,000 steps. We tuned the weights of the eleven terms
empirically, with single-batch overfitting tests and by bal-
ancing the measured gradient norm of each term; the equa-
tions and the weights are given in the supplementary mate-
rial.

Training Recipe and Deployment.
Each model is trained
for 600 epochs of 400 k samples, batch 256 on four GPUs,
with AdamW and a one-cycle learning-rate schedule peak-
ing at 6 × 10−4. Each training crop is either the standard
crop (probability 0.75) or one of three other framings cut
from the full image (zoomed out, zoomed in, shifted), with
synthetic occlusion. This mix is the largest single gain we
measured (Tab. 4): against training on the standard crop
alone, it improves EMDB-1 PA-MPJPE by 10.0 mm (58.8
to 48.8) and 3DPW by 2.7 mm, more than an even or uni-
form mix of the framings. The 288 px input is the second:
at every backbone size, it improves EMDB-1 PA-MPJPE by
4.4–5.5 mm over 224 px (Tab. 4 for the medium backbone,
the supplementary material for the others).
The network exports to a single ONNX graph, which
runs in fp16 on the S23 NPU with all of its 1,607 layers on
the NPU. For the latency we report, the graph also includes
the rig’s forward kinematics and linear blend skinning up to
the posed mesh V.
4. Evaluation protocol
How a method is scored on 3DPW and EMDB moves
its error by several millimetres, as much as the gaps be-
tween methods.
The supplementary material shows this
for InstantHMR-M alone: on EMDB-1, the choice of per-
son boxes, focal length and body-model conversion moves
its PA-MPJPE between 46.9 and 50.1 mm, and a learned
conversion network [43] raises it to 65.9 mm. Picking the
most favourable setting per benchmark would be protocol
shopping.
Our protocol is not the most favourable one
for us: detector boxes score InstantHMR-M 1.9 mm better
than the ground-truth boxes we report. We fix one protocol
before scoring our models under it: the one of our clos-
est competitors, the MHR-based SAM 3D Body and Fast
SAM 3D Body, and score every method under it.
Settings.
Every method is scored on the same frames,
with the same person boxes and the same camera.

person boxes are the ones the benchmarks provide: EMDB
ships one per frame, and for 3DPW, which ships none, we
take the extent of the annotated 2D keypoints enlarged by

Reproduction.
We run each competitor from its released
code and weights, with its own crop and preprocessing,
and compare our score with the one printed in its paper,
on the joint set of that paper.
We call a method repro-
duced when the two PA-MPJPE differ by less than 1 mm.
NLF-S and TokenHMR reproduce on both benchmarks, and
CameraHMR on 3DPW. NLF-L scores 1.9–2.2 mm better
than its paper, and SAM 3D Body, Fast SAM 3D Body,
HybrIK, and CameraHMR on EMDB-1 score 1.3–4.5 mm
worse. On RICH, CameraHMR reproduces its published
number (0.4 mm better), SAM 3D Body scores 2.6 mm
worse and Fast SAM 3D Body 1.7 mm better than their
papers.
HMR2.0b scores 9.9 mm worse than the run of
it reported by SAM 3D Body, whose crop is not stated,
while our harness reproduces HMR2.0b on EMDB-1 within
0.4 mm. Such gaps can come from settings that a paper does
not fully specify, and we do not claim that any published
number is wrong.
Latency.
Latency is the model’s time per person crop at
batch 1, from the crop to a posed mesh of up to 6,890
vertices; detection and cropping are excluded for every
method.
Desktop timings are medians over interleaved
rounds, so background load affects all methods alike. Phone
timings use Qualcomm AI Hub [31] on physical devices,
the median of three jobs. Only complete methods are timed:
a method that does not export whole is timed in its own re-
lease (HybrIK, SAM 3D Body, Fast SAM 3D Body), and
partial timings are left to the supplementary material.
5. Experiments
We evaluate on EMDB-1 [15], 3DPW test [40] and RICH
test [11] under the protocol of Sec. 4. None of the three
benchmarks’ images are in InstantHMR’s training data;
four competitors trained on 3DPW-train (marked †), and
three on RICH (‡), which makes EMDB-1 the fairer of the
three.
Speed (Tab. 1).
On the Galaxy S23 NPU, InstantHMR-M
takes 5.49 ms per person, and 4.01 ms on the S24. Only one
other method is real time on the phone: SMPLer-X-S32, at
11.12 ms, but 22 mm less accurate on EMDB-1. Every ViT-
H and DINOv3-based method (CameraHMR, TokenHMR,
HMR2.0, SAM 3D Body, Fast SAM 3D Body) fails to run
on either phone NPU, for lack of memory or because the
compiled model fails. On the desktop GPU, InstantHMR-
M takes 0.98 ms with TensorRT, the fastest of all methods
(nano: 0.70 ms).
Why InstantHMR is fast on the NPU.
On the S23’s
CPU, InstantHMR-M (93 ms) and NLF-S (103 ms) are
close, and on the desktop GPU they are 1.6× apart, but on
the NPU NLF-S is 12× slower. Both models run entirely
on the NPU (807 of 807 layers for NLF-S, 1,607 of 1,607
for InstantHMR-M), so the gap does not come from lay-
ers falling back to the CPU: it comes from how each model
produces the mesh. NLF estimates each vertex as a sepa-
rate query point with its own 8 × 8 × 8 heatmap, whose
score-weighted average gives the vertex position. For 6,890
vertices, most of the work is moving and summing tensors
of 6,890 × 512 values, which an NPU, built for convolu-
tions and matrix products, does slowly: gathers, reductions,
transposes, reshapes and softmaxes take 71% of NLF-S’s
NPU time, its convolutions under 2%. With 1,048 query
7

points instead of 6,890, the same network runs in 8.1 ms. In-
stantHMR instead regresses the 204 MHR parameters once
per person, with a convolutional backbone and a small de-
coder, operators the NPU is designed for; the mesh then fol-
lows from the rig’s forward kinematics and skinning, a fixed
weighted sum whose cost does not depend on the network.
Accuracy (Tab. 2).
Among the methods that run on
a phone, InstantHMR-M is 20–25 mm more accurate
than SMPLer-X, MeshPose, HybrIK and Multi-HMR on
EMDB-1 (PA-MPJPE), and 9–15 mm on 3DPW. Mesh-
Pose, the closest phone-oriented work, is released only
with its HRNet-32 backbone, which scores 73.7 mm on
EMDB-1 and 49.7 mm on 3DPW against 48.8 and 40.0 mm
for InstantHMR-M; its paper reports its phone-sized vari-
ants, which are not released, 2–3 mm behind that model
on 3DPW2. On hard poses, the gap can be much larger
than these averages (Fig. 3).
Only NLF is more accu-
rate, at 12× (NLF-S) and 23× (NLF-L) the phone la-
tency; on 3DPW, InstantHMR-M is within 0.6 mm of NLF-
S. Without the phone constraint, Fast SAM 3D Body and
SAM 3D Body remain the most accurate, 8–10 mm better
than InstantHMR-M on EMDB-1, at 130–416 ms per per-
son on a desktop GPU. With detector boxes in place of the
ground-truth ones (InstantHMR-M + det.), InstantHMR-M
improves to 46.9 mm on EMDB-1 and 39.3 mm on 3DPW,
the setting NLF is evaluated in. RICH shows the same pic-
ture: InstantHMR-M (41.7 mm) is 18–28 mm more accurate
than HybrIK, Multi-HMR and SMPLer-X, which is trained
on RICH. Among the methods that run on a phone, only
NLF, also trained on RICH, is more accurate; and Cam-
eraHMR, SAM 3D Body and Fast SAM 3D Body are 8–
10 mm better than InstantHMR-M.

6. Limitations and conclusion
Limitations.
InstantHMR has no dedicated hand encoder
or decoder. The hands are read from the same 288 × 288
crop of the whole person and the same 9 × 9 image to-
kens as the rest of the body, where a hand covers only a
few pixels. SAM 3D Body, in contrast, refines the hands
with a second decoder that runs on crops around each hand.
Adding a light hand branch, without losing the real-time
latency on the phone NPU, is left for future work.
All
our phone measurements are on Android phones with Qual-
comm NPUs (Galaxy S23 and S24). We did not evaluate In-
stantHMR on Apple silicon (the Neural Engine of iPhones,
through Core ML), which is also left for future work. In-
stantHMR estimates each frame on its own, and we report
no temporal metric (jitter or acceleration error), although
the applications we target are sensitive to it; evaluating it,
with and without the 1 Euro filter of our released demo,
is future work. Finally, InstantHMR is trained on the re-
leased annotations of the SAM 3D Body dataset, but many
of its images, such as those of SA-1B and AI Challenger,
may only be used for research. For this reason, the nano,
tiny, small and medium models are released for research use
only. InstantHMR-M-C, trained only on commercially us-
able images (Harmony4D and COCO), can be used in com-
mercial products, but it is much less accurate: 72.2 mm PA-
MPJPE on EMDB-1 against 48.8 mm for InstantHMR-M.
Closing this gap requires more training images with per-
missive licences, real or synthetic.
Conclusion.
We presented InstantHMR, a family of
single-image MHR regressors built for phone NPUs. On
the Samsung Galaxy S23 NPU, InstantHMR-M recovers a
6,890-vertex body mesh in 5.49 ms per person, at 48.8 mm
PA-MPJPE on EMDB-1, 40.0 mm on 3DPW and 41.7 mm
on RICH. It is the most accurate method that runs in real
time on the phone; the two methods that are more accurate
8

and also run on a phone NPU, NLF-S and NLF-L, are 12×
and 23× slower there. To make these comparisons fair, we
re-evaluated eleven methods under one protocol, matched
to the closest MHR-based competitors, and reported how
closely each reproduces its paper. Code and models are
available at https://github.com/Naturalpad-
studio/InstantHMR.
