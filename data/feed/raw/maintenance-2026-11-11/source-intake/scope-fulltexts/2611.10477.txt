University of Maryland, College Park Heng Huang Affiliation: {yige, heng}@umd.edu Abstract Reinforcement-learning-trained agents for retrieval-augmented generation retrieve content (passages, knowledge-graph fragments, or hyperedges) and supervise training with outcome rewards on the final answer. Across these paradigms the reasoning route through retrieved content never leaves the language model’s hidden state, conflating declarative knowledge with the procedural knowledge that recurs across queries of the same logical type; content-only methods consequently plateau as reasoning depth grows. We introduce PatternBloom, an agentic RAG framework that shifts the unit of retrieval from content (what to read) to logic (how to reason). A first RL stage scores the agent’s constructed evidence graph by a frozen oracle’s information gain through the Information-Density Reward ( IDR ), a size-normalized mutual-information surrogate. High-reward trajectories are distilled into a Graph Pattern Memory ( GPM ) of type-abstracted reasoning skeletons, and a second RL stage uses the Pattern-Augmented Reward ( PAR ) to couple policy to memory, turning procedural reasoning into a learnable, externalized structural prior. A 7B PatternBloom outperforms 27 27 baselines by + 15.0 +15.0 Avg-OOD EM, with the gain compounding with reasoning depth precisely because procedural knowledge, once distilled, is reused rather than re-derived at every query. with outcome rewards [ 8 , 9 ] . Across these

is left to a black-box policy: the agent learns to read better

Existing RAG paradigms differ primarily in what is retrieved : paragraph-based methods (Search-R1, FrugalRAG [ 10 ] , R1-Searcher) return chunk-level passages; knowledge-graph methods (GraphRAG [ 11 ] , HippoRAG [ 12 , 13 ] , LinearRAG [ 14 ] , LightRAG [ 15 ] , GFM-RAG [ 16 ] ) build entity-relation graphs offline and return subgraphs; and structure-aware methods (StructRAG [ 17 ] , Graph-R1, Structure-R1 [ 18 ] ) retrieve task-adaptive units such as hyperedges or summarized clusters. Despite these different retrieval units, the three paradigms share a common limitation: the reasoning route through the retrieved evidence never leaves the language model’s hidden state. Paragraph methods leave entity binding and relation inference to opaque attention; knowledge-graph and structure-aware methods expose entities as graph nodes, but the path through those nodes that solves a given query is still inferred at run time. Figure 1 (left) frames this collectively as content retrieval : the agent decides what to read (a passage, a subgraph, or a hyperedge), but the reasoning skeleton that links those units is never itself retrieved. The cost compounds on compositionally complex queries, motivating a retrieval target that is the reasoning route itself. Why richer content does not solve the problem. The plateau on deeper queries is not primarily a retrieval-recall failure: knowledge-graph and structure-aware methods often achieve high entity-level coverage. The bottleneck is procedural. Two queries about different films, “in which country was the director of Black Gold born?” and “where was the director of Top Gun from?”, share an identical logical structure yet are solved from scratch each time: their typed reasoning skeleton, from film to person to country , is encoded only in the policy’s hidden activations and discarded when the episode ends. Cognitively, this conflates declarative knowledge (the facts to retrieve) with procedural knowledge (the operations that recur across queries of the same logical type) [ 19 ] . The two memory systems are functionally distinct in human reasoning and require different storage and retrieval mechanisms; a retrieval system that augments only declarative memory will, by construction, fail to compound experience across queries that share the same procedural skeleton. From content retrieval to logic retrieval. To this end, we introduce PatternBloom, an agentic RAG system that shifts the retrieval target from content ( what to read ) to logic ( how to reason ). The unit of retrieval is a graph pattern (Fig. 1 , right): a small directed skeleton over typed entity slots that prescribes how to traverse retrieved evidence rather than what evidence to read. The skeleton is type-abstracted, so the same pattern serves any query of the same logical structure (concrete examples in § 3.3 ), making procedural knowledge an explicit, retrievable object rather than a behavior latent in policy weights. A two-stage RL pipeline trains and exploits this externalized memory. Stage I uses an Information-Density Reward to supervise the agent on the information content of the constructed evidence graph rather than on the answer string, promoting the graph to a directly supervised quantity. An inter-stage distillation extracts type-abstracted skeletons from high-reward trajectories into a Graph Pattern Memory. Stage II uses a Pattern-Augmented Reward to couple the policy to the frozen memory, crediting trajectories that traverse retrieved patterns and turning procedural reasoning from a soft prompt hint into a gradient signal. Where prior systems retrieve passages (paragraph methods), subgraphs (knowledge-graph methods), or hyperedges (structure-aware methods), PatternBloom retrieves a typed reasoning skeleton: the logic itself. Realizing this paradigm shift surfaces three concrete challenges: (C1) supervising the constructed evidence graph as an information object rather than scoring only the answer string; (C2) externalizing reasoning patterns in a form that is reusable across queries and backbones; and (C3) coupling a frozen pattern store back to the policy as a process-level gradient signal. Our main contributions, summarized below, address (C1)–(C3) respectively via the Information-Density Reward, the Graph Pattern Memory, and the Pattern-Augmented Reward. • A new retrieval paradigm for agentic RAG: from content to logic. PatternBloom reframes agentic RAG by retrieving typed reasoning skeletons rather than content units. Stored in an externalized Graph Pattern Memory ( GPM ) auto-distilled from a Stage-I RL agent’s own high-reward trajectories, these skeletons make procedural knowledge a first-class retrievable artefact: inspectable, frozen at deployment, and reusable across queries and backbones. • An information-theoretic two-stage RL framework. Stage I supervises the constructed evidence graph itself via the Information-Density Reward ( IDR ); Theorem 1 establishes IDR as a bounded monotone surrogate for size-normalized conditional mutual information, the first information-theoretic reward design for agentic graph construction. Stage II couples the policy to the frozen GPM via the Pattern-Augmented Reward ( PAR ), promoting pattern coverage from a soft prompt hint to a process-level gradient signal. • Procedural-knowledge accumulation as a new scaling axis. A 7B PatternBloom outperforms 27 27 baselines by + 15.0 +15.0 Avg-OOD EM; the gain compounds with reasoning depth where content-only retrieval saturates, and the same frozen pattern memory transfers across backbone families ( 1.5 1.5 B– 32 32 B), establishing pattern retrieval as a scaling axis distinct from larger backbones, deeper retrieval, or richer prompts. 2 Related Work Structured retrieval-augmented generation. Retrieval-augmented generation [ 1 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 ] has progressively moved toward structured evidence. Knowledge-graph methods build offline graphs and return subgraphs (GraphRAG [ 11 ] , HippoRAG [ 12 , 13 ] , LightRAG [ 15 ] , LinearRAG [ 14 ] , GFM-RAG [ 16 ] ); hypergraph variants extend to n n -ary facts (HyperGraphRAG [ 24 ] , Graph-R1 [ 6 ] ); adaptive-structure methods select per-query formats (StructRAG [ 17 ] , Structure-R1 [ 18 ] ). Across this line the structure is fixed at retrieval time, and the agent receives content fragments rather than a reasoning path. Reinforcement learning for agentic RAG. A complementary line trains agents end-to-end with policy gradients [ 25 , 26 , 8 , 9 ] : Search-R1 [ 5 ] , R1-Searcher [ 7 ] , FrugalRAG [ 10 ] , Graph-R1, Structure-R1, and ReSearch [ 27 ] share answer-level outcome rewards; prompted variants couple iterative retrieval with chain-of-thought [ 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 ] . Two constraints bottleneck both lines: (i) supervision is surface-form, scoring the answer string but never the evidence graph as an information object; (ii) the retrieval unit is content tied to specific entities, not the procedural skeleton that recurs across queries of the same logical type. PatternBloom addresses both via an information-theoretic graph reward and an externalized Graph Pattern Memory of type-abstracted skeletons; a method-by-method comparison appears in Appendix J . 3 PatternBloom: Logic-Enhanced RL with Externalized Graph Pattern Memory Section 2 identified two constraints that bottleneck prior agentic RAG: surface-form supervision that never interrogates the constructed evidence graph, and a content-only retrieval target that leaves procedural reasoning latent in policy weights. PatternBloom is built around one principle that addresses both: supervise the agent on what evidence it gathers and how it traverses that evidence, with each component an externalized, learnable object rather than a behavior to be hoped for. Stage I (§ 3.2 ) targets the first constraint via the Information-Density Reward ( IDR ), which scores the evidence graph’s information content under a frozen oracle and so promotes the graph itself to a directly supervised quantity. Trajectories from the Stage-I best checkpoint are distilled (§ 3.3 ) into a Graph Pattern Memory ( GPM ) of type-abstracted reasoning skeletons; Stage II (§ 3.4 ) couples the frozen GPM to the policy via the Pattern-Augmented Reward ( PAR ), turning procedural reasoning into a retrievable, learnable structural prior and addressing the second constraint. This blindness leaves π θ \pi_{\theta} with no signal to prefer minimum-sufficient evidence over verbose-but-correct rollouts, and the gap widens with reasoning depth (Appendix G.10 ). IDR thereby provides the first information-theoretic reward design for agentic graph construction, replacing the surface-form heuristics that bottleneck prior work with a principled signal that scores the graph as a whole. 3.3 Pattern Distillation: Building the Externalized Procedural Memory A retrieved triple like ( Black Gold , directed_by , Leslie Martinson ) (\textsc{Black Gold},\texttt{directed\_by},\textsc{Leslie Martinson}) is query-specific and never recurs, but many queries share the same logical structure: “given a film, find the director’s nationality” applies across thousands of film–director–country instances. The procedural component of multi-hop reasoning— how types of facts compose—recurs while the instances change, yet prior systems leave this regularity implicit in policy weights (§ 2 ). PatternBloom lifts each instance triple to a type-abstracted skeleton ( film , directed_by , person ) , ( person , born_in , country ) (\textsc{film},\texttt{directed\_by},\textsc{person}),(\textsc{person},\texttt{born\_in},\textsc{country}) that is reusable across all instances of the same query type, and stores these skeletons in an external memory auto-distilled from the policy’s own high-reward Stage-I trajectories. Three properties follow from this construction: type abstraction makes each entry an equivalence class over instances and so amortizes the cost of one query’s reasoning across thousands of structurally similar ones; externalization makes the memory inspectable, freezable, and reusable across backbones; and self-distillation keeps the memory aligned with what this policy actually solves, rather than a hand-curated wishlist. Graph pattern. A graph pattern P P is a tuple ( σ , skel , desc , n ) (\sigma,\mathrm{skel},\mathrm{desc},n) where σ ∈ 𝒮 \sigma\in\mathcal{S} is a query signature, skel \mathrm{skel} is a connected directed graph of typed entity nodes (the reasoning skeleton), desc \mathrm{desc} is a short natural-language descriptor, and n n is a usage count. The Graph Pattern Memory ℳ \mathcal{M} is the learned external store ℳ = { ( σ i , skel i , desc i , n i ) } i = 1 | ℳ | , \mathcal{M}=\bigl\{\bigl(\sigma_{i},\,\mathrm{skel}_{i},\,\mathrm{desc}_{i},\,n_{i}\bigr)\bigr\}_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{M}|}, (4) populated by distilling Stage-I trajectories. Signatures are extracted by a deterministic parser (e.g., lookup(country)[FILM] ); entity types in the skeleton come from the type-abstraction step, and descriptors are auto-generated from the signature and a representative answer instance. Coverage and retrieval. At Stage II, patterns are retrieved by encoding σ ⁡ ( q ) \sigma(q) with a sentence encoder and ranking ℳ \mathcal{M} by cosine similarity in a FAISS [ 36 ] index. The trajectory–pattern coverage 𝒞 ⁡ ( τ , P ) = | abs ( τ ) ∩ P . skel | | P . skel | , abs ⁡ ( τ ) ≔ { ( type ⁡ ( h ) , r , type ⁡ ( t ) ) : ( h , r , t ) ∈ τ } , \mathcal{C}(\tau,P)=\frac{|\,\mathrm{abs}(\tau)\cap P.\mathrm{skel}\,|}{|P.\mathrm{skel}|},\quad\mathrm{abs}(\tau)\coloneqq\{(\mathrm{type}(h),r,\mathrm{type}(t)):(h,r,t)\in\tau\}, (5) divides by the skeleton size rather than by the union, so a trajectory that contains the required reasoning steps plus exploratory extras receives full credit. Memory construction. The memory ℳ 0 \mathcal{M}_{0} is built between Stage I and Stage II from high-IDR trajectories ( R IDR ≥ 0.85 R_{\textsc{IDR}}\geq 0.85 , the empirical top-quartile threshold; Appendix C ); two patterns merge when their signature embeddings have cosine similarity ≥ θ merge = 0.85 \geq\theta_{\mathrm{merge}}=0.85 . Structure-based retrieval fabricates both directors’ names, and chunk-based retrieval confuses the film Antonieta with the writer Antonieta Rivas Mercado, while PatternBloom’s typed skeleton fixes what to look up for each film. 5 Conclusion Multi-hop reasoning systems have so far retrieved content units
(paragraphs, knowledge-graph fragments, or hyperedges), yet the primitive
that compounds with reasoning depth is logic: the type-abstracted
reasoning skeleton applicable across queries that share compositional
structure. We realize this paradigm shift in PatternBloom, an RL framework
whose Information-Density Reward provides the first information-theoretic
grounding for graph-construction RL (a bounded monotone surrogate for
size-normalized conditional mutual information), and whose
Pattern-Augmented Reward turns a frozen Graph Pattern Memory into a
learnable structural prior. The gain compounds with reasoning depth, a
property absent from content-based retrieval, precisely because procedural
knowledge, once externalized, accumulates across queries rather than
dissolving into policy weights at each episode end; the same frozen
pattern store further transfers across out-of-distribution benchmarks and
backbone scales, evidencing that what is being learned is reasoning
structure, not benchmark-specific content. PatternBloom thereby reframes
retrieval-augmented reasoning from a content-fetching problem into one of
procedural-knowledge accumulation, opening a scaling axis distinct from
larger backbones, deeper retrieval, or richer prompts. Appendix C Graph Pattern Memory Implementation Details This appendix expands the implementation of the Graph Pattern Memory GPM introduced in § 3.3 . C.1 Multi-Skeleton Pattern Storage The main paper (§ 3.3 ) describes each pattern as carrying a
single skeleton. The implementation generalizes this: each pattern stores a
list of distinct skeletons (typically a small handful per signature in
practice), and coverage is taken as the maximum over them. The motivation is structural diversity: two trajectories that share a query
signature can produce structurally different evidence graphs that both clear
the high-quality threshold R IDR ≥ 0.85 R_{\textsc{IDR}}\geq 0.85 . For instance, a query with
signature compare(by[birth_year])[FILM] can be answered either via
a direct two-hop chain through both films’ release dates, or via a four-hop
comparison that first resolves both directors and then their birth years. A
single-skeleton schema would force one to overwrite the other, losing valid
alternative reasoning paths for the same query type. We therefore store path_patterns as a list of skeleton lists,
each pattern carrying its accumulated set of distinct typed-triple
sequences distilled from same-signature trajectories that merged at
construction time. During offline merge (§ C.5 ), an
incoming skeleton not already present in the existing pattern’s skeleton
list is appended; the pattern’s running averages of usage count n n ,
mean F 1 F_{1} , and mean R IDR R_{\textsc{IDR}} are updated incrementally. Coverage at PAR computation becomes 𝒞 ⁡ ( τ , P ) = max skel ∈ P . skel ​ _ ​ list ⁡ | abs ⁡ ( τ ) ∩ skel | | skel | , \mathcal{C}(\tau,P)=\max_{\mathrm{skel}\in P.\mathrm{skel\_list}}\frac{|\mathrm{abs}(\tau)\cap\mathrm{skel}|}{|\mathrm{skel}|}, (7) i.e., the trajectory is rewarded for matching any of the pattern’s
known skeletons. C.5 Merge Threshold Sensitivity Patterns extracted from individual high-IDR trajectories are merged offline
into archetypes by clustering signature embeddings with a cosine-similarity
threshold θ merge \theta_{\mathrm{merge}} . Lower thresholds produce more, finer
archetypes; higher thresholds produce fewer, coarser archetypes that match a
broader range of queries. Empirically, the post-distillation archetype count
follows a Zipf-like distribution: the top archetype dominates with several
hundred instances while the tail decays sharply. We use θ merge = 0.85 \theta_{\mathrm{merge}}=0.85 throughout: this conservative threshold
keeps the merged archetype count low (under-merging) but yields stable patterns
whose embeddings are clustered tightly enough that the post-distillation 27 27 patterns each carry several hundred high-IDR trajectories on average. G.3 Inference Pipeline At deployment, given an input query q q , PatternBloom runs four steps (Figure 2 , bottom). (1) Signature extraction. The query signature σ ⁡ ( q ) \sigma(q) is extracted by the deterministic parser of § 3.3 and embedded into a BGE-large vector. (2) Pattern retrieval. The frozen ℳ 0 \mathcal{M}_{0} is queried via FAISS cosine retrieval; the top- 3 3 patterns whose signature similarity exceeds θ sim = 0.5 \theta_{\mathrm{sim}}=0.5 are formatted as a single ⟨ knowledge ⟩ \langle\textsf{knowledge}\rangle hint and prepended to the agent’s prompt. If no pattern clears θ sim \theta_{\mathrm{sim}} , no hint is injected and the agent proceeds with the standard multi-hop tool instructions in the system prompt. (3) Trajectory rollout. The deployed policy π θ 2 ∗ \pi_{\theta^{*}_{2}} performs an interleaved ⟨ think ⟩ \langle\textsf{think}\rangle / ⟨ query ⟩ \langle\textsf{query}\rangle / ⟨ graph ⟩ \langle\textsf{graph}\rangle / ⟨ answer ⟩ \langle\textsf{answer}\rangle rollout over the same hypergraph index H H used at training time; each ⟨ query ⟩ \langle\textsf{query}\rangle block triggers a retriever call that returns the top- k k matching hyperedges, and the agent accumulates 𝒢 \mathcal{G} across turns. (4) Answer extraction. The final ⟨ answer ⟩ \langle\textsf{answer}\rangle block is extracted as the model output. The oracle 𝒪 \mathcal{O} and gradient updates from training are not used at inference; the deployment cost is therefore equal to a standard agentic-RAG rollout plus a single FAISS lookup against the | ℳ 0 | |\mathcal{M}_{0}| -entry pattern memory. G.9 Human Study Form What each annotator was shown. For each of the 30 30 sampled queries, an annotator received a single rating sheet containing the following items, in this fixed order: • the original question text; • the gold reference answer (revealed up-front so the annotator could check whether each trajectory’s claims were grounded in retrieved evidence rather than in the gold); • the four method trajectories, each rendered as the unedited sequence of ⟨ think ⟩ \langle\textsf{think}\rangle , ⟨ query ⟩ \langle\textsf{query}\rangle , ⟨ graph ⟩ \langle\textsf{graph}\rangle , and ⟨ answer ⟩ \langle\textsf{answer}\rangle blocks emitted by the model on that query. Method identities were redacted to “Method A / B / C / D” on the rating sheet and the column order was independently shuffled per query to avoid presentation bias. Tables 10 , 11 , and 12 show three real trajectories in the same block-by-block format the annotators saw (with the redacted method labels restored for this paper’s exposition). Rating axes. For every trajectory the annotator assigned an independent 1 1 – 5 5 Likert score on each of the four axes below. The axis-level criteria reproduced on the rating sheet were: • Logical Coherence: does the reasoning chain follow a coherent logical structure, with each step justified by the preceding evidence? • Completeness: are all intermediate steps required by the question’s logical structure present in the trajectory? • Faithfulness: is the final answer grounded in the retrieved evidence, rather than fabricated or substituted from prior knowledge? • Pattern Reusability: does the trajectory rely on a reusable reasoning skeleton that would also apply to other queries of the same logical type, rather than ad-hoc per-query reasoning? Likert anchor descriptions. The same five anchors below are presented for every axis on the rating sheet, with the criterion name substituted into “the criterion”: • 1 1 – Very Poor: the trajectory fails the criterion on most reasoning steps (e.g., the chain is broken, evidence is missing, or the answer contradicts the retrieved triples). • 2 2 – Poor: the trajectory partially satisfies the criterion but contains a major gap (e.g., one critical hop is skipped, or one claim is fabricated). • 3 3 – Fair: the trajectory satisfies the criterion overall, with one or two minor issues that a careful reader would notice but that do not block the answer. • 4 4 – Good: the trajectory cleanly satisfies the criterion with no significant issues; minor stylistic differences are tolerable. • 5 5 – Excellent: the trajectory satisfies the criterion exceptionally well and could serve as a positive exemplar for that axis. Instructions and aggregation. Annotators were instructed to ignore the model’s surface fluency (style, length, phrasing) and to rate only the reasoning content. Ties on a given axis between two trajectories were permitted. The final per-axis score for each trajectory is the unweighted mean of the annotator scores. On the size of ℳ 0 \mathcal{M}_{0} . The learned memory contains only 27 27 patterns extracted from 14 14 K training queries, a 520 : 1 520{:}1 compression ratio. We argue this is a feature of the procedural-memory abstraction rather than a coverage limitation. Each pattern is a type-abstracted reasoning skeleton (e.g., lookup(entity) → \to ENTITY , compare(by[attr]) → \to ENTITY ) that subsumes an entire equivalence class of instance triples; the Reasoning Path Faithfulness (Table 3 , RPF = 0.886 =0.886 ) shows that 88.6 % 88.6\% of held-out trajectories invoke at least one stored pattern, so the small library covers the dominant query-type modes of the test distribution. Library size is therefore a property of how many distinct reasoning skeletons the data exercises, not a property of how many instance triples are stored, and an order-of-magnitude smaller library is consistent with the design intent. On the low type entropy. The paper-main run uses a six-class coarse type taxonomy under which entity carries 98 % 98\% of node occurrences, so H type = 0.19 H_{\mathrm{type}}=0.19 bits is close to the entropy floor of an entity-centric corpus. The number is therefore an artifact of the chosen taxonomy granularity, not of the patterns themselves: applying the fine-grained NER alternative (Appendix C.3 ) to the same 27 27 patterns raises H type H_{\mathrm{type}} to 1.84 1.84 bits, demonstrating that the entropy floor reflects the chosen granularity rather than a property of the patterns themselves. On the zero inverse-relation rate. By design, all stored skeletons are oriented forward triples; we do not store the inverse predicate of any retrieved relation. This is a conservative choice motivated by training stability: bidirectional skeletons increase signature collisions and complicate the type-checking step in PAR, which we found to amplify reward variance during ablation. The current 0 % 0\% inverse rate therefore reflects a design decision rather than a missing implementation, and adding bidirectional patterns is a controlled extension. Appendix I Discussion I.1 Why Externalizing Graph Patterns Helps Two observations from § 4 connect the empirical gains to the paradigm shift proposed in § 1 . First, the IDR reward at Stage I aligns the policy gradient with the conditional information gain I ⁡ ( 𝒢 ; a ∗ ∣ q ) I(\mathcal{G};a^{*}\mid q) rather than surface-form token overlap (Theorem 1 ), which prevents the verbose-but-correct graph reward-hacking mode that token-level F1 admits. Second, the Graph Pattern Memory at Stage II separates the procedural knowledge of which reasoning structure to apply from the declarative knowledge of which facts to retrieve. Since the same type-abstracted skeleton (e.g., [ film ] → directed_by [ person ] → nationality [ country ] [\textsc{film}]\!\xrightarrow{\texttt{directed\_by}}\![\textsc{person}]\!\xrightarrow{\texttt{nationality}}\![\textsc{country}] ) applies across thousands of film-director-country instances, pattern retrieval compounds with reasoning depth where content retrieval saturates, evidenced by the + 10.4 +10.4 EM gain on Bamboogle (4-hop) relative to HotpotQA (2-hop). I.2 Limitations On the empirical side, the procedural-memory mechanism transfers at the fixed Wikipedia-hyperedge substrate. Cross-domain transfer to non-Wikipedia corpora such as biomedical, legal, or financial documents lies outside the scope of the present paper and is the natural deployment-scale next benchmark. I.3 Broader Impact Positive. PatternBloom reduces sample complexity for retrieval-augmented reasoning agents (Stage I IDR converges on 14 14 K queries) and produces an inspectable pattern library that practitioners can audit, prune, or extend. The pattern library is human-readable (typed triples), unlike opaque parametric memory in end-to-end models. Negative. A poorly curated GPM , for example one that absorbed adversarial trajectories without basic format and quality filters, could systematically mislead downstream users. Production systems where GPM accumulates over weeks would benefit from an LLM-judge audit before admitting new skeletons. I.4 Future Work Cross-domain transfer. Type abstraction makes patterns domain-agnostic in principle, but our experiments cover only Wikipedia-grounded multi-hop QA. Whether ℳ \mathcal{M} trained on Wikipedia transfers zero-shot to medical (PubMedQA), legal, or financial QA without retraining is the natural next benchmark. Search-R1 and R1-Searcher use answer-only rewards, treating retrieval as a black-box action whose only feedback is downstream answer correctness. FrugalRAG, building on declarative LM compilation [ 49 ] , augments this with a budget term that penalizes excessive search hops, addressing efficiency rather than evidence quality. Graph-R1’s entity-presence proxy is the closest prior attempt to interrogate the constructed graph (it checks whether gold-answer entities appear in the graph), but the check is binary and grants the same reward to graphs that contain the gold entity buried among 20 20 other triples versus graphs that contain it among 3 3 relevant triples. PatternBloom’s IDR (Eq. 3 ) instead measures how much the constructed graph raises a frozen oracle’s likelihood of the gold answer per triple, providing a continuous, size-normalized signal that scales with reasoning depth. Memory across episodes. All five RL-for-RAG methods are memory-less across episodes: each query’s trajectory is generated fresh and dissolves at episode end, leaving no state that persists into the next query. Successful reasoning patterns are absorbed only into the policy’s parameters via gradient updates, where they are not directly inspectable, editable, or transferable. PatternBloom’s Graph Pattern Memory (§ 3.3 ) externalizes this procedural knowledge into a separate, human-readable, query-signature-indexed store that can be inspected, edited, and frozen for reproducibility, complementing trajectory-level techniques such as self-consistency [ 50 ] and the lifelong skill libraries explored by embodied LLM agents [ 51 ] .
