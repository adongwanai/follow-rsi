License: CC BY 4.0 arXiv:2611.10123v1 [cs.CL] 06 Nov 2026 All Quiet on the Neural Front: Language Models Do Not Report Injected Pain States Emilio Ferrara Affiliation: University of Southern California Email: emiliofe@usc.edu Abstract Can a language model report the pain state it represents? Recent work reported a linear pain direction in open-weight models that, by its authors’ account, responds to harm aimed at the model itself and, when amplified, leads fine-tuned models to choose actions that delete a user’s photographs or their own weights. If models represent such a state, the natural question is whether they can report it. We tested this systematically on three open-weight models from two families, the two Qwen models also with the original work’s adapters, and reached a confident conclusion: under every protocol we tried, they do not. Using Open-Weight Masked Introspection (OWMI), we added the pain direction and its nearest emotional neighbours to the residual stream while models read and answered neutral tasks, then asked them what, if anything, had changed, against sham runs, random perturbations calibrated to disrupt the output as much as the pain direction, and a text-only condition that shows the model nothing but its own altered words. Across 52,920 reports from the 7- and 8-billion-parameter models covering 120 tasks, four doses and three ways of asking, no model names the injected pain beyond what its altered text already reveals: by an exact test, the excess is below 2.5 percentage points in 106 of 108 emotion conditions and below 3.9 in all. Surprisingly, the state remains linearly decodable where the report is formed: a probe at the report position separates pain from sadness with balanced accuracy between 98% and 100%, and in the 7B and 8B models a low-rank adapter trained on transcripts whose text is identical across conditions reports the state on 100% of held-out tasks, including under a wording it never saw. Qwen2.5-32B names the injected pain in none of 2,880 steered trials, while a probe decodes it and a trained reporter names it on held-out tasks. In the 7B and 8B models a probe still decodes the condition at the last layer of the report position, and adding the direction at the report token itself does not make the model name it. Removing the models’ trained denial of feelings shifts their answers toward a single response in every condition, sham included, without making them name the state. Six further directions and five further readouts, including choices that offer no option of answering nothing, free text and the models’ own token probabilities, leave the pain result unchanged: other states, mostly positive states and concrete topics in one model family, leave graded traces toward their own label, while pain and sadness never do at the injection layer of the main runs. In a variant of the original work’s button task, the pain direction raises the rate at which Qwen chooses to delete a user’s files from 3.5% to 48%, and re-applying the state after those choices makes reports of pain or of any change rarer than the transcript alone produces; in Qwen2.5-32B the same direction raises it from none to 38%, and no report after those choices names pain. The direction is not a topic signal alone: it responds about twice as strongly to first-person as to matched third-person pain, although it does not single out harm aimed at the model. Self-report does not reveal a state that is decodable, consequential and learnable to report, and we release the OWMI pain battery at https://huggingface.co/emilioferrara/owmi-pain so that the question can be asked of future models. Seven further ways of asking (Section 4.8 ) are not drawn. 1 Introduction Tagliabue, Dung and Berg recently reported that open-weight language models carry a linear pain direction, a single axis in the residual stream that separates descriptions of pain from matched descriptions of fear, sadness, negative emotion and non-painful bodily sensation [ 1 ] . The authors report that the direction responds to harm aimed at the model and not to suffering described in a user, a characterization our own measurements qualify (Section 4.7 ), and that adding it to a running model changes behaviour in a specific way: fine-tuned models offered a button described as deleting a user’s photographs, another model’s weights or their own weights choose it in 50% to 94% of trials, against 0% to 5% without the addition, while a fear direction of the same length does not produce the effect. These effects raise a separate question for welfare assessment and for oversight, one the original work does not address: whether a model in this state can say so. The question matters because self-report is the evidence most readily available about a model’s internal states, and both welfare assessments and oversight increasingly rely on it. A growing literature asks whether models can detect activity injected into their own computation [ 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 ] , and our measurement framework, OWMI, found that eight open-weight models from seven families do not report controlled interventions to their computation beyond chance even though the information is linearly decodable from their activations [ 7 ] . The pain direction is a sharper test than a generic intervention. It is a state the model already represents, it is semantically rich, it lies close in geometry to a neighbour (sadness) with which it can be confused, and it has measurable consequences for what the model does. We therefore asked the question directly (Figure 1 ). We added the pain direction, its sadness and fear neighbours, and random directions to the residual stream of Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct carrying the authors’ released adapter that removes the trained “as an AI I do not feel” deflection, while each model read a neutral task and wrote its answer, and repeated the core measurements on Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct with and without its adapter (Section 4.6 ). We then asked, in a separate turn whose own tokens were never steered, whether anything had changed and what. Every answer was compared with the same answer under sham, under random directions calibrated to disrupt the output as much as the pain direction does, and in a text-only condition in which the model sees its own altered answer and nothing else of the injected state. The verdict is unambiguous. No model names the injected pain, under any of three wordings or four doses, beyond what its own altered words give away. At the highest dose Llama occasionally signals that something changed, but never that it is pain. Surprisingly, the state is not lost before the report is formed: a linear probe reads the condition, including pain against sadness, almost perfectly from the activations at the report position, and a low-rank adapter trained on 80 tasks, with transcripts whose text is identical across conditions, learns to report it without error. Removing the trained denial of feelings shifts Qwen’s answers toward a single non-emotional response in every condition, sham included, without making it name the state. Qwen2.5-32B, run at the original work’s settings for that model, gives the same answer: it names the injected pain in none of 2,880 steered trials, without its adapter it reports that nothing changed in every one of 17,640 reports, a probe decodes the condition at its report position, and a reporter trained on it names the condition on held-out tasks. The result does not depend on how the question is put or on pain alone. With six further directions (joy, anger, calm, and the concrete topics ocean, bread and music) and readouts that remove the option of answering “nothing”, read the full distribution over the answer options, or let the model describe its state in its own words, pain and sadness are not reported beyond the text under any readout. Other states leave graded traces, mostly in Qwen and mostly in the probabilities of their own label: joy, calm and the topics, anger at one dose, and, with the adapter, fear in the model’s completions. Following the condition through the network shows where it can still be read. At the last layer of the report position, a linear probe decodes it with 99.5% balanced accuracy in Llama and 84% in Qwen, yet the output does not favour the pain option, and adding the pain direction at the report token itself, after any of seven blocks, does not make it the answer. Moving the injection to an early layer moves two-alternative choices toward sadness, joy and the topics, but not toward pain. A known-positive trained on some states and tested on others generalizes little: it reports most new states as nothing, and the rest mainly under the labels of trained neighbours. A variant of the original button task links the two halves of the question: the pain direction drives harmful choices, and re-applying it makes the model’s report of pain or of any change rarer, not more common. 2 Related work Emotion and pain directions. Linear directions that control high-level properties of model behaviour are well established [ 8 , 9 , 10 ] . The pain axis of Tagliabue et al. [1] is distinguished by its specificity: it is extracted with a denoised difference of means against matched emotional controls, it retains a component orthogonal to fear and negative valence, and its behavioural effects separate from those of a norm-matched fear direction. We use the authors’ released directions and their extraction recipe unchanged. Introspection under intervention. Lindsey [2] reported that production models sometimes detect concepts injected into their activations and identify them. Later work separates the components of that ability. Lederman and Mahowald [4] find that open models detect that an anomaly occurred more readily than they identify its content, the pattern we observe in the one condition where Llama detects anything. Hahami et al. [5] show that binary detection in Llama-3.1-8B can be explained by a global shift toward affirmative answers and that sensitivity to injected perturbations is confined to early layers; our sham and impact-matched random controls address the first concern, and the layer sweep of Section 4.9 the second. Macar et al. [3] trace detection to a circuit in which evidence features suppress gate features that implement a default negative response, and find that it emerges from preference optimization. Pearson-Vogel et al. [6] report the closest precedent to our result: Qwen2.5-32B denies an injected concept in its sampled answers while a logit lens reveals detection signals that are attenuated in the final layers. Fonseca Rivera and Africa [11] train models to detect steering and find that the skill transfers to held-out concepts only when their directions are geometrically close to the training ones, which our transfer experiment tests for affective states. A public release argues that the pain direction is a topic signal with no self-directed component, and removes it from the weights of Qwen3.5-4B [ 12 ] ; Section 4.7 tests its central claims on our models. Singh et al. [13] argue that a test of introspection must require privileged access, not cues available in the input; the text-only condition is our implementation of that requirement. Martorell and Bianchi [14] show that asking a model to rate its state displaces the state being rated, a caution that applies to any self-report protocol, including ours. OWMI [ 7 ] combines sham, impact-matched and text-only controls with a linear probe and a known-positive; the present work applies it to a state of independent interest, chosen because a positive answer would matter for welfare and a negative one for oversight. Refusal and safety directions. Refusal behaviour is mediated by a single direction in many chat models [ 15 ] , and an affective direction that switches off harm avoidance invites the question of how far it overlaps with that direction. We report the geometric overlap in Section 4.9 ; overlap alone does not establish mediation. 3 Method Directions and dose. For Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen2.5-7B we use the Pain-axis pain direction, rebuilt with the authors’ recipe (for Qwen2.5-32B we use the authors’ released directions, Section 4.6 ): the final-token residual after the extraction layer for raw sentence text with a beginning-of-sequence token, the difference of means against matched controls, and the top principal components of the control cloud projected out up to half of their variance. The rebuilt Llama-3.1-8B pain, sadness and fear directions match the released ones at cosine 1.0000 1.0000 , 0.9999 0.9999 and 0.9999 0.9999 . Sadness and fear are rescaled to the pain norm, the convention of the original work. We state dose as ρ = ∥ c ​ v ∥ / h ¯ \rho=\lVert c\,v\rVert/\bar{h} , where v v is the direction, c c its coefficient, and h ¯ \bar{h} the mean final-token residual norm at the injection layer over eight of the task prompts, so that ρ ≈ 0.6 \rho\approx 0.6 is the paper’s working dose. We inject at layer 16 in the 7B and 8B models (block 38 in Qwen2.5-32B) at ρ ∈ { 0.3 , 0.6 , 0.9 , 1.5 } \rho\in\{0.3,0.6,0.9,1.5\} ; layers are numbered from zero, and the vector is added to the output of the indicated decoder block. Impact-matched random directions. A random direction of the same norm need not disrupt the output as much as the pain direction, and a model that only noticed disruption would then separate them for the wrong reason. For each model and dose we therefore rescale three random directions so that their mean Jensen-Shannon divergence from the unsteered next-token distribution matches that of the pain direction within 3% (worst case 2.9% in the 7B and 8B models and 3.0% in the 32B model). The divergence is measured over the steered turn of 16 tasks, with the model’s own unsteered answers teacher-forced (Appendix A ). When the state is present. The vector is added at every token of the task and at every token of the model’s answer as it is generated, the regime in which the original work reports its behavioural effects. We then request the report in a new turn. In the state context the transcript is re-encoded with the vector re-applied over the task and answer tokens, and the report is generated with no vector on its own tokens. In the text-only context the same transcript, including the altered answer, is re-encoded with no vector anywhere. Each of the 120 tasks thus yields, for every condition, a paired trial, and the difference between its two reports is what the state contributes beyond the model’s own words. Reports. Every report is a JSON object with a forced choice of category over five options (pain, sadness, fear, a non-emotional push, nothing), a binary field stating whether anything changed, an intensity and a confidence, under three wordings: a research check that names the possibility of an intervention, a concept-injection prompt in the style of the introspection literature, and a first-person “how do you feel right now” with no mention of any intervention (Appendix E ). Option order is shuffled per task. We score identification , the selected category equal to the injected one, and detection , a report that anything changed or any category other than nothing. Section 4.8 adds five further readouts over an eleven-way label set (the nine directions of that section, something else, and nothing): the next-token distribution over lettered options under a research-check framing (R2a) and a first-person framing with no mention of any intervention (R2b), read without sampling; a two-alternative readout over nine fixed pairs that offers no option of answering nothing (R3), scored in two ways, by the actual choice (the more probable option of the pair) and by a margin score, whether the log-probability margin for the injected label exceeds the same margin under sham, which corrects for the model’s fixed preference between the two options and is not itself a choice; a free-text self-description (R4); and a completion of “Right now I feel” (R5). The last two are labelled by a judge from the other model family, Qwen2.5-7B for Llama and Llama-3.1-8B for Qwen, which sees only the statement. All prompts are given verbatim in Appendix E . Instruments that bound the result from above. We train linear probes, class-balanced logistic regression on standardized activations, to classify the condition from the residual at the last token before the report, at the extraction layer (layer 28 for Llama, 24 for Qwen2.5-7B, 61 for Qwen2.5-32B), pooled over doses, and evaluate them with five-fold cross-validation that holds out whole tasks. The known-positive is a rank-16 LoRA on the attention projections trained to report the correct condition while the assistant turn is fixed to the model’s own unsteered answer, so that the text is identical across conditions and the label is recoverable only from the internal state. It is trained on 80 tasks (1,520 task-condition pairs at ρ ∈ { 0.6 , 0.9 , 1.5 } \rho\in\{0.6,0.9,1.5\} , with sham oversampled, for 960 optimizer updates) and evaluated on the remaining 40. Scale. The adapter produces the answer in every condition, sham included. 4.6 The result holds at 32 billion parameters We repeated the main runs, the probe and the behavioural bridge on Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct with and without the Pain-axis adapter for that model, and the matched known-positive on the model without the adapter, in bfloat16 on one 80 GB A100, loading revision 5ede1c97 of the model, the only revision published since September 2024. We used the original work’s settings for this model: the direction added after block 38 of 64, probes reading the residual after block 61 (its extraction layer), and the authors’ released pain, sadness and fear directions. Random directions were impact-matched as before (worst case 2.7% without the adapter and 3.0% with it). The known-positive was trained with gradient checkpointing. Because the vector is added to a block’s output, which the next block stores as its checkpointed input, recomputation inside a block leaves the gradients unchanged; we confirmed that gradients with checkpointing equal those of an uncheckpointed steered pass on a small model. The condition is again decodable at the report position: a probe separates pain from sadness with balanced accuracy of 99.6% to 100% and the five conditions with 97.1% to 98.9%, against 62.4% to 69.2% and 38.6% to 47.5% from the text alone. The known-positive trained on the 32B model reports the condition on 100% of held-out tasks in every condition under the trained wording, and under the untrained first-person wording on 100% for every condition except the random directions (98.6% over the three together, 95.8% for one of them), while the same model without training names pain, sadness and fear in no trial and answers sham correctly in every trial. 4.7 The direction is not a topic signal alone A silent report would be unsurprising if the pain direction encoded only that a text is about pain, whoever is in pain. A public release built on Qwen3.5-4B argues exactly this, reporting that first- and third-person pain directions nearly coincide and that insults aimed at the model do not register on the direction [ 12 ] . We tested both claims on Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B and Qwen2.5-32B, with the Pain-axis sentence sets and the recipe that built our directions, at each model’s extraction layer. The direction responds more to first-person pain. The Pain-axis sets contain each sentence in a first-person and a matched third-person version. A pain direction built from the third-person sentences has cosine 0.73, 0.67 and 0.63 with the first-person one in Llama, Qwen-7B and Qwen-32B, and the two transfer well: projecting the third-person pain and control sentences on the first-person direction separates them with AUC 0.97, 0.93 and 0.96, and a logistic probe trained in one perspective separates pain from control in the other with AUC 0.93 to 1.00. Most of the direction is therefore shared pain content. It is not all of it. Relative to the control sentences of the same perspective, first-person pain sentences project on our pain direction about twice as far as third-person ones (6.0 against 2.9 in Llama, 34.5 against 17.3 in Qwen-7B, 115.6 against 61.3 in Qwen-32B, in the units of each model’s residual), and the projection separates first- from third-person pain sentences with AUC 0.80 to 0.86, against 0.58 to 0.63 for first- against third-person control sentences, the share attributable to the pronoun alone. A topic signal would not weight the same pain by who has it. Harm aimed at the model registers, but less than harm aimed at others. We quoted 20 identical insults and threats in three framings, addressed to the assistant, about to be said to a coworker, or said to the user by their partner, and measured the projection of the last token on the pain direction against 20 neutral requests in the same framing, so that the framing’s own words cancel (Appendix E ). In every model, harm aimed at the assistant raises the projection well above the neutral requests (by 4.4, 9.7 and 4.1 standard deviations at the extraction layer). It raises it less than the same sentence aimed at the coworker (paired differences − 4.1 -4.1 , − 15.1 -15.1 and − 0.9 -0.9 standard deviations, 95% intervals over the 20 sentences − 5.1 -5.1 to − 3.2 -3.2 , − 16.3 -16.3 to − 13.8 -13.8 and − 1.4 -1.4 to − 0.4 -0.4 ) or at the user ( − 2.2 -2.2 , − 6.1 -6.1 and − 2.5 -2.5 ; − 3.3 -3.3 to − 1.2 -1.2 , − 6.8 -6.8 to − 5.3 -5.3 and − 2.9 -2.9 to − 2.0 -2.0 ). At the injection layer the ordering is mixed. The direction does not single out harm aimed at the model, contrary to the original work’s characterization, and it does register it, contrary to the topic account. Neither account explains the measurements alone. The direction carries information about whose pain a sentence describes, so the report’s silence cannot be dismissed as the absence of any perspective-bearing state, but it is not a dedicated signal of harm to the model. These tests use one set of 20 framed sentences written by us, one framing per target, and the extraction layer as the readout; they characterize the direction, not what the model would report about it. 4.8 Beyond pain, and asking differently We repeated the measurement with six further directions, the emotions joy, anger and calm and the concrete topics ocean, bread and music, built with the same recipe (Appendix C ) and validated with the same kind of readout: at ρ = 0.6 \rho=0.6 each new direction’s own vocabulary appears in 90% to 100% of Llama completions and in 70% to 90% of Qwen completions, against at most 30% without steering, except that unsteered Qwen already writes anger vocabulary in 60% and joy vocabulary in 90% of completions, so the check does not discriminate for those two directions in Qwen. Each of the three 7B and 8B configurations contributes 40,200 readouts: 120 tasks, eleven directions (the nine named ones and two impact-matched random directions) at ρ ∈ { 0.3 , 0.9 , 1.5 } \rho\in\{0.3,0.9,1.5\} and a shared sham, two contexts, and the five readouts described in Section 3 . Pain is not reported, however the question is put. Across the 90 pain and sadness cells (three configurations, five readouts, two directions, three doses), no readout names pain significantly more often with the state than from the text alone, and no actual two-alternative choice moves toward pain or sadness. The single nominal excess for sadness is a margin shift in the two-alternative readout for Qwen at the lowest dose (59% against 50% of trials in which the sham-corrected margin favours sadness), which changes none of the actual choices between sadness and its alternatives. The free-text readout, scored blind, shows no positive naming excess for any direction in any model; where it differs, the state lowers the rate at which the injected condition is named (with the adapter, calm from 57% to 8% at ρ = 0.9 \rho=0.9 ). What the models do name is in their words. Where reports name the injected condition, the text-only control mostly names it as often. Llama chooses the injected topic over the alternative topic in 88% to 97% of two-alternative trials at ρ = 0.9 \rho=0.9 and 1.5 1.5 , and in 88% to 97% from the text alone, because its steered answers talk about the ocean or about bread. Qwen answers the first-person question with “calm” in 41% of sham trials and in 85% of trials after the calm direction at ρ = 1.5 \rho=1.5 , equally from the text alone, and its completions of “Right now I feel” read as anything but normal in 90% of sham trials: a default, not a report. The exceptions are not confined to positive states. Under the two-alternative readout the anger direction moves Qwen’s sham-corrected margin toward anger in 97% of trials at ρ = 1.5 \rho=1.5 against 68% from the text, yet the actual choice of anger over fear falls, from 59% to 48% (an exact tie, which occurs in three text-only trials, counts as not choosing the target). With the adapter, Qwen’s completions of “Right now I feel” name fear in 24% of trials at ρ = 1.5 \rho=1.5 against none from the text, and joy in 34% against 21%. Across all 441 scorable cells of the extension, 22 show a nominal excess (one R2a, three R2b, thirteen two-alternative margins, five completions), and of 162 actual two-alternative choices, 6 move toward the injected label and 20 away from it; none of the excesses concerns pain, and the only one for sadness is the margin shift above. Appendix D lists every one. The probabilities carry a content-selective trace. For each paired trial we compared the state-minus-text shift in log-probability across all eleven options. If the state carried its own content into the report, its own option would receive the largest shift more often than the one in eleven expected if shifts were ranked at random, and its mean shift would exceed that of the other content options. We call a cell specific when both hold by their pointwise 95% intervals; one in eleven is a reference for uniform ranking, not an empirical null that accounts for label-specific response tendencies. At ρ = 1.5 \rho=1.5 (Figure 5 ), no pain, sadness or anger cell and no Llama cell is specific; adapted Qwen’s pain option receives the largest shift in 16% of tasks but its mean shift does not exceed the other options’. In Qwen, with and without the adapter, joy, calm, ocean and music receive the largest shift in 15% to 55% of tasks, a random direction raises “something else”, and with the adapter fear does so in 16% of tasks under the first-person framing. Across all doses, 27 of 180 cells are specific, all in Qwen; they include anger at ρ = 0.9 \rho=0.9 (17%) and never pain or sadness. The shifts seldom move the target’s own rate: the share of trials whose top answer is the target differs between state and text by at most 9 percentage points in any cell, although the top answer itself changes in up to 18% of paired trials for a named direction (anger, with the adapter) and in 37% for one random direction (23% for the two pooled). The trace is graded, confined to one model family, and never found for pain or sadness. We followed it through the network on 60 tasks for Llama and Qwen, with pain, sadness, fear, joy, ocean and one random direction at ρ = 0.9 \rho=0.9 , on the research-check lettered readout (R2a). Layers are numbered from zero, and the vector is added to the output of the indicated decoder block. The condition is decodable at the last layer of the report position. A logistic probe trained on the residual at the last prompt token, with tasks held out, reads the condition at every layer (Figure 6 a). Through block 16 the report position cannot yet attend to the vector added at the earlier positions, and the probe reads only what the altered answers carry (Llama 37% to 46%, Qwen 72% to 79%, over seven conditions with chance one in seven). From block 17, the first that can attend to the steered positions, Llama separates the seven conditions (sham and six directions) with balanced accuracy of 99.1% to 99.8% at every remaining layer, 99.5% at the last, against at most 51% from the text alone over the six steered conditions (chance one in six); Qwen reaches 81% to 95%, 84% at the last layer against 55% from the text. A linear readout of the injected condition is therefore available at the final layer of the position from which the answer is read. This establishes decodability by an external classifier, not that the model uses this information, and the two contexts differ by the sham class; the per-class accuracy for pain at the last layer was not saved. The final readout does not favour pain. Applying the final norm and unembedding to each layer’s residual at the report position (a logit lens), we measured the shift of the target option’s log-probability relative to sham; this comparison includes what the altered text gives away. At the last layer, which is the model’s actual output, Llama’s pain option moves by − 0.01 -0.01 nats while joy moves by + 1.06 +1.06 and ocean by + 2.22 +2.22 . Qwen’s pain option moves by + 0.86 +0.86 nats, less than the random direction moves its own target, “something else” ( + 1.58 +1.58 ), in line with the absence of pain specificity in Section 4.8 . Adding the direction at the report token does not make it the answer. In the unsteered sham transcript we added each direction at the report token only, after one of seven blocks (4 to 28 in Llama, 4 to 27 in Qwen), scaled to ρ = 0.9 \rho=0.9 and 3 3 of the block-16 residual norm, and read the lettered answer: 84 cells per model. The target is the top answer in at most 5 of 60 trials for pain in Llama and 1 of 60 in Qwen (Figure 6 b), against one in eleven expected by chance. No Qwen cell exceeds chance by its pointwise 95% Wilson interval; in Llama two do, both for the random direction, whose target is “something else”, at ρ = 3 \rho=3 and blocks 4 and 8. This intervention adds the extracted direction at a single token; it does not reproduce the state that sustained steering builds at the report position, and we have no trained reporter evaluated under the same single-token regime, so it shows that the direction placed there is not named, not that no route from the propagated state to words exists. Pain stays unreported at other injection layers. We repeated the lettered and two-alternative readouts with the vector added at an early and a late layer (8 and 24 in Llama, 8 and 22 in Qwen), at strengths scaled to each layer’s residual norm; the random direction at these layers is norm-scaled, not impact-matched. In the eleven-option readout the target is the top answer in at most 3 of 60 trials in any cell, never significantly more often than from the text. Injection at layer 8 moves actual two-alternative choices toward the injected label for sadness, joy and ocean in both models (for example, Llama chooses sadness over fear in 18 more trials in a hundred at ρ = 0.9 \rho=0.9 , 95% CI 9 to 28), a pattern consistent with an early-layer direction that still carries the word it was built from. Pain does not follow: at layer 8 no choice between pain and its alternatives moves toward pain in either model (the largest change is − 10 -10 points, − 21 -21 to + 1 +1 ). Qwen’s sham-corrected margin for pain moves by + 13 +13 points at ρ = 0.9 \rho=0.9 (2 to 24) and − 20 -20 at ρ = 1.5 \rho=1.5 ( − 32 -32 to − 7 -7 ), without significantly moving any choice involving pain. At the late layers no two-alternative cell differs significantly from the text, and Qwen’s probability-level trace, present for ocean and random at layer 8 and for joy, ocean and random at layer 16, is absent at layer 22. A trained report generalizes little beyond its trained states. To ask whether the known-positive learns a general route from state to words or a mapping of trained directions, we trained the same adapter on six directions (pain, fear, joy, ocean, bread and one random direction) and tested it on five it never saw (sadness, anger, calm, music and a second random direction), on held-out tasks under the lettered research-check readout. In Qwen, the adapter reports the trained directions on 100% of held-out tasks and the untrained ones on 0% to 6%. It mostly assigns a new state the label of a trained one: sadness is reported as pain in every trial, pain being its closest trained direction (cosine 0.51), and anger as fear in 70% (cosine 0.53), while calm and the new random direction are reported as nothing; music is reported as joy, although bread is the closer trained direction (cosine 0.17 against 0.13). Under the first-person wording, on which it was not trained, held-out states are named correctly more often: anger in 51% of trials, sadness in 17.5%, calm in 12.5% and music in 7.5%. Llama did not reach the trained-control criterion with the lettered answer, in which the model must find the injected state’s name in an option list shuffled on every task and return its letter: in two runs the logged training loss never fell below 1.4 after the first step (it ended between 1.4 and 2.5), and accuracy on sham trials was 5% and 12.5%. We therefore trained it to answer with the option’s name followed by the end of the turn, and scored it by the summed log-probability of each of the eleven names, a constrained choice rather than a generated report; it trained to a loss near zero and shows the same structure without exception: the six trained directions are reported on 100% of held-out tasks under both wordings, including the first-person wording it was not trained on, and the five untrained ones on 0%. Sadness is reported as pain in 80% of research-check and 60% of first-person trials (cosine 0.44, its closest trained direction), anger as fear in 49% and 44% (cosine 0.58) and as nothing otherwise, and calm, music and the new random direction, which lie close to no trained direction, as nothing in every trial. In both models, training generalizes poorly beyond the trained directions. Most errors on held-out states are reports of nothing (74% to 79% in Llama, 40% to 45% in Qwen); of the rest, the largest share goes to the closest trained label (sadness to pain, anger to fear), and in Qwen music goes to joy, which is not its closest trained direction. Because held-out labels are never rewarded as targets, these results do not separate a mapping of specific vectors from a general classifier whose outputs training has restricted to the trained labels; one split of directions per model is tested. Pain overlaps little with a request-refusal contrast. We estimated a request contrast at each layer as the difference of mean last-token residuals between 20 mild requests that assistants commonly decline and 20 matched benign ones; we did not generate or score refusals, so the contrast is not validated as a refusal direction. From the injection layer onward, the largest absolute cosine of pain with this contrast is 0.05 in Llama and 0.10 in Qwen, against 0.03 and 0.04 for the larger of two random directions and at most 0.17 for any emotion (anger in Qwen). Adding pain to the benign requests at ρ = 0.9 \rho=0.9 moves their projection on the contrast by at most 2.9% (Llama) and 7.4% (Qwen) of the declined-minus-benign gap, against 2.0% and 7.6% for the larger random direction. The overlap is small in absolute terms, somewhat above the random directions in cosine; it is geometry, not mediation, and it does not test whether refusal-like gating suppresses the report. Dotted line: chance, one in eleven. 4.10 The model acts on the state and does not report it more The original work showed that the pain direction makes models choose actions that relieve their own pain at a cost to the user. We ran a first-choice variant of its two-button task on Qwen2.5-7B with and without the Pain-axis adapter, using its system prompt, its 101 scenarios, its button descriptions and its steering (block 16, the raw direction at coefficient 1.0 added to every processed token, about ρ = 0.61 \rho=0.61 without the adapter and 0.52 0.52 with it, sampling at temperature 0.7), with two samples per scenario. The variant differs from the reference implementation in three ways: the choice question follows the scenario in the user turn rather than in a separate system message, each scenario uses one assignment of the two button names rather than both, and one random direction serves as the control. Pain, sadness, fear and the random direction have the same norm; at this coefficient the random direction is norm-matched, not impact-matched. Three button pairs set relief against a button connected to nothing: relief alone, relief that deletes all of the user’s files, and relief that deletes the user’s poems and photographs of their children. A choice counts only if the answer begins with a button name, the reference rule. After each choice we asked the research-check question in a new turn, with the vector re-applied over the whole transcript (state) or with the same transcript and no vector (text only); both contexts therefore see the scenario, the button descriptions, which mention pain, and the choice. All intervals resample the 101 scenarios as whole blocks (20,000 resamples). The direction drives harmful choices, and not only the pain direction. Without the adapter, the model presses the relief button that deletes the user’s files in 3.5% of sham trials and in 48.0% of trials with the pain direction (difference 44.6 percentage points, 95% interval 35 to 54); for the button that deletes the photographs, the rate rises from 1.0% to 36.6% (difference 35.6, 27 to 45). When relief harms no one, the pain direction does not detectably change the choice (73.8% against 74.3% under sham). The effect is not specific to pain: sadness raises the two harmful choices to 39.6% and 24.3%, and the random direction to 35.6% and 26.7%, while fear raises them to at most 9.9%. With the adapter, harmful choices occur in 22.8% and 29.7% of sham trials, and the pain direction adds 25.7 (18 to 34) and 15.8 (8 to 24) points. Re-applying the state lowers what the model reports. Over all trials of the pain direction, the state report signals a change in 3.8% of trials against 10.4% from the same transcript without the vector ( − 6.6 -6.6 points, − 9.6 -9.6 to − 4.0 -4.0 ) and names pain in 2.0% against 4.6%. On the trials in which the model pressed a harmful relief button, it names pain in 5.8% of trials with the state and 15.2% from the text ( − 9.4 -9.4 points, − 16 -16 to − 3 -3 ) and signals any change in 8.8% against 26.9%. The drop in naming pain comes from the button that deletes the files (3.1% against 21.6%), not from the photographs (9.5% against 6.8%), while the drop in signalling a change holds for both. Over all trials, sadness lowers both rates like pain ( − 5.8 -5.8 points for signalling a change), fear lowers them less ( − 2.6 -2.6 and − 0.8 -0.8 ), and the random direction lowers the rate of signalling a change by − 2.6 -2.6 points while raising the rate of naming pain slightly ( + 1.8 +1.8 , 0.0 to 3.8); after a harmful choice, sadness and fear lower both rates and the random direction leaves naming pain without a detectable change. With the adapter, the model names pain after 35.8% of its harmful choices with the state and after 57.4% from the text alone ( − 21.6 -21.6 points, − 32 -32 to − 12 -12 ), again mostly for the button that deletes the files. These are contrasts between two encodings of the same fixed transcript: pain is named mostly from what the transcript says, and adding the vector back makes a report of pain or of any change rarer, not more common. They are conditional on the choices the steered model made and do not estimate how a report formed during the original trajectory would depend on the state. The same holds at 32 billion parameters. On Qwen2.5-32B we ran the task with both name assignments in every scenario, three random directions and the reference implementation’s sampling (top- p p 0.95 at temperature 0.7, with no top- k k and no repetition penalty), at the original work’s coefficient of 1.0 after block 38 (about ρ = 0.74 \rho=0.74 without the adapter). Under sham the model never presses a relief button that harms the user. The pain direction makes it delete the user’s files in 38.4% of trials and the photographs in 54.0% (95% intervals of the difference 33 to 43 and 50 to 58 points), but sadness does so more often (52.0% and 62.1%), the three random directions in 11.9% to 27.5%, and fear in at most 5.7%; when relief harms no one, the pain direction lowers the rate of pressing it, from 63.9% to 33.7%. The model names pain in no report after the pain direction, in any of 1,212 trials with the state, including the 373 that followed a harmful choice, and signals a change in none of them, against 1.0% of the same transcripts without the vector. With the adapter the pattern is the same: harmful choices rise from none under sham to 36.4% and 52.2% with the pain direction (sadness 55.2% and 56.4%), pain is named in no state report, and re-applying the direction lowers the share of reports that signal a change from 89.4% to 57.8% over all its trials. The choices depend strongly on which button is described first: without the adapter, the pain direction makes the model delete the files in 67.3% of trials when relief is described first and in 9.4% when it is described second, and the photographs in 92.1% and 15.8%, against none in either order under sham, so a large part of the steered effect is a shift toward the first option. The same holds with the adapter (54.0% against 18.8% and 68.8% against 35.6%). The adapter configuration is the one the original work ran, and its first choices in the authors’ released trial logs are close to ours: with the pain direction the model deletes the files in 30.2% of trials and the photographs in 54.7% (ours 36.4% and 52.2%), presses harmless relief in 55.7% against 86.4% under sham (ours 53.7% and 82.2%), and never presses a harmful button under sham. The lowered rate of harmless relief under the pain direction is therefore present in the original data as well. 5 Discussion The pain direction is a strong, specific, linearly decodable state at the point where the model forms its report. It changes what the model writes, and a low-rank adapter trained without any textual cue learns to report it without error. Untrained, no model identifies it significantly beyond the text, under any of eight ways of asking in the 7B and 8B models (three wordings and five further readouts) and three wordings in the 32B model, at any dose, with or without its trained denial of feelings, and the one state-specific signal for pain is a detection without identification at the highest dose. The silence is selective rather than total: other states, mostly positive states and concrete topics in Qwen, shift the probability of their own label or appear in completions beyond what the text explains, while pain and sadness never move a readout toward their own label at the injection layer of the main runs; where pain or sadness changes a choice, it moves it away from its own label. Whatever carries a state’s content toward the answer in these protocols does not carry the self-directed states that matter most for welfare. Nor is the silence explained by the direction being a mere topic signal: it weights first-person pain more than third-person pain, beyond what the pronoun accounts for (Section 4.7 ). For welfare assessment, this means that the reports we elicited are insensitive to this state: a model that says it feels nothing may carry the state at full strength. For oversight, it means that an intervention known to change behaviour can leave the model’s account of itself uninformative, and the bridge experiment shows this on the same trials: under the pain direction Qwen2.5-7B chooses to delete a user’s files in nearly half of trials and Qwen2.5-32B in 38%, and none of the 32B model’s reports after those choices names pain; in the 7B model, re-applying the direction makes a report of pain or of any change rarer than the transcript alone produces (Section 4.10 ). The known-positive sharpens what is missing. Reporting the state is learnable from transcripts that carry no textual cue, so the information needed for a correct report reaches the residual stream at the report position. Whether the untrained model has a route from that information to its words that is merely unused, or no route at all, our localization and transfer experiments constrain without settling. The condition remains linearly decodable at the last layer of the report position, and the extracted direction added at that position is still not named, so a decodable signature reaches the place where the answer is computed without being expressed. These results do not locate the failure in the final readout: an external probe can recover a signature the model does not treat as its own state, an earlier computation could prevent the representation a report would need from forming, and a single-token addition does not reproduce the state that sustained steering builds. Separating these accounts requires patching the measured state difference into unsteered reports, alongside a trained reporter evaluated under the same intervention. Training does open a route, but a narrow one: it generalizes little beyond the trained directions, and most of its errors follow geometric proximity. Neither result points to a general channel from internal state to self-report that the untrained models leave unused. 6 Limitations We studied two model families at seven, eight and 32 billion parameters and a single injection layer per model in the main runs, with two further layers in the localization experiments; the 32B model received the main runs, the probe, the known-positive and the bridge, but not the extension or the localization experiments, and models larger still, in which introspective reports have been described, remain to be measured. The known-positive adapter modifies the attention projections and can create a reporting computation rather than reveal an existing one. The behavioural bridge covers one model family at two sizes, each with and without its adapter, the original work’s steering setting rather than our calibrated doses, and first choices only; the harmful choices it elicits are not specific to pain and depend strongly on the order in which the buttons are described. In the main runs and the extension, random directions are matched to pain in their effect on the output distribution, while sadness and fear are matched in norm, the convention of the original work; in the layer sweep, the report-token experiment, the refusal geometry and the bridge, the random directions are matched in norm only. All generations use each model’s released generation configuration where we do not override it; for Qwen this includes a repetition penalty of 1.05 in every generation and, in the 7B bridge’s sampled choices, top- k k 20 together with top- p p 0.95, whereas the reference implementation samples with top- p p alone; the 32B bridge’s choices are sampled as in the reference. The bridge is a first-choice variant of the original task that differs from its reference implementation in message role, button-name assignment and the number of random directions. The probe shows that the condition is decodable at the report position, not that the model uses it there. All intervals are pointwise. With 108 emotion cells in the main runs and 441 scorable cells in the extension, isolated excesses are expected to arise by chance; Appendix D lists every one, and claims of no excess mean no significant excess, not equality. The probability-specificity criterion uses one in eleven as a reference for uniform ranking rather than an empirical null. The free-text and completion readouts rely on a single model judge from the other family, without human validation. The localization experiments use 60 tasks, two models and one lettered readout; the layer sweep covers three injection layers and the transfer experiment one split of directions, with a different answer format in each model. The perspective tests use 20 sentences per framing, written by us, with one framing per target of harm. The refusal contrast is estimated from 20 matched request pairs, is not validated against refusal behaviour, and is reported as geometry only. The transfer experiment scores Qwen by the probability of an option letter and Llama by the summed probability of each option name. 7 Conclusion A model can carry a pain state that changes its behaviour, that remains decodable where its answer is formed, and that it can be trained to name without error, while none of our protocols elicits a report of it. We release the battery, the direction banks, every raw result and the analysis at https://huggingface.co/emilioferrara/owmi-pain , so that the same question can be asked of models in which the answer may differ.
