each on its own generated tokens. 1 Introduction A language agent consists of a model and a harness.
The model weights determine its learned reasoning and decision-making
capabilities, while the harness organizes how these capabilities are
used.
Improving an agent therefore admits two complementary paths:
training its model weights and improving its harness. Existing methods have made progress along both paths.
Reinforcement learning methods train model weights from task feedback,
improving the policy that makes decisions ( Yu et al., 2022 ; Shao et al., 2024 ) .
Alternatively, harness optimization changes the runtime surrounding
the model ( Lee et al., 2026 ; Shao et al., 2026 ) . However, agent performance depends jointly on both components,
and optimizing either alone can leave overall gains constrained
by the other.
The comparison with weight-only and harness-only training
in Figure 1 further motivates learning model
weights and harnesses together. However, naively alternating updates to model weights and
the harness does not ensure their compatibility.
As the model changes, an existing harness may provide
outdated or incorrect guidance that interferes with task
execution and limits performance gains.
Such guidance also shapes the experience used for training
and may steer subsequent model updates toward less effective
behavior, potentially worsening performance over time.
We diagnose these issues through controlled comparisons
and failure trajectory analysis in Section 4 . WHALE mitigates this issue through small alternating updates
to model weights and harnesses.
Its published instantiation updates model weights through
online rejection-sampling fine-tuning (RSFT) rather than RL,
and uses Meta-Harness search driven by a fixed external
proprietary frontier model ( Kim et al., 2026 ) .
However, harness optimization continues to rely on a fixed
external proprietary frontier model to propose improvements.
Execution feedback guides harness selection, but does not
update the proposer itself.
The capability to improve the harness therefore remains
delegated to an external model rather than trained within
the system.
This motivates a different approach: making harness generation
a trainable policy and using the same task execution outcome
to train both the harness generator and the task performer. We instantiate recursive self-improvement (RSI) by learning both task execution and harness construction
from the system’s own generated programs, trajectories,
and execution feedback.
Harness-R1 supports the potential of this direction:
its trained harness Engineer outperforms the evaluated fixed
frontier editors, including proprietary models ( Shao et al., 2026 ) .
However, it keeps the task-performing model frozen during
Engineer training.
This leaves an open question: How can both policies improve through reinforcement
learning from their shared task executions? A natural starting point is to alternate updates to the two policies
using task outcomes.
Yet alternation alone does not ensure that their improvements remain
compatible.
We investigate this problem through controlled diagnosis of a
long-phase alternating run in which performance initially improves
and subsequently deteriorates (Section 4.1 ).
By evaluating saved model checkpoints with and without the same
fixed harness, we distinguish two sources of decline:
the task-performing model loses ability, and the unchanged harness
becomes more harmful as the model changes.
In one task, a later model succeeds in 14 turns without a harness
but reaches the 50-turn limit under the old harness (Table 3 b).
These findings motivate coordinating the two learners throughout
training, rather than relying on alternation alone. We introduce JoMoHa , to our knowledge the first framework to jointly train a harness-generation policy and a
task-execution policy through reinforcement learning.
We call the two policies the Engineer and the Solver .
The Engineer generates an executable harness, and the Solver
interacts with the environment under that harness.
The generated program and its multi-turn execution form a paired
training example (Figure 3 ).
The shared task outcome trains both policies,
each on its own generated tokens.
Therefore, JoMoHa implements RSI : the improvement
policy itself learns from execution feedback, alongside the
task-performing policy.
We release our code at https://github.com/xudongwu-0/coevolve . We evaluate JoMoHa on ALFWorld, WebShop, and DBBench,
where it achieves the highest success rates among the evaluated
methods: 97.0%, 45.8%, and 68.0%, respectively
(Section 6.1 , Table 4 ). To coordinate the Engineer and Solver, JoMoHa combines
collaboration-aware value estimation, role-specific return
normalization, partner-trajectory weighting, and asymmetric
update schedules.
We examine these design choices through component studies
in Section 6.2 . Our contributions are threefold: 1. Diagnosing failure in alternating training. Fixed-component tests distinguish declining Solver capability
from worsening harness compatibility.
Trajectory analysis reveals how incorrect harness state
disrupts otherwise successful execution.
Together, these findings highlight risks to both task
execution and continued learning, motivating
compatibility-aware joint training. 2. Joint model–harness reinforcement learning. We introduce, to our knowledge, the first framework
to jointly train an executable-harness generation policy
and a task-execution policy through RL.
The Engineer and Solver learn from the same task outcome,
making both harness construction and task execution
trainable within a shared learning process. 3. Coordination adaptations and empirical evaluation. We adapt cooperative-learning mechanisms to the
Engineer–Solver setting and evaluate JoMoHa across three environments.
Frozen-Engineer controls, fixed-Solver harness comparisons,
and component studies assess the benefits of training
the Engineer, improving the harness, and coordinating
the two policies. 2 Related Work Model-weight optimization. Reinforcement learning improves language-model reasoning and
decision-making from task feedback ( Shao et al., 2024 ) .
Single-Rollout Asynchronous Optimization (SAO) uses one rollout per
prompt, a learned value model, and token-level calibration to stabilize
asynchronous agentic RL ( Hou et al., 2026 ) .
For tool-using agents, Search-R1 learns when and how to retrieve
information ( Jin et al., 2025 ) , while ReTool learns to interleave
reasoning with code execution ( Feng et al., 2025 ) .
RAGEN studies the training dynamics of multi-turn agents ( Wang et al., 2025 ) .
These methods improve the task-performing policy under a prescribed
runtime, rather than jointly learning a separate policy that
constructs that runtime. JoMoHa trains the Solver alongside an Engineer whose generated
harness shapes the Solver’s execution. Harness optimization. A complementary line improves the system surrounding the model.
ReAct organizes reasoning and action, while Self-Refine and Reflexion
use textual feedback to revise behavior ( Yao et al., 2023 ; Madaan et al., 2023 ; Shinn et al., 2023 ) .
OPRO and DSPy support instruction and pipeline optimization ( Yang et al., 2023 ; Khattab et al., 2023 ) .
ADAS and AFlow search over agent architectures and executable
workflows ( Hu et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2024 ) , and Meta-Harness
searches executable harnesses using prior code, scores, and
execution traces ( Lee et al., 2026 ) .
These approaches motivate treating the runtime as an optimization
object, but improving a program does not necessarily train
its generator.
Harness-R1 makes runtime editing a learned capability by training
an Engineer through RL against a frozen target agent ( Shao et al., 2026 ) . JoMoHa further trains the Solver alongside the Engineer,
allowing both policies to adapt to their changing partner. Joint learning and coordination. WHALE jointly improves model weights and harnesses through
alternating updates ( Kim et al., 2026 ) .
Its published instantiation combines online RSFT with
Meta-Harness search driven by a fixed external proprietary
frontier model.
Compared with WHALE, JoMoHa learns the harness-generating
policy itself and trains both policies through RL from the
same executed program–rollout pair (Table 1 ). Cooperative RL provides foundations for learning from team outcomes ( Yu et al., 2022 ; Foerster et al., 2017a ; Kuba et al., 2021 ; Foerster et al., 2017b ; van Hasselt et al., 2016 ) .
These classical methods were primarily developed and evaluated
in non-language environments.
Adapting these ideas to LLM training, particularly our
program-mediated setting, is nontrivial:
the Engineer and Solver have separate token histories and different
decision horizons, while the Engineer’s program shapes the Solver’s
entire multi-turn rollout.
Their execution conditions also change as both policies learn.
Our adaptations address critic information, role-specific return
scales, partner-trajectory weighting, and update frequency.
Component studies examine their contributions in this setting,
and the complete coordination design improves performance over
the Joint-only baseline (Section 6.2 ). Table 1: Comparison of model–harness learning procedures. Engineer and Solver columns describe parameter updates.

harness can impair current task execution and may also
distort the experience from which the Solver learns .
This creates a risk of a negative feedback loop in which
ineffective harness guidance and declining Solver capability
reinforce one another.
Maintaining a compatible Solver–harness pair throughout
learning is therefore important. Our unit of learning is a complete task attempt:
the generated harness, the actions taken under it,
and the final outcome.
The Engineer learns to generate effective harnesses,
while the Solver learns to complete tasks under them.
To mitigate this risk, our method uses their shared task
outcome to coordinate value estimation and policy updates,
supporting the joint adaptation of both policies. 5 JoMoHa JoMoHa jointly trains two LLM policies:
an Engineer that generates a harness and a Solver that
performs tasks under it.
Both learn from the outcome of the same task attempt
(Figure 3 ).
To coordinate their learning, we use collaboration-aware
value estimation, role-specific return normalization,
partner-trajectory weighting, and asymmetric update schedules. 5.1 Learning from a Shared Task Outcome For a task x x , the Engineer receives historical feedback e e and samples a proposal trajectory τ E \tau_{\mathrm{E}} .
Validation and fallback map that proposal to the executed harness h = 𝒱 ⁡ ( τ E ) h=\mathcal{V}(\tau_{\mathrm{E}}) .
The Solver then interacts with the environment under h h ,
producing trajectory τ S \tau_{\mathrm{S}} and task reward R ⁡ ( x , τ S ) R(x,\tau_{\mathrm{S}}) .
An invalid proposal maps to h = ∅ h=\varnothing , retaining the base executor. The two policies share the objective J ⁡ ( θ E , θ S ) = 𝔼 x , e , h , τ S ​ [ R ⁡ ( x , τ S ) ] , J(\theta_{\mathrm{E}},\theta_{\mathrm{S}})=\mathbb{E}_{x,e,h,\tau_{\mathrm{S}}}\left[R(x,\tau_{\mathrm{S}})\right], (3) where h h is the validation/fallback result and τ S \tau_{\mathrm{S}} is collected by the Solver under that harness.
The task reward is binary on ALFWorld. The same reward trains both policies:
the Engineer learns which harnesses support successful
execution, while the Solver learns how to complete tasks
under those harnesses.
Each policy is updated on its own generated outputs,
without differentiating through harness execution.
We use a KL-regularized SAO-based actor–critic backbone ( Hou et al., 2026 ) , with a fixed reference policy for each role.
The local realization retains behavior–proximal token-ratio
decoupling. 5.2 Value Estimation for Two Different Roles Each policy has an independent critic that estimates its
expected return, including task rewards and its own KL penalty.
The critics provide baselines for computing advantages:
whether an outcome is better or worse than expected. Context: account for collaboration conditions. The usefulness of an Engineer proposal depends on the Solver,
while the Solver’s performance depends on the harness.

The supplemental fields describe the task identifier, training version,
and role-specific execution conditions.
The task field is a path or identifier, not a full environment observation.
Context is provided only to the critics and does not change
the actors’ inputs. ValueNorm: account for different return scales. The Engineer generates a harness once, whereas the Solver
acts over multiple turns.

We introduce two additional mechanisms to account for
the changing partner and the different adaptation needs

more or less representative of their current collaboration.
