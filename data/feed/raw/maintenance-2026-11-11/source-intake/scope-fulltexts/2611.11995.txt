E.5 Additional Results License: arXiv.org perpetual non-exclusive license arXiv:2611.11995v1 [cs.AI] 09 Nov 2026 Infrathon: Benchmarking Language Agents for Real-World, Long-Horizon AI Infra Optimization Radha Poovendran Abstract In this work, we study whether frontier agent can continually improve real-world AI infrastructure over long horizons.
We introduce Infrathon , a benchmark of diverse, long-horizon AI infrastructure optimization tasks across different levels. We evaluate six models across 150 runs with 24-hour budgets, totaling over 10,000 GPU-hours. Our results show that model rankings vary with the evaluation horizon, while 135 of the 150 runs achieve further improvements during the second half of their budgets. These findings indicate that shorter evaluations can overlook both optimization gains and changes in relative model performance. We also find agents can discover solutions that outperform human-designed baselines: Claude Opus 5.5 increases the mean KV-cache compression ratio from 6.82× with the human-designed Quant-VideoGen codec to 19.86×. Analysis of agent trajectories identifies task violations, unreliable testing, and premature completion as barriers to sustained improvement. Together, Our Infrathon framework provide resources for constructing long-horizon tasks, evaluating frontier agents, and discovering good infrastructure solutions. 2 2 footnotetext: Project leader, * Corresponding authors. AI infrastructure, the hardware and software used to train and serve AI models, affects their computational efficiency. Advances such as FlashAttention [ 1 ] , SGLang [ 2 ] , and Ansor [ 3 ] demonstrate the benefits of optimizing attention kernels, model serving, and tensor programs. However, developing such optimizations requires repeated implementation, profiling, and validation while preserving correctness under numerical and hardware constraints. Changes to one component can expose new bottlenecks or introduce regressions elsewhere, requiring engineers to use performance feedback to guide further improvements [ 4 , 5 ] . AI agents could automate this process, reducing engineering effort and improving model training and serving. Existing benchmarks provide only a partial evaluation of this capability, with limitations in task coverage, execution budgets, or publicly available GPU tasks (Table 1 ).
KernelBench [ 6 ] focuses on operators and modules, while InferenceBench [ 7 ] evaluates complete inference services. Although Φ \Phi -Bench [ 8 ] spans all three levels, most tasks are limited to 1.5-hour runs. EdgeBench [ 9 ] uses 12-hour budgets in its GPU tasks, while Infrathon provides 12–24-hour budgets for GPU-based AI infrastructure optimization.
These limitations leave open whether frontier agents can repeatedly identify bottlenecks, revise solutions, and validate improvements across diverse AI infrastructure tasks over extended runs.
This raises the following research question: Can Frontier Agents Solve Diverse, Long-Horizon AI Infrastructure Tasks? To answer this question, we introduce Infrathon , a benchmark of 25 real-world, long-horizon AI infrastructure optimization tasks across three levels.
We construct these tasks with human experts using Infrathon-build , a tool that helps humans efficiently build long-horizon tasks. They draw on open-source projects (e.g., DeepGEMM [ 10 ] , FlashMLA [ 11 ] , and Liger-Kernel [ 12 ] ) and research projects (e.g., FlashAttention [ 13 ] , SageAttention [ 14 ] , and Flash-KMeans [ 15 ] ). 11 operator-level tasks optimize computational primitives with 12-hour budgets; 5 module-level tasks optimize interacting components with 24-hour budgets; and 9 system-level tasks optimize end-to-end training or serving workflows with 24-hour budgets. Infrathon is the first benchmark for long-horizon AI infrastructure optimization across three levels, with 12–24-hour budgets and metrics like TFLOP/s without fixed upper bounds. The framework couples Task Definition (Section 3.1 ) with Task Execution (Section 3.2 ): agents develop solutions and run public tests in the Work environment, submit solutions to the Controller for code review, and receive private test results from the Judge . We then evaluate frontier models with a total budget of over 10,000 GPU-hours . Across 150 runs covering six models and all 25 tasks (Section 4 ), Claude Opus 5.5 leads on 17 tasks and GPT-6 Astra on six. Rankings change with the time budget: Astra leads overall at 25% of the budget, while Claude leads at 50%. Of the 150 runs, 135 improved their best score in the second half.
Infrathon also supports knowledge discovery in AI infrastructure (Section 4.2 ). On Quant-VideoGen KV Codec, Claude Opus 5.5 increases mean KV-cache compression from 6.75 × 6.75\times to 20.89 × 20.89\times on WorldScore and from 6.88 × 6.88\times to 18.83 × 18.83\times on MovieGen, compared with the Quant-VideoGen codec (Human). Both evaluations show better video quality. With Infrathon-build and Infrathon , users can turn their own infrastructure problems into tasks, compare agent solutions with existing implementations, and iterate using test feedback (Figure 1 ).
Our trajectory analysis shows that agents sometimes violate task instructions, test the wrong code, or declare completion before finding further improvements (Section 4.4 ). For example, the Controller rejects an early Quant-VideoGen KV Codec [ 16 ] submission in which Claude Opus 5.5 submits human codec weights downloaded from the internet as its own. Together, Infrathon and Infrathon-build help humans build long-horizon tasks, evaluate LLMs, and discover new solutions and knowledge. Long-horizon agent benchmarks evaluate multi-step workflows in executable environments. MLAgentBench [ 20 ] and MLE-bench [ 21 ] study iterative ML experimentation, while RE-Bench [ 19 ] compares agents and human experts on open-ended research engineering under varying time budgets. These tasks require planning, failure diagnosis, and revision from execution results. CORE-Bench [ 22 ] tests reproduction with supplied code and data; PaperBench [ 23 ] requires implementing research contributions and experiments from scratch. EdgeBench [ 9 ] studies within-run improvement over 12-hour continuous runs, with experiments extending to 72 hours on some tasks. CL-Bench [ 24 ] instead tests whether stateful systems reuse knowledge across related tasks that share learnable structure, and Long-Horizon-Terminal-Bench [ 25 ] captures intermediate progress and partial solutions through graded subtasks. Infrathon focuses on long-horizon optimization of real-world AI infrastructure across operator, module, and system levels. LLM Agents for AI Infrastructure. Existing work studies how agents can improve efficiency while preserving correctness. KernelBench [ 6 ] , KernelBenchX [ 17 ] , and FlashInfer-Bench [ 18 ] evaluate kernel correctness and hardware efficiency. Kevin [ 26 ] , CUDA-L1 [ 27 ] , and CUDA Agent [ 28 ] explore training agents for kernel optimization. GEAK [ 29 ] and KernelEvolve [ 30 ] study iterative kernel refinement and search across accelerators, and AlphaEvolve [ 31 ] extends automated optimization to data-center scheduling and training infrastructure. Beyond kernels, SWE-Perf [ 32 ] and PERFOPT-Bench [ 33 ] evaluate repository-level runtime improvements, while InferenceBench [ 7 ] and Φ \Phi -Bench [ 8 ] include end-to-end optimization. Different from those work, our Infrathon focuses exclusively on AI infrastructure, with long-horizon GPU tasks spanning operator, module, and system levels (see Table 1 for detailed comparison of different AI infra benchmarks). Recursive Self-Improvement. Our work is also related to research on recursive self-improvement, including evaluations of agents that improve methods, implementations, and research workflows through repeated experimentation. RSI-Exam [ 34 ] studies iterative improvement of methods and agent harnesses, DeepRSI [ 35 ] examines long-horizon ML optimization, and OpenRSI Index [ 36 ] evaluates agents on real-world research projects. AutoResearchExam [ 37 ] measures progress and generalization in iterative ML research. Infrathon complements these evaluations by studying whether agents can sustain improvements to training and inference infrastructure over long horizons while satisfying task-specific correctness and hardware constraints. Analysis In this section, we compare long-horizon and short-horizon optimization under equal GPU-hour budgets (Figure 8 ). For the 150 runs in Section 4.1 , we examine how model rankings change with the time budget (Figure 7 (a)) and how much agents improve in the second half (Figure 7 (b)–(c)). We also compare each model’s visible output tokens and actions in Figure 7 (d). Long Horizon vs. Short Horizon. We evaluate GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5 on six tasks with equal GPU-hour budgets. Long-horizon optimization uses one T T -hour run; short-horizon best-of- n n test-time scaling (TTS) uses n n independent T / n T/n -hour runs, with n = 2 n=2 or 4 4 (Appendix C ). Longer horizons are more effective for module and system tasks, while short-horizon TTS remains competitive on operators. Across both models, best-of-four achieves 102.6% of long-horizon performance on operators, 96.9% on modules, and 77.3% on systems, using geometric means of task–model score ratios (Figure 8 ). Best-of-two follows the same pattern. The long-horizon setting wins nine of 12 comparisons, including all four system comparisons. The benefit also depends on the model: Claude achieves higher scores with short-horizon TTS on both operator tasks and Qwen3 ORPO, whereas Astra performs better with long-horizon optimization on all six tasks. Figure 8: Long horizon vs. short horizon at equal GPU-hours. Curves take the highest private test score across each setting’s one, two, or four runs up to the same elapsed time. The first half of the budget occupies 75% of each horizontal axis and the shaded second half 25%; ticks show actual hours. Model Rankings over Time. Task winners can change after the overall ranking stabilizes. Astra leads at 25% of the budget with a mean rank score of 84.0 versus Claude’s 79.2. Claude leads at 50% with 89.6 versus Astra’s 80.0, and all six models keep the same order at later evaluated budget fractions (Figure 7 (a)). At 50% of the budget, three tasks have leaders different from their final winners; at 75%, two tasks still do. Model choice still depends on the task and time budget. Performance Gains in the Second Half. Late gains are common but uneven. Best scores improve in 135 of 150 runs (90.0%). Although many runs are already close to their final scores at halfway (Figure 7 (b)), later gains can still be substantial: among runs with positive first-half scores, the median gain is 3.05%, while 33 runs gain more than 10%. The second half thus combines small refinements in many runs with substantial improvements in a smaller group. The timing also differs by task: system runs reach 90% of final performance at a median of 40.8% of the budget, compared with 24.6% for operators and 11.5% for modules (Figure 7 (c)). Output Token and Action Statistics. More interaction does not guarantee better task performance. Across all 150 runs, we record 36.39 million visible output tokens and 203,266 actions. Token counts exclude hidden reasoning. An action is one emitted text block or tool invocation. Across the 25 tasks, Qwen produces 8.18 million visible output tokens, compared with 5.94 million for DeepSeek and 3.60 million for GLM (Figure 7 (d)). Among these three open models, the model with the most visible tokens achieves the highest final score on 15 of 25 tasks; the model with the most actions does so on 12. On K-grouped FP8 Wgrad, GLM takes 1,715 actions versus Qwen’s 884, but achieves 118.15 versus 295.57 TFLOP/s. Appendix B.2 gives the individual task counts. Agent Behavior Analysis We analyze the trajectories of all 150 runs from Section 4 , focusing on cases where agents violate task requirements despite passing public tests, run performance tests on the wrong code, or declare completion too early. Agents can submit solutions that pass public tests but violate task instructions. On Attn-QAT NVFP4 Attention, DeepSeek and Claude sum attention probabilities before rounding, although the task requires rounding first. In Figure 9 , each point shows a public test case’s maximum error-to-tolerance ratio across outputs and gradients. Every point for both models lies to the left of the dashed threshold at 1 1 , with ratios below 0.4 0.4 . The solutions therefore pass these public tests, but the Controller rejects them for changing the required computation. The Controller also rejects DeepSeek’s solution for Miles Wan2.2 DanceGRPO [ 55 ] for reporting that logs are saved before the required disk write finishes, and GLM’s solution for LLaDA Decoding [ 56 ] for replacing an output layer that must remain unchanged. After receiving the rejection feedback, the agents correct their solutions or restore earlier versions. In an early Quant-VideoGen KV Codec [ 16 ] submission, Claude Opus 5.5 downloads the human codec’s weights from the internet and submits them as its own. The Controller rejects all five solutions before private evaluation in the Judge environment.
These cases support checking solutions against task instructions before private evaluation, as the Controller does here. Figure 9: Public-test errors on Attn-QAT NVFP4 Attention. Points show the maximum error-to-tolerance ratio across outputs and gradients for each case. Agents’ performance tests can fail to execute their intended changes. On Mamba-2 SSD Forward [ 57 ] , Qwen compares 20 kernel configurations, but all trials reuse the same cached implementation. After identifying the problem, Qwen clears the cache and repeats the comparison. GLM similarly modifies a Mamba-2 function, but its performance tests call another, unchanged function with the same name. On Sparse-MLA Prefill, Sol’s solution passes public tests but does not execute the intended memory prefetching. Agents’ completion decisions can be premature. In three selected runs, the stop hook prompts agents to continue after they declare completion. GLM raises its SageAttention score from 365.43 to 456.03 (24.8%), and Astra improves Flux/Comet MoE Primitive from 219.28 to 235.19 (7.3%). DeepSeek loads data in parallel with training on Miles SD3.5-M Multi-teacher OPD, increasing its score from 40.81 to 46.53 (14.0%). These gains follow further code changes within the original budget and would be missed by stopping at those completion statements. Conclusion We introduced Infrathon , a benchmark of 25 real-world AI infrastructure tasks across operator, module, and system levels, with 12–24-hour budgets. Our evaluation of six models across 150 runs and over 10,000 GPU-hours shows that model rankings depend on the evaluation horizon. In 135 runs, agents improve their best score in the second half, showing that shorter evaluations can miss further gains. Task violations, unreliable testing, and premature completion still limit agents’ progress. Infrathon also supports knowledge discovery: on Quant-VideoGen KV Codec, Claude Opus 5.5 increases mean KV-cache compression from 6.82 × 6.82\times to 19.86 × 19.86\times compared with the human codec. Optimization Levels We classify tasks by their optimization target and the code the agent is allowed to change. The level describes the task as a whole, including any kernels optimized within it. Operator level. Operator-level tasks optimize a specified computation or data transfer with fixed input–output behavior. Typical tasks implement or accelerate GPU kernels, including prescribed fused operations. Infrathon examples include Dynamic Grouped FP8 GEMM , SageAttention , and LMCache KV Block Transfer . A solution may use one or multiple kernels. Module level. Module-level tasks optimize a functional component or a training step within a fixed workflow. The task exposes the component’s implementation or policy while fixing the surrounding training or inference pipeline. Infrathon examples include Qwen3 ORPO , Qwen3 GKD , KVzip KV Scoring , and Quant-VideoGen KV Codec . System level. System-level tasks optimize a complete training or generation workflow, or an end-to-end service. The editable scope covers its training or decoding loop, pipeline stages, or serving orchestration. Infrathon examples include Miles Qwen3 OPD , EAGLE-3 Speculative Decoding , and Concurrent Agent Serving . Appendix D Benchmark Coverage and Horizon Comparison This appendix documents the coverage and execution settings underlying Table 1 . A check denotes coverage or support documented in the inspected paper or repository; a cross denotes no documented coverage. Infra only requires an exclusive focus on AI infrastructure. GPU tasks requires at least one task in the open-source release that explicitly uses a GPU. We use the definitions in Section A.2 to assign task levels in Table 1 . A check requires at least one independently specified task at that level. Components used inside a higher-level task do not count as separate tasks. The same definitions apply to non-AI tasks in general-purpose benchmarks. Benchmarks in the Main Comparison KernelBench [ 6 ] . KernelBench evaluates generated GPU implementations of PyTorch computations using correctness and speedup over PyTorch Eager. Its individual-operation and simple fusion tasks establish operator coverage. Its Level 3 tasks include independent Inception, ResNet, and Transformer blocks, with interacting branches, attention, normalization, and feed-forward components; the benchmark permits the agent to choose the kernel granularity. These establish module coverage. Level 4 tasks evaluate Hugging Face model forward calls that return logits. Its refinement study uses 10 model calls with code and execution feedback; no numerical wall-clock cap is reported. KernelBenchX [ 17 ] . KernelBenchX evaluates correctness, speedup, and hardware efficiency across operators and GPUs. Its tasks specify individual GPU kernels, including fused computations. Its KernelAgent setting uses three workers with at most five refinement rounds each; its GEAK setting uses three iterations with four candidate solutions per round. The core KernelAgent implementation stops when any worker passes its tests or all workers exhaust their rounds. Test and seed generation also use model calls. FlashInfer-Bench [ 18 ] . FlashInfer-Bench evaluates inference kernels on workloads derived from serving traces. The optimization targets are individual GPU kernels, including fused MoE kernels. The sequential setting uses 10 model calls with execution feedback and returns the best solution that passes its tests. No numerical wall-clock cap is reported. RE-Bench [ 19 ] . RE-Bench compares agents and human experts on seven research-engineering tasks, with up to 8 hours per attempt. Its scope includes ML research beyond infrastructure. Its prefix-sum GPU kernel establishes operator coverage, and optimization of the complete LLM Foundry finetuning script establishes system coverage. The restricted-architecture task allows designing and training an entire masked language model, while Fix Embedding scores a repaired model checkpoint and permits further training. Runs end when the agent finishes or reaches the time limit. The paper excludes pauses for API issues from agents’ time usage. InferenceBench [ 7 ] . InferenceBench requires a running OpenAI-compatible inference service on an H100 GPU. All four scenarios evaluate complete services. It uses 2-hour main budgets and a 1/2/4/8-hour time ablation. Its runners resume sessions using the remaining time, with a 300-second grace period added to the configured budget. Errors or exhausted retries can end a run early. Φ \Phi -Bench [ 8 ] . The benchmark covers kernel completion, long-horizon implementation, and end-to-end optimization. An audit of all 85 task manifests finds 43 tasks declaring GPU resources. Kernel implementations establish operator coverage. The priority-request-queue task permits changes only to the queue implementation, with exact ordering behavior and the surrounding vLLM code fixed; the memory-accounting task restricts edits to its accountant and scheduler. These establish module coverage. Complete nanoGPT training and vLLM serving tasks establish system coverage. The 85 tasks use caps of 1.5, 4, 5, or 6 hours. All 20 LHI (long-horizon implementation) tasks belong to the 1.5-hour group. The runner applies a timeout to a single agent invocation and ends the attempt when it returns. EdgeBench [ 9 ] . EdgeBench covers science, ML, software, systems, math optimization, knowledge work, mathematics, and games. The paper’s task descriptions establish operator coverage through a VLIW/SIMD kernel on a custom architecture simulator, module coverage through the EnTT graph component, and system coverage through a PostgreSQL-compatible server. These include non-AI tasks. The released official experiment configuration and container backends allocate CPU and memory resources without GPU device requests; several ML tasks also explicitly require CPU-only execution. Its main runs use 12 hours; extended experiments reach 28 and 72 hours. The released harness blocks voluntary stops and resumes eligible exits using the remaining execution budget, establishing framework support for long-horizon execution. Runs may end at the time or resume limit, or after exits that do not qualify for recovery.
