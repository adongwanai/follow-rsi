For visual agents, however, tool use presents two related challenges. Models may overlook useful tool-returned observations, while poorly grounded actions can limit the evidence available for subsequent distillation. We introduce Reframe , a post-training framework that treats the observation state, rather than only the output distribution, as an object of alignment. First, Ground-Drop-GRPO (GD-GRPO) trains spatial and temporal specialists with evidence-aware rewards and stochastic removal of the original visual input, encouraging them to acquire grounded evidence and use the returned observations. Second, Observation-Gated-MOPD (OG-MOPD) replaces deficient tool actions with canonical ones mid-rollout and resumes distillation from the repaired observation. The two stages complement each other, as learning to rely on tool-returned observations can help the policy benefit from observation repair. Neither stage requires a task-specific SFT cold start. Extensive experiments over 14 image and video benchmarks demonstrate that Reframe outperforms both Mix-RL and vanilla MOPD. Ablation results show that GD-GRPO increases observation dependence and tool gain compared with outcome-based GRPO. https://github.com/EliYuan30/Reframe Figure 1 : Overview of Reframe: visual agents should produce reliable intermediate results and truly look at them. (d) Our OG-MOPD repairs deficient tool actions before continuing distillation. 1 Introduction Agentic visual reasoning addresses the evidence-sparse bottleneck in perception-centered tasks by equipping vision-language models (VLMs) with tools to acquire fine-grained evidence. Related studies include think with images [ 32 , 46 , 48 , 13 , 33 , 34 , 25 ] and think with videos [ 45 , 41 , 12 , 28 ] . However, invoking a tool does not guarantee that the model uses what it returns. We term this problem observation neglect , where a model acquires an intermediate observation but its subsequent reasoning is largely unaffected [ 37 ] . Alongside efforts to improve when tools are invoked [ 33 , 42 , 34 ] , this raises a complementary question of how to train models to make effective use of the acquired observation. Meanwhile, most previous works focus exclusively on a single modality, while the few that simultaneously incorporate image and video tools rely on a mixed-data SFT-then-RL training paradigm. However, this often leaves the final unified model significantly lagging behind the one trained on a single modality. We argue that this is the combined result of the pattern conflict of off-policy SFT training and the supervision sparsity of RL. A more promising alternative is a densely supervised, predominantly on-policy training paradigm. On-policy distillation (OPD) serves as a foundational approach: by supervising the student’s own rollouts with dense token-level feedback, it bypasses both the sparse credit assignment of RL and the reference bias of off-policy imitation [ 1 , 31 ] . Extending this paradigm, multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) [ 24 ] routes student-generated trajectories to domain-specialized teachers, and has been increasingly adopted by frontier models [ 23 , 44 , 14 , 5 , 4 ] . Subsequent works further refine it in terms of capability imbalance [ 11 ] , heterogeneous convergence [ 2 ] , behavioral drift under tool use [ 29 ] , and cross-modal routing [ 20 , 27 ] . However, existing paradigms primarily refine how supervision is delivered without intervening in rollout trajectories. This is a critical limitation for agentic visual reasoning since subsequent supervision is all conditioned on what tools returned. Table 1 : Comparison with representative visual agents. Together, the two training stages enforce the two core properties for effective tool use: GD-GRPO makes the acquired observation necessary , while OG-MOPD keeps it valid . GD-GRPO directly targets the neglect problem by augmenting observation-based reward and stochastically dropping the original visual input. It forces the model to genuinely rely on the tool-returned results. Subsequently, OG-MOPD ensures that consolidation remains conditioned on valid trajectories by repairing deviated tool use. Across 14 image and video benchmarks, Reframe outperforms both Mix-RL and vanilla MOPD, even surpassing the dedicated experts on average. Furthermore, diagnostic analyses on observation dependence confirm that Reframe exhibits a markedly stronger causal reliance on tool-returned results than previous methods. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose Reframe , the first tool-use MOPD framework that unifies image and video agentic reasoning. Its OG-MOPD actively intervenes in rollout trajectories and repairs deviated tool actions, promoting observation validity throughout cross-modal consolidation. • We address observation neglect —the issue where agentic VLMs invoke a tool yet overlook what it returns. Through GD-GRPO , we enforce observation necessity via input dropping and observation rewards. • We develop the Reframe-100K dataset to support both training stages. Evaluations across 14 image and video benchmarks show consistently improved performance. Code, dataset, and model are open-sourced. 2 Related Work Agentic visual reasoning. Multimodal reasoning often uses textual chain-of-thought (CoT) [ 6 , 7 ] , but its benefits vary across visual tasks [ 43 , 9 , 22 ] . Recent approaches instead incorporate visual information into reasoning [ 25 , 16 ] , including interface- or code-based tool calls that acquire query-relevant observations. These tools allow models to inspect image regions and video segments in finer detail that may be difficult to resolve from the original input. However, acquiring finer visual evidence does not guarantee its use. Recent studies find that tool-returned observations may have little causal effect on subsequent predictions [ 37 , 26 ] . On-policy distillation. OPD provides dense token-level feedback on student-generated rollouts, reducing the state-distribution mismatch of off-policy imitation [ 1 , 31 ] . Effective transfer depends on compatible reasoning and novel teacher capabilities [ 19 ] , coverage of student-visited states [ 10 ] , and teacher corrections within the student’s learnable token support [ 36 ] . Extensions support black-box teachers through logit-free verification [ 50 ] , temporal video grounding [ 17 ] , and supervision through teacher-generated reasoning steps [ 39 ] . These methods primarily refine supervision along student-generated trajectories. For visual agents, however, preceding tool actions also determine which observations are available when that supervision is applied. Token-level feedback on a continuation cannot retroactively recover evidence that the preceding action failed to acquire. Multi-teacher on-policy distillation. MOPD consolidates independently trained specialists by routing teacher supervision over a shared student’s rollouts [ 24 ] , an approach increasingly used in frontier-model post-training [ 23 , 44 , 14 , 4 ] . Open-MOPD studies imbalance from token budgets, convergence drift, and stale supervision [ 11 ] , while D 3 -MOPD adapts domain sampling to different convergence rates [ 2 ] . Multimodal variants route specialists for GUI interaction and video understanding [ 20 , 27 ] . Other work identifies distortions of decision-critical tool tokens despite sufficient retained teacher probability mass [ 29 ] . These advances focus on routing and supervision while generally taking rollout observations as given. Reframe instead considers both whether acquired observations are used and whether they provide valid evidence for subsequent distillation, supporting unified image and video reasoning. 3 Method In this section, we introduce the data construction and the two-stage training procedure of Reframe. We make three design choices: (1) We restrict the toolset to two fundamental vision tools, CropImage and SelectFrames , for simplicity. (2) We adopt Qwen3.5-9B as our base model. (3) Both RL and OPD stages use the same data corpus. 3.1 Training Data Construction Reframe-100K comprises 63K image QA pairs and 37K video QA pairs, constructed from heterogeneous spatial and temporal grounding annotations. As illustrated in Fig. 2 , we establish a five-step cascaded data construction pipeline guided by the following three principles. Figure 2 : Construction pipeline of Reframe-100K. We first collect public data sources and preprocess them into a raw data pool. We then drop samples answerable from text alone, and audit the rest with 8 rollouts on the full input ( F F ) and on the annotated evidence alone ( E E ), discarding samples that are (1) too easy or too hard; (2) insufficiently supported by their evidence. Remaining suspicious samples are diagnosed by Qwen and removed only when a final reviewer confirms an answer or evidence error. Quality control of grounding annotations. The quality of grounding annotations is critical to our framework, as unreliable evidence can undermine both evidence-aware rewards and subsequent observation repair. We therefore place emphasis on evidence quality throughout our data-cleaning pipeline, incorporating multiple rounds of evidence auditing and verification. As illustrated in Fig. 2 , these procedures aim to ensure that the retained annotations provide reliable supervision for visual tool use. Data-centric tool applicability. Determining whether a tool call is beneficial remains challenging, particularly when the model has not yet developed reliable tool-use capabilities. Unlike model-dependent strategies [ 33 , 34 ] , we derive tool-applicability labels from annotation coverage, distinguishing beneficial, neutral, and non-beneficial examples without relying on base-model rollout accuracy or stronger-model judgments of tool utility. This avoids introducing model-prediction bias into applicability labels. Importantly, examples are not discarded solely because the base model can answer them without tools, as even correctly answered questions can provide valuable spatial or temporal tool-use supervision. Context-aware evidence. Grounding annotations typically localize minimal objects or temporal events, whereas visual question answering usually requires surrounding context for recognition, comparison, or disambiguation. We therefore expand each spatial bounding box by scaling its width and height by 1.5 1.5 about its center, clipping the result to the image boundary. For temporal evidence, we extend each contiguous annotated segment by one frame at both ends. This context-preserving relaxation provides additional evidence without introducing excessive irrelevant visual content. We apply it only once before computing evidence coverage and constructing tool supervision. Finally, spatial and temporal annotations are converted into executable tool metadata under a unified format. The resulting Reframe-100K supports both RL and OPD. 3.2 GD-GRPO Figure 3 : Overview of GD-GRPO. The policy samples multi-turn tool-use rollouts. After the first successful tool call, the original input o 0 o_{0} is dropped with probability 0.5 while tool-returned observations o t o_{t} remain visible, so the answer must rely on what the tools have retrieved. We train spatial and temporal specialists independently on the image and video subsets of Reframe-100K using GD-GRPO. The core idea is to couple observation acquisition with observation reliance . While observation rewards guide the policy toward informative tool actions, original-observation dropout reduces its tendency to bypass the resulting observations, thereby establishing the necessity of the acquired observations. By removing the original visual input, it turns tool-returned observations from supplementary cues into essential information for subsequent reasoning. This provides the foundation for observation-aligned capability consolidation in the subsequent OG-MOPD stage. 3.3 OG-MOPD For tool-use models, the effectiveness of distillation depends not only on the selected teacher but also on the observations available when its supervision is applied. A deficient tool action can place the student in an uninformative visual state, constraining all subsequent teacher feedback. Although token-level optimization can improve future actions, feedback on the current continuation does not retroactively repair the observation already returned. OG-MOPD therefore intervenes at the tool-action boundary to improve observation validity , while retaining the student’s generation. At inference, neither observation gating nor specialist teachers are required. Training details are provided in Appendix B . 4 Experiments 4.1 Experimental Setup Benchmarks. We evaluate Reframe on seven image benchmarks and seven video benchmarks, testing whether targeted visual inspection translates into both fine-grained perception and broader visual understanding. For images, V ∗ [ 40 ] , HR-Bench 4K/8K [ 35 ] , VisualProbe [ 15 ] , and ZoomBench [ 38 ] stress the ability to find and resolve task-relevant details within complex visual inputs. MME-RealWorld [ 47 ] extends the evaluation to diverse real-world scenes, while PerceptionBench [ 21 ] probes atomic visual perception. For videos, Video-MME [ 8 ] , Video-MME-v2 [ 9 ] , MVBench [ 18 ] , and MLVU [ 49 ] assess understanding across diverse tasks and temporal scales. MME-VideoOCR [ 30 ] and VSI-Bench [ 43 ] target video text understanding and spatial reasoning, respectively. V-STaR [ 3 ] probes spatial and temporal evidence understanding. Baselines. We conduct two groups of comparisons. The first compares training paradigms using five baselines: Qwen3.5-9B; Image-RL and Video-RL, the modality-specific specialists trained with GD-GRPO on single-modality data; Mix-RL, trained with GD-GRPO on the combined image and video data; and MOPD, which consolidates the two specialists through vanilla MOPD. The second compares Reframe with existing agentic visual reasoning models: Pixel-Reasoner, Thyme, DeepEyes, and DeepEyesV2, in terms of performance, observation dependence, and tool gain. Furthermore, outcome-based GRPO serves as a baseline to diagnose the proposed GD-GRPO. Evaluation protocol. We evaluate all models under shared visual-token budgets using greedy decoding. Each original image receives up to 5,120 tokens, and each tool-returned image receives up to 1,960 tokens. For videos, we use 64 frames with 128 tokens per frame. Each selected frame receives up to 400 tokens, subject to a cumulative budget of 4,096 tokens across all select calls. GD-GRPO improves the image average by 2.07 points. In the tool-gain evaluation, enabling tools adds 3.82 points with GD-GRPO versus 1.97 with GRPO, while tool-free accuracy improves by only 0.22 points. These results suggest that GD-GRPO yields only modest gains in general visual capabilities, while its main benefit lies in enabling the model to translate visual tool use into better reasoning performance. To better understand this tool-related improvement, we examine how GD-GRPO changes the model’s response to different tool observations. GD-GRPO improves accuracy under GT, Random, and Blank by 5.23, 3.67, and 3.14 points, respectively, with the largest gain under GT. We summarize this behavior with the dependence score (Appendix C.2 ), defined as the fraction of errors under uninformative observations (the mean of Random and Blank) that are corrected once the ground-truth observation is supplied. It measures sensitivity to observation content rather than overall capability. GD-GRPO raises it from 52.59 to 69.03, while also improving accuracy under corrupted observations. Together with the larger tool gain, these results support better use of returned observations rather than greater vulnerability to misleading observations. 4.3.2 Comparison of Training Paradigms We compare Mix-RL, MOPD, and Reframe against the modality-specific experts in Table 2 . Mix-RL falls short of the specialists. Mix-RL trails Image-RL and Video-RL in their respective modalities by 1.17 and 1.37 points (Table 2 ), retaining 74% and 55% of their gains over the base model. Although it uses the GD-GRPO reward, these signals are aggregated into trajectory-level advantages rather than separate assessments of each acquisition and reasoning decision. The shared policy must therefore discover spatial and temporal strategies jointly, yet gradient updates favoring one modality do not necessarily benefit the other. This couples exploration across modalities with coarse credit assignment, providing a plausible explanation for the specialist gap. In contrast, the MOPD results show that near-specialist performance in both modalities is attainable, suggesting that the learning process does matter. MOPD approaches specialist performance. MOPD retains 106% and 85% of the gains achieved by Image-RL and Video-RL, respectively. It surpasses both teachers on 5 of the 14 benchmarks. The key difference from Mix-RL lies in the supervision density. Mix-RL uses a trajectory-level advantage, while MOPD provides specialist feedback for each generated token. This gives the student more direct guidance on intermediate generation choices, reducing the need to infer their value from an aggregate response-level signal. Such dense supervision can make better use of each rollout, making MOPD a more practical choice for consolidating specialists. Observation repair yields additional gains. With the same GD-GRPO specialists as teachers, Reframe improves over MOPD by 1.29 points on images and 0.78 on videos. The largest gain occurs on VisualProbe (+4.86). MOPD already teaches both actions and reasoning, but its feedback on a continuation is conditioned on the observation the student has obtained. Repair provides task-relevant evidence before distillation continues. Its contribution is therefore focused on deficient actions in tool-applicable examples, complementing the broader consolidation already achieved by MOPD. The central trade-off in observation repair is between observation validity and the degree to which the rollout remains on-policy. An externally supplied action changes the history from which the student continues, even though the continuation itself remains student-generated. OG-MOPD limits this intervention by preserving valid student actions and repairing only deficient ones. NLL on the injected actions encourages the student to produce the corrected actions itself at inference. Visual reasoning partially transfers across modalities. Image-RL improves the video average from 49.75 to 50.39, while Video-RL raises the image average from 63.46 to 66.35. The transferable component may include primitive visual capabilities such as entity recognition, OCR, and spatial intelligence, as well as a shared tendency to seek fine-grained evidence. Grounding-oriented training can improve general perception beyond its training task. 4.3.3 Comparison with Existing Agentic Models Reframe leads on all 14 benchmarks in Table 4 , exceeding the strongest external image and video averages by 11.71 and 8.86 points, respectively. Under shared visual budgets, we examine how models use acquired observations and benefit from tools. Reframe uses the evidence it acquires. On V ∗ , we examine model performance with ground-truth (GT), random, and blank cropped images as tool-returned observations. Direct evaluates the model without tools, while Natural lets the model decide when and how to use them. Reframe’s dependence score is 75.53, compared with at most 41.93 for the other models. Its accuracy reaches 96.86 with GT, versus 85.86 with Random and 88.48 with Blank, despite the original image remaining available. Reliance on returned observations can be beneficial when tool actions reliably acquire relevant evidence. Reframe’s larger gap across conditions, strong GT performance, and positive tool gain together suggest that it can translate this reliance into improved reasoning performance. Tools contribute more to Reframe. Tool gain complements the controlled-observation analysis by measuring the benefit of autonomous tool use. Across seven image benchmarks, tools improve Reframe from 64.25 to 69.48, a gain of 5.23 points versus at most 1.47 for the other models. Relative to the tool-free Qwen3.5-9B (63.46), 87% of the overall gain stems from tool use, indicating that Reframe’s advantage largely arises from interaction with visual tools. Useful observations must be acquired through the model’s own actions. The larger tool gain, therefore, provides evidence that Reframe can combine evidence acquisition and use to improve its final answers. 5 Conclusion Reframe consolidates image and video reasoning predominantly through on-policy training. GD-GRPO encourages reliance on tool-returned observations through grounding rewards and original-input dropout, while OG-MOPD repairs deficient tool actions before continuing distillation. Across 14 benchmarks, Reframe improves over Mix-RL and vanilla MOPD, even surpassing both specialists. Diagnostic evaluations show stronger observation dependence and larger tool gains than the compared agentic visual models, highlighting the importance of both acquiring useful evidence and learning to use it. C.2 Observation-dependence Study The study uses the 191 multiple-choice questions of V ∗ , with one sample per question under each of five conditions. In Direct , the model answers without tools. In Natural , the model chooses its own tool actions. For each model and question, the three controlled conditions share an injected canonical crop action, the same pre-continuation text, and the same tool-response text. Only the returned image content changes. Each controlled tool response ends with the same instruction: “This was your only tool call. Answer the question now from the images above, without calling any more tools.” • GT : the annotated target region, with the context-preserving
expansion used to construct the canonical crop. • Random : a same-size crop at a different location in the
original image, using a question-dependent random seed. • Blank : a same-size constant gray image, with RGB value ( 128,128,128 ) (128,128,128) . All three returned images use the same resizing and visual-token budget. Random crops target IoU at most 0.1 0.1 with GT. If none of 100 sampled positions meets this target, we choose the lowest-IoU position among the samples and four image corners. The full original image remains visible in all five conditions. After the controlled observation is inserted, the model generates one continuation and no further tool action is executed. Attempts to call a tool again are logged separately. Final choices are scored deterministically against the option letter. Model outputs without a parseable final answer receive zero credit. The external-agent probe also marks an attempted continuation tool call as an invalid completion, including when an answer accompanies it.
