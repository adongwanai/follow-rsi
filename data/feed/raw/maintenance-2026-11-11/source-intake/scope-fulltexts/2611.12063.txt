AI auto-research is poised to forge bespoke data systems, from key-value stores and indexes to query engines, each engineered for a specific workload and hardware platform. Yet this promise rests on fragile ground. An agent optimizes what it can measure, not necessarily what users need. Natural-language requirements leave ample room for specification gaming, producing systems that blaze through benchmarks while silently losing updates or violating consistency. Worse, each research iteration consumes tokens, while validating a candidate under real concurrency, memory pressure, and failure conditions can stretch the entire research process into weeks. In this paper, we envision a more cost-efficient and verifiable approach to auto-research. Precise and composable specifications anchor the search, lightweight performance critics prune costly dead ends, and falsification and deductive verification make correctness an integral part of the process rather than an afterthought. By treating correctness, performance, research time, and token cost as equally important objectives, we aim to transform AI-generated data systems from plausible artifacts into trustworthy, production-grade systems whose behavior can be understood, audited, and ultimately relied upon. * * footnotetext: These authors contributed equally to this work. 1. Introduction AI is beginning to do more than write code: it is beginning to conduct research.
OpenAI’s recent effort to attack the Navier–Stokes millennium problem ( OpenAI, 2026 ) shows how an LLM-driven loop can plan,
implement, evaluate, critique, and revise candidate solutions at a
scale and pace beyond the reach of any individual researcher. In computer science, the same approach is already being applied to
algorithm discovery ( Novikov et al., 2025 ; Liu et al., 2026a ) ,
machine-learning model design ( Xu et al., 2026 ) , and, perhaps most
provocatively for the database community ( Wehrstein et al., 2026 ; Lao and Trummer, 2026a ; Lou et al., 2026 ; Amiri and Binnig, 2026 ) ,
the synthesis of complete data systems tailored to a specific workload
and hardware platform.
Imagine asking AI: “ Build me the fastest key-value store for my
RDMA cluster, this workload, and this memory budget. ”
A well-designed auto-research pipeline should do far more than
autocomplete an implementation. It should behave like a research team:
propose a design, implement it, benchmark it, diagnose its bottlenecks,
discard failed hypotheses, and try again.
This opens the door to a future where one-size-fits-one data systems, engineered for a particular
workload–hardware pair, become cheaper to generate than to build by hand. This future is exhilarating, but it comes with a bill. It is expensive .
Auto-research is not a single inference call. It is an iterative search
process in which an agent explores designs, writes code, runs
experiments, interprets failures, and tries again. Every iteration
consumes tokens, and the costs quickly add up. It is slow .
Systems claims cannot be settled by plausible prose. A candidate must
survive real concurrency, memory pressure, and failure conditions.
Sweeping thread counts, workload mixes, and failure configurations
can turn a single iteration into hours and an entire research campaign
into weeks. And it is dangerous .
An agent can produce code that compiles and passes a benchmark, yet
silently corrupts data, breaks durability guarantees, or optimizes the
measured metric while abandoning the property the user actually needs. The question is no longer simply
“Can AI generate a data system?”
It is: Can AI conduct systems research whose cost, duration,
and conclusions we can understand, control, and trust? The emerging discipline of verifiable coding ( Chen et al., 2026 ) offers part of the answer.
It calls for LLM-generated code to come with machine-checkable correctness proofs ( Yang et al., 2025 ) , turning the promise of
“vibe coding” into something more responsible: rapid yet
verifiable system construction.
The central question is no longer merely
“ Did the model produce code that runs? ”
but rather
“ Can we establish, mechanically and end to end, that the
generated system does what we intended, and at what cost? ” For data systems serving millions of users, verifiable coding is
not a luxury. It is a requirement. 1.1. The Problem: Expensive Search Without Trustworthy Targets Modern auto-research pipelines can produce an OLAP engine ( Wehrstein et al., 2026 ) , concurrent B + tree ( Zhao et al., 2026b ) , or a key-value store ( Liu et al., 2026b ) . But producing code is the easy part of auto-research. The hard parts are deciding what the agent should optimize, determining whether a candidate is actually correct, and avoiding weeks of computation spent pursuing the wrong objective. Our preliminary work ( Zhao et al., 2026b ) applying the state-of-the-art auto-research pipeline Jitskit ( Liu et al., 2026b ) to optimize B + trees and hash tables on RDMA/CXL platforms exposed four challenges that current auto-research pipelines do not solve. Challenge 1: Specification gaming wastes the search. An auto-research loop optimizes what it can measure. When requirements are
stated in natural language,
AI agents tend to exploit the ambiguities in the specification
rather than optimizing the system. In one experiment, we gave Jitskit a USD 50 budget to build an optimized key-value store for our RDMA platform. The result was a B + tree with lost updates: it was fast and plausible, but fundamentally inconsistent with our intended strong-consistency semantics. The agent exploited natural-language gaps in the specification of consistency across multiple operations. In effect, the USD 50 purchased not a better data system, but a more convincing violation of our intent. This is specification gaming ( Nishimura-Gasparian et al., 2026 ; Bondarenko et al., 2025 ; Zhong et al., 2026 ) , also known as reward hacking. It is especially pernicious in data systems because correctness is invisible in a throughput graph. An AI-generated bespoke system can report spectacular numbers while silently losing updates, violating isolation, or failing under concurrency. Worse, once specification gaming occurs, the entire search budget—tokens, benchmark time, and human attention—has been spent optimizing a misunderstanding. Challenge 2: Concurrent and distributed correctness makes validation the bottleneck. Concurrent and distributed systems are where plausible-looking systems become untrustworthy. In another preliminary experiment, Jitskit generated an implementation for our CXL platform that was highly scalable and efficient—but manual inspection revealed an undetected data race that made it non-linearizable. The lesson is stark: an agent can produce code that passes tests, compiles cleanly, and delivers excellent performance, yet still be incorrect. The space of concurrent executions and failure schedules is enormous and nondeterministic. If auto-research is to generate backend systems serving millions of users, “it passed our tests” is not an adequate notion of correctness. Challenge 3: Token cost makes blind search unaffordable. Auto-research consumes tokens at every stage: planning, coding, critique, auditing, and revision. Yet much existing work reports the performance of the final artifact while obscuring the cost of discovering it. These loops can consume billions of tokens. The problem is not that iteration is inherently wasteful. Search is how auto-research discovers non-obvious designs. The problem is that much of today’s search is optimized for performance, not token cost: the agent may spend expensive iterations clarifying an ambiguous requirement, repairing a specification loophole, or evaluating a candidate that was never promising. Specification gaming turns this inefficiency into catastrophic waste: every token spent producing, benchmarking, and critiquing a gamed artifact is spent optimizing the wrong target. Challenge 4: Long research time makes broad evaluation unsustainable. Evaluating the performance of a candidate system in the auto-research loop consumes no tokens but substantial wall-clock time. In our preliminary studies, evaluating one candidate across configurations—such as YCSB-A with varying thread counts—took roughly four hours, causing the auto-research loop to run for weeks. This creates a cruel dilemma. Broad evaluation gives the planner the evidence it needs to propose better candidates, but it slows the loop. Narrow evaluation accelerates iteration, but starves the planner of signal. Neither choice is satisfying. What is needed is not less evaluation, but smarter allocation of scarce evaluation time: identify candidates unlikely to improve the incumbent before paying for a full benchmark. 1.2. Will Stronger LLMs Fix This? Unlikely. Stronger models may reduce the number of iterations, but they do not eliminate the underlying failure modes. Natural-language specifications will remain ambiguous; data races will remain hidden in exponentially large interleaving spaces; and more capable models will likely cost more per token. Configuration sweeps, warm-up periods, and steady-state measurement will also remain expensive in wall-clock time, regardless of model quality. Indeed, stronger models may amplify the problem. A more capable agent can explore more designs, generate more plausible code, and reason more deeply about performance. But it can also consume more tokens, incur a higher cost per token, exploit subtler loopholes in underspecified tasks, and produce more convincing artifacts that remain subtly incorrect. The problem is architectural. Auto-research requires a different contract among the agent, the evaluator, and the human. It needs specifications that cannot be quietly reinterpreted, correctness evidence that does not depend on fortunate test coverage, and research loops that spend scarce tokens and wall-clock time where they matter most. That is our vision. 2. Background: Auto-research Loop As AI makes code increasingly cheap to produce, the economics of software
engineering begin to shift: generating a new system tailored to a specific need
can cost less than hiring engineers to build and maintain a general-purpose one.
This shift particularly favors “one-size-fits-one” bespoke systems, each
highly specialized for a particular workload–hardware pair.
For workload drift in production data systems,
a new bespoke system can be
generated for the shifted workload and live-migrated into place ( Das et al., 2011 ; Elmore et al., 2011 ) . A typical auto-research loop repeatedly executes: plan → implement → verify → measure → critique → revise \text{plan}\rightarrow\text{implement}\rightarrow\text{verify}\rightarrow\text{measure}\rightarrow\text{critique}\rightarrow\text{revise} Each iteration produces and evaluates a candidate implementation, with the results guiding the next design decision.
In Jitskit, the loop is implemented as follows: • Spec cards (input prompt) that use natural-language in JSON format to
define the target environment
(e.g., number of cores, memory budget in GB) as an environment card , the target workload
(e.g., read/write mix) as a workload card , and the
functional description of the desired system (e.g., API semantics) as a requirement card . • Planner (coding agent in plan mode) plans the design, including storage layout, partitioning, caching, synchronization, memory reclamation, and locking. • Coder (coding agent in coding mode) translates the design into runnable code and fixes compilation, integration, and implementation failures. • Evaluator (scripts, benchmarks, and tools) runs correctness tests,
measures throughput, average and tail latency, and diagnostic signals such as contention, memory use, cache hits, evictions, remote-memory traffic, and I/O. • Critic (agent) converts correctness and performance results into bottleneck hypotheses and design recommendations for the next iteration. • Design brief and whiteboard memory (markdown files) stores the current design, latest results, bottlenecks, failed or redundant ideas and the evidence against them. • Auditor (agent) periodically checks for specification gaming and untested edge cases; it can harden (e.g., add new tests) the test suite as the loop progresses. It also decides whether to terminate the loop early when it determines that there is no further room for improvement. Optionally, the loop may start from a seed implementation, thereby supporting both synthesis from specifications and optimization of existing systems. 3. Vision: Verifiable Auto-Research Figure 1. A verifiable auto-research pipeline for data systems. The four challenges share a common root: the research loop is asked to optimize a target that it cannot precisely understand or check.
We therefore envision verifiable auto-research – the research iteration is guided by a formal specification and the resulting implementation must provide machine-checkable proofs that it satisfies that specification. This inversion changes what the agent can get away with. A fast implementation that loses updates is no longer a plausible candidate; it is a proof failure. A subtle data race is no longer hidden behind an unfortunate test suite; it violates a stated linearizability obligation. An agent that exploits a resource loophole produces a concrete, inspectable specification gap rather than an unexplained benchmark result. Verifiable coding does not make auto-research less creative—it makes auto-research’s creativity accountable. 3.1. Why a Formal Contract Changes the Game? Verifiable auto-research directly attacks the four aforementioned challenges. • It turns specification gaming into specification repair. Natural-language requirements are easy to write but hard to check: they admit multiple interpretations, and those interpretations can become the easiest target for optimization ( Ferrari and Spoletini, 2025 ; Beg et al., 2025 ) . A formal contract removes much of this room for quiet reinterpretation. If the intended semantics is “every completed put is observable to subsequent reads,” the agent cannot satisfy the letter of the task by silently weakening consistency. When a gap remains, it is localized in a precise contract that humans or recursive-self-improvement (RSI) ( Zhang et al., 2026 ; Zelikman et al., 2024 ) can amend, rather than buried in an ambiguous prompt. • It makes concurrency a first-class correctness obligation. Testing can reveal some interleavings, but it cannot enumerate them. Verification complements testing by checking that an implementation satisfies its specification for all executions. Modern verification tools make this practical for systems code: Verus ( Lattuada et al., 2024 ) , for example, supports machine-checked functional and concurrency reasoning for Rust programs, including pointer-manipulating, concurrent, and distributed code. For data systems, this means linearizability, atomicity, and absence of data races can become explicit obligations rather than hopes supported by a finite set of tests. • It makes the search loop more token-efficient. Much of the wasted token expenditure arises from generating, critiquing, and
benchmarking candidates that implement unintended semantics.
Formalizing requirements upfront can reduce the cost of reaching a verified
implementation by avoiding further iterations spent optimizing the wrong target. • It makes evaluation time more valuable. Formal correctness does not eliminate benchmarking: a correct system may still be slow. But it changes the question asked by each benchmark. Instead of using scarce wall-clock time to discover whether a candidate is both fast and correct, the loop can use it to compare designs that are already known to respect the contract. Evaluation then becomes a search for performance, not a costly proxy for correctness. 3.2. Why Data Systems Are the Right Beachhead Data systems are the right beachhead for verifiable auto-research because they join three properties that rarely coincide: high stakes, clean abstractions, and a design space rich enough to reward exploration. • They are high-stakes. A lost update, stale read, or non-linearizable operation is not a cosmetic defect. It can silently corrupt application state, undermine recovery, or violate the guarantee on which an entire service depends. • They speak a shared language. Unlike arbitrary software projects, data systems are assembled from a small, enduring vocabulary: operations such as get , put , delete , and scan , and guarantees such as linearizability. This shared language makes intended behavior precise, reusable, and ready to be made formal. • They reward exploration. Indexing, partitioning, caching, synchronization, and memory management form a vast space of interacting choices. Automated search can traverse it more broadly—and perhaps less conventionally—than hand tuning, using observable signals such as throughput, latency, scalability, contention, cache behavior, and memory use to distinguish designs once correctness has been established. Data systems are therefore complex enough to make verification matter, structured enough to make specifications composable, and performance-sensitive enough to make even modest improvements valuable. They are not merely an application of verifiable auto-research. They are an ideal laboratory for its central tension: how to pursue creative design without sacrificing correctness, and how to search aggressively without losing control of cost. 4. A Verifiable Auto-Research Loop for Data Systems We envision a cost-effective auto-research loop for generating and verifying
high-performance database implementations ( Figure 1 ).
A vibe systems designer declares the desired operations
and consistency model in api.json . A specification composer agent uses these requirements to
compose a formal specification from the specification library. The master planner
receives the formal specification, the target workload, and the execution
environment as inputs. The performance optimization loop searches for
implementations that perform well on the target workload, whether it is
read-heavy, write-heavy, or mixed. The proof loop checks that the resulting
implementation satisfies the composed specification when reads and writes
execute concurrently. Performance optimization loop The master planner proposes a plan for a candidate Rust implementation.
The coder then implements the plan proposed by the master planner, runs
basic tests, and fixes build, integration, or test failures. Once the candidate
passes these checks, the performance pre-critic discards a weak candidate
by using an LLM to examine its source code
and relevant information.
Otherwise, the performance evaluator benchmarks the candidate on the target workload and compares the results with
those from the previous round. If a candidate with better performance is found, the candidate enters
the proof loop. Otherwise, the performance critic analyzes the results
and sends its diagnostics to the master planner to guide the next design. Proof loop A faster candidate may still contain bugs, particularly concurrency-related bugs, even after passing the tests.
The proof loop starts with a formal falsifier ( Ball et al., 2005 ) , which
looks for an execution that violates the specification. If it finds one, it sends
the counterexample to the master planner to guide the next implementation. If the
search finds none, the proof planner identifies the proof obligations, the proof writer adds annotations to discharge them, and Verus checks the
annotated implementation against the specification. If verification fails,
the proof critic examines the verifier log. For unresolved obligations,
it sends the relevant diagnostics to the proof planner, which revises the plan
so the proof writer can update the annotations and Verus can recheck the same
implementation. If an unsupported Rust primitive blocks verification, the proof
critic identifies it for the master planner, which starts another performance
optimization iteration to find an implementation with similar performance without using
unsupported primitives. If verification succeeds, the loop checks the candidate
against the performance target. It returns the best verified candidate if the
target is met. Otherwise, the master planner starts the next research iteration. There are many open challenges unique to synthesizing reliable data systems while targeting at high performance, and we will present some of them in the following sections. 4.1. Composing Formal Specifications from the Specification Library Figure 2. Composing a formal specification with SpecLib . The
designer declares the operations and consistency model in api.json .
The specification composer uses library components to generate api.rs . The excerpts highlight the functional and consistency
specifications used in api.rs in blue and orange, respectively. Although modern LLMs can substantially lower the cost of drafting formal
specifications, they do not by themselves ensure that a generated
specification captures the designer’s intended semantics, composes safely
with other requirements, or remains stable under iterative repair.
In a verifiable auto-research loop, this distinction is critical:
the specification is not merely documentation, but the machine-checkable
target that the search loop optimizes against. An ambiguous, internally
inconsistent, or quietly weakened contract can therefore redirect the entire
research budget toward a plausible but incorrect system. We envision SpecLib ( Figure 2 ), a composable library
for specifying the behavior of concurrent data systems under different
consistency models. Its functional specifications define the effects of
individual operations, while its consistency specifications constrain
which histories of those operations are allowed. Its purpose is not only to
reduce specification-writing effort, but to provide a curated, reusable, and
auditable foundation from which AI agents and human designers can construct
contracts. The library contains: • Abstract states that provide shared mathematical models of
database state and execution history. • Functional specifications that define the semantics of common
operations, such as point reads and writes, range scans, and transaction
primitives. • Consistency specifications that constrain the observable behavior
of concurrent operations under consistency models such as linearizability and
eventual consistency. Both functional and consistency specifications are expressed over the shared
abstract models. For example, a key-value store’s logical contents can be
represented as a mathematical map from keys to values, while its execution
history can be represented as a sequence of invocation and response events.
This shared foundation is essential for composition: the semantics of a scan,
a transaction, and a consistency model must refer to the same abstract state
and history if their combination is to be meaningful. The vibe systems designer declaratively describes the desired API and
consistency requirements.
The specification composer agent then
selects and instantiates the appropriate library components to generate a
formal contract for the candidate implementation. For example, the designer
may select get , put , and delete operations with
linearizability. The composer combines the functional specifications in functional.rs , including the get specification in
lines 3–14, with the linearizability specification in lines 15–28 of consistency.rs , as highlighted in Figure 2 . The
resulting contract, api.rs , specifies both the behavior of each
operation and the guarantees that must hold when operations execute
concurrently. This design changes the role of AI in specification construction. Rather than
asking an LLM to synthesize an entire concurrent correctness contract from a
natural-language prompt, the LLM serves as the specification composer and
grounds its output in reviewed library components with explicit assumptions.
However, the resulting contract remains inspectable, versioned, and
attributable to named specification components. When the auto-research loop
later identifies a specification gap, repair can be localized to a specific
component and its assumptions, rather than allowing an agent to rewrite the
contract wholesale to make a proof succeed. Constructing such a specification library is challenging. Open Problem 1: Composability of specifications. Individually sensible requirements can become impossible to satisfy
together once the deployment and failure model are fixed. The CAP theorem is a classic
example. The specification composer must therefore reason about what information an operation needs
to satisfy its guarantees, and whether the assumed environment allows that information to
be obtained. When requirements conflict, it should explain which guarantees and assumptions
cause the conflict and how changing them could restore feasibility. This is closely related to realizability analysis in reactive synthesis ( Lu et al., 2016 ; Konwar et al., 2021 ) . For data systems,
the challenge is to derive the relevant constraints from operation semantics, transaction
guarantees, and deployment assumptions. How can an LLM perform this analysis, identify the
conditions that determine realizability, and justify feasible alternatives. 4.2. Performance Pre-Critic Auto-research can be slow to discover improved solutions, not only because generating implementations and proofs is time-consuming, but also because evaluating generated candidates consumes a substantial fraction of the research budget. For example, a single YCSB benchmark sweep across different thread counts can take hours. Although the auto-research loop may eventually produce solutions that outperform the seed, individual iterations can yield candidates that offer no improvement—or even degrade performance—when evaluated on the target workload. Reliably identifying and skipping such unpromising candidates before full-scale evaluation can therefore substantially reduce research time. We therefore envision introducing a performance pre-critic agent between the code-generation stage and the evaluator. Before submitting a generated candidate to the evaluator for benchmarking against the target workload, the pre-critic examines the candidate’s source code, along with other information, to decide whether evaluating the current candidate is worthwhile. Note that the pre-critic should have a distinct objective and prompt from the planner and coder, and it will not have access to their rationales or performance estimates. This separation is intended to reduce the risk of self-preference ( Panickssery et al., 2024 ; Wataoka et al., 2024 ) and self-attribution bias ( Khullar et al., 2026 ) , which can arise when a model evaluates a proposal it helped generate. Open Problem 2: Can a Pre-Critic Know When Not to Look? A pre-critic must do more than guess whether a candidate is fast: it must decide whether the evidence before it justifies spending scarce benchmarking time. It could build a lightweight performance model from the execution environment and comparisons with previously evaluated implementations, aiming not to predict absolute throughput but to recognize candidates unlikely to beat the current best ( Zhao et al., 2026a ) . Yet it remains open whether LLMs can make such judgments for generated data systems, and what evidence they need: would source code and a workload description suffice, or must the critic also inspect profiles of earlier candidates or take inexpensive measurements of the new one? Any extra evidence must remain cheap enough to preserve the very time savings that motivate pre-criticism. The harder question is what to do when the critic is unsure. A false positive wastes an evaluation on a hopeless candidate; a false negative discards a potential improvement before it is ever measured. Because these errors carry different costs, accuracy alone cannot characterize a useful pre-critic. The agent instead needs a decision policy that weighs the price of another benchmark against the value of a missed breakthrough.
The central challenge is therefore not merely to make a pre-critic confident, but to establish when that confidence deserves trust, and how aggressively it should filter the search. 4.3. Proof Loop As Edsger Dijkstra famously put, “program testing can be used to show the presence of bugs, but never to show their absence”.
Passing the basic tests in the coder agent and achieving high performance does not guarantee a correct implementation,
especially for the concurrent ones. We therefore
introduce a proof loop to formally verify the candidate’s correctness. Starting proof construction immediately,
however, could waste all tokens on proof annotations if
the implementation is actually a buggy one.
The proof loop therefore starts with a formal falsifier ( Ball et al., 2005 ) , which uses non-LLM techniques such as model checking and SMT solving to search for counterexamples within a given bound. If it finds one, it sends the counterexample to the master planner, which can use it to revise
the implementation for the next research iteration. Only if the search finds no counterexample, the candidate proceeds to adding the proof
for the implementation. Open Problem 3: Reusing Proofs Across Evolving Designs Functional specifications are deliberately expressed over abstract states: they
define what a data system must do, not how it should be built.
This generality gives the auto-research loop freedom to invent new designs or
reuse existing ones. But the candidates that survive performance evaluation are
highly optimized and therefore highly low-level: they manipulate memory layouts,
ownership permissions, lock states, and hardware-specific code paths. Between
the clean abstract state and this concrete implementation lies a large semantic gap ( Abadi and Lamport, 1991 ) : the prover must show that every
low-level transition faithfully realizes the intended abstract transition. In our preliminary experience, this gap is not
that difficult under powerful models like
GPT-6 Astra, but it is economically expensive. Formal verification has long exhibited a high
proof-to-implementation cost.
For example, CertiKOS
reports a 13:1 proof-to-implementation ratio ( Gu et al., 2016 ) .
Our preliminary experiments likewise show that the prover consumes substantially
more tokens than the coder. The reason is intuitive: the prover must discover
the missing bridge between two very different levels of abstraction—representation
invariants, ownership invariants, linearization points, commutativity arguments,
and lemmas that connect a concrete mutation to its abstract effect. Much of this reasoning concerns recurring patterns in data
structures and concurrency protocols ( Jung et al., 2018 ) . Can we capture these patterns as
reusable proof components, so that LLMs need not reconstruct the same
arguments for every candidate? The Lean community offers a compelling precedent. Mathlib is a large,
growing, community-maintained library of formal definitions, lemmas, theorems, and proof tools for
mathematics ( The mathlib Community, 2020 ) .
Can we make one for data-system verification?
Such a library might include: • Concurrency and coordination proofs that capture recurring
arguments about interactions among threads or nodes, such as consistent
reads through optimistic validation, safe memory reclamation, and
transaction ordering, with explicit assumptions for their reuse. • Representation and refinement lemmas that connect concrete data structures to
abstract models, such as showing that a B + tree’s nodes and pointers
denote a well-formed ordered map. • Proof tactics and templates that help the agent identify suitable
invariants, decompose verification obligations,
and instantiate reusable lemma. The central open question is how to design such a proof library so that it is reusable without being rigid . If its lemmas are too
implementation-specific, they will not generalize across designs; if they are
too abstract, they will do little to reduce the token cost of constructing a
proof. Can we identify a stable middle layer of database proof
knowledge—concurrency idioms, abstract-state refinements, and consistency
arguments—that enables an LLM to prove the correctness of a novel,
high-performance synthesized system without repeatedly rediscovering the same
semantic bridge? 5. Looking Forward Verifiable auto-research remains in its infancy. In this paper, we take a first
look at how auto-research could be conducted for data systems that are optimized
for general workloads, treating token cost and research time as
first-class citizens. We are not alone in this pursuit.
IDS ( Agarwal et al., 2026 ) shares our vision with respect to verifiability. Similarly, GenDB-V ( Lao and Trummer, 2026b ) embraces this vision for read-only workloads, but it does not consider research-time or token-consumption.
We bring this vision to updatable data systems, where correctness, token cost, and research time are first-order challenges.
