Corresponding author: Yawei Li. Abstract We present PhysioWave-T, a token-efficient wavelet foundation-model family for
electroencephalography (EEG), electrocardiography (ECG), and surface
electromyography (sEMG). Dynamic scale folding recombines the J + 1 J{+}1 wavelet
subbands before tokenization, compressing the sequence from ( J + 1 ) ​ C ​ S (J{+}1)CS to C ​ S CS tokens for J J decomposition levels, C C channels, and S S temporal patches.
Route-specific wavelet front ends and learned channel-name embeddings let one
Transformer per modality accommodate diverse channel counts and sampling rates,
while unseen configurations reuse the pre-trained backbone via a lightweight
front end. Together, these components scale PhysioWave to EEG: we pre-train a
30M-parameter EEG foundation model on over 15M windows spanning 19 to 128
channels, alongside ECG (30M) and sEMG (12M) encoders. Across four EEG routes,
folding cuts Transformer latency by 4.0–7.2 × \times and peak memory by
6.8–12.7 × \times over scale expansion without accuracy loss. The EEG encoder
leads eight of nine metrics against BENDR, BIOT, LaBraM, and EEGPT, and
surpasses LUNA-L on wearable seizure detection with fewer parameters.
Reliability-aware latent fusion (RALF) then integrates summary tokens from the
independently pre-trained encoders using per-sample reliability weights. On
SeizeIT2, fusion raises event sensitivity at 11 false alarms/h from 61.4 % to
74.4 % with RALF, while RALF boosts
window-level AUPRC from 0.321 to 0.399 over the best unimodal model and
outperforms weighted-logit and concatenation fusion in AUPRC across all six
training variants. PhysioWave-T also sets new state-of-the-art sEMG accuracies and
the highest macro-F1 on PTB-XL Super and Chapman–Shaoxing, establishing an
efficient, extensible foundation for biosignal representation learning. Code
and models are available at https://github.com/ForeverBlue816/PhysioWave . Index Terms: Biosignal foundation models, self-supervised learning,
token efficiency, heterogeneous acquisition, multimodal fusion. I Introduction Physiological signals reflect the underlying dynamics of the
human body across diverse temporal scales, yet their acquisition hardware and
recording protocols are far from uniform. Across clinical and consumer settings,
EEG spans two-channel wearables to 128-channel scalp arrays; ECG ranges from
single-lead ambulatory patches to clinical twelve-lead systems; and sEMG is
captured by wristbands, forearm arrays, and high-density grids, each governed by
a distinct sampling rate and sensor geometry. A truly reusable representation
must capture multiscale physiological structure while flexibly accommodating this
hardware diversity, yet these two desiderata pull directly against each other.
Wavelet decompositions are well suited to non-stationary biosignals, but exposing
every scale to a Transformer incurs a steep quadratic cost: a J J -level
decomposition of C C channels and S S patches yields ( J + 1 ) ​ C ​ S (J{+}1)CS tokens, causing
channel density, window duration, and decomposition depth to lengthen the token
sequence in tandem. Mapping every recording onto a single fixed input layout
sidesteps this variability, but intermediate heuristics such as channel
selection, padding, or resampling inevitably discard acquisition-specific
information along the way. Our prior works, WaveFormer [ 1 ] and PhysioWave [ 2 ] , learn multiscale representations of ECG
and sEMG through adaptive wavelet selection, soft gating, cross-scale feature
aggregation, and frequency-guided masked pre-training. Nevertheless, three design choices in PhysioWave limit how far this paradigm scales. Its
tokenizer expanded every wavelet scale into a separate token row, and each
encoder was constrained to a single fixed input configuration. Both limitations
are most severe for EEG, whose clinical montages vary widely across datasets and
whose dense electrode arrays rapidly multiply the token count. PhysioWave’s multimodal experiments
relied on an external pre-trained backbone and combined modalities using fixed
learned weights that could not adapt to the signal quality of individual samples.
These bottlenecks raise three central questions that anchor this work:
how to retain multiscale time-frequency information without multiplying tokens,
how to share a single architecture across heterogeneous acquisition setups, and
how to fuse modalities whose reliability varies across recordings. PhysioWave-T addresses the first two questions by decoupling acquisition-specific
processing from representation learning. Each acquisition route, defined by a
channel count and sampling rate, receives a dedicated wavelet front end and
sampling-rate-specific patch projection, while sharing a single Transformer
backbone and a name-based channel vocabulary across the modality. Within each
front end, dynamic scale folding recombines wavelet subbands prior to
tokenization using weights predicted from local subband statistics and refined
by learned synthesis filters. Consequently, the backbone processes C ​ S CS rather
than ( J + 1 ) ​ C ​ S (J{+}1)CS tokens regardless of decomposition depth: deeper analysis
enriches individual tokens instead of multiplying sequence length. Because
folding contains no channel-specific parameters, it transfers unchanged across
montages; an unseen downstream configuration requires only attaching a
lightweight front end to the frozen or fine-tuned backbone and appending any
new sensor names to the vocabulary. These mechanisms allow PhysioWave to scale to EEG, yielding a unified EEG
foundation model as a central contribution of this work. Without coercing
recordings into a common montage, we pre-train a 30M-parameter encoder on over
15 M four-second windows spanning clinical, research, brain–computer
interface, and high-density corpora (19–128 channels at 256 and 512 Hz).
Across four EEG routes, scale folding reduces Transformer latency by
4.0–7.2 × \times and peak memory by 6.8–12.7 × \times over scale expansion,
cutting per-batch memory on 128-channel recordings from ∼ 32 {\sim}32 GB to
2.5 GB. Against BENDR, BIOT, LaBraM, and EEGPT, our encoder leads on eight of
nine metrics across sleep staging, P300, and error-related negativity
detection—including on Sleep-EDFx, whose 100 Hz derivations are unseen
during pre-training. On two-channel behind-the-ear seizure detection, it reaches
61.4 % sensitivity at ≤ 11 {\leq}11 false alarms per hour, outperforming LUNA-L
with 25.4 M versus 40.4 M parameters. Pre-training also boosts label
efficiency: using just 10 % of KaggleERN labels, the encoder surpasses a
fully supervised scratch model. Finally, the learned representations capture
intrinsic physiology: channel embeddings recover scalp topography without
coordinate supervision, and the slowest wavelet scale’s weight progressively
increases from wakefulness to deep sleep. For the third question, reliability-aware latent fusion (RALF) combines this EEG
encoder with independently pre-trained ECG and sEMG encoders, without joint
multimodal pre-training. Each encoder contributes four summary tokens. A
per-sample reliability score gates that modality’s contribution to attention
and the residual unimodal prediction, and missing modalities are masked
explicitly. On SeizeIT2, with simultaneous wearable EEG, ECG, and EMG, RALF
raises event sensitivity at 11 false alarms per hour from 61.4 % to 78.9 %
and window-level AUPRC from 0.321 to 0.399 relative to the best EEG
model. All six training variants exceed weighted-logit and concatenation fusion
in AUPRC, most clearly on ECG-containing combinations. RALF also retains an
AUPRC of at least 0.33 under every tested single-modality corruption. Relative
to PhysioWave, the contributions are: • Token-efficient multiscale modeling. Dynamic scale folding
shortens the sequence by a factor of J + 1 J{+}1 while retaining adaptive wavelet
analysis. We quantify its effects on accuracy, latency, memory, and
computation. • An EEG foundation model across acquisition configurations. Route-specific front ends and extensible channel vocabularies let one shared
30M-parameter backbone learn from heterogeneous montages and transfer to
unseen ones, including two-channel wearable EEG. • Reliability-aware multimodal prediction. RALF fuses compact
modality summaries using sample-specific reliability estimates. We evaluate
it on seizure detection under complete, missing, and corrupted inputs. Fig. 1: Overview of PhysioWave-T, shown for EEG. (a) Channel count and sampling

folded-wavelet patches against stop-gradient targets. II Related Work II-A Physiological Foundation Models Following BERT [ 3 ] and MAE [ 4 ] , masked
reconstruction and predictive learning have produced transferable EEG
representations. BENDR [ 5 ] and Brant [ 6 ] established contrastive and predictive pre-training. BIOT tokenizes
channel-wise spectral patches [ 7 ] , LaBraM predicts discrete
neural codes [ 8 ] , EEGPT combines masked reconstruction with
spatiotemporal alignment [ 9 ] , and CBraMod separates spatial and
temporal interactions through criss-cross attention [ 10 ] .
Beyond EEG, ST-MEM [ 11 ] , HuBERT-ECG [ 12 ] ,
and ECG-FM [ 13 ] apply masked modeling or self-distillation to
ECG, and large wristband datasets support sEMG models for movement and
interaction [ 14 , 15 ] . Generic time-series
models such as MOMENT [ 16 ] and UniTS [ 17 ] pursue broader transfer. These models differ mainly in their tokenization and
pre-training targets. PhysioWave-T instead addresses the interface between
acquisition and representation: configuration-specific processing precedes a
shared token space, and multiscale analysis does not lengthen the backbone
sequence. II-B Multiscale Representations and Heterogeneous Inputs Wavelets localize signal structure in time and
frequency [ 18 , 19 ] . Best-basis
selection [ 20 ] and coefficient
shrinkage [ 21 ] adapt this representation to signal
structure or noise, and neural wavelet operators make its filters
trainable [ 22 ] . WaveFormer [ 1 ] introduced
soft wavelet selection for sEMG, and PhysioWave [ 2 ] paired
learnable wavelet analysis with frequency-guided pre-training for ECG and sEMG.
However, PhysioWave allocated a separate token row to every band. PhysioWave-T retains these analysis components but folds the subbands before tokenization,
so decomposition depth no longer lengthens the sequence. Generalizing classical best-basis selection, the fold replaces discrete wavelet-packet tree search with a learned, continuous scale mixture. Heterogeneous acquisition poses a separate challenge. Perceiver
architectures compress variable inputs into latent
arrays [ 23 ] , mixture-of-experts models route tokens
among specialized subnetworks [ 24 , 25 ] ,
and channel-independent designs process each channel
separately [ 26 ] . In EEG, name-based embeddings identify
electrodes [ 8 , 9 ] , graph models encode spatial
adjacency [ 27 ] , and LUNA [ 28 ] maps variable
electrode sets to a fixed set of latent queries. PhysioWave-T selects a front end
deterministically from acquisition metadata rather than learning token-level
routing. It also extends name-based identity to ECG leads and sEMG electrode
sites, adding vocabulary entries during downstream adaptation. II-C Multimodal Physiological Fusion Combining biosignals improves prediction when modalities carry complementary
information, yet acquisition failures rarely affect all sensors equally.
Even available modalities may differ substantially in their signal quality.
Latent and bottleneck fusion restrict cross-modal interaction to compact
summaries [ 23 , 29 ] , avoiding attention
over every unimodal token. RALF follows this design and adds a sample-specific
reliability estimate for each modality, supervised by corruption severity,
while explicit masks handle absent inputs. III PhysioWave-T Architecture PhysioWave-T is built on a simple principle: acquisition-specific processing
happens before tokenization, and representation learning happens after it.
Route-specific front ends absorb differences in channel count and sampling
rate, mapping every recording into a common token space in which one
Transformer per modality learns shared physiological structure. These front
ends retain PhysioWave’s learnable wavelet analysis [ 2 ] but differ in two respects: they fold the wavelet scales into one signal before
tokenization, and they attach an explicit identity to every channel. The first
change keeps the sequence short, and the second lets the same backbone
recognize sensors across configurations. Concretely, Fig. 1 traces a window X ∈ ℝ C × T X\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times T} , with C C channels and T T samples, through the
encoder. The front end first decomposes each channel into J J detail subbands
and one approximation, then folds these J + 1 J{+}1 bands back into a single signal X ~ ∈ ℝ C × T \tilde{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times T} with the shape of the input.
Fixed-duration patches of X ~ \tilde{X} are projected into tokens, to which
learned channel-name embeddings and sinusoidal position embeddings are added.

PhysioWave’s scale expansion produces ( J + 1 ) ​ C ​ S (J{+}1)\,C\,S . Decomposition depth
therefore enriches the content of each token without lengthening the sequence,
an advantage that grows with the number of channels and matters most for dense
EEG arrays. The following subsections describe each stage in turn. III-A Routes and Modular Front Ends Physiological corpora rarely share a single input format, and forcing them into

encoder can then be adapted to a downstream configuration or combined with the
other two through reliability-aware latent fusion. IV-A Self-Supervised Pre-training Pre-training extends PhysioWave’s masked reconstruction in two ways: masking
acts on the raw input, and the encoder reconstructs two complementary targets
(Fig. 1 (c)–(d)). Masking tokens alone would not suffice for
this front end, because convolution and cross-scale fusion mix neighboring
samples before tokenization, leaving hidden content accessible through adjacent
features. Each window is therefore processed in two views. The clean view
supplies reconstruction targets and mask-selection scores, while the online
view zeros the selected raw-signal patches before the front end, so their
samples never enter the online features. After tokenization, the masked
positions are replaced by a learned mask token. Patch selection follows
PhysioWave’s frequency-guided strategy: spectral importance e e is computed from
the summed magnitude spectrum along the clean-view token sequence and combined
with uniform noise z z as s = 0.6 ​ e + 0.4 ​ z s=0.6\,e+0.4\,z . The highest-scoring 70 % of
valid tokens are masked, so spectrally dominant patches are hidden most often
and must be inferred from context. For ECG, the six limb leads within a
temporal patch are masked together, since their linear dependencies would
otherwise leak the hidden content, and precordial leads are masked
individually. Unavailable channel slots are excluded from both mask selection

learned synthesis filters account for 0.7 points, since replacing every g s g_{s} with a delta costs that much, and the data-dependent weights α \alpha account
for the remaining 0.9. Both components are therefore needed to match and then
exceed scale expansion, and the full fold achieves this with a predictor
shared across channels. Fig. 8 quantifies the cost side on one A100 in bf16
with matched route-specific batch sizes. Across the four EEG routes, folding
reduces Transformer latency by 4.0–7.2 × \times , peak memory by
6.8–12.7 × \times , and FLOPs by 4.3–5.5 × \times . Memory shrinks more than
FLOPs because the attention maps, whose size grows quadratically with sequence
length, dominate activation memory; latency shrinks less than memory because
the front end and the linear projections keep their cost. All three savings
grow with token count and peak on the 128-channel route, where a batch needs
about 32 GB without folding and 2.5 GB with it in this profiling setup.
These figures use matched batch sizes and therefore differ from the
pre-training memory reported in Section V-A . The fold itself,
including its subband statistics and weighting network, accounts for only
1.5–2.6 % of the forward pass, so its accuracy benefit comes at negligible
cost. Fig. 8: EEG encoder efficiency on one A100 in bf16, averaged over three seeds. (a) Latency per window. (b) Peak memory per batch. (c) Savings versus token count. Pre-training and adaptation. Removing pre-training lowers the four-metric average by 3.7 points, with the
loss concentrated on KaggleERN, where balanced accuracy and AUROC fall by 4.7
and 6.3 points. The adaptation ladder shows why the two tasks differ. On
PhysioNet-P300, the frozen encoder is within one balanced-accuracy point of
full fine-tuning, and tuning the top two or four blocks changes little, so the
pre-trained representation already separates target from non-target responses.
On KaggleERN, the frozen encoder reaches only 53.7 %, below the 57.0 % of
the scratch model, and balanced accuracy climbs monotonically with the number
of tuned blocks: 57.9 % with two, 59.8 % with four, and 61.7 % with the
full backbone. Error-related negativity is a brief, low-amplitude deflection
embedded in ongoing frontocentral activity, and pre-training on four-second
windows of clinical EEG does not isolate it on its own. The pre-trained weights
nonetheless provide the better starting point, since fine-tuning from them ends
4.7 points above training from scratch. Channel identity. Setting the channel-encoding gate to zero in ( 12 ) lowers the
average score by 1.8 points, and shuffling channel names lowers it by 2.1. The
second number is the more informative one: the shuffled model keeps the same
embedding parameters and receives spatial information that is simply wrong,
and it performs worse than a model given no spatial information at all. The
benefit of correct identity therefore comes from the scalp geometry the
embeddings encode (Section VI-A ), not from added
capacity. The losses concentrate on PhysioNet-P300, where removing channel
encoding costs 2.6 balanced-accuracy and 2.8 AUROC points, compared with 0.9
and 1.1 on KaggleERN. The P300 response has a well-defined parietal
distribution, so knowing which electrode produced each token helps locate it;
the error-related negativity is spatially more diffuse, and the task relies
less on electrode identity. Readout. Mean pooling outperforms the EEGPT temporal head by 2.3 points and attentive
statistics pooling by 1.1 points on average, with the temporal head losing 3.7
balanced-accuracy points on PhysioNet-P300. Both alternatives add trainable
readout capacity on top of the backbone, and both hurt. Under full fine-tuning,
the backbone can already arrange its tokens so that a simple average is
linearly separable, and the extra readout parameters mainly add variance on
datasets of this size. The temporal head, designed for probing a frozen
encoder in EEGPT, is the more complex of the two and suffers most. Label efficiency. Reducing supervision to 10 % of the labels costs the pre-trained encoder 4.8
points on average, against 8.7 points for the matched scratch model, so
pre-training roughly halves the loss from scarce labels. The absolute numbers
on KaggleERN are more telling: with 10 % of the labels, the pre-trained
encoder already exceeds the scratch model trained on all labels, by 0.8
balanced-accuracy and 1.2 AUROC points, and with 25 % the margins widen to
2.6 and 3.8 points. One tenth of the annotations is therefore enough to match
full-data training from scratch on this task. The effect is weaker on
PhysioNet-P300, where the scratch model is itself strong; there, 25 % of the
labels bring the pre-trained encoder within 0.9 balanced-accuracy points of
full-data scratch. Missing electrodes. Dropping 25 % and 50 % of the electrodes at test time, without imputation
or retraining, lowers the average score by 2.1 and 4.2 points, so performance
degrades gradually and in proportion to the electrodes removed. With a quarter
of the electrodes missing, KaggleERN balanced accuracy remains 60.4 %, still
above EEGPT with the full montage (58.4 %), and with half missing it nearly
matches that baseline at 58.3 % and still exceeds the scratch model with every
electrode (57.0 %). Because absent slots are masked and the remaining channels
keep their name embeddings, the encoder continues to interpret the electrodes it
receives without any adaptation to the reduced montage. VI Analysis of Learned Representations We examine the trained encoders through their spectral responses, scale allocation,

largely below the range the pre-trained sEMG filters emphasize. Basis selection becomes more decisive with depth (Fig. 10 b). At
level one, every route keeps a mixture over its five candidate wavelets, whereas
at level three the weights concentrate on a single basis within each modality’s

blend of wavelet shapes, while the smoother coarse approximations are described
well by one basis, and the selector learns this division without supervision. VI-C Scale Allocation Across Sleep Stages Fig. 11: Scale allocation across sleep stages. Rows show Fpz-Cz and Pz-Oz. Sleep staging offers a direct test of whether the folding weights track

We analyze the encoder fine-tuned on Sleep-EDFx over 37,902 thirty-second

responding differently to their raw low-frequency power, showing that it
allocates scales according to what the low-frequency content represents on
each derivation. VII Discussion PhysioWave-T rests on one organizing principle: everything that depends on how a
signal was acquired is handled before tokenization, and everything that
depends on what it contains is learned afterward by a shared backbone. This is
why a single 30 M-parameter EEG encoder can learn from 19- to 128-channel
montages and then transfer to a two-channel wearable, and why decomposition
depth becomes a choice about the content of each token instead of the length
of the sequence. The analyses show that the efficiency costs nothing in
physiological meaning: identically initialized front ends settle on the QRS,
100–200 Hz, and γ \gamma ranges for their modalities, the folding weights
track sleep stages without stage labels, and the channel vocabulary recovers
scalp topology from electrode names alone, which makes coordinates an
unnecessary input and lets the same identity mechanism cover ECG leads and
sEMG sites. The experiments also clarify when pre-training matters and how to use it. Its
benefit is smallest where downstream data already cover the test conditions
and largest under distribution shift and label scarcity, as in inter-session
sEMG and KaggleERN, where a tenth of the labels match full-data training from
scratch. Some tasks are served by the frozen representation, whereas subtle
responses such as error-related negativity require adapting the backbone, so
probing first and fine-tuning when needed is the natural recipe. For
multimodal monitoring, RALF shows that per-sample reliability pays off when
artifact-prone ECG must be weighed against EEG and at strict false-alarm
budgets, and its scores fall in proportion to how much signal a corruption
leaves. The corruptions studied here are synthetic; prospective recordings
with natural sensor failures, joint multimodal pre-training, and new routes for
modalities such as photoplethysmography are the next steps. VIII Conclusion PhysioWave-T shows that a biosignal foundation model can accommodate heterogeneous
acquisition without paying for it in sequence length. Route-specific wavelet
front ends and name-based channel embeddings let one backbone per modality
learn from any sensor configuration, dynamic scale folding keeps multiscale
analysis within C ​ S C\,S tokens, and the resulting EEG, ECG, and sEMG encoders
lead the compared models across sleep staging, event-related potentials,
cardiac diagnosis, and gesture recognition while cutting Transformer memory by
up to an order of magnitude. Reliability-aware fusion extends them to
multimodal seizure detection under missing and corrupted inputs, and new
acquisition setups attach to the released backbones with a new front end
instead of a new model. Acknowledgments This research was supported by NTU Nanyang Assistant Professor Startup Grant 025661-00021:Efficient Signal Processing Algorithms and Hardware for Accelerated Machine Learning. PhysioWave was partly supported by the EU Horizon Europe projects IntelliMan
