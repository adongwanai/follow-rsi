SWAM: Scaling World Action Models with Cross-Modal Alignment

1 Introduction

Pretrained video models learn associations between language descriptions, visual content, and visual dynamics, acquiring visual and dynamics priors across scenes, objects, and interactions [1, 2, 3, 4]. These experiences provide a foundation for robots to understand manipulation goals and predict environmental changes [5, 6]. World action models (WAMs) jointly predict video and actions conditioned on language, connecting visual changes to robot actions and providing a route from video pretraining to robot control [7, 8]. This paper studies how to better use these priors to extend language-conditioned video modeling into generalizable action capabilities.

When we introduce action prediction, the language–video capabilities acquired in video pretraining should be preserved. This requires further alignment between language, video, and action. It bridges the gap between language-described targets, visual observation, and executed actions. Existing work on WAMs largely focused on model architectures and training mechanisms [9, 10]. Methods on video pretraining often treat the language–video alignment as an established foundation [7, 8]. However, introducing action learning raises a basic question: how to preserve established language–video prior and further solve language–video–action misalignment? The roles of different experiences and forms of supervision in this process, particularly for control generalization, remain unclear [9, 10, 11, 12].

To study these questions, we first construct a multisource data pool with approximately 47,000 hours, comprising approximately 11,000 hours of general videos, 11,000 hours of egocentric videos, 15,000 hours of in-house UMI demonstrations, and 10,000 hours of robot demonstrations, as shown in Figure 2(a). These sources provide different forms of cross-modal supervision. General and egocentric videos primarily provide language–video supervision, covering general visual dynamics and human interactions, respectively. UMI demonstrations record video and action trajectories of humans manipulating objects with handheld grippers, while robot demonstrations provide observations, states, and actions during robot task execution. Both include language descriptions corresponding to the manipulation process, supporting language–video–action learning in addition to language–video supervision. UMI demonstrations provide scalable human manipulation experience, whereas robot demonstrations provide direct but more limited robot control experience [13, 14, 15]. The sources differ in scene and task coverage and in the completeness of language and action supervision. Multisource pretraining therefore requires combining these experiences appropriately: language–video supervision preserves video pretraining capabilities, while human and robot action supervision establishes video–action correspondences [16, 17, 18].

Building on the constructed multisource experience, we conduct systematic studies at three levels: language–video alignment, language–video–action alignment, and joint learning. Specifically, at the language–video level, we examine how to preserve the visual prior in pretrained video generation with the design of language supervision. At the language–video–action level, we investigate how to utilize action supervision from UMI and robot demonstrations to help action prediction and robot control. In joint training, we study the data recipe and model architecture for better cross-modality modeling. The data recipe determines the coverage of the visual and interaction experience in tasks, scenes, and the proportions of different experiences. The model architecture answers how to conduct cross-modal supervision through modality interactions and language conditioning pathways, as shown in Figure 2(b). We further examine the benefits of scaling training with the resulting data and supervision recipe.

Our study shows that preserving language–video capabilities and extending language–video–action capabilities jointly support control generalization in WAMs. Detailed manipulation descriptions and environmental cues improve language–video supervision, which maintains language–video alignment and thereby improves control generalization. And UMI demonstrations improve robot control in simulation and real scenes, proving the value of human manipulation experience with action supervision for robot learning. In joint learning, the benefits of multisource experience depend both on data and model supervision recipe. Experiments show that increasing training investment under the selected data and model recipe further improves control generalization. These findings provide empirical guidance for data collection and organization, supervision design, and joint training in WAMs. The pretraining recipe derived from these findings achieves success rates of 88.10% and 61.56% under the RoboTwin Easy (Clean-to-Clean) and Hard (Clean-to-Random) settings, respectively. Ours achieves 40.00% SR under the Hard setting across three real-world tasks, exceeding OpenWAM’s 23.33%. This result indicates better generalization to unseen objects and backgrounds.

2 Related Work

World Action Models. World action models aim to connect visual observations with executable robot actions [7]. Early work treated video as a transferable visual plan or dynamic representation. UniPi first generates a language-conditioned visual plan and then recovers actions with an inverse-dynamics module [19]. VPP conditions a policy on predictive representations from a video model [6]. Motus, DreamZero, and LingBot-VA place visual dynamics and action prediction within unified or interactive architectures, exploring discrete tokens, latent actions, joint flow matching, and causal video–action modeling [20, 7, 8]. Fast-WAM and InternW0-Δ\Delta further distinguish future supervision during training from action generation at inference, showing that the prediction target and the deployment interface can be separated [21, 17]. VPP2 and LingBot-VA adapt video pretraining to robot manipulation by studying how manipulation data organization, temporal and video representation modeling, and action-branch adaptation can preserve video prediction capabilities while supporting action learning [18, 8]. We build on this foundation and focus on how language–video capabilities from video pretraining can transfer to language–video–action learning and action control.

Multisource Data for Robot Learning. Robot learning increasingly draws on cross-embodiment trajectories, general videos, egocentric videos, and human manipulation demonstrations rather than a single source of robot data. Open X-Embodiment, Octo, and OpenVLA aggregate and filter multi-robot data to study shared representations, observation adaptation, and action-space alignment, showing that cross-embodiment learning requires source normalization and interface alignment [22, 23, 24]. General and egocentric videos expand coverage of scenes, objects, and interaction dynamics, but usually lack action labels directly corresponding to robot execution [25, 26]. EgoMimic and EgoScale study how such human experience can be transferred through view and embodiment alignment, including settings with limited robot data [27, 28]. UMI complements these sources by using portable interfaces to record manipulation trajectories in an end-effector-like interaction format, thereby providing action supervision associated with the manipulation process [13, 14, 15]. EgoWAM, Ego4WAM, and Motus further demonstrate the complementary roles of video, action trajectories, semantic descriptions, and world-prediction targets [29, 10, 20]. The central issue in multisource learning is therefore not simply adding sources, but understanding differences in scene coverage, interaction dynamics, embodiment interfaces, and available supervision. We curate approximately 47,000 hours of multisource data. We use this pool to systematically examine how experience combinations and supervision design affect WAM pretraining.

3 Method

This section presents the world action model and its multimodal supervision. We first present the problem formulation, model architecture, and training objectives, then describe multisource experience curation and supervision construction. Pretraining uses four types of experience: general videos, egocentric videos, UMI demonstrations, and robot demonstrations. These sources differ in scene and task distributions, data quality, manipulation diversity, and available action supervision. General and egocentric videos provide language–video supervision, while UMI and robot demonstrations additionally provide state and action signals where available and valid. In Table 2 and Figure 2, we summarize the composition, scale, and video-duration distribution of the pretraining pool after data curation and cleaning.

3.1 World Action Modeling

3.1.1 Problem Formulation

We consider language-conditioned robot manipulation. Given the visual observations acquired so far, a language instruction, and its current proprioceptive state, the robot predicts actions over a future interval. Let nn be a prediction start point sampled from a trajectory, K>0K>0 a fixed prediction horizon, V0:nV_{0:n} the visual history including the current observation, PP the language condition, and SnS_{n} the current state. The control task requires learning an action policy πθ\pi_{\theta} conditioned on visual history, the language instruction, and the current state:

An:n+K∼πθ(⋅∣V0:n,P,Sn),A_{n:n+K}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid V_{0:n},P,S_{n}), (1)

where An:n+KA_{n:n+K} denotes the action sequence over the target interval, and θ\theta denotes the model parameters.

Control learning primarily supervised by observation–action pairs must jointly address scene understanding, interaction dynamics, and action generation. We adopt a world action model (WAM) based on a pretrained video model, using the associations between language descriptions and visual dynamics learned during video pretraining and learning correspondences between manipulation processes and executed actions through action supervision. The model jointly predicts video changes and actions over the same interval. The video sequence Vn:n+KV_{n:n+K} describes observable scene evolution during manipulation, while the action sequence An:n+KA_{n:n+K} describes the corresponding manipulation behavior. The two sequences span the same physical time interval but may use different sampling rates.

We denote the joint predictive distribution of video and actions by pθp_{\theta}:

=pθ(Vn:n+K∣V0:n,P)pθ(An:n+K∣V0:n+K,P,Sn).\displaystyle=p_{\theta}(V_{n:n+K}\mid V_{0:n},P)\,p_{\theta}(A_{n:n+K}\mid V_{0:n+K},P,S_{n}).

The final equality follows from our modeling choice that the video branch is not conditioned on the proprioceptive state. The first term learns language-conditioned video prediction, corresponding to language–video learning. The second term predicts actions from language, video information, and the current state. Together, the two terms constitute joint language–video–action learning.

Starting from a pretrained video model, we train a unified WAM end to end with a joint prediction objective. The action branch uses visual dynamics and language conditioning information supplied by the video branch. Training retains the video prediction objective while introducing action supervision, aiming to continue using existing language–video modeling capabilities and to learn correspondences between manipulation processes in video and executed actions.

Multisource experience supplies different parts of the supervision for joint prediction. General and egocentric videos provide language–video correspondences for learning language-conditioned visual dynamics. UMI and robot demonstrations additionally provide actions temporally associated with video, enabling action prediction alongside video learning. All samples participate in video learning. Samples with valid action annotations additionally participate in action learning. Missing or invalid action targets contribute no action supervision. Experiences with different supervision coverage can therefore participate in unified WAM pretraining.

3.1.2 Model Architecture

Given visual observations V={vi}i=1TV=\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T}, a language instruction PP, and available states SS, we adopt a WAM that jointly predicts video and actions. Whereas n:n+Kn:n+K above denotes a trajectory-level prediction interval, we use tt below to index temporally aligned video–action chunks. Each chunk contains 2 video latent frames and 48 action steps. For chunk tt, let Vt∈ℝTv×H×W×3V_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{v}\times H\times W\times 3} denote the corresponding video, St∈ℝDsS_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{s}} the available state at the chunk start, and At∈ℝHa×DaA_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{H_{a}\times D_{a}} the normalized target action sequence. Here, TvT_{v} is the number of video frames, HH and WW are frame height and width, DsD_{s} is the state dimension, and HaH_{a} and DaD_{a} are the number of action steps and the action representation dimension, respectively. A VAE encodes video into latents xtx_{t}. Separate encoders map actions and states to tokens. A text encoder maps the language instruction to condition pp.

We illustrate the model architecture and the attention masks used during training and inference in Figure 3. The model uses a MoT architecture [30], with the video branch initialized from Wan [2] and the action branch randomly initialized. The action DiT branch comprises action and state encoders, transformer blocks, and an action decoder. Video and action maintain separate residual streams, with joint attention passing information unidirectionally from video to action. The video token stream passes through the video branch and causally attends to current and past video tokens. The action token stream passes through the action branch and attends to video tokens from past and current chunks, as well as state and action tokens within the current chunk. This unidirectional interaction allows samples with video supervision alone to continue training the video branch without directly updating the action branch through the video objective, supporting the use of videos without action supervision.

The video branch receives encoded language conditions through cross-attention. The action branch uses language information only through attention interactions with the video branch and has no direct text cross-attention. This conditioning pathway aims to reuse the language–video correspondences established during video pretraining, allowing action prediction to use semantic information associated with the manipulation process. The video branch predicts flow velocities in video latent space, while the action branch decodes its output tokens into flow velocities in action space.

3.2 Language–Video Alignment

3.2.1 Video Sources and Curation

In Figure 4, we summarize the curation process used to turn the four experience sources into training-ready video–language examples. We perform quality checks, standardize the video records, segment long videos into subtasks where applicable, and generate multi-level captions before mixing the resulting data for pretraining.

General videos. We use OpenVid [32] and Panda70M [33], totaling 10,909.6 hours. These open-source video generation datasets contain videos and text descriptions covering diverse scenes, objects, and visual dynamics. We retain their videos and original descriptions to provide language–video experience beyond manipulation demonstrations.

Egocentric videos. We use Ego4D [25], EgoVerse [26], and EgoSuite [34], totaling 371,212 trajectories and 10,986.3 hours. Ego4D primarily records daily activities across real-life settings. EgoVerse covers more diverse scenes, adding egocentric visual experience across environments. EgoSuite is currently the largest open-source egocentric manipulation video dataset. We retain the videos and language descriptions from these sources.

Data preparation. We package all sources in LeRobot 2.1 format. We retain only samples whose videos, language descriptions, and metadata can be linked. We exclude samples with unassociated video, language, or metadata, videos with encoding or decoding failures, and known low-quality samples. Video records retain their corresponding language descriptions and metadata. The inventory counts the recorded episodes and videos at their source durations.

Visual content filtering. For general video datasets such as OpenVid and Panda70M, we use original annotations and VLM-based content review to remove animations, static scenes, television programs, and other unsuitable content. For egocentric videos, we use a VLM to assess motion blur, brightness, occlusion, and whether a clip contains meaningful actions, reducing the impact of low-quality imagery and uninformative behavior on training. The UMI and robot demonstrations described below use similar filtering rules.

Video model adaptation on general videos. Our WAM modifies Wan’s video modeling design to support robot control, which may disrupt pretrained visual and dynamics priors. To ease this adaptation, we first train the video branch on general videos, whose distribution is close to that of Wan’s pretraining data. During this stage, we use the same input frame count, spatial resolution, and causal attention scheme as subsequent WAM training. This stage aims to preserve these priors during adaptation, before introducing embodied data.

3.2.2 Language Annotation

Language annotations describe manipulated objects and executed actions and may additionally include interaction relationships, manipulation processes, and environmental information. For WAM pretraining, what semantic information helps translate video experience into control capabilities? Can these annotations help preserve language–video alignment and improve downstream generalization? We organize language supervision along three dimensions: the level of detail in manipulation descriptions, environmental information, and temporal granularity.

Level of detail. Task descriptions cover executed actions, interaction objects, and their attributes during manipulation. We define three levels of descriptive detail, Brief, Medium, and Detailed, with requirements and examples in Table 3. These levels define controlled language-annotation variants for evaluating how description detail affects the transfer of pretrained video capabilities to robot control.

The three descriptions convey the same manipulation. The Brief level specifies the main action and object, the Medium level adds the object’s appearance, and the Detailed level further specifies hand involvement and action order.

Scene information. We use Qwen 3.8 Max [35] and Seed 2.1 Pro [36] for subtask segmentation and caption annotation. Scene annotations supplement task descriptions with the working environment, spatial layout, and initial state. Lighting annotations describe brightness, exposure, illumination uniformity, and shadows. For example, the scene annotation for one clip is: “The manipulation takes place on a table covered with a black-and-white striped tablecloth, with various packaged snacks scattered across the surface.” Its lighting annotation is: “Indoor lighting is bright and uniform, with no obvious shadows or overexposed regions. All snacks and the manipulation area are clearly visible.”

Temporal granularity. Episode descriptions cover an entire trajectory, while Subtask descriptions correspond to local manipulation segments with start and end times. A VLM segments continuous video into subtasks and outputs each segment’s timestamps and description. Descriptive detail and temporal granularity are independent annotation dimensions: the same subtask can have descriptions at different detail levels, and an entire trajectory can also have a brief or detailed description. During training, the Episode setting uses the description of the full trajectory, while the Subtask setting uses the annotation of the corresponding time interval as the language condition.

3.3 Language–Video–Action Alignment

UMI and robot demonstrations follow the video curation and filtering procedures in Section 3.2.1 and use the language annotations described in Section 3.2.2. Building on these language–video correspondences, we align video segments with state and action records to construct language–video–action supervision. We describe the collection and curation of these demonstrations below.

UMI demonstrations. We use 411,754 in-house UMI trajectories totaling 15,284.9 hours. We collect human manipulation demonstrations using RoboPocket [15]. The system integrates a smartphone with a handheld adaptive gripper into a portable collection device. Operators interact with objects by moving, opening, and closing the gripper. Its structure and interaction mechanism resemble robot end effectors, reducing interface differences between human demonstrations and robot execution. Our collection tasks include mouse placement, block sorting, seasoning pouring, towel folding, bimanual snack bagging, and other everyday manipulation tasks. They involve pick-and-place, long-horizon manipulation, large wrist rotations, deformable object manipulation, and bimanual coordination. Collection environments include indoor and outdoor scenes with different lighting and background textures.

During collection, head and bilateral wrist views record the overall manipulation scene and local interaction details, respectively. Smartphone visual–inertial tracking provides action trajectories, while external encoders record gripper opening and closing. A shared coordinate system and time reference establish spatial and temporal correspondences among the videos of both hands, motion trajectories, and gripper states. The collection procedure includes equipment checks and synchronization verification before recording, tracking stability and kinematic feasibility checks during recording, and video and AR trajectory playback afterward. These checks identify anomalies such as localization drift and trajectory jumps and verify task completion.

Robot demonstrations. We curate data from AgiBot World Alpha/Beta/2026 [37], RoboCOIN [38], RoboMIND [39], InternData-A1 [40], Galaxea Open-World [41], and ABC-130K [42]. These sources span different robot embodiments, camera configurations, and manipulation environments. We retain only demonstration subsets collected with bimanual robots that include head and bilateral wrist views and meet our quality requirements, maintaining consistent data interfaces and observation semantics. We exclude subsets with missing views, inconsistent view semantics, insufficient image resolution, overly simple tasks, single-arm robot embodiments, or other issues. After applying the configured episode exclusion lists, we retain 768,356 trajectories totaling 9,836.0 hours.

State–action representation. UMI and robot demonstrations use a shared 16-dimensional bimanual end-effector representation. Each hand has a three-dimensional position, a four-dimensional rotation quaternion in wxyz order, and a one-dimensional gripper value. State and action records are associated with their corresponding video segments. Each source is normalized using its own q1 and q99 statistics.

Trajectory quality. For our in-house UMI demonstrations, we construct action-supervision masks from the state and action validity annotations provided by the collection pipeline. Videos participate in video learning, while valid action labels additionally provide action supervision and invalid labels are masked. For the collected robot demonstrations, we first check action dimensions, missing values, NaNs, non-finite values, and quaternion validity. For subsets with the required joint and end-effector state fields, we further exclude trajectories with abnormal joint angles, excessive changes in joint angles or end-effector poses between adjacent frames, or no movement throughout the trajectory. Trajectories that pass these quality checks are used for action training.

4 Experiments

We aim to use multisource experience to preserve the language–video capabilities acquired during video pretraining and extend them to generalizable robot control. Our experiments examine how language–video alignment, language–video–action alignment, joint training, and pretraining scale affect downstream generalization. We organize our study around the following four questions.

  • •

    How to establish effective cross-modal alignment?

  • •

    How to integrate multisource experience?

  • •

    Does scaling pretraining improve control generalization?

  • •

    How well does our model perform in unseen environments?

4.1 Experimental Setup

Pretraining.

We pretrain the model using the architecture and multisource corpus introduced above. The video branch is initialized from Wan2.2-TI2V-5B [2], and the action branch is randomly initialized. All model parameters are updated during training. Pretraining and post-training both use the joint video and action objective defined above. We use AdamW with an initial learning rate of 4×10−54\times 10^{-5} and a default global batch size of 256. Training steps and specific settings for each study are given in the corresponding experiment descriptions and table captions.

Simulation post-training.

We use the 50 manipulation tasks in RoboTwin 2.0 [43]. For the main simulation comparisons, we include VLA baselines (StarVLA [44], π0.5\pi_{0.5} [45], Xiaomi Robotics-0 [46], and X-VLA [47]) and video-based WAM baselines (Fast-WAM [21], LingBot-VA [8], OpenWAM [9], X-WAM [48], and 4D-WAM [49]). Observations include head and bilateral wrist-camera views and the end-effector state. The model predicts bimanual end-effector actions. Each task provides 50 Clean demonstrations, yielding 2,500 demonstrations in total. Starting from the corresponding pretrained checkpoint, we jointly post-train a single model on all 50 tasks for 20k steps with a global batch size of 128.

Simulation evaluation.

We evaluate control generalization on RoboTwin under two settings. Models are post-trained exclusively on Clean demonstrations. Easy evaluates the resulting models under the same clean conditions used for post-training (Clean-to-Clean, C2C). Hard evaluates the same models under randomized conditions, including variations in background textures, clutter, lighting, table height, and camera placement (Clean-to-Random, C2R). For each task and setting, we run 25 evaluation episodes for ablation studies and 100 episodes for main results. We report full-task success rate (SR).

Real-world post-training.

We use a bimanual Flexiv Rizon 4 platform for cup stacking, towel folding, and gift-in-box. For real-world evaluation, we select StarVLA, π0.5\pi_{0.5}, Fast-WAM, LingBot-VA, and OpenWAM as representative VLA and WAM baselines. Observations comprise two fisheye wrist-camera views and one fixed head-camera view. All compared models use the same task-specific post-training data. Starting from upstream pretrained weights, we adapt a separate model to each task for 50k steps with a global batch size of 128.

Real-world evaluation.

We evaluate under Easy (C2C) and Hard (C2R) settings. Easy varies the positions of familiar objects, and Hard changes objects, backgrounds, or both. Each task and setting uses ten layouts fixed before evaluation and shared across all models. We run three trials per layout, yielding 30 trials per task and setting. All models use synchronous inference and execute actions at 30 Hz. Success rate (SR) measures the proportion of trials that complete the entire task. Partial success rate (PSR) measures the average fraction of predefined subtasks completed per trial. We compute both metrics per task and average them equally across the three tasks.

4.2 How to establish effective cross-modal alignment?

We examine language–video and language–video–action alignment through the design of language annotations, paired action supervision, and complementary video experience. Downstream control evaluation measures the effects of these supervision choices.

4.2.1 Language–Video Alignment

Detailed and accurate language descriptions improve downstream control generalization. Pretrained video models learn rich information about scenes, objects, and motion from large-scale language–video data. During WAM pretraining, we provide informative language supervision that accurately matches video content. We aim to help the model retain pretrained priors and reduce forgetting of existing language–video capabilities when adapting to robot manipulation data. Across all settings, we pretrain on a mixture of egocentric videos and UMI demonstrations for 20k steps, examining description detail, scene information, and temporal granularity as three aspects of language supervision. For description detail, we compare three levels, Brief, Medium, and Detailed. We also compare settings with and without scene descriptions covering backgrounds, spatial layouts, and lighting. For temporal granularity, we compare Episode descriptions covering complete trajectories with Subtask descriptions of local manipulation segments.

We compare these language supervision choices in Table 6. At each temporal granularity, more detailed descriptions yield higher Hard success rates. At the Subtask level, the Hard success rate increases from 47.20% to 49.60% and 53.52% with Brief, Medium, and Detailed descriptions, respectively. Adding environment information further raises it to 55.04%, achieving the highest Easy and Hard success rates in this group. For temporal granularity, replacing Episode descriptions with Subtask descriptions under the Detailed setting increases the Hard success rate from 51.60% to 53.52%. These results indicate that richer manipulation descriptions, additional environment information, and subtask-level language supervision improve downstream control generalization. We further randomly shuffle the correspondence between Detailed Subtask descriptions and video segments within each trajectory, reducing the Hard success rate from 53.52% to 52.00%. This result further highlights the importance of accurately matched language supervision for language–video alignment.

We also find that many trajectories in the open-source robot data we examine share the same simple task instruction. Some datasets use task names or short instructions as video captions, without detailed descriptions of the manipulation process and environment. For this study, we randomly select nine subsets of the robot data, containing 145,430 trajectories but only 592 task-level instruction entries. These shared instructions do not adequately capture differences in manipulation processes and scene conditions across trajectories, limiting fine-grained correspondence between language supervision and trajectory-level visual content. We construct two variants to enrich language supervision. Rephrased provides 100 semantically equivalent paraphrases per task instruction, randomly sampling one during training to increase linguistic diversity. Detailed + Env. provides a detailed description of the specific manipulation process and environment for each trajectory, further enriching the content and diversity of language supervision. Relative to the 50.80% Hard success rate of Original, paraphrasing alone yields a gain of 3.96 percentage points, while Detailed + Env. achieves the highest success rate in this group at 56.16%, as shown in Table 6. These results further indicate that enriching linguistic expression and descriptive content helps improve language–video alignment and generalization.

4.2.2 Language–Video–Action Alignment

Human manipulation experience provides effective language–video–action supervision. Language–video supervision associates language descriptions with manipulation processes in videos, while paired action labels further provide supervision for language–video–action alignment. Direct post-training from Wan achieves a Hard SR of 34.56%. With robot-data pretraining, it reaches 56.16% in Table 6, demonstrating the value of robot manipulation data. We further examine whether human manipulation experience from UMI can provide effective cross-embodiment language–video–action supervision. To examine the contribution of UMI action supervision, we retain all UMI videos and vary only the proportion of trajectories providing action labels during pretraining. Videos without action labels continue to participate in video learning. At each coverage level, the subset of trajectories providing action supervision remains fixed. All settings use the same pretraining budget, followed by 20k steps of downstream post-training. From Table 6, we observe that as action-label coverage increases from 0% to 100%, the Hard success rate increases across all five coverage levels, rising from 46.72% to 51.28%, a gain of 4.56 percentage points. These results indicate that paired actions from human manipulation experience provide effective supervision for robot control, although UMI data differ visually from robot execution. Given that UMI demonstrations can be collected with handheld devices across diverse scenes, this finding supports human manipulation demonstrations as a source for scaling pretraining experience for robot control.

4.3 How to integrate multisource experience?

After examining the roles of language–video and language–video–action supervision, we study how to integrate multisource experience for pretraining. We first examine the benefits of increasing training on General videos, egocentric videos, and UMI demonstrations. Given differences across sources in data quality, scene and task distributions, and action supervision, we then study how the Ego–UMI sampling ratio affects downstream control generalization under a fixed training budget. Finally, we compare model architectures and conditioning designs under mixed-source training, examining how the pretrained model’s existing language–video capabilities can support action learning.

Transitional pretraining on General videos provides a stronger starting point. We first examine the role of pretraining on General videos. Their content distribution is close to that of the original video pretraining data, while WAM adjusts input frame count, resolution, and attention computation to support robot control. We therefore first use General videos to help the model adapt to these computational changes under a content distribution close to its pretraining distribution. This transition aims to better preserve existing video priors and provide a foundation for subsequent embodied learning. To this end, we add 25k steps of transitional pretraining using only General videos before 20k steps of mixed training on egocentric videos and UMI demonstrations, increasing the Hard success rate from 53.84% to 56.48%, as reported in Table 7.

Increasing pretraining exposure to individual data sources improves WAM performance. Egocentric videos broaden the range of egocentric manipulation scenarios, and UMI demonstrations provide manipulation experience paired with actions. We next increase training exposure to egocentric videos or UMI demonstrations while keeping exposure to the other source fixed. We mix egocentric videos and UMI demonstrations during training. Each source’s training exposure is calculated from the total training steps and its sampling proportion. As shown in Table 7, increasing training on egocentric videos or UMI demonstrations generally improves downstream control performance.

Equal Ego–UMI sampling yields the best performance in unseen environments. The preceding experiments increase both source exposure and the training budget. We further examine how to allocate a fixed budget across the two sources. Egocentric videos provide diverse visual experience across tasks and scenes, while UMI data provide both video and action supervision. The sampling ratio determines how these forms of supervision are allocated during training. We fix the pretraining budget at 20k steps and compare five Ego-to-UMI sampling ratios of 1:0, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2, and 0:1. In Table 8, we observe that all three mixed settings achieve higher Hard success rates than training on either source alone. Equal sampling achieves the highest Hard success rate of 53.84%, improving over UMI-only training at 51.28% by 2.56 percentage points. These results indicate that an appropriate Ego-to-UMI sampling ratio helps balance language–video and language–video–action supervision and improves control generalization in randomized scenes.

Appropriate architectural choices help preserve pretrained video priors. Under the same 30k-step multisource pretraining setup, we compare Single-stream, in which video and actions share a residual stream, with Dual-stream, which maintains separate residual streams. We also examine the language conditioning pathway of the action branch. We separate video and action feature spaces to accommodate both the reuse of pretrained video representations and the learning of new action representations. The video branch has already learned correspondences between language and visual dynamics during pretraining. The newly introduced text conditioning pathway in the action branch, however, lacks sufficient pretraining. We therefore aim to reuse the semantic information available in the video branch for action prediction, reducing potential interference from the new text conditioning pathway. To this end, we retain language conditioning in the video branch and let the action branch access language information only through video features. As reported in Table 9, Single-stream and standard Dual-stream achieve similar Hard success rates of 52.56% and 52.48%, respectively. The dual-stream model using this language conditioning pathway achieves the highest Hard success rate in this group at 57.36%, a gain of 4.88 percentage points over standard Dual-stream. These results support conditioning the action branch on language through video features.

For visual conditioning, we examine whether image-encoder features can provide benefits. We pretrain for 20k steps with equal sampling of egocentric videos and UMI data. Using Native as the baseline, we separately add image features extracted by CLIP, DINOv2, SigLIP2, and Qwen3-VL 2B [35] through cross-attention to provide additional visual conditioning. Native achieves the highest Easy and Hard success rates in this group, at 83.44% and 49.52%, respectively. Adding features from the four image encoders lowers the Hard success rate to 39.76%–42.96%, as summarized in Table 9. These results support retaining the pretrained video model’s native visual inputs. The fusion pathway for the additional image features has not undergone the same large-scale language–video pretraining as the video branch. This may lead the model to rely more on appearance cues in the current training distribution, weakening generalization to unseen scenes.

4.4 Does scaling pretraining improve control generalization?

Building on the preceding supervision design and multisource training recipe, we evaluate different pretrained checkpoints in simulation and on real robots. Within each evaluation domain, we use the same downstream post-training settings to examine how additional pretraining affects control generalization.

Increasing pretraining investment in multisource experience provides a stronger WAM. We study how model capabilities scale with pretraining, measured in training video hours. Here, 50k pretraining steps correspond to approximately 45k video hours. We use a mixture of egocentric videos and UMI demonstrations with equal sampling weights. For this study, we combine the configurations marked by gray rows in Tables 6, 8, and 9, such as using Detailed Subtask descriptions with scene and lighting information as language conditions. The 0-hour setting skips multisource pretraining and proceeds directly to downstream post-training from the video-model initialization.

As shown in Figure 5(a), multisource pretraining improves both Easy and Hard success rates over the 0-hour baseline. The highest Hard success rate reaches 59.60%, compared with 34.56% without pretraining. In Figure 5(b), we show that real-world Hard SR and PSR, averaged equally across three tasks, increase consistently as training video hours grow from 22.5k to 90k, rising from 33.33% and 46.90% to 48.89% and 61.43%, respectively. The 0-hour setting fails to complete any task. These results show continued gains within the evaluated range, supporting longer pretraining on both sources for control under real-world scene changes.

After 75k steps of pretraining on egocentric videos and UMI demonstrations, we continue pretraining on robot data for another 25k steps and apply the same RoboTwin post-training settings. In Table 10, we observe that success rates under individual changes in background, lighting, clutter, and table height improve as pretraining investment increases, with the Hard success rate ultimately reaching 61.52%.

4.5 Does our model perform well in unseen environments?

Building on the preceding findings and training recipe, we compare our models pretrained on our full multisource dataset with existing policies in RoboTwin simulation and real-world manipulation.

Simulation results. We compare VLA and WAM methods on the 50 manipulation tasks in RoboTwin 2.0. Each model is post-trained on Clean demonstrations and evaluated under Easy (C2C) and Hard (C2R) settings. As reported in Table 11, our model achieves a Hard success rate of 61.56%, outperforming all compared methods and exceeding the strongest baseline, OpenWAM, by 12.86 percentage points. Its Easy success rate is 88.10%, slightly below OpenWAM’s 89.30%, while its average success rate across both settings reaches 74.83%, the highest among the compared methods. Our model and OpenWAM perform similarly under Clean conditions, while our model achieves a higher overall success rate under the Hard setting, indicating better generalization across scene conditions.

Real-world results. We evaluate cup stacking, towel folding, and gift-in-box on a bimanual Flexiv platform. All three tasks start from the same pretrained checkpoint and undergo 50k steps of task-specific post-training. Each task-specific model is evaluated under both Easy (C2C) and Hard (C2R) settings. All compared methods use the same task-specific post-training data. Under the Hard setting, our model achieves the highest SR and PSR among the evaluated methods, as shown in Table 11 and Figure 6. Averaged equally across the three tasks, SR reaches 40.00%, exceeding OpenWAM’s 23.33% by 16.67 percentage points. PSR reaches 50.36%, exceeding π0.5\pi_{0.5}’s 37.78% by 12.58 percentage points. These results show improvements in both full-task success and subtask completion under unseen object and background conditions, supporting the generalization benefits of the training recipe in real-world robot manipulation.

Real-world visualizations.

Figure 7 shows how our model and the baselines perform the three tasks, with light red dashed boxes highlighting the baselines’ main execution errors. In cup stacking, Fast-WAM misplaces the first cup of the second layer, causing it to fall onto the cups below, and cannot complete the stack afterward. For towel folding, our model lifts and spreads both sides before completing both folds, whereas OpenWAM lifts only one side and fails to fold the towel properly. In gift-in-box, π0.5\pi_{0.5} grasps the object but gradually loosens its gripper, causing the object to fall, and remains unsuccessful after repeated attempts. Our model retains its grasp and places the object into the box. To provide a fuller view of our model’s execution, Figure 8 presents head and external camera recordings. The gift-in-box model generalizes to fruit placement with a different scene, manipulated objects, and target container. Trained with same-color cups on a gray tablecloth, the cup-stacking model also completes rainbow stack with multicolored cups on a patterned tablecloth. These examples illustrate how our model transfers learned manipulation skills to unfamiliar objects and scenes.

5 Discussion and Conclusion

In this work, we studied how to extend pretrained language–video capabilities to generalizable robot control with multisource experience. Using approximately 47,000 hours of experience, we systematically investigated language–video alignment, language–video–action alignment, and their joint learning. We found detailed and accurate language descriptions preserve pretrained language–video capabilities. Meanwhile, action supervision from UMI demonstrations further supports robot control, demonstrating the value of human manipulation experience for robot learning. Our findings prove the importance of experience organization and supervision usage. The resulting training recipe demonstrates generalization to unseen conditions in simulation and real-world evaluations, with further gains from continued pretraining. We release the data, code, and model weights to support future research.

Upon the pretrained video model, the proposed method offers limited support for complex reasoning and explicit multi-stage planning, despite outperforming VLA models in our control generalization evaluations. The limited historical context may also hinder the execution of long-range tasks. In the future, we plan to explore memory mechanisms and hierarchical planning to support tasks that require reasoning over past observations and planning subsequent actions.

Appendix A Data Annotation Details

General videos retain their original captions. For egocentric videos, UMI demonstrations, and robotic demonstrations, we construct manipulation annotations using Qwen 3.8 Max primarily for subtask segmentation and Seed 2.1 Pro for caption annotation. The procedure consists of the following steps.

Subtask Segmentation. We use Qwen 3.8 Max to divide egocentric videos and UMI recordings into temporally ordered, non-overlapping subtasks according to action intent, recording each subtask’s start and end time. Robotic demonstrations are short, so each complete episode is treated as one subtask without further segmentation.

Caption Annotation. We use Seed 2.1 Pro to annotate each egocentric or UMI subtask and each robotic episode. The model identifies whether the segment contains manipulation and whether the visual evidence is sufficiently clear, and describes the scene, lighting, and actions at multiple levels of detail. Segments that contain no manipulation or whose visual evidence is insufficient are discarded entirely.

Action Quality Filtering. For UMI and robotic data, we further screen the associated state and action trajectories. Samples that fail one or more of the following checks are removed from action training:

  • •

    Missing or non-finite values, including NaNs, invalid dimensions, or invalid quaternions.

  • •

    Excessive absolute joint values, with any |q|>20​rad\lvert q\rvert>20\,\mathrm{rad}.

  • •

    Adjacent-frame joint jumps, with any |qt+1−qt|>1​rad\lvert q_{t+1}-q_{t}\rvert>1\,\mathrm{rad}.

  • •

    End-effector position jumps, with the Euclidean distance between adjacent 3D positions exceeding 0.2​m0.2\,\mathrm{m}.

  • •

    End-effector orientation jumps, with a rotation-angle difference exceeding 90∘90^{\circ} for either arm between adjacent frames.

    Persistent abnormal jerk in the joint or end-effector trajectories.

Appendix B Training Details

B.1 Common Settings

Optimization.

Pretraining and post-training share the optimization settings in Table 12. Batch sizes, training steps, and learning-rate schedules vary across training stages, as detailed in Section B.2.

Model inputs and trainable components.

We use three camera views. Each view is resized to 128×160128\times 160 and arranged into a 2×\times2 grid, yielding a final 256×320256\times 320 composite frame. The head-camera view is duplicated across the top row, while the left- and right-wrist views occupy the bottom row. During training, each view is randomly cropped to 95% of its original extent before resizing, followed by color jitter with brightness, contrast, saturation, and hue parameters of 0.3, 0.4, 0.5, and 0.08, respectively. We represent bimanual end-effector states and actions as 16-dimensional vectors and predict actions in chunks of 48 steps.

B.2 Training Configurations

Initialization.

In pretraining, we initialize the video DiT from Wan2.2-TI2V-5B [2] and load its pretrained UMT5-XXL text encoder and Wan2.2 video VAE. The action branch, including the action and state encoders, transformer blocks, and action decoder, is randomly initialized. For each experiment, post-training starts from the corresponding pretrained weights.

Pretraining.

Training uses BF16 mixed precision. The default global batch size for ablation studies is 256. We increase it to 512 for pretraining in the scaling study and subsequent robot-data pretraining described in Section 4.4, as well as for pretraining the final models evaluated in Section 4.5. For the scaling study, we sample egocentric videos and UMI demonstrations with equal probability and use detailed subtask descriptions with scene and lighting information. We evaluate checkpoints at 25k, 50k, 75k, and 100k steps, as shown in Figure 5. Table 13 compares pretraining in the scaling study with simulation and real-world post-training.

Simulation post-training.

We jointly train a single model on all 50 RoboTwin 2.0 tasks using 50 Clean demonstrations per task, following the same post-training protocol across simulation experiments. Each sample contains left-wrist, right-wrist, and head-camera views and the current bimanual end-effector state. The same three-view preprocessing and augmentation settings described above are used during simulation post-training. We use absolute end-effector states and actions and normalize both with their q99 statistics.

Real-world post-training.

For each real-world task, we train an independent model using task-level instructions. We apply this procedure to cup stacking, towel folding, and gift-in-box.

Appendix C Real-World Evaluation Protocol

C.1 Post-Training Data Preparation

Post-training data.

We adapt models separately to cup stacking, towel folding, and gift-in-box using task-specific data for post-training following general-purpose pretraining on egocentric videos and UMI demonstrations. Table 14 summarizes the post-training data for each task.

Observations and actions.

We use a bimanual Flexiv Rizon 4 platform. Observations comprise two fisheye wrist-camera views and one fixed head-camera view. Actions encode the end-effector position, orientation, and continuous gripper width for each arm. For OpenWAM, we follow its official default configuration and use a 6D rotation representation. All other models represent orientation with a wxyz quaternion, yielding a 16-dimensional action vector across both arms.

C.2 Evaluation Protocols and Metrics

Evaluation protocols.

We evaluate under Easy (C2C) and Hard (C2R) settings. Easy settings retain the scene conditions and physical objects used in post-training, including their shapes and colors, while varying their initial configurations across layouts. Hard settings vary across scene and object attributes, including background, tabletop material, object instance, shape, color, size, and texture. The evaluation layouts are illustrated in Figure 9. All models use synchronous inference and execute actions at 30 Hz.

Layouts and trials.

A layout specifies the initial object configurations, target positions, and scene appearance. For each task and setting, ten layouts are fixed before evaluation and shared across all models. We run three trials per layout to account for variability in object placement and model sampling, yielding 30 trials per task and setting. To reproduce a layout, we align the fixed live head-camera view with a reference photograph using an image overlay.

Scoring and termination.

Each task is divided into predefined, equally weighted subtasks, scored by reviewing execution videos. The protocol permits up to five additional autonomous replanning attempts after an initial failed attempt, retaining the current environment state and execution history without human assistance. All models follow the same retry and termination rules. A trial is marked as a failure if the cup tower collapses or an object leaves the workspace.

Metrics.

We report success rate (SR) and partial success rate (PSR). For a task with N=30N=30 trials and KK subtasks, let si​k∈{0,1}s_{ik}\in\{0,1\} indicate whether subtask kk succeeds in trial ii. Complete-task success requires all subtasks to succeed, so its indicator is zi=∏k=1Ksi​kz_{i}=\prod_{k=1}^{K}s_{ik}. The two metrics are

SR=1N​∑i=1Nzi,PSR=1N​K​∑i=1N∑k=1Ksi​k.\mathrm{SR}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i},\qquad\mathrm{PSR}=\frac{1}{NK}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}s_{ik}. (6)

SR measures complete-task success. PSR measures progress at the subtask level. The three-task score is the equally weighted mean of the task-level rates, computed separately for Easy and Hard and expressed as a percentage.

Task details.

Figure 10 shows the three real-world tasks and their corresponding ordered subtask decompositions. Cup stacking requires arranging cups into a stable pyramid, towel folding requires folding a towel in half twice, and gift-in-box requires placing a gift inside an open gift box. We divide these tasks into seven, four, and two subtasks, respectively, corresponding to observable milestones in task execution. These decompositions define the task-specific subtask counts KK used in the PSR calculation above.
