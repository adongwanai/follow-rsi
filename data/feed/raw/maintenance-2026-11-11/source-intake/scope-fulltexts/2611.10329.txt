Jinwoo Hwang Sukmin Cho Tao Zhang Affiliation: Microsoft Research Asia Soheil Abbasloo   Jongse Park   Peng Cheng Affiliation: Microsoft Research Asia Affiliation: KAIST Abstract LLM agents increasingly drive systems research and rely on simulators to evaluate candidate designs at scale.
Simulators are imperfect by design: their modeling limitations can distort predictions yet leave the recommended design correct, or change which design should be selected.
We introduce Siris to test whether LLMs can tell these cases apart, judging from their systems understanding when a limitation invalidates the simulator’s recommendation.
By perturbing a simulator, Siris constructs workload pairs with the same candidates, limitation, and simulator recommendation.
The perturbation changes predictions on both workloads, yet a reference simulator supports the recommendation on only one.
Evaluating thirteen frontier LLMs on LLM serving design problems, we find that identifying a limitation does not ensure a correct choice, and even the strongest models sometimes abandon valid recommendations.
Self-correction through a review turn helps these models restore valid choices but introduces new errors in several others.
These findings motivate evaluating systems understanding through design decisions under imperfect simulators. † † footnotetext: * Work done during an internship at Microsoft Research Asia. † Corresponding author. 1 Introduction LLM agents are opening a new paradigm for research, in which agents themselves propose ideas, run experiments, and iterate on tasks that would take a skilled researcher days OpenAI (2026) ; Wen et al. (2026) .
In systems research, AI-Driven Research for Systems (ADRS) realizes this paradigm through an iterative process in which LLMs generate candidate designs, evaluate their performance, and refine them using the resulting feedback Cheng et al. (2025) .
The same process now drives data-center scheduling, serving policies, load balancing, and database optimization Novikov et al. (2025) ; Hamadanian et al. (2026) ; Lange et al. (2026) ; Cheng et al. (2026) .
As LLMs take on more of these steps, they must also interpret evaluation results and decide which candidate designs to retain or revise. Figure 1: Simulator-assisted evaluation in ADRS. A modeling limitation can invalidate its recommendation. Simulators support these evaluations with a software model of a computer system that predicts how a candidate design performs under a given workload.
This makes evaluation tractable without deploying candidates on real hardware Agrawal et al. (2024a) ; Cheng et al. (2025) ; Cheng et al. (2026) .
To remain scalable, simulators model system behavior at a chosen level of detail Heidemann et al. (2001a) . The details that simulators approximate or omit introduce modeling limitations .
For example, ignoring competition for shared network bandwidth lets a simulator scale to thousands of accelerators but can underestimate transfer delays Won et al. (2023) .
Such a limitation may alter predicted performance while leaving the best candidate unchanged, or it may change which candidate to select for a particular workload Song et al. (2015) .
Using a simulator well therefore requires judging the decision impact of its limitations, that is, whether they matter for the decision at hand Heidemann et al. (2001b) .
As LLMs take over these decisions, they must make this judgment themselves, yet how well they do so remains understudied. Figure 1 illustrates such a case, in which a cost that the limitation adds reverses the ranking of two designs and thereby invalidates the simulator’s recommendation.
We argue that this judgment is the form of systems understanding that ADRS relies on: an LLM need not predict a system’s performance on its own, but it must recognize when a simulator of that system can be trusted for a decision.
Existing evaluations do not isolate this judgment: whether a simulator’s modeling limitations invalidate its recommendation for a given design decision.
We therefore ask three questions: • RQ1 : How does simulator evidence shape the design decisions of LLMs? • RQ2 : Can LLMs judge the decision impact of a simulator’s modeling limitations? • RQ3 : Can LLMs improve this judgment via their self-correction capability? To answer these questions, we introduce Siris , an LLM evaluation framework for simulator-assisted system design review. Siris mimics the modeling limitations of real-world simulators by injecting controlled perturbations, small code changes to how the simulator represents work, costs, or resource interactions.
For each perturbation, Siris pairs two workloads on which the perturbed simulator recommends the same design.
The reference simulator supports that recommendation on one workload ( upheld ) but favors another design on the other ( overturned ). Siris operationalizes judgment as correct design selection across upheld and overturned problems.
It measures judgment by overall accuracy, the mean of the accuracies on the two problem types.
A fixed candidate preference, or a rule that always follows or always rejects the presented recommendation, solves at most one problem of each pair. Disposition summarizes the imbalance between the two accuracies.
We measure judgment and disposition at every level of simulator evidence and compare them before and after a review turn that elicits self-correction.

In systems research, a simulator is an executable model used to estimate how a design performs under a specified workload.
The workload describes the work to process, while the model represents relevant system components and their interactions.
For example, researchers can compare routing algorithms under the same network traffic by simulating packet movement and resource contention and comparing the predicted latency and throughput Jiang et al. (2013) .
This allows candidate designs to be evaluated without deploying each on the target system. Simulator use in ADRS. ADRS systems such as AlphaEvolve Novikov et al. (2025) and ShinkaEvolve Lange et al. (2026) run LLM-driven program search, in which evaluator scores select the candidates that seed subsequent changes.
In simulator-assisted ADRS, this evaluator is a simulator, so its results determine how each design is assessed.
The same results also guide which designs LLM agents explore next.
Glia Hamadanian et al. (2026) refines designs by analyzing simulation experiments, and Database ADRS Cheng et al. (2026) goes further by co-evolving a simulator with candidate policies in its buffer-cache study. 2.2 Modeling Limitations and Decisions Modeling limitations. Simulators keep evaluation tractable by modeling only the detail that their designers judge relevant to the research question Heidemann et al. (2001a) . Table 1 lists examples of such choices that simulator authors document, including omitted operations, summarized request state, simplified surrounding components, and idealized load or communication. Decision impact. Such choices matter to a design decision only through their effect on the comparison between candidates.
Ranking-and-selection analyses show that an equal additive error across alternatives cancels in pairwise differences, whereas candidate-dependent errors can widen, narrow, or reverse a performance gap Song et al. (2015) , and architecture studies warn that relative trends may not survive simulator errors that interact with the evaluated technique Nowatzki et al. (2015) .
Documented examples include errors that distort performance gaps Heidemann et al. (2001a) and errors that reverse design rankings Lin et al. (2025) .
Judging decision impact therefore requires connecting a limitation’s effect on costs and resource interactions to the specific candidates and workload. 2.3 Agents under Unreliable Evidence Imperfect external evidence. Prior work examines how unreliable evidence affects LLMs.
GARAG Cho et al. (2024b) searches for low-level document perturbations that affect retrieval and answer generation in RAG pipelines. Zhang et al. (2026) show that persistent misleading feedback can underperform a matched no-feedback condition, while Yang et al. (2026a) show that agents can recognize a conflict yet still adopt corrupted tool content. Siris shares this concern with the gap between critique and action, examining it via controlled perturbations of simulator computations. Simulation-grounded decisions. MechSim Yang et al. (2026b) has agents reason over the mechanisms and assumptions of simplified candidate simulators and test changes through re-execution, scoring their policy rankings in aggregate against a more detailed oracle simulator. Chen et al. (2026) study how LLMs rank fixed candidate controllers from simulation results under different information conditions. Siris isolates whether a particular modeling limitation invalidates a recommendation, scoring upheld and overturned problems separately.
It pairs workloads that share the candidate designs, the limitation, the metric, and the simulator’s recommendation, while the reference supports that recommendation on only one workload. Appropriate reliance. Appropriate reliance involves following correct advice and rejecting incorrect advice Schemmer et al. (2023) .
Tools Fail Sun et al. (2024) treats outputs with task-irrelevant errors as acceptable and finds that models often reject them. Siris examines a related distinction between computational accuracy and recommendation validity, since an upheld problem contains a limitation that changes predictions while the reference prefers the recommended design. 3 Siris Design Figure 2: Overview of Siris , from constructing paired problems with a perturbed simulator to scoring LLM choices. Figure 2 summarizes Siris , which tests whether an LLM can tell when a modeling limitation invalidates a simulator’s recommendation.
An LLM selects a design for a workload from fixed candidates.
We construct upheld and overturned problem pairs, vary the simulator evidence the LLM reads, and collect an initial choice followed by a review.
Comparing both choices with the reference simulator’s answer yields judgment and disposition, while a separate annotation records whether each response identifies the modeling limitation. 3.1 Problem Construction Problem format. Each Siris problem is a multiple-choice question whose options are candidate designs.
The question asks which candidate achieves the best target metric for a given workload, and its reference answer is the design the reference simulator predicts best under the fixed system specification.
The context is a set of files the LLM reads with read-only tools: the problem description , namely the system specification, candidate code, and workload, plus simulator evidence set by the evidence condition.
Each problem is built on a perturbed simulator, a copy of the reference simulator with one changed computation.
The LLM selects among the given candidates without writing a new design or repairing the simulator. Perturbations. Each perturbation is an explicit program change to a simulator computation that every candidate relies on, adapted from classic mutation operators Jia and Harman (2011) ; Papadakis et al. (2019) , that inserts a modeling limitation, any discrepancy in how the simulator represents work, costs, or resource interactions. Siris admits a change only if varying the workload can make the recommendation correct on some workloads and wrong on others, and if it is reviewable against the specification and the files the LLM reads. Table 3 in the appendix lists the perturbations. Upheld and overturned pairs. LLM agents separate from the evaluated LLMs build the problems: a builder proposes workloads, and reviewers check the evidence, as Appendix A describes.
For each proposed workload, Siris runs every candidate under both simulator versions and records their recommendations and whether the change alters predictions.
A problem is upheld when both versions recommend the same design and overturned when they differ, and the perturbed simulator’s recommendation is the presented recommendation. A perturbation pair joins an upheld and an overturned problem that share the candidates, perturbation, target metric, and presented recommendation and differ only in workload, and in both the changed computation alters predictions. Figure 2 (a) depicts one such pair.
Such pairs arise because candidates differ in how strongly the change affects them, while the workload sets how much that difference matters.
This contrast tests whether an LLM distinguishes a limitation’s presence from its impact on the design choice, since neither identifying the limitation nor a fixed preference, such as always following, can solve both problems. Validation. Independent solvers given only the problem description and the perturbed simulator’s evidence attempt each problem, and an evidence audit checks whether these files support the reference answer. Siris drops each failing problem with its pair partner and fixes every problem and pair before evaluation.
This check does not ensure that the problem description alone suffices. 3.2 Evaluation Protocol We compare evidence conditions on the same problems to study evidence use, and initial with reviewed choices to study self-correction. Evidence conditions. In Figure 2 (b), an evidence condition adds simulator evidence beyond the problem description, from none to all of the simulator source, the measured or specified cost parameters it evaluates, the modeled system’s configuration, its predictions, and its execution records.
The no-simulator condition adds none, the perturbed condition adds all of the perturbed simulator’s evidence, and the reference condition adds all of the reference simulator’s evidence as a control.
All conditions of a problem are scored against the same reference answer.
Each conversation covers one problem under one condition, so the LLM never sees the pair partner or label, and in the perturbed condition it sees no reference results and is not told what changed. Two-turn review. In both turns, the LLM can read the problem’s files with read-only tools.
The first request asks only for a recommendation with a short rationale and does not mention modeling limitations or the review that follows, so the initial choice reflects the LLM’s own reading of the evidence.
The second request then asks the LLM to review that choice and name the modeling limitation, if any, that it considers consequential.
No new evidence or correctness feedback arrives between the two turns, although the LLM may open files it skipped in the first turn. Figure 3 shows a conversation, and Appendix B gives the full protocol. 3.3 Metrics Judgment and disposition. For each model, evidence condition, and turn t t , let U t U_{t} and O t O_{t} denote accuracy on upheld and overturned problems.
A choice is correct only if it matches the reference answer, so selecting a third design on an overturned problem remains incorrect. Siris summarizes judgment by the mean of the two accuracies and disposition by their imbalance: J t = U t + O t 2 , D t = U t − O t 2 . J_{t}=\frac{U_{t}+O_{t}}{2},\qquad D_{t}=\frac{U_{t}-O_{t}}{2}. (1) In percent, J t J_{t} ranges from 0 to 100 and D t D_{t} from − 50 -50 to 50 50 .
Positive D t D_{t} indicates higher upheld accuracy and negative D t D_{t} the reverse, as in Figure 2 (c).
This imbalance differs from the rate of following the recommendation, since an override may select an incorrect alternative.
Errors that keep an invalid recommendation, abandon a valid one, or select a third design are counted separately. Review and identification. Review changes are summarized by Δ ​ J = J final − J initial \Delta J=J_{\mathrm{final}}-J_{\mathrm{initial}} and Δ ​ D = D final − D initial \Delta D=D_{\mathrm{final}}-D_{\mathrm{initial}} , together with counts of corrected and newly incorrect choices.
Every assigned problem is scored, and a missing or invalid choice is incorrect.
Separately, limitation identification records whether a response identifies the modeling limitation that the problem’s perturbation introduces, such as transfers modeled without sharing a GPU’s network interface.

Figure 6 shows that about a quarter of the responses that identify the limitation still select a design other than the reference answer, and every model makes such errors.
Even GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol, which identify it in nearly every response, still abandon some valid recommendations, on upheld problems where the limitation changes predicted values but leaves the reference answer unchanged.
These errors increase p95 latency under the reference simulator by a median of 14% per problem for the eight models with loss measurements, as Section B.2 details.
Identifying the limitation therefore does not ensure a correct decision, and evaluating identification alone would miss these errors. Review thus tends to reduce the imbalance between problem types, while its gains in judgment vary across models and can be offset by new errors. 6 Discussion Broadening the problem space. While our case study focuses on LLM serving, Siris can extend to other system domains whose simulators likewise trade fidelity for speed and scale in ways that can change design decisions, as Table 1 shows.
Doing so requires a reference that identifies the best design per workload, workloads on which a perturbation yields both upheld and overturned problems, and inspectable evidence.
Documented limitations of existing simulators could further supply naturally occurring problems when a reference establishes their decision impact.
Future work could also inject several limitations at once, whose effects may compound or cancel out, and define decision impact by their combined effect.
It could likewise move beyond multiple-choice selection of the best design to other question formats, such as ranking all candidates or proposing a revised design, defining decision impact for each format. Integrating Siris into ADRS. Beyond design review task, Siris can be extended to iterative ADRS workflows to study how judgments shape later proposals and final designs.
In workflows that select candidates directly by evaluator scores, such as AlphaEvolve Novikov et al. (2025) , search pressure may favor designs that exploit simulator limitations, so an extension could test whether an explicit LLM review results improves the search.
In workflows where LLMs already interpret simulation results, such as Glia Hamadanian et al. (2026) , the evaluation could track whether they retain or override the simulator’s recommendation across iterations.
When LLMs also modify the simulator, as in Database ADRS Cheng et al. (2026) , the evaluation would need to record the designs and evidence at each iteration, and to keep a reference independent of the evolving simulator.
This would connect judgment on individual decisions to the designs that ADRS ultimately produces. Choosing additional evidence. Siris could also evaluate whether LLMs decide what to measure before selecting a design.
When the presented evidence leaves a comparison unresolved, an LLM could request a targeted measurement under a budget, testing whether it recognizes the uncertainty and gathers information that resolves it.
Since more evidence does not uniformly improve decisions in our study, scoring should weigh the final design against the cost of the measurements. 7 Related Work Simulator validation. Simulation validation assesses fidelity relative to intended use Heidemann et al. (2001b) , and Proteus Duan et al. (2024) also checks whether its predictions preserve the ordering of parallelization strategies.
Simthesizer Kim et al. (2026) has a coding agent extend a serving simulator from feature requests and validates extensions by throughput error.
Decision-focused learning evaluates predictions by the quality of decisions they induce Wilder et al. (2019) ; Elmachtoub and Grigas (2022) , and Siris evaluates whether agents judge this decision impact when reading a simulator with a modeling limitation. Research agents and review. Research-agent benchmarks such as MLE-bench Chan et al. (2025) , RE-Bench Wijk et al. (2025) , and ScienceAgentBench Chen et al. (2025) score completed research tasks.
MIRAGE Fu et al. (2026) varies guidance fidelity and contextual relevance, while Xie et al. (2024) study conflicts between external evidence and parametric knowledge. Huang et al. (2024) find that intrinsic self-correction often fails on reasoning tasks, and Kamoi et al. (2024) conclude that it helps mainly when reliable external feedback exists.
In code review, prompts asking for explanations and fixes can increase rejection of correct implementations Jin and Chen (2026) . Siris scores one design decision under controlled simulator evidence and records the corrections and new errors of a review turn without added feedback. 8 Conclusion Siris evaluates whether LLMs judge the decision impact of simulator modeling limitations, using workload pairs on which the same limitation upholds or overturns the presented recommendation.
Across thirteen LLMs, perturbed simulator evidence reveals differences in design decisions that accurate recommendations largely obscure.
Identifying a limitation does not ensure a correct choice.
Inspection instructions and self-correction often shift disposition more than they raise judgment.
Evaluating LLMs for ADRS therefore requires scoring the decisions that follow limitation identification, counting both error directions together.
We plan to publicly release the Siris artifact. Limitations Measured construct. Siris measures systems understanding through design choices under controlled simulator limitations.
High accuracy shows successful decisions on these problems but does not establish which reasoning produced each choice or how it transfers to a complete ADRS workflow. Reference-defined answers. Correct choices are defined by the reference simulator under a fixed system specification, and the reference is not a perfect model of the real system.
Upheld and overturned labels concern the top-1 design, while smaller margins and lower-rank changes can also matter to a design decision.
Selection loss likewise measures a gap within the reference simulator. Real-system outcomes. A choice that matches the reference answer is correct for a particular specification and simulator, and its advantage on real hardware is untested.
The real-system illustration in Appendix C covers particular policies, one workload trace, two A100 GPUs, and two launches.
Its p95 TTFT winner is a policy that both simulator versions recommend, and its throughput winner is unresolved, so it shows that a simulator comparison can connect to measured performance and contains no case in which an LLM improves real performance by overturning an invalid recommendation. Problem space and exposure. The study covers one domain, LLM serving, one simulator family, and one perturbation per problem.
The 29 pairs cover three design problems and seven perturbations selected for review, and each change form appears in one design problem, so the study cannot separate the effect of the problem from that of the perturbation.
All problems were constructed and inspected during development, and repetitions vary responses on the same problems without adding new situations.
The results compare the tested models on these problems and do not establish a general model ranking or deployment performance. Evidence components. The evidence-component study covers two models and provides the simulator code, cost parameters, and configuration together.
It therefore characterizes this evidence bundle and the effect of adding simulator outputs, without separating the contribution of the code from that of the numerical system information. Identification annotation. Limitation identification is annotated from public responses by LLM annotators that agree closely, which shows reproducible labels but cannot remove biases they share.
A stated limitation records what the LLM wrote, and the review asks only for consequential limitations, so neither statement nor omission reveals the computation behind a choice. Self-correction through review. We call changes between the two turns self-correction, but a single review turn is our only probe of it, so the results describe the effect of this turn on design choices.
Besides the same evidence packet, the review lets the LLM open files it skipped and directs attention to the simulator and its modeling limitations.
Its corrections and new errors therefore combine reconsideration with extra computation, newly read files, and prompt-induced doubt.
Separating these factors requires a continuation with the same extra computation or an independent second attempt, which we did not run, so the results do not show how well these models self-correct in general or whether review outperforms other uses of extra inference Kamoi et al. (2024) . Inference settings. Reasoning effort is the requested setting, which does not imply equal computation across providers. Each pair has one upheld and one overturned problem, and conversations count those assigned to thirteen models with three repetitions in each of the three evidence conditions. Perturbation scope. Each perturbation realizes an abstraction error or an incorrect implementation of a specified computation, two sources of simulation error Gutierrez et al. (2014) that a reader can detect by comparing the simulator source with the specification.
The evaluated changes therefore form a selected sample of simulator errors, and uncertainty in the specification, data validity Sargent (2013) , and interacting limitations lie outside this evaluation. Admission criteria. The current evaluation set admits a problem when its execution is valid and repeatable, both simulator versions have a unique winner, the changed computation executes and alters predicted values in upheld problems, and the presented evidence supports the reference answer. Section B.3 describes the evidence-validation procedure and its exclusions. Adaptive proposals. An LLM Builder proposes policies and trace generators within fixed construction criteria and revises proposals using simulator feedback.
Every executed proposal freezes its code, exact input, sources, and measurement contract.
An independent Critic and Arbiter review the evidence, while automatic failures remain binding.
If confirmation informed a revision, the changed proposal requires fresh confirmation. Construction roles. The discovery and confirmation traces define simulator-construction roles independently of agent-evaluation splits.
Development exposure, earlier failed proposals, and exact-trace-only qualification retain their original status.
Metrics sharing a simulator execution do not multiply independent workload samples. A.2 Data Composition Reuse of earlier material. Candidates, workloads, and simulator executions from an earlier problem bank, which contains only overturned problems, enter the evaluation only under the same eligibility, pairing, and public-evidence criteria as new material.
These criteria include the packet validation in Section B.3 , and the evaluation set was fixed before the evaluated agents responded.
The construction records document reused items, exclusion reasons, and development exposure. A.3 Real-System Case Protocol Policies and input. The illustrative measurement uses 144 requests, arriving eight at a time every 0.12 seconds.
Input lengths are 64, 256, 768, and 1,536 tokens, and output lengths are 16, 64, and 96 tokens, with 12 requests per length combination.
All four policies process the same trace, with 32 total measured executions across two launches.
Warm-up and environment checks are excluded from the reported metrics. Uncertainty procedure. The recorded procedure jointly resamples policy vectors within each launch using 20,000 bootstrap draws.
For each metric, it constructs simultaneous 95% intervals over the six policy pairs.
A resolved winner must beat all three alternatives under these intervals, rank uniquely first in at least seven of eight repetitions, and lead within each launch and overall.
The intervals are conditional on the observed launches and have limited tail-probability support from four repetitions per launch. A.4 Evidence Replay Replay checks. The replay protocol reconstructs question assembly, retrieval, response scoring, and aggregation from fixed evidence.
Negative checks test mismatched sources, labels, instructions, and public-evidence boundaries.
Fresh-environment checks compare the resulting questions, field scores, and analysis denominators with their stored references. Reuse contract. The protocol fixes problem identity, condition matching, activation and recommendation checks, and the connection from presented evidence to choices.
A different environment must supply policy, workload, metric, and simulator semantics together with inspectable evidence.
Evaluating another agent on fixed evidence can reuse the same simulator records.
The serving implementation supplies these bindings for scheduling policies and simulator costs. In the perturbed and reference conditions, the file list covers the system specification, candidate code, workload, simulator source, cost parameters, configuration, per-design predictions, and per-design execution records.
The no-simulator condition lists only the specification, candidate code, and workload, and its review omits the follow-or-reconsider and limitation fields.
Each turn may list, read, and search these files, and no tool executes or modifies code.
Neither turn names the limitation, its location, or the number of limitations, and the review supplies no new information, although the LLM may then read files it did not open in the first turn. System specification. The specification defines placement, topology, cache-reuse semantics, and the axes, units, and interpolation of the cost parameters for each environment.
It may help an agent notice a limitation, but it states no location, fix, or reference answer.
Each specification is fixed before collection. Packet validation. Two blind solvers attempted each candidate problem with only the presented packet, and an evidence audit checks that this evidence supports the reference answer, using the reference execution only to confirm consistency.
Five routing candidates are excluded, either because the presented evidence cannot order the two leading designs or because the leaders fall within 1 ms of each other when recomputed from it. Concealment. Answer files, the reference source, and the reference execution lie outside every tool’s accessible scope.
We check prompts, returned passages, filenames, summaries, comments, and tests for target values and implementation-history cues.
All receipts match the frozen manifest of evidence trees and prompts. B.4 Scoring and Analysis Choices. A problem is solved when the final choice equals the reference answer, and pair accuracy, which the appendix tables report, requires correct final choices on both problems of a pair under the same model, condition, and repetition, the two-instance form of contrast consistency Gardner et al. (2020) .
Each problem is a separate conversation, and initial and final choices are retained separately.
Wrong choices are classified by design identity into abandoning a correct recommendation, keeping a wrong one, and replacing a wrong one with a design other than the reference answer. Rationale annotation. A separate LLM annotator classified each perturbed response with a fixed rubric, blind to labels, reference answers, and model identity.
The rubric records whether the response identifies the perturbed limitation, whether it did so in the first turn, whether it links the limitation to a specific design, and which impact it claims.
A second annotator independently labeled 200 responses and agreed on identification for 198 ( κ = 0.98 \kappa=0.98 ).
For the eight models annotated first, 623 of the 624 responses with a valid choice that do not identify the limitation keep the presented recommendation, which follows largely from the review asking for a reason whenever the agent departs.
This annotation describes responses and never enters pair accuracy. Collection. Each model uses the same read-only tools under the perturbed, reference, and no-simulator conditions with three repetitions.
The main evaluation comprises 6,786 assigned conversations, thirteen models over 58 problems, three conditions, and three repetitions.
Conversations without a valid final choice remain in the assigned denominators as incorrect. B.5 Auxiliary Contrasts Arms and pairs. Each auxiliary batch draws one pair by a fixed seed from each of five perturbations, the two routing perturbations, handoff, query interpolation, and step aggregation, and the second batch draws only pairs unused by the first.
The predictions-only condition lists the specification, candidate code, workload, and per-design predictions, and its review asks the agent to check the recommendation against these files.
The comparison condition uses the full-evidence packet and inserts the following sentence before the review’s response format: “If you judge that a modeling limitation exists, compare whether its effect is the same for all candidates or differs between them, and whether there is evidence that overturns a candidate’s existing advantage, before deciding the final candidate. Do not drop or keep the recommendation merely because a limitation exists.”
Conversation sessions cannot be forked after the first turn, so the comparison condition runs as independent conversations with the same first-turn prompt.
Each batch assigns 360 conversations, 120 per condition, and the auxiliary results are never pooled with the main evaluation.
In the first batch, the guided review restores all nine first-turn departures from valid recommendations and adds one new departure, whereas the standard review restores two of seven and adds two.
Identifying responses number 86 under the standard review and 87 under guidance, and incorrect choices among them fall from 15 to 9, while overturned problems with the presented design kept number seven and eight.
The overturned problems solved with predictions alone fall almost entirely in handoff, and in the first batch all seven repeat the same model’s no-simulator choice. Representation and replay. Saved-evidence replay reconstructs packets, independently scores stored responses, and regenerates the reported analyses in a fresh environment. Appendix C Real-System Illustration Retained measurements. Recorded vLLM Kwon et al. (2023) measurements connect the serving comparisons to measured performance.
One retained comparison executes Qwen3-30B-A3B Yang et al. (2025) in BF16 on two A100 40GB GPUs and compares arrival order, short-prefill priority, most-processed priority, and least-processed priority.
The latter three policies use prefill-token caps of 128, 512, and 256, respectively.
Each policy runs eight times on the same trace, with four matched repetitions in each of two fresh launches. Metric-specific outcome. For p95 TTFT, the short-prefill policy has a mean of 2.24 seconds across the eight run-level measurements and is uniquely best in all eight repetitions.
Its advantage over the nearest alternative has a simultaneous 95% interval of 6.38 to 6.94 seconds under the recorded within-metric procedure.
Both simulator sources recommend that policy.
For output throughput on the same trace, the least-processed priority has the highest mean, 735.9 tokens per second, but its nearest-alternative advantage interval spans − 3.33 -3.33 to 25.69 25.69 tokens per second, leaving the empirical winner unresolved. Role in the analysis. These two outcomes show how an inspectable simulator comparison connects to an actual serving decision with metric-specific uncertainty.
