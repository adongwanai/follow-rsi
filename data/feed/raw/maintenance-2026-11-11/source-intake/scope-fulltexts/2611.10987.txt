License: CC BY 4.0 arXiv:2611.10987v1 [cs.LG] 07 Nov 2026 Horizon-Scalable Q-Learning Haoran Xu Pragya Srivastava Xinjie Liu Siddhant Agarwal Amy Zhang The University of Texas at Austin haoran.xu@utexas.edu Website ∙ \bullet GitHub Abstract Learning accurate off-policy state-action value functions (Q-functions) from offline datasets becomes harder as the task horizon grows. One-step temporal-difference (TD) learning bootstraps after every transition, so small per-step errors accumulate along the horizon, and early states can be badly misvalued even when the TD loss is small. Multi-step TD shortens this chain by following the dataset trajectory for several steps; however, since the behavior policy is often suboptimal, every followed action adds a bias, and no fixed number of steps suits a whole trajectory. In this paper, we propose Horizon-Scalable Q-Learning (HQL), a simple method that enables accurate long-horizon value learning by automatically balancing between one-step and multi-step returns. HQL extends a target through a dataset action only when the critic values it at least as highly as the best-of-N actions sampled from the BC policy, and otherwise falls back to a one-step target. We show that this rule is a principled off-policy correction that needs no action densities, which makes HQL compatible with expressive flow and diffusion policies. Experiments show that HQL avoids the failures of both one-step and multi-step TD on a combination-lock task across horizons, and outperforms prior horizon-reduction methods on long-horizon OGBench and OCBench tasks. 1 Introduction Off-policy reinforcement learning (RL) learns the state-action value function (Q-function) of a target policy from an offline dataset collected by a different behavior policy, which enables RL to reuse logged experience in real-world domains such as robotics ( Levine et al., 2016 ; Kalashnikov et al., 2018 ) and industrial control ( Zhan et al., 2022 ) . However, off-policy value learning becomes harder as the effective horizon grows ( Laidlaw et al., 2023 ) , i.e., the number of transitions across which the critic must carry value information before reaching a state whose value it already knows.
Most off-policy actor-critic methods learn the critic with one-step temporal-difference (TD) backups ( Fujimoto et al., 2019 ; Kumar et al., 2020 ; Kostrikov et al., 2022 ; Xu et al., 2023 ; Park et al., 2025c ) . A one-step target bootstraps after a single logged transition, which makes it robust to off-policy data but moves value back by only one transition per update. We show that the value error at a state is exactly the sum of the one-step Bellman residuals along the rest of the trajectory, so a critic can reach a small TD loss and still misvalue the start states by an amount that grows with the horizon. Multi-step returns ( Watkins, 1989 ; Sutton and Barto, 2018 ) or Q-chunking ( Li et al., 2025b ; Zhao et al., 2023 ) shorten this chain by following the dataset trajectory for several steps before bootstrapping. Nevertheless, this imposes a trade-off between fast value propagation (longer backups) and low off-policy bias (shorter backups): we show that each spanned suboptimal action adds a systematic bias that grows toward a limit linear in H H . Since offline trajectories could be mixed with suboptimal actions, no fixed backup length is optimal for every TD update. Per-decision off-policy corrections such as Retrace ( Precup et al., 2000 ; Munos et al., 2016 ) make this choice at every transition with importance ratios. However, these ratios require policy densities, which flow or diffusion-based policies ( Park et al., 2025c ; Mao et al., 2024a ; Li and Levine, 2026 ; Xu et al., 2026 ) do not expose. In this paper, we propose Horizon-Scalable Q-Learning (HQL), which shortens the effective horizon of Q-learning without inheriting the bias of suboptimal dataset actions. HQL achieves this by automatically balancing between one-step and multi-step returns at every transition (Figure 1 ). More specifically, HQL attaches a binary gate to every logged action, i.e., every action stored in the dataset: if the target critic values it at least as highly as the best-of- N N actions sampled from the BC policy, the gate is open and the target continues through it for one more step; otherwise, the target falls back to a one-step bootstrap. Our key observation is that this gate is principled without any density: for the optimal policy π ∝ μ ​ exp ⁡ ( τ ​ Q ) \pi\propto\mu\exp(\tau Q) that behavior-regularized RL approximates ( Peters et al., 2010 ; Peng et al., 2019 ; Xu et al., 2025b ) , the behavior density cancels from the importance ratio, and as τ → ∞ \tau\to\infty the largest gate that preserves Q π Q^{\pi} becomes exactly the comparison of critic values. HQL uses an estimated behavior model only to sample action candidates and never evaluates its density. Since the gate depends only on the ordering of critic values, it is also invariant to the reward scale, and the only hyperparameter in HQL is the number of candidates N N . We evaluate HQL on a combination-lock task, whose true Q Q -values are known exactly, and on long-horizon OGBench and OCBench tasks ( Park et al., 2025a ; Park and Levine, 2026 ) . On the combination lock, HQL keeps the lowest value estimation error across different horizon, whereas one-step TD accumulates error and n n -step TD fails on corrupted dataset. On OGBench and OCBench, at a matched budget of critic targets, HQL outperforms one-step TD, n n -step TD, Q-chunking ( Li et al., 2025b ) , and Long-horizon Q-Learning ( Abraham et al., 2026 ) , especially on the most challenging long-horizon tasks. Error propagation through bootstrapping has been studied for approximate value and policy iteration ( Munos, 2005 ; Farahmand et al., 2010 ) , TD with function approximation ( Tsitsiklis and Roy, 1997 ) , and n n -step and TD( λ \lambda ) updates ( Kearns and Singh, 2000 ) ; its instability with off-policy data is the deadly triad ( van Hasselt et al., 2018 ) , and the horizon governs the difficulty of evaluation and learning ( Liu et al., 2018 ; Laidlaw et al., 2023 ) . Multi-step off-policy correction. Multi-step returns and eligibility traces shorten the backup chain ( Watkins, 1989 ; Sutton and Barto, 2018 ; De Asis et al., 2018 ) , and a small fixed n n is standard in deep RL ( Hessel et al., 2018 ; Kapturowski et al., 2019 ; Hernandez-Garcia and Sutton, 2019 ) . Per-decision importance sampling ( Precup et al., 2000 ) and its clipped variants (Retrace ( Munos et al., 2016 ; Touati et al., 2018 ) , off-policy Q ⁡ ( λ ) Q(\lambda) ( Harutyunyan et al., 2016 ) , V-trace ( Espeholt et al., 2018 ) , trajectory-aware traces ( Daley et al., 2023 ) ) correct the mismatch step by step, and emphatic TD reweights updates ( Sutton et al., 2016 ) . Watkins’s Q ⁡ ( λ ) Q(\lambda) cuts traces at non-greedy actions while Peng’s Q ⁡ ( λ ) Q(\lambda) keeps them ( Peng and Williams, 1996 ; Kozuno et al., 2021 ; Kim and hwan Oh, 2026 ) ; other work adapts the trace ( White and White, 2016 ; Rowland et al., 2020 ) , avoids ratios via action-dependent bootstrapping ( Mahmood et al., 2017 ) , gates traces at non-greedy actions ( Daley, 2026 ) , or adds multi-step inequalities ( Abraham et al., 2026 ) . However, these corrections need action probabilities or a discrete greedy target policy. Horizon reduction. Instead of correcting within a backup, horizon-reduction methods change what a decision is. Hierarchical methods act over options or subgoals ( Bacon et al., 2017 ; Vezhnevets et al., 2017 ; Nachum et al., 2018 ; Nachum et al., 2019 ; Xu et al., 2022 ) , and action chunking treats a short action sequence as one decision ( Seo and Abbeel, 2024 ; Li et al., 2025b ; Li et al., 2025a ; Song et al., 2026 ) . However, within each segment they use a fixed, uncorrected backup and inherit the bias; HQL is orthogonal and contains both as ungated special cases.
Another line exploits the structure of goal-reaching tasks through quasimetric value functions ( Wang et al., 2023a ; Zheng et al., 2026 ) , graph search over subgoals ( Eysenbach et al., 2019 ) , and goal-conditioned hierarchies ( Park et al., 2023 ; Ahn et al., 2025 ; Zhou and Kao, 2025 ; Park et al., 2026 ) . These methods rely on a goal-reaching reward and thus apply only to goal-conditioned RL; in contrast, HQL makes no assumption on the reward. Policy extraction with expressive policies. Offline RL methods regularize the policy toward the data, either explicitly through divergence penalties ( Wu et al., 2019 ; Kumar et al., 2019 ; Xu et al., 2021 ; Fujimoto and Gu, 2021 ) or implicitly through in-sample value learning and weighted behavior cloning ( Peng et al., 2019 ; Nair et al., 2020 ; Kostrikov et al., 2022 ; Xu et al., 2022 ; Xu et al., 2023 ; Xu et al., 2025a ) . Recent work extracts expressive diffusion or flow policies by value-guided training ( Wang et al., 2023b ; Mao et al., 2024a ; Park et al., 2025c ) , rejection sampling from a behavior model ( Chen et al., 2023 ; Hansen-Estruch et al., 2023 ) , adjoint matching ( Li and Levine, 2026 ) , or value gradient flow ( Xu et al., 2026 ) . These methods keep a one-step critic; HQL only changes the critic’s target and can serve any of them. 3 Preliminaries We consider the problem presented as a finite-horizon Markov decision process (MDP) ℳ = ( 𝒮 , 𝒜 , P , r , H ) \mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,r,H) , where 𝒮 \mathcal{S} and 𝒜 \mathcal{A} are the state and action spaces, P ⁡ ( s ′ | s , a ) P(s^{\prime}|s,a) and r ⁡ ( s , a ) r(s,a) denote the transition dynamics and reward function, and H H is the horizon; an episode that reaches its goal early enters a zero-reward absorbing state, so every episode has length H H . We begin with a gated multi-step target and design the gate based on value comparison (Section 4.1 ). We then show that this gate is a limiting case of per-decision importance sampling yet needs no action density (Section 4.2 ). Finally, we provide several practical considerations for HQL and discuss its connection to previous work (Section 4.3 ). 4.1 Gated Multi-Step Targets Our key idea is to let a backup continue through a logged action only when that action is at least as good as the actions the target policy would take, i.e., its advantage under the target policy is non-negative, which keeps it out of the bias term of Eq. 3 while reducing the horizon of TD learning via multi-step bootstrapping.
To do so, we attach a gate c i ∈ { 0 , 1 } c_{i}\in\{0,1\} to every transition and define the HQL target backward along a logged segment, Y t hql = r t + γ ​ V ¯ π ​ ( s t + 1 ) ⏟ one-step target + γ ​ c t + 1 ​ ( Y t + 1 hql − Q ¯ ​ ( s t + 1 , a t + 1 ) ) ⏟ downstream correction , Y_{t}^{\textsc{hql}}=\underbrace{r_{t}+\gamma\,\overline{V}^{\pi}(s_{t+1})}_{\text{one-step target}}\;+\;\gamma\,c_{t+1}\underbrace{\big(Y_{t+1}^{\textsc{hql}}-\overline{Q}(s_{t+1},a_{t+1})\big)}_{\text{downstream correction}}, (6) with the correction set to zero at the end of the segment, and γ \gamma kept for generality. To allow for more flexible decision-making, we let one decision span several environment steps. More specifically, with stride L L , a decision is a chunk of L L primitive actions that the critic scores as a unit, Q ( s t , a t : t + L ) Q(s_{t},a_{t:t+L}) , and a window of H H chunks reaches L ⋅ H L\!\cdot\!H environment steps while still producing H H targets. Note that the gate is unchanged, now comparing the logged chunk with the best-of- N N chunks drawn from the policy, and the backup of Eq. 6 holds with γ L \gamma^{L} in place of γ \gamma and per-chunk reward sums. Connections to existing methods. To summarize, in HQL or Chunked-HQL, L L sets how many environment steps one decision covers, and H H sets how many decisions one target may chain (Figure 2 ). With L = 1 L{=}1 , H = 1 H{=}1 gives one-step TD, an ungated window of size H H gives uncorrected H H -step TD, and a gated window gives HQL. With L = k L{=}k , H = 1 H{=}1 is Q-chunking with chunks of k k actions ( Li et al., 2025b ) , since a single chunk leaves nothing to gate, and H > 1 H{>}1 gives Chunked-HQL at stride k k .
Note that the two variables extend the maximal reach of a target, L ⋅ H L\cdot H environment steps, in different ways. Increasing H H lets one target makes more decisions while the policy outputs less actions per step, and the gate decides for each target how many of these decisions it actually uses. In contrast, increasing L L makes each decision cover more environment steps, but each chunk then runs open loop and the policy must search a larger, L ⋅ dim ( 𝒜 ) L\cdot\dim(\mathcal{A}) -dimensional action space. 5 Experiments The goal of our experiments is to answer four questions. One might expect a multi-step method to win by sending reward information far back along the trajectory. To understand why HQL works better than the TD baselines, we log how many steps each target’s trace survives (Figure 7 ). We provide more details on the ablation setup in Appendix A.8 . 6 Conclusion and Limitations In this paper, we propose HQL, which shortens the effective horizon of Q-learning without inheriting the bias of suboptimal logged actions. HQL gates each logged action with a density-free value comparison and achieves strong results on long-horizon OGBench tasks, both alone and as a drop-in critic for QAM and VGF. We believe that HQL represents a concrete step toward horizon-scalable off-policy RL. One limitation is that the gate relies on the critic’s own ranking. One future work is to apply HQL over subgoals in a hierarchical manner.
