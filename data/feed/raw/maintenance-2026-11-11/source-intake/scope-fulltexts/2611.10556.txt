We introduce onóna, a two-table parameterization that increases output expressiveness while preserving input–output weight sharing. The Transformer takes the average of two embedding tables as input, and the output head combines the softmax distributions defined by those same tables. A gate conditioned only on the current input token determines the mixture weight. Its values can be precomputed for inference, and the Transformer body remains unchanged. We evaluate onóna across Dense, Gated DeltaNet hybrid, mixture-of-experts, and Attention Residual architectures at 142M, 330M, and 1B scales. Under matched token budgets, onóna lowers final validation loss and external perplexity in 11 of 12 comparisons and raises the average accuracy over eight downstream tasks in 10 of 12. The one exception on loss is the 1B MoE model. The cost is one extra embedding table and one extra vocabulary projection. 1 Introduction Weight tying uses the same table for the input embedding and output layer of a language model ( Press & Wolf, 2017 ; Inan et al., 2017 ; Liu et al., 2024 ; Qwen Team, 2025 ) . The table 𝑬 ∈ ℝ V × d {\bm{E}}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times d} maps each input token to a vector and, in its role as the unembedding , scores every vocabulary entry as a possible next token. Sharing these roles saves a V × d V\times d matrix, a substantial fraction of a small model’s parameters. Tying also improved perplexity in the original experiments ( Press & Wolf, 2017 ) , and the fully untied 400M control in Batley & Saha (2026) underperformed its tied baseline. Both roles contribute to learning, but early in training the tied matrix is shaped mostly by output gradients ( Lopardo et al., 2026 ) . The usual tied head scores the vocabulary with a single table followed by a softmax. Its matrix of log-probabilities across contexts has rank at most d + 1 d+1 , limiting its ability to represent high-rank next-token distributions. This restriction is known as the softmax bottleneck ( Yang et al., 2018 ) . It matters when the next token has several plausible modes ( Chang & McCallum, 2022 ) and has been linked to the saturation of small models ( Godey et al., 2024 ) . A mixture of softmaxes (MoS) addresses the restriction by combining predictions from K K transformed contexts under one table, at a cost of K K vocabulary projections per position ( Yang et al., 2018 ) . Two obvious fixes are to untie the output table or to add a second softmax component under the tied table. Neither works well in our controls. An untied head adds V × d V\times d parameters but gives up input–output sharing, and finishes 0.009 nats above the tied baseline at 142M. A K = 2 K{=}2 MoS keeps sharing, but every component sends its gradient into the same input table, and it finishes 0.024 nats above the baseline. We want instead a head that gains capacity while every table used to score the vocabulary stays tied to the input. Twin embeddings with tied unembeddings implement this design with two tables of the same shape, 𝑨 {\bm{A}} and 𝑩 {\bm{B}} (Figure 1 ). The body reads their average 𝑴 = 1 2 ​ ( 𝑨 + 𝑩 ) {\bm{M}}=\frac{1}{2}({\bm{A}}+{\bm{B}}) , which serves as a single input table. The head scores the vocabulary with both tables and mixes the resulting distributions using a weight g t g_{t} that depends only on the input token x t x_{t} . We call this model onóna. Whereas MoS combines K K contexts under one table, onóna combines two tables under one context. It adds a second table and d d gate weights, and each tied table receives its own output-projection gradient. Conditioning the gate on token identity allows its values to be computed once for the whole vocabulary, following the use of token identity in hash-based expert routing ( Roller et al., 2021 ) . At serving time, the input tables fold into 𝑴 {\bm{M}} and the gate becomes a lookup in a vector of V V values. The output still requires two vocabulary projections. We train Qwen3-style decoders on ClimbMix ( Diao et al., 2025 ) at 142M, 330M and 1B. At 142M, onóna lowers final validation loss by 0.0285 nats for Dense, 0.0134 for the Gated DeltaNet hybrid, 0.0116 for MoE and 0.0226 for Attention Residuals. At 330M, the corresponding improvements are 0.0065, 0.0037, 0.0144 and 0.0119 nats. Both external perplexity benchmarks improve in all eight pairs, and all sixteen one-table ablations have higher loss than their mixture (Table 1 ). The gains extend to the 1B Dense, GDN hybrid and AttnRes models. The 1B MoE model, however, has worse validation loss and perplexity despite a higher downstream average (Table 2 ). In a 2 × 2 2\times 2 input–output ablation, neither role of the second table recovers the gain on its own (§ 4 ). 2 Twin embeddings with Tied Unembeddings onóna expands the output family of a tied language model by sharing two vocabulary tables between the input and output. Their average feeds a single decoder, whose hidden state is projected through each table to form two softmax distributions. A token-conditioned gate combines these distributions. Twin embedding averages two input representations, twin unembedding produces two output distributions from the same hidden state, and a token gate learns how to combine them (Figure 1 (a)). Figure 2 gives a log-space implementation, and Appendix B covers the gradient paths and token routing. 2.3 Properties and computational cost Tying and expressivity. Both scoring tables also serve as input tables. Mixing their distributions after the softmax can exceed the single-softmax d + 1 d+1 rank bound; Appendix A gives an explicit construction and contrasts it with mixing the tables before the softmax. The tied baseline is a special case, since 𝑨 = 𝑩 = 𝑬 {\bm{A}}={\bm{B}}={\bm{E}} recovers it for every gate value. Our experiments do not start from this construction; the two tables are initialized independently (Appendix C ). Relation to MoS. MoS combines distributions from transformed hidden states under one table, whereas onóna uses two tables with the same hidden state. The gate in onóna depends on token identity, while MoS uses a contextual prior. Sections 3.3 and 4.1 compare the two designs empirically. Cost. The second table and gate vector add V ​ d + d Vd+d trainable parameters, and training evaluates both output components, incurring extra projection FLOPs. At inference, conditional computation can avoid this extra projection, analogous to sparse activation in MoE ( Fedus et al., 2022 ) . The input tables fold into 𝑴 {\bm{M}} and gate values are precomputed. For untruncated sampling at temperature 1, we first select a component with probability g t g_{t} and then compute only its vocabulary projection and softmax. This produces an exact sample from the mixture, restricting the extra projection cost to training for this generation setting. Exact scoring, greedy decoding, and other temperatures or top- k k /top- p p truncation generally still require both projections. Appendix B gives the sampling argument, memory costs and measured throughput. 3 Experiments 3.1 Experimental setup Models and data. We train Qwen3-style decoders ( Qwen Team, 2025 ) from scratch on ClimbMix ( Diao et al., 2025 ) at 142M, 330M and 1B scales, using GPT-2 BPE ( Radford et al., 2019 ) and 1,024-token contexts. The four architectures are Dense, GDN hybrid ( Yang et al., 2025 ) , MoE ( Fedus et al., 2022 ) and AttnRes ( Kimi Team, 2026 ) . At 142M and 330M, they share depth and width, with MoE active parameters matched to Dense. The smaller models use a common 7.18B-token training pool, and the 1B models use a disjoint 23.9B-token pool. Training repeats data when the token budget exceeds the pool size. Training and comparisons. All experiments, including controls, use a budget of 40 tokens per parameter (TPP) based on the Dense Standard parameter count. Variants at each size therefore receive the same number of training tokens. We use AdamW ( Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019 ) with a warmup–stable–decay schedule ( Hu et al., 2024 ) . Standard uses a single tied table. onóna retains its decoder body and changes the vocabulary tables and output head. The twin tables are initialized independently, so their average has a lower initial variance than the Standard input table. We compare alternative output layers in Section 3.3 and examine component and resource controls in Section 4 . Appendix C provides model configurations, hyperparameters and initialization details, and Appendix D lists the implementation names. 3.2 Main results We compare onóna with the single-table Standard baseline at the final 40-TPP checkpoints. We have not run the corresponding 1B control. 5 Related Work Tied embeddings. Sharing the input and output tables ( Press & Wolf, 2017 ; Inan et al., 2017 ) is standard in small and mid-sized models ( Liu et al., 2024 ; Qwen Team, 2025 ) . Output gradients largely shape the shared matrix early in training ( Lopardo et al., 2026 ) . The fully untied 400M control of Batley & Saha (2026) underperforms its tied baseline, consistent with our untying controls. Over-tokenized Transformers ( Huang et al., 2025 ) increase input vocabulary capacity through extra tables while retaining a single output table. Twin embeddings instead use two tables over the same vocabulary and tie both to the output. The softmax bottleneck. Yang et al. (2018) established the rank bound for a single-softmax head and proposed MoS to overcome it. Subsequent work reduced the cost of richer output layers with Mixtape ( Yang et al., 2019 ) and changed the output activation without adding parameters with Sigsoftmax ( Kanai et al., 2018 ) . Studies of the bottleneck link its effects to multi-mode next-token distributions ( Chang & McCallum, 2022 ) and the saturation of small models ( Godey et al., 2024 ) . MoS and onóna increase output expressiveness in different ways. MoS varies the context under one table, while onóna varies the table under one context and uses a token-dependent prior. Our MoS controls compare the two directly (Tables 5 and 3 ). Token-conditioned routing. Hash layers ( Roller et al., 2021 ) route tokens to experts by identity rather than by a learned function of context. They are competitive with learned routing in the reported sparse-model experiments. onóna also uses token identity, but learns soft output-mixture weights rather than fixed hash assignments. Each previous-token type learns how much the two tables contribute to the next-token distribution. Multiple embeddings per token. Multi-sense embeddings ( Neelakantan et al., 2014 ) and dynamic meta-embeddings ( Kiela et al., 2018 ) keep several input vectors per word and use context to select or attend among them. GloVe combines its two tables by summation at serving time ( Pennington et al., 2014 ) , while multi-embedding recommenders retain several tables per feature during serving ( Guo et al., 2024 ) . Twin embeddings also retain two vectors per token. Their distinguishing feature is that both tables are tied to the head, where their output distributions are mixed. 6 Conclusion onóna shares two vocabulary tables between input and output, feeds their average to the decoder, and combines their softmax distributions with a gate conditioned only on the input token. At 142M and 330M, it lowers validation loss and both external perplexities for all four architectures. The downstream average improves in six of eight pairs, with small declines for 142M GDN hybrid and MoE. At 1B, Dense, GDN hybrid and AttnRes improve, while MoE has worse loss despite a higher downstream average. In the ablations, neither role of the second table recovers the gain on its own, and neither untying nor MoS reproduces it under the tested recipe. The gain costs an extra vocabulary projection, and at 330M a deeper Standard model does better with a similar budget. The separate 142M controls and additional evaluations use unequal numbers of validation windows, and hardware, GPU layout and software versions vary across runs. None of the downstream averages has a confidence interval or a replicate seed. Equal token budgets do not match parameters or FLOPs, since onóna adds a table and a projection; the roughly resource-matched depth control exists at 330M only. Width and depth change together between 142M and 330M, and the larger budget repeats the shuffled data, so the runs do not separate width, training length and data repetition. At 1B, MoE has worse loss and perplexity but better downstream accuracy. The 1B runs use separate validation windows, and we have no explanation for the disagreement. The baseline and twin models also start from input tables of different scale, and no control isolates that difference. The one-table ablations change input and output together, so their loss penalties show neither output-only specialization nor the source of the gain over Standard. We have not tested larger vocabularies or longer contexts, and we have not looked at which token types the gate assigns to which table.
