Affiliation: TMLR Group, Department of Computer Science, Hong Kong Baptist University Hang Li Affiliation: ByteDance Seed Abstract Spatial reasoning requires understanding how objects and observers are arranged in a scene and how their relations change with viewpoint or movement.
Vision-language models (VLMs) typically perform both steps through model-internal reasoning, forming an implicit scene interpretation from partial views and imagining the consequences of spatial transformations.
A misread scene can lead to a wrong answer, while even a correct interpretation does not ensure that imagined transformations satisfy spatial constraints.
We introduce Spatial Harness , which represents scenes as explicit and executable world states to ground spatial reasoning in physics-engine feedback.
A Perceiver integrates scene constraints extracted from the question with specialized visual estimates to place objects and cameras in a shared coordinate frame.
The resulting state is instantiated in a physics engine, where a Reasoner executes viewpoint changes and uses computed measurements and rendered views to guide reasoning.
We evaluate on MindCube, SAT, MMSI-Bench, and SpatialRGPT-Bench with open- and closed-weight VLMs.
On MindCube, Spatial Harness improves Qwen3.5-27B from 52.70% to 71.14%, surpassing pySpatial by 7.52 percentage points.
Given identical annotated object and camera poses, it reaches 98.25% versus 71.81% for model-internal reasoning.

The Reasoner therefore turns again to inspect the opposite direction, identifies the grey-green decorative wall, and selects C (steps 7–10). 1 Introduction Spatial reasoning requires vision-language models (VLMs) to infer spatial relations beyond those directly observed in the input views [ 17 , 14 ] .
Determining what lies behind an observer after a turn, for example, entails recovering the scene layout and evaluating object relations in the transformed reference frame.
Despite advances in multimodal reasoning [ 15 , 27 , 7 , 26 ] , this remains challenging: GPT-5 achieves 47.59% accuracy on MindCube, compared with 32.35% for random guessing [ 17 ] . The difficulty is that direct reasoning leaves both scene representation and spatial transformation within the model (Figure 1 , top).
A VLM may infer an incorrect layout and reason consistently from it, or infer the layout correctly but misapply the requested transformation.
The final answer alone cannot distinguish these failures, and a fluent explanation provides no independent check of either step.
Additional views, as generated by MindJourney [ 23 ] , provide visual evidence but do not by themselves enforce consistency between scene estimates and subsequent spatial transformations. Our central premise is that a scene representation should determine the consequences of spatial operations.
An explicit world state makes estimated objects, poses, and relations inspectable, but providing these facts as text still leaves their consequences to model-internal reasoning.
Making the state executable instead allows an engine to compute measurements and render views from the specified geometry (Figure 1 , bottom).
Successive operations then share a common scene, against which the model can check its spatial judgments.
Execution does not eliminate perceptual uncertainty, but it makes the assumed layout and the results of operations separately available for inspection. We introduce Spatial Harness , which implements this principle with a Perceiver and a Reasoner in MuJoCo [ 16 ] .
The Perceiver integrates specialized visual estimates with constraints stated in the question, including camera movements that relate observations across views, to place objects and cameras in a shared coordinate frame.
The Reasoner queries the instantiated state through Python, using viewpoint changes, geometric measurements, and rendered views to resolve the question.
Language constraints and visual geometry jointly define the scene used for spatial computation. Figure 2 presents a concrete example: determining what lies behind an observer after a 180 ∘ 180^{\circ} turn.
After executing the turn, the first rendering shows a window ahead, whereas the question asks what is behind.
The Reasoner therefore performs another 180 ∘ 180^{\circ} turn to inspect that direction, identifies the grey-green decorative wall, and selects C.
The sequence illustrates how execution feedback guides subsequent queries while the engine preserves a shared world state throughout the reasoning process. We evaluate four benchmarks, compare reasoning with matched states (Figure 3 ), and analyze scene construction, execution feedback, and state quality (Sections 4 – 5 ). 2 Related Work Agentic Multimodal Reasoning. ReAct, ViperGPT, and VisProg organize reasoning around external actions or executable programs [ 24 , 15 , 6 ] .
DeepEyes and THYME learn visual tool use [ 27 , 7 , 26 ] , while agent frameworks develop planning and verification [ 11 , 29 , 30 ] .
These approaches chiefly concern how tools are selected, invoked, and coordinated during reasoning.
Spatial Harness complements such orchestration by specifying the geometric state shared by spatial tools, so successive calls return measurements and views derived from the same explicit and inspectable scene assumptions. Spatial Reasoning in VLMs. SpatialVLM, Visual Spatial Tuning, and SpatialRGPT enhance spatial understanding through training or geometric inputs [ 1 , 21 , 3 ] .
SpatialPIN and APC construct spatial priors or scene abstractions with visual experts [ 13 , 9 ] .
Among tool-based methods, pySpatial executes programs over reconstructed 3D geometry [ 12 ] , while GCA constrains computation by formalizing the query’s reference frame and objective [ 2 ] .
Spatial Harness uses question-stated object relations and camera motions to constrain the estimated world state itself.
These constraints determine the scene layout, rather than only the reference frame used to query it. Simulation-Grounded Reasoning. MindJourney synthesizes additional viewpoints with a video world model [ 23 ] .
Mind’s Eye simulates physical outcomes from textual descriptions [ 10 ] , and LLMPhy estimates physical parameters and layouts through simulation feedback [ 4 ] .
Spatial Harness uses a physics engine for spatial measurements and viewpoint rendering over an estimated static scene.
Its world state specifies object and camera geometry, so these queries require neither physical parameter estimation nor prediction of object dynamics. 3 Method Figure 4: Overview of Spatial Harness. The JSON and code are illustrative excerpts. 3.1 Executable World States for Grounded Reasoning Problem formulation. We consider spatial question answering. Each instance consists of a set of images x = { I i } i = 1 m x=\{I_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m} of a scene with m ≥ 1 m\geq 1 , a textual query q q that poses the question and may describe part of the scene, and a ground-truth answer y ∈ 𝒴 y\in\mathcal{Y} . A model takes ( x , q ) (x,q) and predicts y ^ ∈ 𝒴 \hat{y}\in\mathcal{Y} . Answering q q involves two steps: perception , which establishes the scene facts that the question depends on, such as where the entities are and from where each image was taken, and reasoning , which derives the answer from these facts, for example by working out how the scene looks from another viewpoint. A VLM that answers directly from ( x , q ) (x,q) carries out both steps within one generation. The perceived facts are not represented separately from the reasoning, so an error in perception cannot be told apart from an error in reasoning, and neither step can be checked or improved on its own. Spatial Harness separates the two steps: it makes the perceived facts explicit as a world state and grounds the reasoning in computation on that state, so that every spatial judgment, such as what a viewpoint change brings into view, is computed by a physics engine from the state rather than produced by the VLM. The two steps can then be checked and improved separately. World state. A world state is an explicit representation of the scene facts that q q depends on. Let 𝒪 = { o j } j = 1 n \mathcal{O}=\{o_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n} denote the entities that q q involves; the state covers these entities and the properties the question depends on, namely their spatial configuration and that of the cameras, rather than the whole scene. We require the state to be executable : it specifies the geometry concretely enough that geometric quantities, such as the distance between two entities, the direction of an entity from a chosen viewpoint, or the view from a camera placed anywhere in the scene, can be computed from it. The world state has two levels: the observations from each image, and the arrangement of all entities and cameras in one scene (Figure 5 ). The observation level holds what each image shows: Ω i \Omega_{i} lists the entities visible in I i I_{i} with their direction, distance, and facing relative to camera i i , measured metrically or described qualitatively. Observations from different images are expressed relative to different cameras and can be related to each other only once the camera poses are known. The layout level places all entities and cameras in a single coordinate system, which we call the shared frame: ℒ \mathcal{L} gives the position and heading ( 𝐩 j , θ j ) (\mathbf{p}_{j},\theta_{j}) of every entity and ( 𝐜 i , ϕ i ) (\mathbf{c}_{i},\phi_{i}) of every camera, obtained by aligning the observations through the camera poses, and it is the part that is instantiated for computation. The constraints 𝒞 \mathcal{C} collect the scene facts stated in q q , such as a relation between two entities or between two viewpoints; they hold exactly and apply to both levels. The observation level is kept alongside the layout because the alignment can lose or distort what an individual image shows, and the Reasoner can consult the observations directly. The world state is thus s = ( ℒ , Ω , 𝒞 ) s=(\mathcal{L},\Omega,\mathcal{C}) with Ω = { Ω i } i = 1 m \Omega=\{\Omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m} . Decoupling perception from reasoning. Spatial Harness consists of a Perceiver 𝒫 \mathcal{P} , which estimates the state from the images and the query, and a Reasoner ℛ \mathcal{R} , which answers the query by operating on the scene ℰ ⁡ [ s ^ ] \mathcal{E}[\hat{s}] that the engine instantiates from the estimate (Figure 4 ): s ^ = 𝒫 ⁡ ( x , q ) , y ^ = ℛ ⁡ ( x , q , s ^ , ℰ ⁡ [ s ^ ] ) . \hat{s}=\mathcal{P}(x,q),\qquad\hat{y}=\mathcal{R}\!\left(x,q,\hat{s};\,\mathcal{E}[\hat{s}]\right). (1) Both components are built on the same VLM, which the Perceiver equips with perception models and the Reasoner with the engine. The perceived facts are thereby exposed in s ^ \hat{s} for inspection and evaluation, and the reasoning is grounded: the consequences of spatial operations are computed by the engine from s ^ \hat{s} rather than generated by the VLM. The next two sections describe them. Figure 5: World state and its engine instantiation. Camera poses align per-view observations into a shared layout (Eq. 2 ), instantiated as labeled proxies. The query determines which entities and properties the state must contain and may state some of the facts, whereas the images are the source of the observations and the camera poses. A text channel and a visual channel process the two inputs, for one image or several alike, and compilation turns their outputs into the layout. Text channel. A VLM parses q q into four kinds of information. The entities 𝒪 \mathcal{O} are the objects and viewers the question involves. The relations are facts that q q states about pairs of entities, such as one being to the left of another. The viewpoints are facts that q q states about the cameras, such as the camera turning left between two images. The query is the spatial question to be answered together with the types of information it requires, such as direction, distance, facing, or camera motion. Relations and viewpoints, when stated, form 𝒞 \mathcal{C} and constrain the layout and the camera poses; the required information types later decide whether s ^ \hat{s} can support the answer (Section 3.3 ). Visual channel. The visual channel estimates Ω i \Omega_{i} for each image and the relative poses of the cameras when there are multiple images. It applies specialized models in four steps: detection localizes each entity in 𝒪 \mathcal{O} in the image, which with the camera intrinsics gives its direction; depth estimation gives its distance; orientation estimation gives its facing; and, for multiple images, camera pose estimation gives the rotation and translation between cameras. The VLM also describes each image qualitatively, e.g., which entities are visible and how pairs are ordered, which covers entities the models fail to measure. If no observation relates a pair of entities the answer depends on, the Perceiver queries the VLM about this pair directly, so that every relation the question hinges on has at least one source. Compilation. Compilation turns the observations and constraints into the layout. The shared frame is defined by the camera that took the first image I 1 I_{1} : this camera is placed at the origin and its viewing direction serves as the reference heading, so ( 𝐜 1 , ϕ 1 ) = ( 𝟎 , 0 ) (\mathbf{c}_{1},\phi_{1})=(\mathbf{0},0) . The poses of the other cameras come from up to four sources: the viewpoints stated in q q , the camera pose estimator, the entities seen in common with an earlier image, and the camera motion described by the VLM. We use the sources in a fixed order of priority, taking the first one that is available. For the heading, the order is stated viewpoints, common entities, pose estimator, VLM description; for the position, it is stated viewpoints, pose estimator, VLM description. The two components are chosen separately, and a component that no source provides remains unknown. Each observation is then projected into the shared frame. For an entity j j visible in image I i I_{i} , let 𝐫 i ​ j \mathbf{r}_{ij} denote its planar displacement in the camera frame and α i ​ j \alpha_{ij} its facing relative to the camera’s forward direction. The entity is placed at 𝐩 i ​ j = 𝐜 i + 𝐑 ⁡ ( ϕ i ) ​ 𝐫 i ​ j , θ i ​ j = ( ϕ i + α i ​ j ) mod 2 ​ π , \mathbf{p}_{ij}=\mathbf{c}_{i}+\mathbf{R}(\phi_{i})\,\mathbf{r}_{ij},\qquad\theta_{ij}=(\phi_{i}+\alpha_{ij})\bmod 2\pi, (2) where 𝐑 ⁡ ( ϕ i ) \mathbf{R}(\phi_{i}) is the rotation by ϕ i \phi_{i} . An entity seen in several images receives several candidates; its position 𝐩 ^ j \hat{\mathbf{p}}_{j} is the candidate with the highest confidence, which accounts for the measurement and the camera pose, and its heading is a vote over all candidates. The candidates respect the measurements but may violate a stated relation or an observed pairwise order because of projection errors. We therefore search for the layout that best satisfies the relations while staying close to the candidates, ℒ ∗ = arg max ℒ ∑ k w k [ ℒ ⊧ r k ] − λ ∑ j ∥ 𝐩 j − 𝐩 ^ j ∥ , \mathcal{L}^{*}=\arg\max_{\mathcal{L}}\ \sum_{k}w_{k}\,\mathds{1}\!\left[\mathcal{L}\models r_{k}\right]-\lambda\sum_{j}\big\|\mathbf{p}_{j}-\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{j}\big\|, (3) where r k r_{k} ranges over the pairwise relations in 𝒞 \mathcal{C} and in the observations, ℒ ⊧ r k \mathcal{L}\models r_{k} denotes that the layout satisfies r k r_{k} , and w k w_{k} reflects the reliability of the source of r k r_{k} . The relations in 𝒞 \mathcal{C} are hard constraints, the second term keeps the layout near the measurements, and the maximization is carried out by a deterministic local search that moves one entity at a time over the grid (Appendix C.2 ). 3.3 Reasoner: Grounded Reasoning with a Physics Engine The Reasoner instantiates s ^ \hat{s} in the physics engine and answers q q by querying it (Figure 4 , right). Figure 6: Engine interface exposed to the Reasoner. Representative mujoco_sim calls control the camera pose, return geometric measurements, and render views of the same scene. The pose persists across calls. Appendix C.3 gives the full signatures, argument defaults, and return values. Physics engine as a tool. A MuJoCo [ 16 ] scene is built from a scene description that gives each body a shape, either a mesh asset or a geometric primitive, and a pose. Generating a faithful mesh for each entity from the images is beyond what current models do reliably, so each entity is represented by a proxy instead: a cylinder of fixed size at ( 𝐩 j , θ j ) (\mathbf{p}_{j},\theta_{j}) , with a heading marker and the entity’s name as its label. Each input image corresponds to a camera at ( 𝐜 i , ϕ i ) (\mathbf{c}_{i},\phi_{i}) . The proxies carry exactly the information in the layout, on which the spatial questions depend. The resulting scene is ℰ ⁡ [ s ^ ] \mathcal{E}[\hat{s}] . The Reasoner operates on it by writing code against a tool interface (Figure 6 ; full signatures in Listing ), and the engine returns printed results and rendered images. The operations form three groups: • Viewpoint control places the camera at a given pose, such as the pose of an input image or a position described in the question, or moves and rotates it by given distances and angles. The camera keeps its pose between calls, so each operation starts from where the previous one left the camera, and a multi-step movement is executed step by step, one call at a time. • Measurement returns the current camera pose, the distance from the camera to each entity, or the camera motion between two views, i.e., the translation and rotation from one to the other. • Rendering returns the current view, with proxies labeled by name and distance. Every result is computed from s ^ \hat{s} , so the feedback is deterministic and consistent with it. Interactive reasoning. With the scene in place, the Reasoner answers q q in a loop with the engine. The context h 1 h_{1} initially holds x x , q q , s ^ \hat{s} , and a description of each operation in the interface, giving its purpose, arguments, and return value. At step t t , the Reasoner reads the context, issues an operation a t a_{t} , and receives the feedback f t f_{t} , i.e., the printed results and, if requested, the rendered view, which is appended to the context together with the operation: a t = ℛ ⁡ ( h t ) , f t = ℰ ⁡ [ s ^ ] ​ ( a t ) , h t + 1 = h t ⊕ ( a t , f t ) . a_{t}=\mathcal{R}(h_{t}),\qquad f_{t}=\mathcal{E}[\hat{s}](a_{t}),\qquad h_{t+1}=h_{t}\oplus(a_{t},f_{t}). (4) In this loop, the Reasoner chooses operations whose results answer the question. For example, to decide whether moving forward brings the observer closer to an entity, it measures the distance before and after the movement; to find what is on the observer’s left after a turn, it performs the turn, faces left, and renders the view. Each result confirms or revises its current judgment and determines the next operation, and the loop ends when the Reasoner answers or exhausts a budget of T max T_{\max} steps; an answer is accepted only after at least one engine call. A routing step decides beforehand whether the engine is used: the loop is entered only when the information types that q q requires are among those the state describes; otherwise, the VLM answers directly from the images (Appendix C.3 ). 4 Experiments 4.1 Experimental Setup Benchmarks. It marks the positions and facing directions of the relevant objects and cameras on a 10 × 10 10\times 10 bird’s-eye-view grid. Base reads the map as text in its prompt, whereas Spatial Harness instantiates the map in the engine and the Reasoner queries it.
Because both methods start from the same scene information, this setting compares how they reason with it, separately from errors in state estimation.
In the end-to-end setting, methods receive only the images and question.

The grounded feedback of the engine could also be used to check the world state itself.
The engine can render it from the estimated pose of each input view.
A rendering that disagrees with the corresponding input image would locate an error for the Perceiver to correct. Limitations. Our world state describes a static scene at a single moment, which suffices for the spatial questions in our benchmarks but cannot represent change over time, such as objects moving in a video. A time dimension is the natural next step: recording the poses of entities and the camera at each frame would let the engine instantiate the scene at any moment and measure how it changes between frames, which would support questions about motion and the order of events. We plan to support this temporal dimension in future work, together with the video benchmarks that require it. 6 Conclusion Answering a spatial question involves perception, which establishes the scene facts, and reasoning, which can be computed exactly once those facts are explicit.
We therefore propose Spatial Harness, which separates the two steps: a Perceiver estimates the scene facts from the images and the question as an explicit world state, and a Reasoner answers by operating on this state in a physics engine, so that every measurement and rendered view is computed from the state rather than imagined.
Experiments on four spatial reasoning benchmarks show that Spatial Harness outperforms strong baselines, and ablations attribute the gains to both the explicit state and the grounded feedback.
We hope this work encourages the use of physics engines as a general substrate for grounding multimodal reasoning about the physical world, where perception and reasoning can be checked separately. Appendix Appendix A Broader Impacts Spatial Harness may benefit embodied agents by improving spatial reasoning through explicit world states and executable feedback. Making scene interpretations and computed evidence available for inspection could support the development of more reliable and transparent embodied systems. Since errors in scene estimation or reasoning can still affect downstream decisions, applications in physical environments should include appropriate validation. The text channel and the qualitative image descriptions use the evaluated VLM. LocateAnything-3B [ Wang et al., 2026b ] detects each entity by name, and Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B [ Zhang et al., 2025a ] binds detector labels to entity names when the two do not match exactly. MoGe-2 [ Wang et al., 2025 ] estimates metric depth and camera intrinsics, and the depth of an entity is the median depth over the central half of its box. Orient Anything V2 [ Wang et al., 2026c ] estimates the facing direction of an entity in the camera frame, which is kept when its front confidence is at least 0.5 0.5 . For multiple images, Reloc3r [ Dong et al., 2025 ] estimates the relative rotation and the translation direction between the first image and each other image. The perception models are served on A100 GPUs. Image descriptions and pair queries. For each image, the VLM reports whether each entity is visible, its coarse horizontal position (left, center, or right), its depth rank, its coarse facing, and the left–right and front–back order of entity pairs. A detected box that the VLM does not confirm as visible receives a low confidence. When the question names fewer than two entities, the VLM proposes landmarks in each image, which locate the cameras and are placed on the map but do not count as question entities. Pair queries cover at most eight entity pairs for which the image descriptions give no left–right or in-front-of order, starting with the pairs that involve the reference entity of the question. A pair visible in one image is queried in that image for its left–right and front–back order; a pair seen in different images but never together is queried across two images, and only its left–right order is kept. Camera poses. Each camera pose has three components: two position coordinates and a heading. Each source supplies the components it can measure with a fixed confidence. For the heading, these are the text ( 0.95 0.95 ), landmark correspondences between images ( 0.6 0.6 ), Reloc3r ( 0.5 0.5 ), and a VLM estimate of the motion from the previous image ( 0.4 0.4 , capped by the confidence of the previous pose); for the position, the text ( 0.95 0.95 ), Reloc3r ( 0.7 0.7 ), and the VLM estimate ( 0.4 0.4 , capped likewise). The VLM estimate is requested for every image after the first, except an image whose pose the question states and whose components the text, landmark, and Reloc3r sources already cover. A landmark heading that reports no rotation is demoted below Reloc3r when Reloc3r measures a turn of at least 15 ∘ 15^{\circ} , and a landmark heading chained through an earlier image ranks below a direct one. Each component is taken separately from its most confident source, and a component that no source measures is marked as unknown, except that a camera motion stated in the text sets its omitted displacement components to zero, and a stated rotation in place keeps the previous position and its confidence in preference to visual estimates unless the text gives absolute coordinates. Fusion and layout. Each observation of an entity is weighted by the product of its confidence (at least 0.05 0.05 ), a source prior ( 1.0 1.0 for metric estimates and 0.6 0.6 for VLM descriptions), a camera-pose factor 0.35 + 0.65 ​ c 0.35+0.65c , where c c is the lower of the heading and position confidences of its camera, each at least 0.2 0.2 , and a factor of 0.45 0.45 for qualitative observations. A metric observation with bearing and depth takes precedence over a qualitative one for the same entity. The entity takes its position from the highest-weighted observation, and an entity observed only by cameras with an unknown position or heading is left off the layout. Its facing is the weighted vote of the Orient Anything V2 estimates over four 90 ∘ 90^{\circ} bins. The layout uses a 10 × 10 10\times 10 grid, the format of the MindCube cognitive maps; the first camera is placed at the grid center facing up, and the joint camera and entity coordinates are mapped onto the grid by a monotone map that keeps interior coordinates and compresses the outlying ones. This layout is kept when the text states, with confidence at least 0.9 0.9 , that every image after the first is taken from the first camera’s position with its own heading, no relations are stated, and no entity is judged to have moved with confidence at least 0.8 0.8 . Otherwise, greedy passes attempt to enforce the stated lateral and front–back orders and separate entities that share a cell. Finally, with the endpoints of satisfied stated relations fixed, a deterministic local search moves the other entities to maximize the total weight of satisfied pairwise relations minus that of violated ones: stated relations ( 20 20 ), in-front-of orders from image descriptions ( 3.2 3.2 ), left–right orders from detector bearings that differ by more than 15 ∘ 15^{\circ} ( 2.5 2.5 ), and left–right orders from image descriptions ( 1.6 1.6 ), with a term of 0.5 0.5 that keeps the queried entities apart. A cost of 0.1 0.1 per cell of displacement from the initial placement keeps that placement in ties. State passed to the Reasoner. The Reasoner receives the layout as a map listing each entity’s position and facing and each camera’s position and heading, with unknown pose components left empty. The observation level follows as text: the stated relations, the per-view observations when there are multiple images, the estimated camera motions with flags where the sources disagree, the unknown pose components, observed entities left off the map, and verdicts on whether an entity moved, for example when its positions across views disagree beyond what the camera poses explain. For questions that order distances, the distances computed from the map are appended. Metric state for quantitative questions. SpatialRGPT-Bench asks for distances and sizes in metres about regions given as masks in a single image. For these questions, the given regions replace detection, and MoGe-2 back-projects the pixels of each region into 3D. The state records, in the camera frame and in metres, each region’s center, its height along the estimated vertical direction, its width as the longer side of its horizontal footprint, and the center, horizontal, vertical, and surface distances between regions. The Reasoner receives these values as text, and the engine represents each region by a box at its measured center with its extent along the camera axes, with the first rendered view taken from the original camera and its field of view widened when needed to include all regions. The engine operations then use metres instead of grid cells. The Reasoner takes at most five turns. The world state describes positions, headings, and camera poses, and the distances and directions that follow from them; a question about appearance or occlusion lies outside this description and cannot be answered from it. Between perception and reasoning, a deterministic routing step therefore checks that q q is representable and that s ^ \hat{s} contains what the answer needs: (i) every information type that q q requires, as identified by the text channel (Section 3.2 ), must be among those the state describes; (ii) s ^ \hat{s} must load in the engine; (iii) every entity in 𝒪 \mathcal{O} must be present in s ^ \hat{s} ; and (iv) the camera pose components that the required information types depend on must be measured. If any check fails, the engine is not used, and the VLM answers from the images and the original question. Questions for which the text channel extracts no requirement stay on the engine path, so an extraction miss cannot disable the engine. C.4 Baseline Implementation In each comparison, all baselines use the same VLM as Spatial Harness and receive the same images and question; none of them receives the world state estimated by the Perceiver. Base. The VLM answers directly from the images and question, on MindCube with the prompt in Figure 9 . In the GT-map setting, the GT map is added to the prompt as text (Figure 10 ). The SAT result with Qwen2.5-VL-72B in the submitted version ( 60.27 % 60.27\% ) was affected by a scoring error; Table 3 reports the corrected value ( 68.60 % 68.60\% ). MindJourney. We use the official implementation of MindJourney [ Yang et al., 2025b ] , with Stable Virtual Camera [ Zhou et al., 2025 ] as the video world model. The VLM proposes camera trajectories, the world model generates views along them, and a spatial beam search keeps the views that the VLM judges helpful; the VLM then answers from these views and the original images. We keep the search settings of the original paper: a search depth of 3 3 , a beam width of 2 2 , and a threshold of 8 8 for both the exploration and helpfulness scores. Each action moves the camera forward by 0.75 0.75 m or rotates it by 27 ∘ 27^{\circ} , and a trajectory moves at most 1.5 1.5 m forward and turns at most 60 ∘ 60^{\circ} . Stable Virtual Camera generates 8 8 views per trajectory with 20 20 diffusion steps and a guidance scale of 4 4 , and each search layer keeps at most 18 18 helpful views. pySpatial. pySpatial [ Luo et al., 2026 ] lets the VLM write Python programs that call 3D reconstruction, camera-pose, and novel-view tools, and the final answering stage receives the original images together with the program output. The official code was updated after our submission; all pySpatial results in this paper use the updated release, which scores higher than the earlier release used in the submitted version (for example, 63.62 % 63.62\% instead of 59.10 % 59.10\% on MindCube with Qwen3.5-27B). GCA. GCA [ Chen et al., 2025 ] formalizes the question as geometric constraints and solves them with geometric tools on a scene reconstructed from the images. We run its official implementation with the same VLM on MindCube (Appendix D.2 ). Reference maps, answers, and reasoning traces are excluded from inference. The Reasoner answers by querying the instantiated state. For multiple-choice questions (MindCube, SAT, and MMSI-Bench), it decides whether the final answer explicitly selects the reference option with the prompt in Figure 17 . The evaluator operates after inference and provides no feedback to the Reasoner. If the Reasoner’s final request still yields no option, the VLM answers from the images and question, as in Algorithm 1 . Accuracy is the fraction of questions scored as correct. Quantitative questions. On SpatialRGPT-Bench, the judge extracts a value with its unit, or a clock direction, from the final answer. A distance or size is correct when it lies within [ 0.75 , 1.25 ] [0.75,1.25] times the reference value after conversion to metres, with a value without unit read as metres; a direction is correct when it is within one hour of the reference on a twelve-hour clock. The success rate is the fraction of correct answers and is distinct from multiple-choice answer accuracy. VLM-generated maps are produced directly from the images and question without the two-channel Perceiver (Figure 16 ); when a generated map fails the format check ( 237 237 of 1,050 1{,}050 questions), the VLM answers directly from the images and question. Without the text channel, the VLM lists the visible objects from the images without the question, and the relations and camera poses stated in the text are dropped. Without the visual channel, it estimates the visual fields itself while retaining the text channel’s structured output. Full Perceiver (Spatial Harness) uses both channels; the GT map supplies the annotated-state reference. These conditions distinguish direct map generation from replacing one channel within the structured pipeline. Text-module interventions. Figure 7 (a) varies the structured entity-and-relation module and the text-derived camera/view constraints separately, keeping the remaining state-construction stages and Reasoner fixed. The former extracts question-relevant entities and relations; the latter recovers stated camera motion and cross-view constraints. They are separate interventions rather than a joint removal of the entire text channel: the first replaces the extracted entities with the objects the VLM lists from the images and drops the stated relations while keeping the camera constraints, and the second drops the camera constraints while keeping the entities and relations. Visual-estimator substitutions. Each condition in Figure 7 (b) replaces one specialist with the VLM performing the same task in the same units. The detector replacement supplies regions to the unchanged depth and orientation estimators. Replacing depth, orientation, or relative pose preserves the other estimators. This substitution is necessary because deleting a parent output would also disable its dependent stages and measure the loss of an entire branch. D.4 Grounded Feedback Conditions Table 5 uses Qwen3.5-27B and a fixed GT map; the conditions differ in the feedback available to the Reasoner and the corresponding interaction protocol. Base answers from the map without engine feedback. Multi-turn self-review makes a second pass over the initial answer without new scene information. Numerical feedback only exposes geometric computations without rendered images. Rendered feedback only exposes rendered views after viewpoint changes; distance queries and camera-motion descriptions are removed, and only the camera pose remains readable. Spatial Harness provides both feedback types. These comparisons isolate reasoning over a supplied state from errors in state estimation.
