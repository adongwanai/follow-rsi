Abstract
Typed-decision encoders answer a question about a text in one forward pass, but their behaviour outside English
is rarely measured on a fixed yardstick. We release PolyRoute: 322 native-language support messages in 12
languages and 9 writing systems, 156 code-mixed or romanised messages (Hinglish, Spanglish, Arabizi), 967
optional native training rows, and a protocol for a three-way department-routing decision. Labels come from
Qwen3.8-27B with a blind second pass (91% of candidates kept); an independent model, gemma4-31b, agrees on
97.2% of test items, and the largest character 5-gram similarity between any test text and any training text is
0.33. A leaderboard of 13 systems gives 61.2% for the English-root checkpoint, 77.3% for the shipped
multilingual one and 96.6% for the best fine-tune; TF-IDF with logistic regression reaches 91.9%. On four
withheld languages the three multilingual systems score 77%, 90% and 93%. On code-mixed text the English
root (85%) is nominally ahead of the shipped multilingual checkpoint (79%; p=0.134). The split resolves
differences only above about 4 points. All texts and labels are LLM-generated.
Keywords: multilingual benchmark; typed-decision routing; code-mixed text; cross-lingual transfer; dataset card; LLM-
generated labels; leaderboard.
Key findings
1. Dataset. 322 native items (12 languages, 5 script groups) and 156 code-mixed items; independent-labeller
agreement 97.2% [94.8–98.5], lowest in Spanish (86%); no test text has a 5-gram Jaccard similarity above 0.33
to any training text.
2. Tracks separate, the top does not. Shipped checkpoints 61%/63%/77%; fine-tuned multilingual systems
94%–97%; 4 systems are statistically tied with the best entry.
3. Strong cheap baselines. TF-IDF + logistic regression on all rows scores 91.9%; an independent LLM scores
97.2%.
4. Weak spots are script groups, not token counts. Fertility–accuracy correlation ρ=-0.34 (root) and -0.17
(shipped multilingual), n=12; the shipped checkpoint is weakest on the Indic group (63%).
5. Reduced-exposure transfer. On four withheld languages: shipped 77%, no per-language rows 91%, withheld-
language fine-tune 90%, all-language fine-tune 93%; the base rows already contain about 30 generic rows per
language.
6. Code-mixed text is a different test. The English-root fine-tune scores 96% on code-mixed items but 74% on
native text; the English root is nominally ahead of the shipped multilingual checkpoint (85% vs 79%, p=0.134).
7. Resolution limit and pitfalls. Differences below about 4 points among the top systems are unresolvable at
n=322; rotating option names drops the best fine-tune to 7%.

1  Motivation and contributions
A typed-decision encoder answers a question about a piece of text in one forward pass: it reads one hidden state per
answer option and returns a distribution over the options, with no token-by-token generation. For a support desk that is
attractive, because routing becomes cheap and predictable. What a deployment cannot take for granted is that such an
encoder behaves in other languages as it does in the one it was developed in. The public model family we study ships an
English ModernBERT-large root [Warner et al. 2024] (421M parameters) and a separate multilingual checkpoint (322M
parameters, 256k-token vocabulary), with a small router that chooses between them. Which checkpoint to trust for which
language, and how much task-specific fine-tuning adds, are questions that need a fixed yardstick, not ad-hoc spot checks.
This paper provides one. PolyRoute is a test collection of 322 native-language support messages in 12 languages and 9
writing systems, a second collection of 156 code-mixed and romanised messages, an optional pool of 967 native training
rows, and an evaluation protocol with documented baselines. The question is deliberately simple (three departments), so
that the quantity under test is the language and not the difficulty of the question. The format is the typed decision used by
the model family, which lets every item be scored for accuracy, calibration and selective prediction with no prompt
engineering.
Intended uses. (i) Comparing multilingual encoders or fine-tuning recipes on routing-style decisions; (ii) auditing a
system for language gaps before deployment; (iii) studying cross-lingual transfer through a ready-made withheld-language
variant; (iv) testing robustness to code-mixed text. Not intended as a measurement of real-world traffic: all texts are LLM-
written (Section 13).
1.1  Contributions
1. A native-language test split with per-language label statistics and an independent label audit (97.2% agreement; lowest
in Spanish at 86%).
2. A code-mixed split (Hinglish, Spanglish, Arabizi) disjoint from every training file.
3. Five fine-tuning sets with nested native content, one of them withholding four languages, together with an explicit
account of what each contains — including a block of 399 generic multilingual rows that even the ‘withheld’ set still
contains.
4. A leaderboard of 13 systems with intervals, rank-stability analysis and the smallest difference the split can resolve (55
of 78 system pairs are separated at p<0.01).
5. Analyses a user will want to reproduce: per language and script, tokenizer fertility, the shipped router, learning curve,
transfer to withheld languages, calibration, stress conditions and an error taxonomy with real examples.
1.2  Where it sits among multilingual benchmarks
Established suites such as XNLI [Conneau et al. 2018] and XTREME [Hu et al. 2020] test cross-lingual transfer on standard
tasks, built on multilingual pre-trained encoders [Pires et al. 2019; Conneau et al. 2020]; English intent-detection sets such
as Banking77 [Casanueva et al. 2020] are the closest in task shape; MASSIVE [FitzGerald et al. 2022] covers intent
classification in 51 languages with human translation; GLUECoS [Khanuja et al. 2020] targets code-switching. PolyRoute is
narrower. It fixes one decision type and one question text and is shaped by the typed-decision interface, so that models
which score options through placeholder tokens can be evaluated without a generation step. The price of that narrowness
is that conclusions about other tasks do not follow; the benefit is a compact split with documented provenance. We follow
datasheet [Gebru et al. 2018] and data-statement [Bender and Friedman 2018] practice in Sections 4 and 13, and the advice
on statistical power in NLP evaluation [Card et al. 2020; Berg-Kirkpatrick et al. 2012] in Section 7.

2  Task definition
2.1  The typed decision
Every item is a pair (text, question). The question is of type choice: an instruction (“Which department should handle
this?”) and an ordered set of named options with a one-line description each: billing (invoices, payments, refunds),
technical (bugs, outages, system errors) and other (everything else). Instruction and options are in English in every item
whatever the language of the message, mirroring a deployment in which the operator writes the schema once and
messages arrive in many languages. The gold answer is one option.
2.2  How the model under test reads an item
The Laya family packs question and text into one sequence: a classification token, the question and instruction, a
separator, a placeholder token in front of each option name and description, a separator, and the message, truncated to 512
tokens in total (192 for the question part). The encoder runs once; a two-layer option head reads the hidden state at each
placeholder and emits one score per option, and a softmax over the scores gives the confidence. The root checkpoint has
28 encoder layers of width 1024; the multilingual checkpoint has 22 layers of width 768, with 61% of its parameters in
the embedding table. Neither the data nor the metrics depend on this architecture: any system that returns a distribution
over the three options can be submitted.
Figure 1. The benchmark item. A message and a typed question are packed into one sequence with a placeholder in front of
each option; the model reads the hidden state at every placeholder and returns one probability per option (the numbers shown
are the actual output of the best fine-tuned system on the first test item).
2.3  Why routing
Department routing is topical: the cue is lexical or semantic (a refund, an outage) and not syntactic (negation, word order).
That keeps the items answerable in every language with simple vocabulary, so an error is unlikely to be explained by the
question being too hard for a native speaker. The price is a high ceiling: tuned systems score above 95% (Section 8), so the
benchmark discriminates mainly among systems not tuned to it, and Section 7 quantifies how little room is left at the top.
3  Construction of the data
3.1  Generation
For each language and each department the teacher model (Qwen3.8-27B, sampling temperature 0.9) is asked for up to ten
diverse, realistic support messages in that language only, in its native script, with no English words except product names,
each of which has the requested department as its correct answer (label-conditioned self-generation in the style of [Wang

et al. 2022]); length, style and vocabulary are varied through a per-call variation seed. Across the native test split and the
native training rows this produced 1,420 candidate texts.
3.2  Blind filtering
The candidates of each call are shown to the same model again without their intended labels, together with the three
options, and it picks one option per text. A text survives only if the blind choice equals the intended label: 1,289 of 1,420
candidates (90.8%) did. Retention differs by department (billing 95%, technical 97%, other 80%) and by language, from
86% for Hindi to 97% for Turkish (Figure 2). The filter removes some genuinely ambiguous messages, but it is a self-
consistency check and not a human one, which is why Section 5 adds an audit by a different model family.
Figure 2. Filter retention for every language-by-label cell. Low cells flag where the teacher’s intended label and its blind
reading diverge, which is a first, language-level indicator of label difficulty.
3.3  Separation of files
The test split (322 items) and the training rows (967 rows) come from two generation runs with different variation seeds,
and during each run any text identical to a text in another split file is dropped. The code-mixed split was generated the
same way from three prompts: Hindi in Latin letters freely mixed with English; Spanish mixed with English inside a
sentence; and Arabic written in Latin letters and digits, mixed with English. The prompts ask for chat-like text and not for
textbook code-switching. Of 180 candidates, 158 passed the filter and 156 are in the released file.

Figure 3. How the benchmark files were produced and what survived at each step (counts are the real generation log; the test
split and the native training rows come from separate generation runs with different variation seeds).

4.1  Length and tokenization
Character length varies by a factor of 4.6 between languages (the shortest messages, in characters, are Korean, Chinese,
Japanese), but token counts matter more to an encoder. The English-root tokenizer needs 2.0× as many tokens as the
multilingual one on average, and up to 3.1× for Tamil (Table 2). The longest message is 211 root tokens, so truncation at
512 tokens never occurs: inflation changes what the model sees, not how much of it.

4.3  A block of generic multilingual rows in the base training set
The 2,818 base fine-tuning rows, shared by every training track, include 399 department-routing rows produced by an
earlier generic prompt that asked for texts “in a different language from a list of twelve”. They are not labelled by language;
by dominant script they split as Latin 138, Han + kana 34, Arabic 34, Cyrillic 33, Han 33, Bengali 33, Hangul 32, Tamil 31,
Devanagari 31, i.e. roughly 30–35 rows for each non-Latin language. They are disjoint from the test split by the filters of
Section 3, but they mean that no training track is free of exposure to the twelve languages. The track names below
say ‘no per-language rows’ or ‘withheld’, never ‘zero-shot’.
4.4  Language fidelity of the generated text
The script-purity column of Table 2 is a coarse check. Two cheap, stricter checks find generation artefacts that we left in
place. First, 3 of the 217 non-Latin-script test messages contain at least one letter from a writing system that is neither the
expected one nor Latin (Bengali 1, Hindi 1, Japanese 1). Second, a stop-word heuristic flags 2 of the 105 Latin-script test
messages as English text filed under Turkish, which script purity cannot detect. These items are valid routing questions
with correct departments, but they are not clean samples of the named language, and a user who needs strictly
monolingual text should filter them (the indices are reproducible from the released file).
5  Label quality and leakage
5.1  Independent labelling
Because the gold labels come from one model family, we asked a different family (gemma4-31b) to label every test item
blind, with the same prompt format as the generation-time filter. It agrees with the gold label on 313 of 322 items (97.2%
[94.8–98.5]). Agreement is high in every language (Figure 4): the lowest per-language point estimate is Spanish (86%, 4 of
28 items disputed) and 6 of 12 languages are at 100%. At about 27 items per language the intervals are wide, and only
Spanish is distinguishable from the pooled value by eye; we make no per-language claims about label quality.
Figure 4. Label quality measured by an independent model family. Left: per-language agreement of gemma4-31b with the gold
label (band: interval for the pooled split; dashed line: pooled agreement). Right: gold-versus-independent confusion counts; the
off-diagonal cells are the disputed items.
The disagreements are not spread evenly across departments: agreement is 99.2% for billing (n=118), 99.1% for technical
(n=116), 92.0% for other (n=88). The most frequent cell is gold ‘other’ labelled ‘technical’ by the independent model (6
items), consistent with the residual class being the least well delimited: a message that mentions a payment problem

caused by an outage can legitimately be argued either way. We define the consensus subset as the 313 items on which
both labellers agree, and report it as a secondary view in the leaderboard (the confusion counts are in the right panel of
Figure 4).
5.2  Does label noise explain the model errors?
It can be bounded directly. The best fine-tuned system (ML fine-tune, all native rows) scores 97.1% on the consensus
subset against 77.8% on the 9 disputed items; the shipped multilingual checkpoint scores 79.2% against 11.1%. Systems
are less accurate on disputed items, as expected when gold labels are noisy there, but the 9 disputed items could account
for at most 9 of the 125 errors of the root checkpoint, so the gap between weak and strong systems is real and not an
artefact of the labels. For strong systems the picture is different: 2 of the 11 errors of the best fine-tuned system fall on
disputed items (a minority), so label noise is a visible but not dominant share of what remains.
5.3  Leakage audit
For every test text we computed the maximum Jaccard similarity of its character 5-gram set to any text in the pool of all
3,785 training rows (and, for the native split, to the rest of the test split). Table 4 and Figure 5 summarise the result: no
native test text exceeds 0.33 against the training pool, no code-mixed text exceeds 0.20, and the most similar pair of native
test texts has similarity 0.17; there are 0 exact duplicates with training rows and 0 inside the test split. Several items in the
same language share topical vocabulary (refund, invoice, crash), which probably accounts for most of the similarity mass
near 0.05–0.15, but nothing resembling a character-level near-duplicate (a paraphrase would not be caught by this
measure).

attributable to that quantity.

6.3  Evaluation protocol
1. Present each item with the fixed question text and the options in the canonical order (billing, technical, other); do not
rename options, because Section 10 shows how strongly the models follow option names.
2. Obtain one probability per option; the prediction is the arg-max, the confidence is its probability.

3. Report accuracy with a Wilson interval on the native split and, separately, on the code-mixed split; also report
language-macro accuracy and the worst language.
4. For pairwise claims use an exact McNemar test on the shared items and state the number of comparisons made.
5. Never tune on the test items; if a threshold is calibrated, calibrate it on a held-out part of the training rows.
6. Declare the exposure of the fine-tuning set to the twelve languages (including generic multilingual rows) and the
number of seeds.

7.2  How large a difference can the split resolve?
A benchmark with 322 items cannot rank systems whose accuracies are close. For a paired comparison in which two
systems disagree on a fraction d of the items, the smallest difference detectable at α=0.01 with 80% power is about
3.42√(d/n) (a normal approximation to McNemar’s test). Table 8 evaluates this for the sizes that occur in this benchmark;
Figure 7 shows the curve and places every pair of leaderboard systems against it. The top five fine-tuned systems disagree
on 3.6% of the items on average, so on the native split the resolvable difference among them is 3.6 points — larger than
the largest gap between them (2.8 points). For a single language (about 26 items) even a 25%-discordant pair needs a 34-
point gap, which is why we do not rank systems per language. We use the single threshold p<0.01 without correction for
the 78 leaderboard pairs; with a Bonferroni threshold of 0.01/78, 48 of the 55 separated pairs remain separated. Anyone
claiming a gain of a few points should repeat training with several seeds and use the paired bootstrap over items.

8.2  Leaderboard
Tables 10 and 11 give the full results; Figure 8 draws the primary metric. Four observations follow.
1. Tracks separate cleanly; the top does not. The shipped checkpoints (track A) score 61.2%, 62.7% and 77.3%;
every fine-tuned multilingual system (tracks B and C) scores between 93.8% and 96.6%. The highest accuracy is
gemma4-31b as classifier at 97.2%, and 4 other systems cannot be separated from it at p<0.01 (ML FT w/o 4 langs,
ML FT 25%, ML FT 50%, ML FT 100%).

2. A bag of n-grams is a serious baseline. TF-IDF + LR trained on all rows scores 91.9%, +4.7 points (p=0.014) from
the best fine-tuned model; trained on the base rows only it scores 77.0%, level with the shipped multilingual
checkpoint (-0.3 points (p=1.000) for TF-IDF relative to it). Department routing is lexically easy, and any claim about a
multilingual encoder on this task has to be read against that.

97.2%, +0.6 points (p=0.804) relative to the best fine-tuned model. It was not trained on these labels, so this is also a
ceiling of sorts for the label audit; it costs a hosted model and a network round trip per item, against 2.0 ms for the
fine-tuned encoder.
4. Multilingual fine-tunes are better calibrated. ECE falls from 0.058 (root) and 0.078 (shipped multilingual) to
0.011 for the best fine-tune, whereas the English-root fine-tune has 0.166 (Section 10).

8.3  Rank stability and pairwise structure
Resampling the test items 2,000 times (Figure 9) shows how well each position is determined. By mean bootstrap rank
the order at the top is gemma4-31b (1.4), ML FT 100% (1.7), ML FT w/o 4 langs (3.9), ML FT 50% (4.3), ML FT 25%

8.4  Seed variation
Three seeds of the best recipe score 96.6%, 96.9%, 95.7% (standard deviation 0.6 points, range 1.2). Any two seeds
answer 2.7% of the items differently, a floor on the discordance that a one-seed comparison cannot get below. For
comparison, the 25%, 50% and 100% native-row models differ from one another by 1.9 points; that exceeds the seed-to-
seed range, but it is small against the sampling interval, so we treat them as one cluster in the rest of the paper.

9.3  Tokenizer fertility and resource tier
Tokenizer fragmentation is the usual explanation for weak performance on non-Latin scripts [Rust et al. 2021; Ahia et al.
2023; Petrov et al. 2023]. We test it on the benchmark with the standard fertility measure (tokens per character). Across
the twelve languages the Spearman correlation between fertility and accuracy is ρ=-0.34 for the root checkpoint with its
own tokenizer (permutation p=0.28) and ρ=-0.17 for the shipped multilingual one (p=0.60): moderate negative and
negligible negative respectively, and neither is distinguishable from zero at n=12. The ratio of root to multilingual token
counts per language correlates with the accuracy gain from root to multilingual at ρ=+0.47. Figure 11 shows why
correlations are weak: the three highest-fertility languages under the root tokenizer are Korean (62%), Chinese (79%),
Tamil (36%) and the three lowest are Spanish (68%), Portuguese (82%), German (81%), so accuracy does not follow
fragmentation monotonically. A moderate negative correlation is compatible with these data and cannot be excluded at

n=12, but fertility alone is not a usable predictor of per-language accuracy on this task; it is also compatible with accounts
in which the amount of pre-training data per language matters [Wu and Dredze 2020].

9.4  Withheld-language transfer
The withheld-language variant (Figure 6) fine-tunes on the base rows plus native rows of eight languages. Table 14 and
Figure 12 give the result on Tamil, Bengali, Korean and Arabic. The shipped multilingual checkpoint scores 76.7% on
them; the model fine-tuned without them scores 90.3% (+13.6 points, p=0.004); the model fine-tuned on all twelve scores
93.2% (a further +2.9 points, p=0.250). Task fine-tuning therefore transfers to languages without dedicated rows. The
additional gain from in-language rows is not significant at this sample size. The model with no per-language rows at all
scores 91.3% on the same four languages, so the transfer here comes from the base rows and not from the other eight
languages’ native rows (-1.0 points for adding the eight languages, p=1.000).
Caveat on the word ‘withheld’. The base rows contain generic multilingual texts (Section 4.3): 31 in Tamil, 33 in Bengali,
32 in Korean, 34 in Arabic. These are about 40% of the 80 dedicated rows each of those languages has in the full set. The
result is thus reduced exposure, not zero-shot transfer; an exposure-free comparison would need a base set rebuilt without
the generic block. The bag-of-n-grams model shows what withholding does to a model with no pre-training: trained
without the four languages it scores 74.8% on them against 93.2% with them.

9.5  Native-row learning curve
Adding native rows to the base set moves accuracy from 93.8% (no per-language rows) to 94.7% (241 rows), 94.7% (483)

10  Stress conditions and confidence
10.1  Stress conditions
Two protocol choices could change a score without changing the model: the order in which the options are listed and the
way they are named. Table 18 and Figure 16 quantify both on the native split. Cycling the option order changes the answer
for 37% of root predictions, 8% for the shipped multilingual checkpoint and 1.6% for the best fine-tune, so a fine-tuned
system is much less order-sensitive, but only a system with a flip rate near zero would make the canonical order
irrelevant. Replacing the names by letters, numbers or opaque tokens lowers accuracy of the best fine-tune from 96.6% to
91.6–94.4%; rotating the names while keeping the descriptions drops it to 6.5%, and the model follows the name of the
gold option on 92% of items. The fine-tuned systems read the option names, not only the descriptions, and the benchmark
question must therefore be used exactly as published (rule 1 of Section 6.3) [Pezeshkpour and Hruschka 2023; Zheng et al.
2023].

10.2  Confidence and abstention
A router is only useful if it knows when it is wrong [Guo et al. 2017; Geifman and El-Yaniv 2017]. The root checkpoint’s
mean confidence is 59.5% against an accuracy of 61.2%; the shipped multilingual checkpoint is 84.7% confident at 77.3%
accuracy; the best fine-tune 97.7% at 96.6% (Table 19, Figure 17). Selective accuracy rises when low-confidence items are
withheld: the shipped multilingual checkpoint goes from 77.3% to 83.3% on its most confident 80%, the root from 61.2%
to 67.3%. Fine-tuning also makes the confidence more informative about errors (lower AURC and ECE). A caveat for users
of the benchmark: with 11 errors, the best system’s calibration is estimated from very few mistakes, and ECE in particular
is noisy at that count.

11.3  Where the shipped checkpoint is confidently wrong
The shipped multilingual checkpoint makes 17 confident errors (confidence ≥0.9) among its 73, and the best fine-tune
corrects 16 of them. These are the cases a deployment would route wrongly without any warning from the confidence
score.
12  Recommended usage and pitfalls
Report the two splits separately. The English-root fine-tune and the shipped multilingual checkpoint score 73.6%
and 77.3% on the native split (3.7 points apart) but 96.2% and 79.5% on the code-mixed split (16.7 points apart). A
single pooled number would hide that the two splits measure different things.
Do not rank systems within about 4 points of each other on the native split. The split cannot resolve it (Section
7); use several seeds (0.6 points of seed standard deviation here) and the paired bootstrap.
Do not make per-language claims from this split. About 26 items per language give cell intervals of roughly ±11–
19 points. Pool by script group or tier if a language-level statement is needed.
Keep the question text and option names fixed. Rotating the names while keeping the descriptions reduces the
best fine-tune to 6.5% (Table 18); changing the schema changes the task.
Read the withheld-language track as reduced exposure. The base rows hold 399 generic multilingual texts
(Section 4.3).
Compare with a bag of n-grams. TF-IDF + LR on all rows reaches 91.9%; a multilingual-encoder result is
informative only to the extent that it exceeds what lexical cues give.
Use the consensus subset as a sanity check, not as the headline. It removes 9 disputed items and changes the
best system’s accuracy from 96.6% to 97.1%.

Do not tune on the test items. Both splits are small enough to be over-fitted by repeated model selection [Bowman
and Dahl 2021].

13.1  Limitations
All labels and texts are LLM-made. The labels were produced and filtered by one model family; the independent
audit (97.2%) bounds but does not remove the dependence, and agreement between two language models is not
agreement with speakers [Northcutt et al. 2021; Zheng et al. 2023]. The text itself may be more regular and more
standard than what customers write; dialect, typos, scripts mixed within a word and domain vocabulary are under-
represented.
Machine-written text in low-resource languages. The teacher’s fluency and cultural fit probably vary across the
twelve languages; no native speaker has reviewed the texts. This matters most for Tamil and Bengali.
One task, one schema. Routing is topical and has a high ceiling (97.2% for the best entry); the benchmark says little
about tasks where syntax or negation decides.
Small cells. About 26 items per language and 156 code-mixed items: only large gaps are resolvable (Section 7).
Exposure in the base rows. 399 generic multilingual rows are shared by all training tracks.
Other limits. Generated text contains artefacts (foreign-script fragments, English text filed under a Latin-script
language; Section 4.4); only the best recipe has three seeds (the others are single runs); the resource tiers are an author-
assigned grouping, not a measurement; and no classical or LLM baseline was run on the code-mixed split.
13.2  Ethical considerations
The data contain no real personal information. The main risk is over-reading: a benchmark of synthetic text could be used
to certify a system for lower-resource-language speakers who were never represented in it, and a router that is wrong more
often for some languages would then deliver slower or wrong service to those users. We therefore ask that results be
reported with the intervals, the split names and the withheld-language caveat above, and that deployments be validated on
human-written messages from the target community. Generated text can also carry the teacher’s stereotypes about

languages and countries; we did not audit for that. Compute cost is small: the benchmark runs in seconds on one GPU (2.0
ms per item for the best encoder).
14  Conclusion
PolyRoute turns the question ‘does this typed-decision encoder work outside English?’ into a measurement with a
documented provenance: 322 native-language messages in 12 languages, 156 code-mixed ones, an independent label audit
(97.2% agreement), a leakage audit (maximum similarity 0.33), five nested training sets and thirteen leaderboard entries.
The first results are that the English root scores 61.2% and the shipped multilingual checkpoint 77.3%; fine-tuning the
multilingual encoder reaches 96.6% and most of that gain needs no dedicated native rows; a bag of n-grams is within 5
points of the best fine-tune; and on code-mixed text the English root is nominally ahead of the shipped multilingual
checkpoint (85% vs 79%, p=0.134). The split cannot separate systems closer than about 4 points, and its labels, texts and
languages come from language models; those limits, not the leaderboard, are the main message for anyone who builds on
it.

Appendix D  Prompts and scoring recipe
D.1  Generation request (paraphrased)
Write k (at most 10) diverse, realistic inputs for a classifier. Question: “Which department should handle this?” The correct answer
for every input must be <department> (with its one-line description). Write every text in <language> only (native script), with no
English words except product names. Make them unambiguous to a careful human and vary length, style and vocabulary
(variation seed s). Return only a JSON array of strings.
D.2  Blind relabelling request (paraphrased)
Question and the three options with their descriptions are shown, followed by the numbered texts of one call; for each text choose
exactly one option key; return only a JSON array of keys in order. The intended label is not shown. A text is kept if and only if the
returned key equals the intended label.
D.3  Scoring recipe
1. Load data/ml_test.jsonl and data/mix_test.jsonl; each row has state (the message), questions (the typed
question) and expected (the gold option).
2. For every row build the input exactly as published and obtain one probability per option in the order billing, technical,
other.
3. Prediction = arg-max; confidence = its probability; correct = prediction equals the gold option.
4. Report accuracy with a Wilson interval, language-macro accuracy, worst language, macro-F1, NLL and ECE, on the
native and code-mixed splits separately, and the track under which the system was trained.
