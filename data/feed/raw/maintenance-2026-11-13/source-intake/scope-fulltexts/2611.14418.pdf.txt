Abstract
Local decoding usually fixes one weight precision for the whole generation. We study a different allocation:
store 8-bit weights, decode with their top 4 bits, and redo a step at 8 bits only when the low-precision
output distribution looks uncertain (entropy above a threshold or a small top-two margin). Quantization is
simulated, the model is Qwen3-0.6B, and the benchmark is GSM8K. Cost is counted in weight bytes read per
token. On 36724 replayed steps the entropy of the low pass ranks argmax flips with AUC 0.93. At a 15%
escalation budget it catches 78% of flips and the margin gate 94%, against the 15% expected for a random
gate of equal rate (a computed expectation, not a run). In 150 live problems all-high scores 64.0% and all-
low 55.3% (McNemar p=0.053). An entropy gate with a 16% escalation rate scores 62.7% at 0.87 of the all-
high weight bytes. Its paired difference is -1.3 (-8.0 to +5.3) points to all-high and +7.3 (+0.0 to +14.7) points
to all-low (p=0.080), so the live data do not separate the gate from either end. Verified speculation reaches
zero KL at similar bytes in the same cost model. A live random control, a thinking-mode phase rule, a second
model and live KL were not run.
Keywords: local language models; quantization; nested bit-planes; uncertainty gating; weight bytes; speculative
decoding.
Key findings (recomputed from the frozen result files at build time)

1  Introduction
A language model that runs on one machine spends most of its time reading weights from memory. For
small models decoded one token at a time, the number of bytes read per token is a good first-order
measure of cost, and unlike wall-clock time it does not change with the load on the machine. Low-bit
weights cut those bytes at some cost in accuracy, and the usual choice is a single precision for the whole
model and the whole generation.
This paper tests another way to spend the same bytes. The model keeps one stored copy of the weights at 8
bits and decodes every step with only the top 4 bits of each code. When a low-bit step looks uncertain, the
step is redone with all 8 bits and that answer is used. The rule for “uncertain” is cheap because it reads only
the output distribution of the low pass: either the entropy of that distribution or the gap between its two
largest logits.
The main question is whether, at a given escalation rate, an uncertainty gate recovers more of the all-high
behaviour than escalating at random steps, and what that recovery costs in weight bytes. A second question
concerns Qwen3 thinking mode, where a long reasoning span precedes a short answer: a phase gate would
decode the span at low precision and the answer at high precision. The thinking-mode run did not take
place, so that question stays open here.
1.1  Scope, data and status
Everything reported comes from one model (Qwen3-0.6B), one benchmark (GSM8K) and one simulated
quantizer. A live run decodes 150 test problems under ten configurations and scores the answers. A replay
analysis takes the all-high trajectories of 150 test problems (36724 generated steps) and asks what each gate
would have escalated if it had been attached to them.
The live run covers 150 problems and no more, so the sample is small and the intervals are wide. Table 1 lists
every stage of the plan and whether it ran, and the claims in the paper are limited to the rows marked done.

1.2  Summary of findings
In replay, the entropy of the low pass ranks flip positions with an area under the ROC curve of 0.928 (95%
interval 0.924 to 0.932), and the margin gives 0.959 (0.956 to 0.962). A random gate gives 0.5 by
construction. At a 15% escalation budget the entropy gate catches 78% of the argmax flips and 59% of the
KL mass, the margin gate 94% and 55%, and random escalation 15% of each.
In the live run the all-high configuration scores 64.0% and the all-low configuration 55.3% on 150 items. The
gap is -8.7 (-16.7 to -0.7) points with an interval that excludes zero, with a lower bound close to zero, and the

exact McNemar test gives p=0.053, so even the all-low loss is borderline at this sample size. The entropy
gate with a 16% realised rate scores 62.7% and reads 0.874 of the all-high bytes per token in the cost model
of Section 3. Its paired difference is -1.3 (-8.0 to +5.3) points to all-high and +7.3 (+0.0 to +14.7) points to
all-low (p=0.080). Section 10 discusses what such intervals can support.
2  Related work
The closest work in mechanism is Entropy Adaptive Decoding [9], which switches from a smaller to a larger
model when a rolling entropy of the logits crosses a threshold and accepts that the output diverges from the
large model instead of verifying it. For one Llama pair on MATH, its abstract reports 96.7% of the 11B score
while using the 11B model for 43% of tokens. The gate here is the same kind of rule applied to a different
switch: bit-planes of one stored weight tensor in place of two separate models. Because a bit-nested switch
shares storage and the KV cache, the cost model changes accordingly.
Nested precision is already established as a storage idea. Any-Precision LLM [8] overlays models of 3 to n
bits in the footprint of one n-bit model, Matryoshka Quantization [10] trains one checkpoint whose int2 and
int4 slices live inside int8, and MatGPTQ [11] does the same post-training with budget-aware per-layer bit
search. QuEPT [3] builds one model that switches among bit-widths at run time. These papers answer how to
build weights that can be sliced and leave open a per-token policy for choosing the slice while decoding. The
substrate used here is much simpler than any of them: round-to-nearest codes, with the low version
obtained by dropping the bottom 4 bits.
The speculative family uses a low-bit version of the target as the proposer and then verifies. BitNest [2]
embeds the low-precision proposer in the target's own weights and reports 95.2% average acceptance and
1.48 to 1.61 times speedup on 7B to 8B models. QuantSpec [12] pairs 4-bit weights with a hierarchical 4-bit
KV cache in a self-speculative scheme with over 90% acceptance, and Quasar [13] quantizes the verification
pass. Kim et al. [5] quantize a Qwen3.5-4B target and a trained block-diffusion proposer. Zhang et al. [4]
show that tree verification can erase the memory advantage of a 4-bit target, and TriSpec [14] lets a light
proxy approve easy proposals so that the full target runs only on uncertain tokens.
Uncertainty signals are used elsewhere too. MARS [6] relaxes rejection sampling when the top candidates of
the target have a small margin, and entropy-aware speculative decoding [18] penalises tokens where both
models are uncertain. CALM [17] exits layers early on confidence, and Faster Cascades [15] implement a
deferral rule through speculative execution. Don't Waste Bits [7] picks a KV-cache bit-width per token with a
learned controller that uses an entropy-based feature; among the abstracts reviewed it is the closest per-
token precision switch, and it acts on the cache where this paper acts on the weights.
Two empirical papers motivate the entropy signal. Lotfi et al. [1] report that the token-level KL between a
quantized and a full-precision model is high exactly where next-token entropy is high, and they cut chain-of-
thought length with a logit penalty. Liu et al. [16] study quantized reasoning models and find W8A8 or
W4A16 near lossless and lower bit-widths risky. The replay analysis in Section 5 gives a measurement in the
same direction for a different model, a different quantizer, and a different metric (argmax flips as well as KL).
2.1  Differences from earlier work, and where verification wins
In the abstracts reviewed, none combines a per-token uncertainty gate on weight precision, no verification
step, a matched random control, and a byte-based cost account. That statement is limited to the searches
logged for this paper, which used titles and abstracts only; full texts were not read, so a relevant detail may
be missing.
Verification-based methods win on one property: the output is exactly what the target would have produced
under greedy decoding, so accuracy and KL to the target are unchanged by construction. A gate offers no
such guarantee, and its error is whatever it misses. Replay in Section 6 gives numbers for this: at similar
bytes, verified speculation has zero KL and the gate has a positive KL. Verification also wins when proposals
are accepted at a high rate, because one high-precision pass then serves several tokens.

A gate can win in two situations. It needs no second pass over a block of proposals, so it never pays for a
rejected block. It also suits settings where acceptance is hard to predict or where the cost of a high-precision
pass is dominated by something that a proposal block cannot share. Whether either situation holds for a
given model and hardware is open, and this paper tests it only through a byte model.
3  Method
3.1  Simulated nested quantization
Every linear weight of the transformer blocks (query, key, value, output, gate, up and down projections) is
quantized once, in memory, with round-to-nearest on an asymmetric 8-bit grid, and groups of 64
consecutive input weights share a scale and an offset. The high version dequantizes all 8 bits. The low version
keeps the top 4 bits of each code and reconstructs at the midpoint of the dropped cell. Both come from the
same stored byte, so no extra weights are held for the low model. The embedding table and the output head
stay in bf16.
This is a simulation: the dequantized weights are bf16 tensors and the matrix products run in bf16. No
packed kernel exists, so no speedup is measured. The cost of a configuration is computed from the numbers
of low and high passes it makes, multiplied by the bytes each pass would read.
Figure 1. Both precisions are read from one stored 8-bit code. The gate looks only at the low-pass distribution; if it fires,
the step is redone with the full code.
3.2  The gates
Both gates look at the low-pass logits z of the current step. The entropy gate escalates when the entropy H
of softmax(z) exceeds a threshold, and the margin gate escalates when the difference between the largest
and second-largest logit falls below a threshold. Both thresholds are set on a calibration run of 32 GSM8K
training problems decoded with the all-high model, chosen so that the low-pass signal crosses the threshold
on 3, 7, 15 and 30 percent of calibration steps. The entropy thresholds are 1.55, 1.16, 0.76, 0.31 nats and the
margin thresholds are 0.25, 0.50, 1.25, 2.88, and the names ent0 to ent3 and mar0 to mar3 follow that order.
Escalation is decided after the low pass, so every step pays for a low pass and an escalated step pays for a
high pass as well. This waste is part of the cost accounted below. A variant that reuses the previous step's
signal would avoid it and was not run.
3.3  Cost model and metrics
The cost of one pass is the weight bytes it reads. Block weights cost 8.5 bits per weight in the high version
and 4.5 bits in the low version, including 32 bits of scale and offset per group of 64. The bf16 output head is
read in every pass. Table 2 lists the byte counts.

Because the head is read in full in every pass, a low pass costs 0.717 of a high pass, while the block ratio

The live runs report accuracy with a bootstrap interval over problems, the paired difference to all-high with a
bootstrap interval, and an exact McNemar test on the discordant problems. They also report mean generated
tokens, the realised escalation rate, and the relative bytes per generated token, with the prompt (prefill)
priced at one high pass for every configuration except all-low. KL to the all-high model is reported from
replay only.
3.4  Controls
The matched random control escalates each step with a probability equal to the realised rate of the gate. Its
expected recall of flip positions equals the rate, and its expected KL after escalation is the all-low KL scaled
by one minus the rate, so in replay it is an analytic expectation that needs no sampling and has no interval. A
live random control was planned and did not run, so no live comparison of the gate with random escalation
exists. An oracle control ranks steps by their true KL to the high pass and bounds what any gate based on the
low pass could catch.
3.5  Phase gating (design)
Qwen3 in thinking mode writes a reasoning span and closes it with a dedicated token before the final
answer. A phase gate runs the span at low precision and switches to high precision after that token. The
reverse assignment and a combined phase-plus-entropy rule are natural controls, and the driver supports
these modes, but none was run on thinking-mode generations, so no result is given.
4  Setup
4.1  Model, data, decoding
The model is Qwen3-0.6B [20] with its published weights, and the benchmark is GSM8K [19] with problems
drawn from the test split by a fixed shuffle. Thresholds come from 32 problems of the training split. The
prompt asks for step-by-step reasoning with the final answer in a boxed expression, in the non-thinking chat
format, and generation is greedy with a cap of 384 tokens. An answer is correct when the last boxed number
after the optional closing think tag equals the gold number. A generation that reaches the cap usually has no
boxed answer and scores wrong, so Figure 17 shows how often each configuration hit it.
Generations of different problems share a batch of 12 sequences, each with its own position and its own
gating rule, and a finished sequence is replaced by a new problem. Results are written to disk every 20
finished generations, so a stopped run can resume.
4.2  Which items are compared
The live run processes problems in order and runs ten configurations on each, so every table of live results
uses the 150 problems that finished under all ten configurations and every comparison is paired. The replay
analysis uses the all-high trajectories of 150 problems, which are the same problems when 150 equals 150.

5  Step-level analysis
The replay analysis feeds the all-high trajectory to the model and runs the low pass at every position,
recording the entropy and the margin of the low-pass distribution, the KL from the high to the low
distribution, and whether the two argmax tokens agree. A step where they disagree is called a flip. Over
36724 steps from 150 problems the mean KL is 0.0203 nats and 3.79% of steps flip.
Flips are rare and each one can change the rest of a generation, which is why the gates aim at a small
fraction of steps. Figure 2 shows the entropy of the low pass separately for steps that flip and steps that do
not, with the calibration thresholds marked. Figure 3 shows the margin the same way.
Figure 2. Entropy of the low pass, split by whether the argmax flips. Dashed lines are the thresholds that select 3, 7, 15 and
30 percent of calibration steps. Log scale.
Figure 3. Margin of the low pass, split by flip. Dashed lines mark the margin thresholds for the same four rates.
The area under the ROC curve for predicting a flip is 0.928 for entropy and 0.959 for a small margin, with
intervals from 100 resamples of problems of 0.924 to 0.932 and 0.956 to 0.962. For predicting the top 10% of
steps by KL the values are 0.924 and 0.908. These numbers describe ranking quality over all steps, and the
gate uses only the extreme tail.

6  Controls in replay
Replay works as a controlled experiment here: each gate is attached to the all-high trajectory, and what it
would have escalated is compared with random and oracle gates of equal rate. An escalated step is assumed
to match the high distribution exactly, so the result is a first-order estimate of KL to all-high that ignores how
a flip changes later context in a live run, which Section 7 measures through accuracy.

Figures 10 and 11 show both gates against the random diagonal and the oracle. At a 15% budget the
entropy ordering reaches 78% of flips and 59% of KL mass, the margin ordering 94% and 55%, and the
oracle 84% and 85%, while the random control reaches 15% of each. These values are for the pooled replay

The two orderings trade places depending on the target. For the calibrated 15% gates (ent2 and mar2, with

rates 6.9% and 6.1%) the figures are 63% against 45% for flips and 33% against 24% for KL. The 55% in the
budget curves for the margin ordering differs from the 54% of the calibrated gate only because the
calibrated gate escalates 14.7% of steps and not exactly 15%. A reader who cares about the argmax token
(and so about the text) and a reader who cares about the whole distribution may therefore prefer different
gates.

6.1  Cost against KL, with verified speculation
Figure 12 places the gates on a plane of bytes per token against KL to all-high, with verified speculation
added from the same trajectories. A round proposes k tokens with low passes, verifies them with one high
pass, and accepts the longest agreeing prefix plus one token. The replay uses the argmax agreement of the
low and high passes as the acceptance test, which is exact for greedy decoding, so its KL is zero by
construction. Its bytes assume that one high pass over k+1 positions costs one pass of weight reads, as in a
memory-bound decoder.

Figure 12. Replay frontier. Each gate point is one calibrated threshold. Random and oracle points use the same rates as
the entropy gate. Verified speculation sits on the zero-KL line.
In this model of cost, verified speculation with k=4 needs 0.845 of the all-high bytes per token and accepts
4.58 tokens per round. The entropy gate at 15% needs 0.867 and leaves a mean KL of 0.0084 nats. At these
byte counts the verified scheme reaches zero KL for similar bytes, so it is the better choice whenever the
target can be run for verification and proposals are accepted at the rate seen here. The gate gives up
exactness and avoids the machinery of proposal blocks and rollback, which this model prices at zero.

step and a second pass on each escalated one. Escalating a third of all steps with this form of the gate is a

loss in the byte model whatever its accuracy, and the lagged variant mentioned in Section 3.2 would reduce
that waste but was not run.

8  Cost model
The cost axis is the weight bytes of a memory-bound decoder, and three facts of the model matter. The
output head is read in every pass and makes up 40% of a high pass and 56% of a low pass at this model size.
The gate pays for a low pass on every step before it knows whether to escalate. In verified speculation the
proposal passes also read the head.
Figure 19 breaks one generated token of four schemes into block bytes at low and high precision and head
bytes. Figure 20 changes the head precision in the model, with all passes using the same head; accuracy
under a lower-precision head was not measured, so this is a cost-model what-if.

rates).

Figure 20. Relative bytes per token when the head is stored at 16, 8 or 4 bits in every pass, with the all-high baseline using

These costs cover weight reads only. Activations, the KV cache and kernel launch costs are not modelled, and
wall-clock time was not used because the simulation has no packed kernels and the GPU was shared with
other jobs. A real implementation would pay for dequantizing the low planes and might not reach the
modelled saving.
9  Phase gating
Thinking-mode generations on Qwen3 contain a long reasoning span and a short answer. If the span
tolerates low precision and the answer does not, a phase rule would spend high precision on a few tokens
only, and it needs no uncertainty estimate, just the token that closes the span.
The run that would test this did not take place, so there is no evidence either way: the non-thinking
generations used here contain no closing tag, and the replay analysis in Section 5.3 gives only a weak proxy
through position effects. Three results would be needed before any claim: all-high, all-low and the phase rule
on thinking-mode generations; the reverse phase rule; and a matched random control with the same share
of high-precision tokens. The driver contains these modes and the cost model prices them, so the test can be
run directly.
10  Statistical power of the live sample
The live comparison has 150 paired problems. Among them, 26 were right with all-high and wrong with all-
low, and 13 went the other way, so the discordant share is 0.26. For a paired difference the approximate half-
width of the 95% interval is 1.96 times the square root of the discordant share divided by the number of
problems, which at this sample size is about 8.2 points. Figure 21 shows how it falls with n.

Figure 21. Approximate 95% half-width of a paired accuracy difference against the number of items, for several discordant
shares. The dotted line marks the sample of this paper.
Take a gate that recovers half of the observed gap of 8.7 points between all-high and all-low. Telling it apart
from all-low needs a half-width below 4.3 points, which at a discordant share of 0.26 means about 532
problems. The sample here is smaller, so confirmatory claims about accuracy need a larger run. The observed
gap is itself an estimate with the interval shown in Table 7, so this figure is a rough guide and not a planned
power calculation. The replay results rest on 36724 steps and are tighter, but they measure agreement of
single steps and give no task accuracy.
Seeds do not enter decoding, since generation is greedy; randomness appears in the problem sample, in the
bootstrap, and in the choice of 32 calibration problems. No second calibration set was tried, so the sensitivity
of the thresholds to that choice is unknown.
11  Limitations
The weights are simulated round-to-nearest quantization with no calibration data, no outlier handling and
no packed kernels. A tuned 4-bit method such as GPTQ or AWQ would have a smaller gap between low and
high, and so less room for any gate. Trained nested weights [10] would behave differently again.
Only one small model was used, Qwen3-0.6B, and the 1.7B model that was planned did not run. Larger
models, other families and mixture-of-experts models may have different entropy and flip structure. The
benchmark is also single: GSM8K in non-thinking mode with a cap of 384 tokens and greedy decoding,
where truncation affects accuracy and interacts with generation length.
The live run has 150 problems, so the intervals are wide and the tests are uncorrected across ten
configurations. There is no live random control, no phase run and no live KL: random escalation is controlled
in replay only, as an analytic expectation and not a sampled run, and the replay assumes that an escalated
step equals the high step. Flips are counted per token, and the first departure of the all-low run has a median
index of 2 tokens, so the flip counts do not say how many flips change an answer.
The cost model counts weight bytes only, with no wall-clock time, activations, KV traffic or dequantization
cost. Thresholds come from 32 training problems and one setting of group size (64) and low bits (4). The
related-work section rests on titles and abstracts of papers found by a limited search.

12  Discussion and next steps
For a reader who runs a small model locally and holds one 8-bit copy of the weights, the replay data say that
a gate on the low-pass distribution finds the steps where the two precisions disagree far more often than
chance would. At a 15% budget the entropy ordering reaches 78% of flips and the margin ordering 94%, and
the oracle reaches 84%, so there is room above the simple gates.
The live accuracy data are too few to rank the gates reliably against each other, and the intervals above show
how far each estimate can move. In the byte model, a gate that escalates 16% of steps costs 0.874 of the all-
high bytes in the live run, a saving of 13% before any implementation cost. The accuracy price of that saving
is -1.3 (-8.0 to +5.3) points against all-high, an interval that cannot exclude a loss of several points, and all-
low saves 28% for a loss that is itself borderline, so the live run does not show that the gate buys accuracy
that all-low lacks. Verified speculation with k=4 costs 0.845 in the same model and has zero KL, so a reader
who can run verification should compare the two directly on their own hardware.
The simplest next steps are the matched random control and a thinking-mode run with the phase rules, on
the same problems and the same cost model. A tuned low-bit quantizer and a lower-precision head would
change both the gap to close and the price of closing it.
