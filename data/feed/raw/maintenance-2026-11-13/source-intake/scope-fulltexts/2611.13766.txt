FAME-QP combines constraint-dependent operator representations with fused single- and two-kernel iterations replayed through CUDA graphs, automatically selecting the execution configuration through device calibration and problem-specific timing. It probes a cached operator bank to select the initial penalty and updates operators lazily when problem matrices change. Numerical benchmarks and simulated humanoid and quadrotor control experiments demonstrate reduced latency relative to competing GPU solvers while maintaining solution accuracy and control performance. I Introduction Quadratic programming (QP) problems arise in a wide range of applications, including statistics, machine learning, finance, and model predictive control (MPC) for robotic and control systems [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] . They also appear as subproblems in nonlinear optimization algorithms, such as sequential quadratic programming (SQP). Many of these applications lead to convex QP problems. Among these applications, MPC requires solving an optimization problem within each control sampling period, making computational efficiency critical for real-time operation [ 4 ] . With linear prediction models, convex quadratic costs, and linear constraints, each MPC update requires solving a convex QP problem, yielding a formulation commonly referred to as linear MPC (LMPC). Common solution approaches for QP problems include interior-point (IP), active-set (AS), operator-splitting (OS), and proximal methods.
Representative IP methods include HPIPM [ 5 ] , qpSWIFT [ 6 ] , PIQP [ 7 ] , and Clarabel [ 8 ] (with QP as a special case). Although IP methods often converge in relatively few iterations, each iteration requires solving a potentially expensive linear system. They are also generally more difficult to warm start, limiting their ability to exploit similarities between successive QP problems in MPC [ 9 ] . AS methods, such as qpOASES [ 10 ] and DAQP [ 11 ] , readily support warm starting and can be highly efficient when initialized with the correct active set. However, their combinatorial worst-case complexity in the number of constraints can limit their scalability to large-scale QPs [ 9 ] . OS methods based on the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM), such as OSQP [ 2 ] , and related proximal methods, such as ProxQP [ 12 ] , are increasingly used in robotic MPC owing to their ease of warm starting and suitability for applications requiring moderate solution accuracy [ 13 , 14 ] . Moreover, their computational structure offers opportunities for parallelization. Nevertheless, their convergence rates can be slower than those of other methods, particularly when high-accuracy solutions are required [ 7 ] . Although most established QP solvers are CPU-based, GPUs offer opportunities to accelerate large-scale QP solving through massive parallelism and higher memory bandwidth, enabling concurrent execution of independent computations and faster data movement [ 13 ] . Growing interest in MPC for robotics and control using world models [ 15 , 16 ] , locally linear latent dynamics [ 17 ] , and Koopman-based linear, bilinear, or semi-linear representations [ 18 , 19 , 20 ] further motivates GPU acceleration to meet the real-time computational demands of large-scale MPCs. Moreover, modern robotics increasingly relies on GPUs for parallel simulation, inference, and learning [ 21 , 22 , 23 ] . Integrating CPU-native QP solvers into such pipelines can introduce additional latency through frequent CPU–GPU data transfers [ 24 ] . Together, these considerations motivate GPU-native QP solvers that keep optimization data and computations on the device. Its contributions are: (i) constraint-dependent operator representations that reduce operator storage by up to 4 × 4\times and iteration time by up to 3 × 3\times , relative to treating all constraint rows as dense; (ii) fused single- and two-kernel iterations with CUDA graph replay and automatic selection using device calibration and problem-specific timing; (iii) initial penalty selection through cached-operator probing and lazy operator updates for changing QP matrices; (iv) numerical and simulated robotic MPC evaluations demonstrating lower latency than the competing GPU methods evaluated in each benchmark, with matched solution accuracy on random QPs and comparable closed-loop control performance; and (v) an open-source implementation. I-A Related Work GPU QP solvers. cuOSQP [ 13 ] parallelizes OSQP through indirect linear solves, while qpax [ 25 ] and CuPIQP [ 26 ] implement IP methods on GPUs, with CuPIQP exploiting kernel fusion and CUDA graph replay.
The closest approach is ReLU-QP [ 9 ] , which reformulates ADMM as a weight-tied neural network and caches pre-inverted operators for penalty adaptation.
Its dense operator and repeated dispatch introduce computational and execution overhead, while its offline precomputation limits online matrix updates. FAME ​ - ​ QP \mathrm{FAME}\text{-}\mathrm{QP} retains the pre-inversion and penalty-bank approach but uses smaller, constraint-dependent operators, fused graph-replayed iterations, and lazy operator updates. GPU MPC solvers. MPCGPU [ 27 ] and TurboMPC [ 28 ] accelerate nonlinear MPC through GPU implementations of SQP and structured linear algebra. π \pi MPC [ 29 ] exploits parallelism across the prediction horizon through consensus ADMM, while CUDA MPC [ 30 ] reduces execution overhead using a persistent cooperative kernel and CUDA graphs.
These methods exploit structure at the optimal-control level, whereas FAME ​ - ​ QP \mathrm{FAME}\text{-}\mathrm{QP} accepts dense convex QPs directly, including general constraint matrices and online matrix changes. Positioning. GPU computations execute as kernels : functions typically launched by the host and executed in parallel by threads grouped into blocks [ 33 ] . Threads within a block share fast on-chip memory and synchronize efficiently, while communication between blocks typically uses global memory. Successive kernels in the same stream execute in order, making kernel boundaries a standard way to synchronize all blocks between computational stages [ 32 ] . A CUDA graph represents a workflow of GPU operations, such as kernel
launches and data transfers, and the dependencies between them.
The workflow is prepared once and can then be executed repeatedly with a single host call, reducing host-side launch overhead [ 32 ] . When a kernel is launched, its thread blocks are scheduled onto
the GPU’s streaming multiprocessors (SMs), where threads execute
in groups of 32 called warps, using values held in registers.
Global memory accesses may be served by GPU caches, while
threads within a block can exchange data through on-chip shared
memory. Registers and shared memory have thread and block
lifetimes, respectively, so intermediate results passed between
kernels are typically stored in global memory [ 32 ] . The runtime of a computation expressed
as a sequence of kernels therefore depends on arithmetic, data
movement through the memory hierarchy, and launch and scheduling
overhead. Kernel fusion combines operations that would
otherwise execute in separate kernels into a single kernel,
allowing some intermediate results to remain in registers or
shared memory. This can reduce both the number of kernel

If the proposal differs from the current ρ \rho by more than a

optimal value is independent of ρ \rho .
The penalty grid remains fixed during each solve. The same bank is also used to select the starting penalty.

the requested tolerances. Trials are compared at the tightest

Sec. III-E . Ties favor the first tested entry.

V Conclusion FAME-QP accelerates dense MPC quadratic programs through constraint-dependent operators, fused GPU execution with automatic configuration selection, penalty probing, and lazy matrix updates. Numerical and simulated robotic experiments demonstrate reduced latency while maintaining solution accuracy and control performance, including with changing prediction matrices.
