A Closer Look at the Platonic Representation Hypothesis Julia Herbst Affiliation: Helmholtz Munich Affiliation: Technical University of Munich Email: julia.herbst@tum.de Simon Roschmann Affiliation: Helmholtz Munich Affiliation: Technical University of Munich Affiliation: Munich Center for Machine Learning Email: simon.roschmann@tum.de Quentin Bouniot Affiliation: Helmholtz Munich Affiliation: Technical University of Munich Affiliation: Munich Center for Machine Learning Affiliation: LTCI, Télécom Paris, Institut Polytechnique de Paris, Palaiseau, France Email: zeynep.akata@tum.de Zeynep Akata Affiliation: Helmholtz Munich Affiliation: Technical University of Munich Affiliation: Munich Center for Machine Learning Email: quentin.bouniot@telecom-paris.fr Abstract The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH) suggests that neural networks are converging toward similar representation spaces. As language models become more capable, their representations are aligning with those of vision models. However, recent work has questioned whether this trend continues to hold for newer language models. Moreover, it has been shown that representation similarity metrics can be confounded by model depth and width, requiring appropriate baselines for interpretation. In this work, we first investigate where alignment emerges between unimodal models across model layers and representation dimensions. Next, we find that the PRH capability-alignment trend persists for newer models as long as the captions are sufficiently clean and informative. Finally, we contextualize cross-modal alignment, introducing unimodal and multimodal baselines on various datasets. Our results show that the cross-modal representational alignment of unimodal models can surpass that of explicitly aligned vision-language models, but remains substantially behind the alignment observed for models of the same modality. Code is available at github.com/ExplainableML/prh-closer-look . 1 Introduction Images and textual descriptions provide two views of the same underlying world. The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH) ( Huh et al., 2024 ) posits that sufficiently capable vision and language models may therefore learn similar abstractions despite being trained on different modalities. This insight has motivated a series of methods ( Huang et al., 2026 ; Maniparambil et al., 2025 ; Roschmann et al., 2026 ; Vouitsis et al., 2024 ; Yacobi et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2025a ) that align pretrained unimodal models with lightweight alignment layers and limited paired data instead of training for cross-modal alignment from scratch ( Radford et al., 2021 ; Tschannen et al., 2025 ; Zhai et al., 2023 ) . Recent work has refined the PRH. Koepke et al. (2026) show that some findings strongly depend on the evaluation protocol and that performance-alignment trends observed for earlier language models no longer extend to newer model families. Gröger et al. (2026) argue that similarity scores are difficult to interpret without suitable baselines, and thus propose calibrating them against a null hypothesis representing the absence of alignment. Moreover, these investigations into the PRH commonly report the maximum similarity across all layer pairs using the full representation of models, obscuring at which layers and dimensions alignment emerges. We take a closer look at the PRH along three axes. First, we analyze where alignment emerges across model layers and dimensions under compression via principal component analysis (PCA). Second, we examine how the relationship between language model capability and cross-modal alignment is affected by caption characteristics. Third, we contextualize the cross-modal alignment of unimodal vision and language models beyond a null baseline. To our knowledge, no prior work has provided a comparison with randomly initialized models, explicitly aligned vision-language models (VLMs), and model pairs from the same modality. 2 Experimental setup Models. We evaluate a broad set of unimodal models. On the vision side, we consider DINOv2 ViT-L ( Oquab et al., 2024 ) , DINOv3 ViT-L ( Siméoni et al., 2026 ) and MAE ViT-L ( He et al., 2022 ) .

We compare their alignment against explicitly aligned VLMs such as CLIP ViT-L/14 ( Radford et al., 2021 ; Ilharco et al., 2021 ; Schuhmann et al., 2022 ) , SigLIP 2 So400M ( Tschannen et al., 2025 ) and Qwen3-VL-Embedding-8B ( Li et al., 2026 ) . For the explicitly aligned VLMs, we extract image and text representations from the joint embedding space after the final projection layer. The text encoders of CLIP and SigLIP 2 support a maximum context length of 77 and 64 tokens, respectively. For captions exceeding these limits, we split the text into chunks that respect the context window, embed each chunk independently, and finally average these chunk representations. Datasets. We evaluate alignment on image-text datasets with different caption properties: WIT ( Srinivasan et al., 2021 ) with short and noisy captions from Wikipedia, CC3M ( Sharma et al., 2018 ) with short and synthetically created DreamLIP captions ( Zheng et al., 2024 ) , COCO ( Lin et al., 2014 ) with concise human-written captions, and DOCCI ( Onoe et al., 2024 ) with long and fine-grained human-written captions. To investigate the effect of caption verbosity for the same set of images, we additionally construct shortened versions of the DOCCI captions. Specifically, we prompt Qwen3-8B ( Qwen Team et al., 2025 ) to summarize each caption to approximately 10%, 25%, 50% or 75% of its original length while preserving the most salient semantic content. The prompt is provided in Appendix H along with a DOCCI sample and its newly generated captions. Metric. We measure alignment using mutual k k -nearest-neighbor overlap ( Huh et al., 2024 ) and its calibrated variant proposed by Gröger et al. (2026) . For each dataset, we sample N = 1024 N=1024 image-caption pairs and compare the indices of the k = 10 k=10 nearest images in vision space with those of the nearest captions in language space. Koepke et al. (2026) find that alignment decreases for fixed k k with increasing gallery size, but also show that it remains stable when the relative neighborhood size k / N k/N is preserved. An ablation study on k k and N N is provided in Appendix G . We use the CLS token for vision models and mean-pooled representations for language models and apply L 2 L_{2} normalization. Implementation. All experiments can be performed on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU with 80 GB memory. We use the LM Evaluation Harness framework ( Gao et al., 2024 ) to obtain the MMLU and ARC Challenge accuracies as well as the GSM8K exact match scores for the generative language models in Section 3.2 . Prior work ( Huh et al., 2024 ; Koepke et al., 2026 ; Gröger et al., 2026 ) reports only the maximum alignment across layer pairs. Figure 1 instead shows the complete layer-wise alignment between two models on WIT. Cross-modal alignment emerges mainly in later layers, once more abstract semantic representations have formed, and peaks at a mutual k k -NN score of 0.143 0.143 between DINOv3 layer 21 and Qwen3-8B layer 30. Within-modality alignment is much stronger. Vision models form three broad block-diagonal regions above 0.3 0.3 , peaking at 0.538 0.538 among later layers. A generative language model (Qwen3-8B) and a language embedding model (NV-Embed-v2) reach a mutual k k -NN score around 0.5 0.5 across many early-to-middle layer pairs. We next investigate whether alignment is localized not only to specific layers, but also within a small subspace of the representations. Specifically, we compress the vision and language representations via PCA before computing mutual k k -NN on WIT. PCA is fit independently for each layer of each model, with separate transformations for the language and vision representations. Figure 2 shows that retaining 40% of the variance preserves or slightly improves alignment for most models, suggesting that lower-variance directions mainly capture modality-specific or noisy information. However, very strong compression causes alignment to collapse, indicating that the shared structure remains distributed across multiple principal components. In Figure 2(b) , we plot alignment against the number of dimensions retained after PCA. This allows us to investigate the effective dimensionality of the subspaces in which the observed alignment resides. In particular, the results suggest that the cross-modal alignment is largely captured within a relatively small subspace of approximately 100 dimensions, compared to the original language spaces with 1,536–5,120 dimensions. Additional results on DOCCI and COCO are provided in Appendix C . Together, these results indicate that cross-modal alignment is localized both across depth and within the representation space: only particular layers exhibit strong correspondence, and within those layers, the correspondence can be captured by relatively low-dimensional subspaces. Figure 4 : Performance of LLMs on ARC and GSM8K vs. alignment with vision models on DOCCI. 3.2 Does higher alignment indicate stronger model performance? The PRH originally suggested that stronger language models exhibit greater representational alignment with vision models on WIT ( Huh et al., 2024 ) . However, this trend does not extend to newer model families on the same dataset ( Koepke et al., 2026 ) . Following Koepke et al. (2026) , we examine the relationship between five-shot MMLU accuracy ( Hendrycks et al., 2021 ) and alignment with DINOv3 across datasets with different caption characteristics. For each dataset, we independently sample 1024 image-caption pairs across five runs and report the mean and standard deviation of the mutual k k -NN score with k = 10 k=10 . Since the original WIT subset provided by Huh et al. (2024) contains only 1024 samples, its evaluation corresponds to a single run. Figure 3 compares the performance-alignment relationship on WIT, CC3M, and DOCCI using both original and summarized captions. We observe a weak to moderate positive correlation for maximally aligned layers on CC3M (Pearson r = 0.26 r=0.26 , p = 0.25 p=0.25 ) and WIT ( r = 0.46 r=0.46 , p = 0.03 p=0.03 ), consistent with the findings of Koepke et al. (2026) . In contrast, DOCCI exhibits a stronger positive correlation when considering the maximally aligned layers ( r = 0.66 r=0.66 , p = 0.001 p=0.001 ), which remains significant with calibrated mutual k k -NN ( r = 0.66 r=0.66 , p = 0.001 p=0.001 ). Importantly, this trend persists when DOCCI captions are compressed to 10% of their original length, with r = 0.59 r=0.59 ( p = 0.005 p=0.005 ) for the maximally aligned layers. Caption verbosity alone therefore does not explain the performance-alignment trend. Instead, the substantially weaker trend on the short and noisy WIT and CC3M captions, together with its persistence on original and summarized DOCCI captions, suggests that sufficiently clean and visually informative data is important for revealing shared structure between vision and language representations. To assess whether these MMLU findings generalize to other measures of language-model capability, Figure 4 relates alignment with DINOv3 on DOCCI to four-shot ARC Challenge accuracy ( Clark et al., 2018 ) and five-shot GSM8K exact-match accuracy ( Cobbe et al., 2021 ) . ARC performance correlates strongly with alignment at the maximally aligned layers (Pearson r = 0.73 r=0.73 , p = 0.0002 p=0.0002 ), and the correlation remains significant after calibration (Pearson r = 0.73 r=0.73 , p = 0.0002 p=0.0002 ). This is in line with the MMLU results and shows that the performance-alignment trend is not specific to a single benchmark. Solving MMLU and ARC requires factual and semantic knowledge about the world that may be represented similarly by both vision and language models. In contrast, GSM8K primarily evaluates mathematical reasoning, which is less directly grounded in the semantic knowledge shared with visual representations, and its performance does not correlate significantly with alignment. Therefore, cross-modal alignment is not a universal proxy for language-model capability, but may be more closely associated with capabilities that rely on semantic knowledge. Results for ARC and GSM8K with alignment measured on the remaining caption datasets are provided in Appendix D . 3.3 How to interpret the magnitude of cross-modal alignment? Alignment scores are difficult to interpret in isolation. A score such as 0.3 does not indicate by itself whether two representation spaces are weakly or strongly aligned. Therefore, we explicitly compare four regimes: randomly initialized models, pairs of unimodal vision and language models as the main object of study, explicitly aligned VLMs as cross-modal references, and pairs of unimodal models within one modality. As displayed in Figure 5 , the cross-modal alignment of unimodal models on COCO and DOCCI reaches a mutual k k -NN score above 0.3, surpassing VLMs such as CLIP and SigLIP 2, but remaining below Qwen3-VL-Embedding-8B. For VLMs, we use the final embeddings in the shared image-text space, using the maximum across layer pairs instead yields nearly identical scores (Appendix E ). Notably, Qwen3-VL-Embedding-8B reports state-of-the-art performance on multimodal retrieval benchmarks and achieves the highest mutual k k -NN cross-modal alignment. This shows that strong downstream retrieval performance is compatible with high cross-modal neighborhood agreement. The cross-modal alignment of unimodal models remains far below a mutual k k -NN score of 0.5 observed for models of the same modality. This gap indicates that unimodal vision and language models learn partially shared representations that support cross-modal neighborhood agreement, but remain substantially less similar than models operating on the same modality. The DOCCI caption variants provide a controlled view of how caption detail affects this comparison while keeping the underlying images fixed. Shortening the captions reduces absolute cross-modal alignment, but leaves the relative ordering of the model pairs largely unchanged. In particular, DINOv2/v3 paired with Qwen3-Embedding-8B or NV-Embed-v2 continue to surpass CLIP and SigLIP 2. Caption detail therefore affects the magnitude of alignment without changing our main contextual conclusions. Results across all DOCCI caption lengths are provided in Appendix F . The high similarity between independently pretrained unimodal models is consistent with prior work: Zhang et al. (2025a) showed that their representations can be mapped into a shared embedding space using lightweight alignment layers and limited paired data, outperforming CLIP. Figure 5 : Alignment for different models and evaluation protocols: DINOv2/v3 (D2/D3), Qwen3-Emb-8B (Q3), NV-Embed-v2 (NV), CLIP, SigLIP 2, Qwen3-VL-Emb-8B (Q3-VL) (mean and standard deviation across 3 runs). 4 Conclusion We revisited the PRH through a fine-grained analysis of alignment between unimodal vision and language models. Cross-modal alignment emerges in later model layers and is preserved under substantial dimensionality reduction. Moreover, we find that performance-alignment trends of the PRH persist for newer language models on image-text datasets with sufficiently informative captions.
Finally, our baseline analysis shows that unimodal vision and language models can reach the representational alignment of explicitly aligned VLMs, but remain far below models of the same modality. Limitations. The conclusions of our empirical work are limited to the evaluated models, datasets, and alignment metrics. We focus on cross-modal alignment between unimodal encoders from the vision and language domain; whether our findings generalize to other modalities remains open. Moreover, mutual k k -NN captures only one notion of representational alignment, and its absolute scores depend on the metric hyperparameters, representation extraction choices, and evaluation data. We mitigate these dependencies by demonstrating that our findings persist with calibrated mutual k k -NN and by interpreting the raw scores relative to a wide set of baselines. Appendix A Broader impacts This work complements prior research on the Platonic Representation Hypothesis by identifying where alignment emerges across layers and dimensions, showing that its correlation with model performance re-emerges for sufficiently clean and informative captions, and contextualizing its magnitude against a broad set of baselines. While our study is diagnostic and does not introduce any new model, our findings may help practitioners select encoders and representations for efficient cross-modal alignment, potentially reducing paired supervision and computation. The evaluated models and image-text datasets may encode societal and cultural biases that aggregate alignment scores cannot reveal. Therefore, downstream applications will require task-specific evaluation.
