Rennes 2, IRISA Schneider Electric † Abstract Which representation should be probed in a transformer-based foundation model? Recent work shows that the standard practice of probing the final layer is often suboptimal, as intermediate layers can provide better features. Yet, representations are still largely studied along depth alone, treating each transformer block as an atomic unit. In this work, we analyze transformer representations layer by layer, module by module . Through more than 144 000 144\,000 linear probing evaluations of vision foundation models (VFMs) and large language models (LLMs), we show that layer and module choices should be considered jointly: the best layer-module pair yields substantially larger gains over the final-layer residual stream than the best layer with the residual stream fixed. Moreover, module performance can vary substantially across layers, showing that useful representations cannot be characterized by depth alone. Building on these findings, we evaluate simple fixed probing strategies: the final-layer feedforward activation ( Act@100 ) for vision and the second normalization layer at 60 % 60\% depth ( LN2@60 ) for language. These strategies improve over the probing baseline in a majority of evaluations and recover, on average, 42 % 42\% and 86 % 86\% of the gains from probing the best layer-module pair for vision and language, respectively. These models are typically pretrained on massive amounts of diverse data ( kimiteam2025kimivltechnicalreport ; shukor2025scaling ) to encode general knowledge in their weights. A common use of foundation models is as frozen feature extractors, which provide rich representations for downstream tasks without retraining or finetuning the model ( oquab2024dinov2 ; lee2025geminiembedding ; roschmann2026tivit ) . Probing intermediate representations. A standard way to evaluate representations is through linear probing: the model is frozen, and a linear classifier is trained on top of the representation. alain2017probe introduced probing as a diagnostic tool for neural networks and observed that classification accuracy increases with depth. Their experiments, however, focused on supervised, task-specific convolutional neural networks ( lecun1998cnn , CNN,) . While the final layer may be optimal for a model trained for a single task, it need not be for a model trained to generalize across tasks. Recent work shows that intermediate layers of LLMs can outperform the final layer across diverse downstream tasks ( skean2025layer ) . For VFMs, however, the picture is less clear. skean2025layer report that intermediate layers are beneficial in autoregressive VFMs ( fini2025aimv2 ; elnouby2024aim ) , while final representations remain optimal in non-autoregressive models such as ViT ( dosovitskiy2021vit ) and DINOv2 ( oquab2024dinov2 ) . In contrast, bolya2025perception find that intermediate layers provide the best embeddings in contrastive VFMs, and uselis2025intermediate observe a similar trend in ViT, particularly under distribution shift. This motivates further investigation. Layer by layer, module by module. The layer-wise analyses discussed above share a common limitation: they all treat the transformer block as an atomic unit. This perspective overlooks the intermediate representations produced within each block, illustrated in Figure 2 , namely the outputs of normalization layers, attention modules, feedforward linear layers, activation functions, and residual connections. 1 1 1 In what follows, we use the terms block and layer interchangeably, as well as module and component, referring to normalization layers, multi-head attention modules, feedforward layers, activation functions, and residual connections throughout the network. These components can exhibit distinct properties, notably in terms of smoothness and adaptability during finetuning ( odonnat2026vision ) . We therefore conduct a large-scale, systematic study of transformer representations in VFMs and LLMs, layer by layer, module by module. We ask two natural questions: 1) How much can optimal representations improve over standard probing? and 2) Can a simple fixed heuristic recover most of the gain at no extra cost? Our contributions. We conduct extensive experiments on VFMs and LLMs. Figure 1 illustrates our findings, showcasing the suboptimality of probing the final-layer residual stream and the joint importance of both layer and module choices. Our main contributions can be summarized as: 1. Layer and module choices matter jointly. Probing the best layer-module pair yields substantially larger gains over the final-layer residual stream than probing the best layer with the residual stream fixed. Moreover, module performance can vary substantially across depth, showing that layer and module choices should not be considered independently. 2. Practical benefits. We evaluate simple fixed probing strategies: final-layer feedforward activation ( Act@100 ) for VFMs and the second normalization layer at 60 % 60\% depth ( LN2@60 ) for LLMs. Both strategies improve performance in a majority of evaluations and recover a substantial fraction of the gains from probing the best layer-module pair. 3. Impact of transfer difficulty. When the discrepancy between pretraining and downstream data is large, transfer becomes more difficult. For VFMs, we find that the benefits of optimal probing locations increase in such settings, with Act@100 yielding larger gains. Moreover, the impact of depth grows under distribution shift, allowing further improvement through the best layer-wise Act representations. 2 Probing transformers layer by layer, module by module The transformer architecture ( vaswani2017attention ) consists of successive transformer blocks, each taking as input a sequence of tokens ( x 1 , … , x n ) (x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}) . As shown in Figure 2 (left), each block is made of alternating multi-head attention modules ( MHA ), with self-attention for encoders and causal attention for decoders, and feedforward networks (FFN). A standard FFN consists of two linear transformations, LN1 MHA + \bm{\mathbf{+}} RC1 LN2 FFN + \bm{\mathbf{+}} RC2 FC1 𝝈 \bm{\sigma} Act FC2 LN1 MHA + \bm{\mathbf{+}} RC1 LN2 FFN + \bm{\mathbf{+}} RC2 FC1 FC3 𝝈 \bm{\sigma} ⊙ \bm{\odot} Act FC2 Figure 2: Transformer representations. We probe transformer representations (colored) across layers in VFMs and LLMs, covering models with standard ( left ) and gated FFN ( right ). denoted FC1 and FC2 , separated by a non-linear activation function Act , such as GELU ( hendrycks2016gelu ) . Assuming the pre-norm standard ( xiong2020prenorm ) , two normalization layers (LayerNorms ( ba2016layernormalization ) or RMSNorm ( zhang2019rms ) ), respectively LN1 and LN2 , precede the attention and feedforward blocks, and two residual connections, respectively RC1 and RC2 , follow them. We will refer to RC2 as the residual stream, i.e., the output of the transformer block. In this work, we track the outputs of these eight sequence-to-sequence operations at each layer, indexing them by a layer-module pair ( ℓ , m ) (\ell,m) . Some large-scale transformers replace the standard two-layer FFN with a gated FFN. It combines the non-linear projection with an additional linear projection through element-wise multiplication. To ensure consistency between standard and gated FFNs, we recover the FFN representations as follows: FC1 is the linear layer fed as input to the non-linear operation σ \sigma , Act is the non-linear transformation with a gated mechanism (thus absorbing the additional linear projection), fed as input to the second linear function, and FC2 is this second linear projection. This is illustrated in Figure 2 , with exact tensor definitions deferred to Section B.1 . Evaluating transformer representations. Following oquab2024dinov2 and muennighoff2023mteb , we evaluate transformer representations via linear probing for classification ( alain2017probe ) , which provides a common evaluation for visual ( chen2020simclr ) and textual ( lee2025geminiembedding ) embeddings. Each probing location ( ℓ , m ) (\ell,m) produces a sequence of tokens ( x 1 , … , x n ) ∈ ( ℝ d ) n (x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n} , where ℓ \ell denotes the relative depth expressed as a percentage 2 2 2 We use zero-based layer indexing, such that for a model with L L layers, probing at relative depth ℓ \ell corresponds to layer int ⁡ ( ℓ 100 ⋅ ( L − 1 ) ) \operatorname{int}(\frac{\ell}{100}\cdot(L-1)) . , and m m is the transformer module being probed. By abuse of notation, d d denotes either the embedding dimension or the FFN hidden dimension, which is generally larger. As in joseph2026interpreting , we embed this sequence in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} by global average pooling over content tokens, namely image patches or text tokens; that is, special tokens, including CLS , register tokens ( darcet2024vision ) , EOS , and BOS , as well as padding positions ( PAD ), are masked before pooling and do not contribute to the representation. This allows us to focus on representations produced within transformer blocks, with a consistent feature extraction procedure across models (e.g., AIMv2 models ( fini2025aimv2 ) do not use a CLS token). Implementation details to ensure a scalable evaluation across layers and modules are provided in Appendix B . Measuring improvement with oracle gains. The performance of the representation at location ( ℓ , m ) (\ell,m) is the probing test accuracy on a given dataset or benchmark, denoted by acc ⁡ ( ℓ , m ) \mathrm{acc}(\ell,m) . We use the final-layer residual stream, i.e., RC2 at 100 % 100\% relative depth, as the probing baseline, denoted by RC2@100 . We define the gain at location ( ℓ , m ) (\ell,m) as the accuracy difference in percentage points (pp) relative to the baseline, i.e., g ⁡ ( ℓ , m ) = acc ⁡ ( ℓ , m ) − acc ⁡ ( 100 , RC2 ) g(\ell,m)=\mathrm{acc}(\ell,m)-\mathrm{acc}(100,\mathrm{RC2}) . (1) The term oracle emphasizes that these quantities are a posteriori upper bounds among the evaluated probing locations, since they require exhaustive evaluation and access to test performance. To further investigate when these representations are beneficial, we conduct a controlled study in Section C.1.1 , where we probe a pretrained model across layers before and after finetuning it on downstream data. Before finetuning, accuracy curves are concave when pretraining and downstream data differ substantially, with intermediate representations achieving the best performance. After finetuning, the curves straighten, with performance increasing monotonically towards the final layers. Finally, Figure 6(d) shows that oracle gains decrease with pretraining scale, computed as the number of tokens seen during pretraining (see Section C.1.2 for details). The relative error reductions follow the same order, with 15.7 % 15.7\% for ViT, 12.1 % 12.1\% for DINOv2, 10.8 % 10.8\% for AIMv2, and 7.6 % 7.6\% for DINOv3. Intuitively, more extensive pretraining increases the chance of seeing samples similar to the downstream images, potentially reducing the discrepancy between pretraining and downstream data distributions and leaving a smaller oracle gain to close. This is consistent with Figure 6(a) . Model size, in contrast, has no significant effect on gains; see Section C.1.6 for details. We note that these findings are observational, not causal, since many confounders exist in our analysis (architectural differences, training objectives, quality and diversity of pretraining data). Takeaway 1. For VFMs, the best probing locations across layers and modules yield substantial performance gains. The simple fixed strategy Act@100 improves performance in a majority of evaluations, recovering nearly half of these gains at no extra cost. Linear probing ( alain2017probe ; belinkov2022probing ) has become a standard evaluation protocol to assess representations in foundation models across diverse modalities, including images ( chen2020simclr ; oquab2024dinov2 ) , text ( muennighoff2023mteb ; enevoldsen2025mmteb ; lee2025geminiembedding ) , video ( assran2025vjepa2 ) , graphs ( velickovic2018deep ) , and time series ( roschmann2026tivit ; goswami2024moment ; feofanov2026mantis ) . Beyond linear heads, attentive probing is increasingly adopted to evaluate models with patch-level features ( chen2024cae ; elnouby2024aim ; fini2025aimv2 ) . While linear probes are still used, the embedding of sequences of tokens from ( ℝ d ) n (\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n} to ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} is learned via an attention mechanism at the level of the sequence. The overall operation thus becomes non-linear and leads to noticeable performance improvements ( elnouby2024aim ) . In these works, features are typically extracted from the final layer after a forward pass. We challenge this convention, showing the benefits of probing intermediate representations, including within transformer blocks. Beyond probing at a single location, some approaches aggregate internal representations throughout the model, combining them with learned layer weights ( peters2018elmo ) , supervised feature selection ( evci2022head2toe ) , attention over all layers ( ciernik2026attentive ) , or attention value vectors pooled across layers ( zhang2026llmbased ) . Our work is orthogonal to these methods: it could help identify which representations across layers and modules are worth aggregating, and, more broadly, improve our understanding of transformer representations. Understanding transformer representations. Intermediate representations can also be studied to understand what models encode rather than to improve downstream performance. Probing intermediate text representations reveals what linguistic knowledge is captured within the model ( liu2019linguistic ) and where: tenney2019bert show that BERT ( devlin2019bert ) represents the steps of the traditional NLP pipeline in an interpretable and localizable way. Similarly, joseph2026interpreting use linear probes to understand where physical information becomes accessible in encoder-based video transformers, and how it is organized. Linear probes are also central to mechanistic interpretability , which aims to reverse-engineer the computations of neural networks, with a strong focus on LLMs. This line of work views the transformer as a set of attention and feedforward modules that read from and write to the shared residual stream ( elhage2021mathematical ) . Probes and interventions have thus been used to analyze attention heads ( li2023inferencetime ) , feedforward modules ( geva2021ffn ) , and the residual stream ( gurnee2024language ; marks2024geometry ) , where the latter can be steered to control the model behavior ( turner2024steering ; rimsky2024steering ) . Note that, akin to feature evaluation, the residual stream is the typical representation studied in the mechanistic interpretability literature, often referred to as residual stream activation . Beyond interpretability, odonnat2026vision analyze the smoothness of individual transformer components to determine which ones to prioritize during finetuning. Our work follows this direction of looking inside transformer blocks. Yet, the goal differs: we aim to identify which internal representations are most useful as frozen features for downstream tasks, not to reverse-engineer computations, elicit a specific behavior in the model, or finetune it. We discuss additional related work in Appendix A . 5 Discussion This paper investigates how useful different transformer representations are for linear probing in vision foundation models and large language models. Our extensive layer by layer, module by module analysis shows that layer and module choices should be considered jointly: the best layer-module pair yields substantially larger performance gains than selecting the best layer with the residual stream fixed, while module performance can vary substantially across depth. Building on these findings, we identify simple fixed probing strategies, Act@100 and LN2@60 for VFMs and LLMs, respectively, that improve performance in a majority of evaluations and recover a large fraction of the gains from the best layer-module pair. For vision models, the benefit of Act@100 increases with transfer difficulty between pretraining and downstream data. These findings highlight the importance of looking beyond depth alone when probing transformer models. We hope our work can contribute to a better understanding of their internal representations. Limitations and future work. Our analysis focuses on linear probing for classification and could naturally be extended to other downstream tasks and modalities, including tabular and time series foundation models. Moreover, our insights into the usefulness of individual transformer representations could help guide the design of aggregation mechanisms that combine intermediate representations rather than treating them uniformly. Better transfer of intermediate layers. Several works propose explanations as to why intermediate representations can generalize better than the final layer, especially under distribution shift. The intrinsic dimension of representations first increases then decreases with depth ( ansuini2019intrinsic ; recanatesi2019dimensionality ) , and final layers exhibit neural collapse, compressing features onto class means ( papyan2020neuralcollapse ) . This phenomenon also appears, more weakly, in intermediate layers ( rangamani2023neuralcollapse ) . In addition, the tunnel effect describes later layers compressing representations that are already linearly separable through intermediate layers, thus degrading out-of-distribution performance ( masarczyk2023tunnel ) . Although these works mostly study task-specific deep neural networks rather than large-scale pretrained foundation models, their observations are consistent with our finding that the optimal probing depth moves to earlier layers as the shift between pretraining and downstream data grows. Intermediate representations for adaptation and embeddings. Intermediate representations can also be used to adapt pretrained models efficiently. Visual Query Tuning ( tu2023vqt ) learns queries that summarize intermediate features of vision transformers, while wu2024structured implement an efficient adaptation model that selects features using the structure of the model and adapts to task difficulty. In language, LLMEmbed ( liu2024llmembed ) combines representations from several layers of lightweight LLMs for text classification. When LLMs are used as frozen embedding models, training-free methods can improve the quality of extracted features by modifying either the input, with prompts ( jiang2024scaling ) , or repetitions ( springer2025repetition ) , or the forward pass, by re-routing internal key-value states ( tang2026kv ) . The adaptation methods aggregate residual stream representations across depth, while the training-free methods read the final-layer residual stream. Both could benefit from our insights by investigating other transformer modules and, for the latter, other depths as well. ViT. The Vision Transformer architecture ( dosovitskiy2021vit , ViT,) consists of a succession of transformer encoders with LayerNorms ( ba2016layernormalization ) and a standard feedforward network with GELU activation ( hendrycks2016gelu ) . Inputs are 2 2 D images that are split into square patches of size P P , which are flattened and linearly embedded in dimension d d . A classification token CLS is prepended to the sequence of tokens before adding positional embeddings. The checkpoints used in our experiments follow the HuggingFace transformers ( wolf2020hftransformers ) implementation available at transformers/models/vit/modeling_vit.py . DINOv2. The DINOv2 backbone ( oquab2024dinov2 ) follows the ViT architecture described above, adding a LayerScale ( touvron2021layerscale ) to stabilize training. All variants use a standard FFN, except the 1.1 1.1 B one, which uses a gated FFN with a SwiGLU activation ( shazeer2020glu ) . The checkpoints used in our experiments follow the HuggingFace transformers’ implementation available at transformers/models/dinov2/modeling_dinov2.py . DINOv3. The DINOv3 backbone ( simeoni2025dinov3 ) follows the DINOv2 one and implements Rotary Positional Embeddings ( su2024rope , RoPE,) . All variants use a standard FFN, except the 7 7 B one, which uses a gated FFN with a SwiGLU activation ( shazeer2020glu ) . The checkpoints used in our experiments follow the HuggingFace transformers’ implementation available at transformers/models/dinov3_vit/modeling_dinov3_vit.py . AIMv2. The AIMv2 model ( fini2025aimv2 ) pairs a ViT backbone with a multimodal decoder to autoregressively generate raw image patches and text tokens. In our work, we only focus on the vision part, which consists of a succession of transformer encoders with RMS norms ( zhang2019rms ) and a gated feedforward network with SwiGLU
activation ( shazeer2020glu ) . Inputs are 2 2 D images that are split into square patches of size P P , which are flattened and linearly embedded in dimension d d . Unlike the original ViT, no CLS token is prepended to the sequence: attention is instead constrained with a prefix causal mask during pretraining, enabling bidirectional attention over image patches at inference without further tuning. The checkpoints used in our experiments follow the HuggingFace transformers’ implementation available at transformers/models/aimv2/modeling_aimv2.py . Feature extraction. Applying linear probing with a logistic regression requires feeding the regressor a matrix of embeddings over the entire train split (resp. test split). However, even over a single batch of 512 512 samples, a forward pass with a “small” model like the ViT-B with 86 86 M parameters amounts to saving 49 49 K tensors in ℝ 768 \mathbb{R}^{768} , taking around 144 144 MiB in memory. Such an extraction over an entire dataset is thus intractable, even on high-performance GPUs like H200. To tackle this issue, we perform the probing in two steps: 1) feature extraction with embeddings saved on disk, and 2) logistic regression over frozen features. For the first step, we save each batch of pooled representations on the CPU and stream it directly to disk to maintain low GPU memory consumption and idle time, independent of the number of layers. This step is done for both the training and test samples. Then, for each (layer, module) pair, we rebuild the dataset-level embeddings over the train split (resp. test split) to fit (resp. evaluate) the logistic regression. Our implementation allows us to use logistic regression even for large-scale VFMs with up to 7 7 B parameters. C.1 Vision foundation models The results presented in this section relate to the experiments conducted on VFMs in Section 3.1 . C.1.1 Intermediate layers under distribution shift skean2025layer observed the benefits of intermediate layers in LLMs but not in VFMs, where performance increased towards the final layers, except for autoregressive models such as AIM ( elnouby2024aim ) . Recent work, however, suggests that intermediate representations can also be beneficial in non-autoregressive VFMs ( bolya2025perception ) , particularly under distribution shifts ( uselis2025intermediate ) . In practice, discrepancies between pretraining and downstream data can degrade performance by making the extracted features less informative ( quionero2009shift ) . To illustrate this effect, we consider a ViT-B ( dosovitskiy2021vit ) pretrained on ImageNet-21K and probe it before and after finetuning on downstream data. The experimental setup is described below. Datasets. We conduct experiments on Cifar10 ( krizhevsky2009cifar ) ; 2 2 variants from Cifar10-C ( hendrycks2018imagenetc ) with severity 5 5 (Contrast and Speckle Noise), and Flowers102 ( nilsback2008flowers102 ) . The preprocessing follows dosovitskiy2021vit and kolesnikov2020bit : for training data, we apply random cropping, a 224 × \times 224 image resizing, and random horizontal flip. For validation and test data, the 224 × \times 224 image resizing is applied before center cropping images. All images are normalized using the ImageNet ( deng2009imagenet ) statistics. It ensures images with mean [ 0.485 , 0.456 , 0.406 ] [0.485,0.456,0.406] and standard deviation [ 0.229 , 0.224 , 0.225 ] [0.229,0.224,0.225] . For Cifar10-C datasets that do not have predefined training and test sets, we manually create deterministic training and test sets following an 80 % 80\% - 20 % 20\% split. The deterministic part is crucial to ensure no data contamination. Finetuning setup. Our finetuning experiments follow the protocol from dosovitskiy2021vit with a resolution of 224 × 224 224\times 224 . We optimize models with Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), a momentum of 0.9, no weight decay, a cosine learning rate decay, a batch size of 512 512 , and gradient clipping at norm 1 1 . For each pair of dataset-configuration, we perform a sweep over 4 4 learning rates, as summarized in Table 12 , and conduct 3 3 runs with different seeds relative to network initialization and dataloaders. For each run, we monitor the training using a validation set ( 20 % 20\% of the training set). The final performance is the test accuracy of the checkpoint that achieves the best validation accuracy. Linear probing measures the linear separability of representations on downstream data. Transformer architectures preserve the representation dimensionality across layers, as the output of each block is passed as input to the subsequent block. However, different modules within a transformer block can operate at different dimensionalities. In particular, residual-stream representations have the embedding dimension d d , whereas FFN intermediate representations have the hidden dimension h h , typically larger than d d (e.g., h = 4 ​ d h=4d in standard FFNs). Prior work has shown that gains from feature augmentation cannot be attributed solely to increased feature dimensionality (see evci2022head2toe , Section 3.2) . Their setting differs from ours, however, as they artificially increase the feature dimension by aggregating embeddings across the network, while we preserve the native dimensionality of each representation. Therefore, Act has a larger dimensionality than the residual stream. To assess whether its larger dimensionality alone explains the gains of Act , we compare it with FC1 \mathrm{FC1} , which has the same FFN hidden dimension h > d h>d . This controlled comparison is on the 307 307 M-parameter variant common to all four model families. As shown in Table 18 , overall, Act@100 outperforms FC1@100 , with better performance in a majority of evaluations. FC1@100 is also subpar compared to the probing baseline RC2@100 , indicating that the gains of Act@100 cannot be explained by its larger dimensionality alone.
