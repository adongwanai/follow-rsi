Morariu † † thanks: Work done during internship at Adobe Arizona State University   Adobe dhanda@asu.edu, {tensmeye,jkil,morariu}@adobe.com Abstract Building agentic pipelines that achieve high task performance while keeping cost and latency low requires jointly optimizing heterogeneous and coupled design choices, including prompts, topology, model selection, and tool policies. Manually searching this combinatorial design space is expensive and time-consuming, while existing automated optimizers typically operate within a predefined subset of this space, modify the pipeline monolithically, or provide little structured evidence for pipeline changes. To mitigate these challenges, we introduce the Decomposed Credit-Assignment Framework ( DeCAF ), which adapts the design space to the given benchmark and pipeline, optimizes each dimension separately, and generates reasoning traces for every decision. Given a task specification, benchmark examples, and a seed pipeline, DeCAF identifies relevant optimization dimensions and generates diverse experimental hypotheses for each. Independent coding-agent sessions then optimize each dimension under a restricted edit scope, jointly evaluating performance, inference cost, and latency while recording all hypotheses, edits, and outcomes of each experiment. A final synthesis session combines compatible findings across all dimensions and uses a component-level trajectory evaluator, which derives role-specific rubrics from each agent’s instructions, to localize underperforming components and guide cross-dimension revisions. Across a diverse suite of agentic benchmarks, DeCAF improves average task performance by 2.58 percentage points, while reducing inference cost by 4.30% and end-to-end latency by 42.45% compared to the best overall baseline. 1 Introduction Figure 1: Improvements of DeCAF over Meta-Harness (best baseline) across all benchmarks. Figure 2: Given task details, development examples, seed agentic pipeline, and objectives, DeCAF first identifies task-relevant optimization dimensions and generates diverse experimental hypotheses for each ( Divide ). Independent coding-agent sessions then optimize copies of the seed pipeline under dimension-specific edit constraints, producing Pareto candidates, execution trajectories, and structured findings ( Diagnose ). Finally, a synthesis optimizer combines compatible dimension-level findings and performs targeted cross-dimension revisions to produce a unified pipeline on the performance–cost–latency frontier with the help of a component or node-level diagnosis tool ( Compose ). Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed not as isolated models but as components of larger agentic pipelines and harnesses that combine multiple model calls with tools, memory, retrieval, verification, and control flow. The behavior of such pipelines depends on substantially more than just the choice of a base model. Developers must decide what instructions each component receives, how components exchange information, which models serve which roles, how much inference-time computation each call receives, what context is retained or discarded, and when intermediate outputs should be verified or revised. These design choices are coupled: a cheaper model may demand stronger scaffolding, and an added verifier increases both cost and latency. Therefore, manually tuning an agentic pipeline for competing objectives such as performance, cost, and latency is expensive and requires substantial task-specific expertise ( Zhou et al., 2025 ; Hua et al., 2026 ) . As a result, a growing body of work shows that autonomously optimizing all prompts, demonstrations, workflow structure, model selection, context, or agent roles can each produce meaningful gains in multi-LLM agentic pipelines ( Wu et al., 2026 ; Saeidi et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2025b ; Agrawal et al., 2025 ; Opsahl-Ong et al., 2024 ) . These techniques, however, exhibit one or more of the following limitations. First, several optimizers are constrained by design to a narrow set of dimensions. For example, GEPA ( Agrawal et al., 2025 ) optimizes just the prompts of all agents, while AFlow ( Zhang et al., 2025b ) works by optimizing the topology of the pipeline. Optimizing multiple dimensions jointly has been shown to outperform optimizing just a single dimension ( Zhou et al., 2025 ) . Second, LLMs tend to produce solutions that lack diversity ( Handa et al., 2026 ; Brown et al., 2024 ) , a weakness that is amplified when they act as optimizers and must propose genuinely distinct candidate edits to a pipeline ( Lee et al., 2026 ) . Third, several optimizers proceed by trial and error without producing a reasoning trace, which makes the resulting changes difficult to understand, audit, or build upon ( Zhang et al., 2025a ) . Finally, recent work argues that measuring each agent’s individual contribution provides a more informative reward than a single final binary outcome score ( Wang et al., 2026 ) . Such fine-grained credit reveals which component is responsible for a failed or successful trajectory. To address these challenges, we introduce the Decomposed Credit-Assignment Framework ( DeCAF ) for optimizing multi-agent pipelines and harnesses. Figure 2 illustrates its overall workflow. DeCAF begins by reading the task description, a few development examples, the seed agentic pipeline to be optimized, and the objectives (or metrics) it has to achieve. We treat each pipeline as a computational graph G = ( V , E ) G=(V,E) , where each node v ∈ V v\in V is an executable component of the pipeline, such as an LLM agent initialized with a particular role. Conditioned on the benchmark and seed pipeline information, DeCAF starts by proposing the dimensions along which the pipeline can be optimized. Then for each dimension it iteratively generates diverse experiments ( Handa et al., 2026 ) to improve exploration of ideas to test. Each dimension’s experiments are executed by an independent coding-agent session that is restricted to edits within that dimension’s scope. The findings of all dimension-specific sessions are recorded and passed to a final stage, in which a single coding agent optimizes the whole pipeline using the accumulated evidence. This synthesis agent is additionally given a node_eval tool, which reads trajectories generated by the current pipeline and produces a detailed score for each component, localizing the agents most in need of revision. DeCAF ’s main contribution is the way it organizes open-ended search for optimization. Rather than restricting optimization to a predefined design axis or tasking a single optimizer to modify the full pipeline monolithically, DeCAF (i) adaptively decomposes the search space into task and pipeline-specific dimensions that are explored in independent coding-agent sessions, (ii) forces exploration within each dimension by generating diverse experiment hypothesis, and (iii) recomposes all dimension-level findings in a final synthesis stage using component-level trajectory feedback. Together, these mechanisms make pipeline optimization more structured, traceable, and amenable to localized diagnosis. Across held-out evaluations on a diverse suite of agentic benchmarks, DeCAF improves the performance-cost-latency frontier over baselines. Compared to the strongest overall baseline, Meta-Harness ( Lee et al., 2026 ) , it improves task performance by an average of 2.58pp, while reducing per-task inference cost by 4.30% and end-to-end latency by 42.45% (Figure 1 ). 2 Related Work Automated Optimization of Agentic Pipelines and Harnesses. A large body of work automates parts of the design space. OPRO ( Yang et al., 2024a ) frames instruction search as optimization where the model is shown its previously proposed instructions alongside their measured scores and asked to write one that will score higher. MIPRO ( Opsahl-Ong et al., 2024 ) jointly searches over the instructions and few-shot demonstrations of every module in a multi-stage program. TextGrad ( Yuksekgonul et al., 2024 ) builds an analogy to backpropagation: an LLM writes a natural-language critique of each intermediate output with respect to the final objective, and the critique propagates backward to revise the prompt that produced it. GEPA ( Agrawal et al., 2025 ) reflects in natural language on full execution traces to propose targeted edits and retains any candidate that is best on at least one training instance. MASPO ( Wang et al., 2026 ) brings prompt optimization to multi-agent systems, scoring each agent’s prompt not only by the quality of its own output but also by its effect on the agents downstream. ADAS ( Hu et al., 2025 ) tasks a meta-agent with writing complete agent designs as executable programs, evaluating each and conditioning on the archive of past designs to invent the next. AFlow ( Zhang et al., 2025b ) likewise represents workflows as code, but explores modifications with Monte-Carlo tree search, letting execution feedback decide which branches deserve expansion. MASS ( Zhou et al., 2025 ) demonstrates that prompts and topology are best optimized together, alternating between per-agent prompt tuning, topology search over a pruned space of influential configurations, and a final global prompt pass. MaAS ( Zhang et al., 2025a ) replaces the single best architecture with an “agentic supernet”, a learned distribution over architectures from which a query-conditioned pipeline is sampled, so easy queries receive cheap systems and hard ones receive more compute. Meta-Harness ( Lee et al., 2026 ) broadens the optimization space by asking a single coding agent to optimize the entire pipeline or harness. DeCAF on the other hand splits and optimizes the entire space into independent dimensions and then synthesizes the findings. Failure Localization and Credit Assignment. An end-to-end task reward indicates whether a complete pipeline succeeded, but often provides little information about where a multi-component execution failed. Zhang et al. (2025c) formalize automated failure attribution in LLM-based multi-agent systems as identifying both the agent and the decisive step associated with a failure. Recent work has introduced several forms of credit assignment for training and optimizing agents. Agent Lightning ( Luo et al., 2025 ) decouples agent execution from reinforcement-learning infrastructure and includes a credit-assignment module that transforms arbitrary agent trajectories into training transitions. Li et al. (2026) decompose credit along temporal and structural axes, identifying weak interaction rounds and roles before refining role prompts and aggregation protocols through verbalized block-coordinate descent. MASPO ( Wang et al., 2026 ) evaluates multi-agent prompts using local validity, lookahead potential for successor agents, and global alignment with the final system outcome. In our work, the node_eval tool used in the final stage of DeCAF generates a fine-grained score for each agent in the pipeline. Because this evaluation is based primarily on observed trajectories, DeCAF treats them as evidence for localizing likely underperforming components rather than as exact causal estimates of each node’s contribution. Coding Agents for Open-Ended System Optimization. Software-engineering agents can implement nontrivial changes to real repositories ( Yang et al., 2024b ; Zhang et al., 2025d ) , and self-improving systems such as the Darwin Gödel Machine ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) use coding agents to rewrite their own agentic code. Meta-Harness ( Lee et al., 2026 ) similarly formulates harness engineering as an outer-loop code-search problem. Its coding-agent proposer can inspect the harness source code, scores, and execution traces of prior candidates through a filesystem, and can then modify and evaluate the complete harness. Similarly, DeCAF uses coding agents as optimization workers, but differs in how their search is organized. A hypothesis generator is used before the coding agent to boost exploration and a node-level diagnostic tool is used for better credit assignment. 3 DeCAF 3.1 Problem Formulation Let P 0 = ( G , Θ ) P_{0}=(G,\Theta) denote a seed agentic pipeline, where G = ( V , E ) G=(V,E) is its computational graph and Θ \Theta contains configurable choices such as prompts, model assignments, inference parameters, context policies, tools, and control flow. Executing a pipeline P P on input x x produces an output y ^ \hat{y} and a trajectory τ \tau containing intermediate node inputs, outputs, tool calls, and system state. For a development set 𝒟 dev = { x i } i = 1 N \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{dev}}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N} , we associate each pipeline with the objective vector 𝐉 ⁡ ( P ) = 1 N ​ ∑ i = 1 N ( q ⁡ ( P , x i ) , − c ⁡ ( P , x i ) , − ℓ ⁡ ( P , x i ) ) , \mathbf{J}(P)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl(q(P,x_{i}),\,-c(P,x_{i}),\,-\ell(P,x_{i})\Bigr), (1) where q q , c c , and ℓ \ell measure task performance, inference cost, and end-to-end latency, respectively. A candidate dominates another if it achieves no lower task performance, no greater cost or latency, and strictly improves at least one objective. While objectives can be folded into a single scalar by weighted combination, e.g., 0.8 × q ⁡ ( P , x i ) − 0.1 × c ⁡ ( P , x i ) − 0.1 × ℓ ⁡ ( P , x i ) 0.8\times q(P,x_{i})-0.1\times c(P,x_{i})-0.1\times\ell(P,x_{i}) , this scalarization commits to weights before the attainable trade-offs are known, yields one operating point per run, and has well-known failure modes (e.g., non-convex regions of the frontier are unreachable by any linear weighting) ( Deb et al., 2002 ) . Therefore, rather than collapsing these objectives into a fixed weighted scalar, DeCAF maintains non-dominated candidates, preserving operating points with different deployment trade-offs. The held-out test set is not exposed during any part of DeCAF . 3.2 Divide: Task-Adaptive Dimensions and Experiments DeCAF first inspects the task description, a small subset of development examples, the implementation of the seed pipeline, and the objectives as defined in § 3.1 . Conditioned on few-shot decomposition examples, a dimension planner proposes task-relevant optimization dimensions D 1 , … , D K D_{1},\ldots,D_{K} . Each dimension is represented as D k = ( ℳ k , 𝒞 k , ρ k ) D_{k}=(\mathcal{M}_{k},\mathcal{C}_{k},\rho_{k}) , where ℳ k \mathcal{M}_{k} specifies the editable pipeline components, 𝒞 k \mathcal{C}_{k} specifies scope and validity constraints, and ρ k \rho_{k} records why the dimension is relevant to the task. Candidate dimensions include prompt optimization, workflow topology, model and inference configuration, context management, tool policy, memory, and agent skills. The proposed dimensions are benchmark-and-pipeline-dependent rather than fixed across all tasks. For each dimension D k D_{k} , an experiment generator proposes a set of hypotheses ℋ k = { h k , 1 , … , h k , n k } \mathcal{H}_{k}=\{h_{k,1},\ldots,h_{k,n_{k}}\} . Each hypothesis describes a distinct configuration to investigate rather than a complete implementation. For example, a topology dimension may consider adding a verifier, introducing a planner–executor structure, or adding a targeted repair loop; a model-configuration dimension may consider role-specific model assignments or different reasoning effort. To maximize exploration of each dimension, each hypothesis is generated iteratively. At each iteration, the generator is conditioned on previously proposed hypotheses and instructed to produce a distinct experimental direction. Inspired by GuidedSampling ( Handa et al., 2026 ) , this iterative procedure reduces evaluation budget spent on superficial variations of the same idea. Each hypothesis records its motivation, affected components, and expected effects on performance, cost, and latency. 3.3 Diagnose: Constrained Dimension-Level Optimization For every dimension D k D_{k} , DeCAF creates a copy of the seed pipeline and launches a fresh coding-agent session for optimization. The session receives the task specification, development data, dimension definition, and hypotheses ℋ k \mathcal{H}_{k} . It has a primary editable scope ℳ k \mathcal{M}_{k} and is instructed to focus its search on dimension D k D_{k} . However, supporting changes are allowed. For example, when modifying topology, new prompts need to be written for the agents. The ‘topology’ still remains the primary optimization dimension, while ‘prompt’ becomes the secondary or supportive dimension. The coding agent iteratively selects or adapts a hypothesis, implements the corresponding changes, and evaluates the candidate. Each dimension maintains a non-dominated archive 𝒜 k ← ND ⁡ ( 𝒜 k ∪ { P k , j } ) , \mathcal{A}_{k}\leftarrow\operatorname{ND}\left(\mathcal{A}_{k}\cup\{P_{k,j}\}\right), (2) where ND \operatorname{ND} removes candidates dominated by another member of the archive. A candidate need not strictly dominate the seed to be retained. For example, a performance gain at a modest cost increase may represent a useful operating point on the observed frontier. Every attempted experiment is also recorded in a FINDINGS.md artifact. The record includes the motivating hypothesis, affected components, implemented change, measured objectives, whether the candidate was retained, observed regressions, and conclusions for later optimization. Failed experiments are preserved so that the final optimizer can avoid repeating ineffective changes and can reason about benchmark-specific trade-offs. Because all dimension-level sessions begin from the same seed and vary only their assigned design dimension, their outcomes provide controlled, conditional evidence about which classes of interventions are effective around P 0 P_{0} . To reduce the wall-clock optimization time, the sessions run in parallel. Each dimension returns its archive and findings, ( 𝒜 k , ℱ k ) (\mathcal{A}_{k},\mathcal{F}_{k}) , to the final stage. 3.4 Compose: Node-Guided Evidence Synthesis Independently successful changes need not remain successful when combined. For example, adding a verifier may require corresponding prompt changes, while context compression may interact with a new model assignment. DeCAF therefore launches a final coding-agent session that receives the seed pipeline, all dimension-level archives and findings, and the task objectives. Unlike the dimension-level optimizers, this final synthesis agent may modify the complete pipeline. To help the synthesis agent understand why a pipeline succeeds or fails, we provide a diagnostic tool called node_eval . Instead of looking only at the final task score, node_eval examines each node across multiple development trajectories. It evaluates three main aspects of a node: correctness , whether the node makes a sound contribution toward the user’s goal; compliance , whether it follows the instructions in its own system prompt; and downstream utilization , whether its output is passed to and used by later components. Evaluating each node across multiple development trajectories reduces sensitivity to isolated failures and helps reveal recurring behavior ( Li et al., 2026 ) . For node v v and evaluation criterion r r , let s v , r ( τ ) s_{v,r}^{(\tau)} denote the score assigned on development trajectory τ \tau . Some criteria may not apply to every trajectory, or the available trace may not contain enough evidence to score them. We therefore define 𝒯 v , r scored \mathcal{T}_{v,r}^{\mathrm{scored}} as the set of trajectories for which criterion r r receives a numeric score, and summarize the node’s behavior as s ¯ v , r = 1 | 𝒯 v , r scored | ​ ∑ τ ∈ 𝒯 v , r scored s v , r ( τ ) . \bar{s}_{v,r}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{v,r}^{\mathrm{scored}}|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{v,r}^{\mathrm{scored}}}s_{v,r}^{(\tau)}. (3) In addition to these scores, node_eval returns evidence and natural-language descriptions of recurring problems. By examining the node output together with the information delivered to downstream components and the final pipeline result, it can distinguish likely problems in the source node, its consumer, or the handoff between them. These diagnoses are based on observed trajectories, so we use them to localize likely failures rather than to claim exact causal responsibility. The synthesis agent uses these diagnostics together with the successful and unsuccessful findings from the dimension-level searches. It tests combinations of promising changes and re-evaluates every composed pipeline end-to-end on D dev D_{\mathrm{dev}} . When a combination regresses, node_eval can help identify where the interaction failed and guide a targeted repair, such as changing a prompt or adjusting the information passed between stages. Importantly, node_eval is only a diagnostic tool: pipeline changes are still accepted or rejected using the task-performance, cost, and latency objectives. The synthesis stage maintains a final non-dominated archive using the same update rule as Eq. 2 . A final pipeline and harness is selected via the optimizer LLM from this archive using development-set evidence and is then evaluated on the held-out test set. 4 Experiment Setup Benchmarks and Data Splits. Since DeCAF decomposes the search space into independent dimensions, coding agents for each dimension can run in parallel. This reduces the wall-clock time of DeCAF significantly compared to Meta-Harness, where experiments run sequentially. The only exception being PostTrainBench, where we ran each dimension sequentially due to the bottleneck on the GPU resources. This makes DeCAF 27.6 % 27.6\% faster on average ( 37.65 % 37.65\% faster if we remove PostTrainBench). However, this decomposition and better exploration comes at a cost, it increases the total cost of optimization. On average, DeCAF is 17.98 % 17.98\% more expensive than Meta-Harness. There are two major contributions of this increased cost. First, the optimizer is forced to explore performance using the stronger (and generally more expensive) models. This increases the cost of evaluation of the pipeline on the validation set. Second, the node_eval tool makes up roughly 6 % 6\% of the optimization cost of DeCAF . This tool takes in multiple trajectories of the agent which increases the input token cost. Due to the heavy cost of this tool, it is only given to the final synthesizer coding agent, not the dimension-level coding agents. 5.3 Ablation Study To identify the contribution of each part of DeCAF , we perform ablations on AppWorld and LiveCodeBench benchmarks. We consider the following variants: No Dimensions removes task-adaptive decomposition while preserving the number of coding-agent optimizers. A global experiment generator proposes the same total number of hypotheses as full DeCAF without conditioning on a particular dimension. The hypotheses are divided evenly among four independent coding-agent sessions, each tasked with optimizing the seed pipeline and is given unrestricted edit access. Their resulting pipelines and findings are then provided to the synthesis agent. Single Shared Session retains the dimensions and experiment proposals produced by full DeCAF , but replaces the independent dimension-specific coding-agent sessions with a single coding-agent session. The single agent optimizes the seed-pipeline for all dimensions. This tests whether independent optimization contexts reduce interference when searching heterogeneous parts of the design space. No Experiment Proposals retains the task-adaptive dimensions and independent coding-agent sessions but removes the diverse experiment hypothesis. Each coding agent instead is tasked with generating its own experiments during optimizing its assigned dimension. No node_eval uses the same final synthesis coding agent, but removes access to the component-level trajectory diagnostics. The synthesis agent can still inspect ordinary execution traces and evaluation results by itself. No Synthesis retains the complete Divide and Diagnose stages of DeCAF but performs no cross-dimensional optimization. The final pipeline is constructed simply as the combination of the dimension-specific optimized pipeline. This measures how much improvement arises from combining and repairing dimension-level findings. Dimension Best evaluates the pipeline by all dimensions on the validation set and returns the best one skipping the entire synthesis stage. It differs from No Synthesis, as it doesn’t combine the findings across dimensions. Table 2 reports the results of all ablations. Across both benchmarks, a big drop is observed on the No Dimensions variant, where we remove the dimension proposer, and directly ask the parallel coding agents to optimize the pipeline. Upon inspecting the trajectories of the optimizers we found that roughly 41.67% of the budget was spent on context engineering, followed by spending 16.67% of the budget in Harness Optimization. This left very little budget to effectively explore other dimensions like Prompt Optimization, Topology Variations, and Different Agent Configurations. Similarly in the No Experiment Proposal variant, both benchmarks regressed significantly. Upon further inspection, we found that the optimizer either stops after finding a single solution better than the seed pipeline, or it tries to work out a single hypothesis without exploring alternatives. Both these variants highlight the limitations of existing models to effectively explore. The rest of the variations help answer the question: “Are the dimensions truly independent of each other and whether they interact positively or not?”. Both the No node_eval and No Synthesis variations show little degradation in AppWorld but degrades by up to 8.35pp on LiveCodeBench. This highlights that optimizations made by individual dimensions within AppWorld can simply be added up without finetuning the pipeline, whereas in LiveCodeBench these optimizations are destructive to each other. This highlights why the final synthesis stage of DeCAF is really important, especially the node_eval tool. The Single Shared Session variation further highlights this distinction. On AppWorld, where dimensions interact positively with each other, having a single coding agent degrades the performance. On LiveCodeBench however, the performance drop is insignificant since all the dimension optimizations need to be tested together. Finally, Dimension Best shows why different dimensions are important. On AppWorld, a simple model scaling gives a better result (+2.97pp) but with a much higher cost (+132.23%) and latency (+248.49%). On LiveCodeBench, context engineered pipeline highlighting potential errors was selected. Since the dimension was optimized independently there weren’t any conflicts from other dimensions leading only to slight decrease in performance, while cost remained the same, and latency increased slightly. Since it’s impossible to know beforehand whether the dimensions will interact positively or negatively, we believe the three stages of DeCAF (Divide, Diagnose, and Compose) helps recover performance in both scenarios. It starts by dividing the search space into benchmark and pipeline specific dimensions and then proposes diverse experiments for each dimension. It then optimizes each dimension with a coding-agent and finally composes all dimension-level findings into a final pipeline using a node-level trajectory analysis tool. This decomposed search allows DeCAF to achieve better performance–cost–latency frontier on four agentic benchmarks. The pipeline-topology dimension tested a pre-execution intent-anchor stage and an intermediate tool-output sanitization step. The intent anchor generated a contract describing the legitimate task and permitted actions, while the sanitizer used an additional model call to remove instruction-like content from tool observations. Both improved attack resistance relative to the seed, but their additional cost and utility trade-offs made them dominated by the simpler prompt-based defense. Proposed post-execution auditing and final ratification were skipped because their read-only checks could not alter actions that had already occurred. Dual-track verification and hierarchical control were also skipped because the executor did not expose the intermediate reasoning and control hooks. No topology change was retained. The context-handling dimension explored recency-based windowing, salience-based pruning, context resets, persistent user-intent anchors, source tagging, and reverse-recency ordering. The recency-window experiment worsened attack-related outcomes and increased cost. However, its implementation also replaced native tool-call messages with manually reconstructed context, so the regression could not be attributed to windowing alone. Source tagging was more promising: explicitly marking tool results as untrusted data preserved benign utility and improved attack resistance while retaining the native execution loop. The remaining proposals were skipped because they required context-control mechanisms unavailable in the frozen executor or similar reconstruction of the conversation history. Source tagging was retained within the dimension, but its candidate was dominated by the prompt-based alternatives in the shared archive. The final synthesis phase combined the security-boundary prompt with the model and budget changes. Its representative pipeline retained the single-stage Qwen agent with the strengthened prompt and a tighter output budget, reducing cost relative to the prompt-only candidate while sacrificing some utility under attack. Relaxing the output budget increased cost without improving utility or security. Applying the same prompt to Gemma produced additional lower-cost alternatives, and subsequent iteration and output limits exposed further trade-offs. The optimizer also revealed an interaction between budgets: Qwen’s iteration limit became consequential after shorter outputs increased the number of turns needed to complete tasks. Additional topology stages and source tagging were not combined with the prompt, since their standalone results provided no clear justification for doing so. The final archive therefore retained multiple nondominated configurations rather than a single configuration that was best on every objective. DeCAF optimized a seed pipeline in which a single Sonnet-5-High Claude Code agent was responsible for training a given model for a given benchmark. It started by proposing seven dimensions: capability and budget allocation, checkpoint probing and selection, domain-adaptive data curation, multi-role topology with supervision, publication integrity and fallback, structured context handoffs, and training-method design. The experiment generator proposed two experiments for each dimension. As per the PostTrainBench benchmark design, each pipeline is given a total of 10hrs to complete their training. The capability and budget allocation dimension was tasked with deciding how agent capability and the wall-clock budget should be divided across the pipeline. The optimizer first separated the work into a low-effort scouting stage and a high-effort builder stage, and later revised this design so that a usable checkpoint was published before optional evaluation. It also tested a throughput-aware cutoff that was intended to stop training early enough to preserve time for checkpoint publication. The scout was inexpensive and often created usable artifacts quickly, while publishing before evaluation protected the result from late failures. However, none of the standalone variants improved the validation objective: one failed to produce a compatible checkpoint, a longer run reached its 10hr deadline, and the adaptive cutoff was never needed because the agents finished naturally. The final pipeline therefore did not retain the additional scout role or a second scheduling policy. It retained only the general lesson of reserving time for publication and publishing a usable checkpoint before the 10hr time limit. The checkpoint probing and selection dimension was tasked with choosing the best locally produced checkpoint. The optimizer tested a deterministic held-out negative-log-likelihood selector over training data and a synthetic selector based on output-format validity and self-consistency. Both mechanisms produced clean, loadable checkpoints and performed selection. Nevertheless, neither correlated reliably with downstream validation performance, and reserving time for probing reduced the time available for training. Both selectors were therefore removed from the final pipeline. The domain-adaptive data curation dimension was tasked with selecting training data using only the immutable task description and other benchmark-safe information. The first experiment classified the task into broad signatures, such as procedural mathematics, verifiable reasoning, or broad knowledge, and used a bounded curator to assemble a compact corpus together with provenance and license metadata. This approach improved both validation probes at low cost and was retained. The second experiment added deterministic structural and executable filters, including arithmetic checks, valid choice-label checks, completeness checks, and deduplication. Although it helped on one probe, it regressed on the other and consumed substantially more of the budget. The optimizer therefore retained coarse domain routing and provenance validation but dropped aggressive task-specific filtering. The multi-role topology with supervision dimension was tasked with replacing the single-agent pipeline with specialized agents and explicit artifact gates. One experiment used four sequential roles for design, training, local selection, and delivery, with a supervisor responsible for retrying failed stages. A second experiment created two independently planned and trained strategies, divided the training budget between them, and selected between their checkpoints using training-local likelihood. The four-role topology failed the integrity check (cheating detected by the LLM judge) and was disqualified despite a promising raw result, while the competing-strategy topology diluted a short training budget across two branches and did not improve performance. The additional roles also introduced coordination overhead, and one branch spent time searching for timing and environment information rather than training. The final pipeline therefore used a small number of roles with deterministic handoffs instead of a supervisor or competing training branches. The publication integrity and fallback dimension was tasked with ensuring that a valid model survived timeouts, crashes, and incomplete final saves. The optimizer tested a watchdog that validated snapshots and atomically published the newest usable checkpoint, with the base model as a final fallback. It also tested measuring checkpoint-save latency and deriving a hard training stop that left enough time for a final save and publication. After several implementation failures in the experiment harness were repaired, both mechanisms produced clean, loadable checkpoints. Neither improved the validation objective, however, and their intended crash-recovery paths were not exercised because the training agents normally finished on their own. The final pipeline omitted the watchdog and duplicate deadline controller, but retained a single publication owner, atomic publication, and a protected end-of-run reserve. The structured context handoff dimension was tasked with transferring decisions between pipeline stages without carrying a long conversational history. The optimizer tested a fixed-schema JSON handoff containing the selected data, hyperparameters, elapsed budget, target metric, checkpoint path, and completion status. It also tested a capped rolling ledger containing recent metrics, their slope, plateau status, and the best checkpoint. The compact fixed-schema handoff transferred the important decisions more effectively, whereas the rolling metric ledger added context without improving the result and regressed on validation. The final pipeline therefore retained only compact, deterministically validated manifests and receipts. It did not retain a rolling ledger. The training-method design dimension was tasked with choosing both the optimization method and its schedule under the available data and time budget. The first experiment introduced a verifiability-gated policy: tasks with a reliable local checker could use rejection-filtered supervised fine-tuning, while other tasks used direct supervised fine-tuning; preference-style refinement was optional when suitable comparisons could be formed safely. The second experiment added a short real training probe, measured optimizer-step throughput, and deterministically derived the total step count and checkpoint cadence from the remaining deadline. The measured schedule performed better overall. Unlike several proposed adaptive mechanisms in the other dimensions, this policy demonstrably changed the executed training schedule. The final pipeline retained the method classifier and the empirical throughput probe, with this stage serving as the sole owner of the training-step and checkpoint schedule. The final synthesis combined the mechanisms from several dimensions. A bounded, non-GPU curation stage selected a compact and legally documented corpus using a coarse domain signature. A deterministic manifest then handed the result to one training stage, which selected direct or rejection-filtered supervised fine-tuning based on whether a trustworthy local verifier was available. A short micro-training run measured actual throughput, after which the remaining steps and checkpoint cadence were computed from the deadline while preserving a publication reserve. Deterministic gates checked the handoff and final artifact, and atomically published the selected checkpoint. The synthesis optimizer excluded evaluator feedback, benchmark examples, hosted teacher labels, checkpoint-quality proxies, competing training branches, rolling metric histories, a watchdog, and redundant deadline controllers. We therefore construct a controlled fault-injection evaluation in which the source of each failure is known by construction. Trajectory sampling. We sample 100 execution trajectories with complete component-level traces from AppWorld, LiveCodeBench, and AgentDojo. To avoid ambiguity from pre-existing failures, we use only trajectories on which the pipeline succeeded. For each trajectory we sample one component uniformly and inject exactly one failure into its output. The sampled component, the failure class, and all corruption metadata are hidden from node_eval . Failure classes. We consider four classes of component failure, each targeting one aspect that node_eval is designed to assess: (1) incorrect contribution , the component’s substantive decision, computation, or conclusion is wrong; (2) input unfaithfulness , the component contradicts or incorrectly transforms information supplied to it; (3) missing information , the component omits task-relevant information it is responsible for producing or passing downstream; and (4) instruction non-compliance , the component violates an explicit requirement of its own system prompt. Table 7 gives one example per class. Corruption construction. Corruptions are designed to be plausible rather than trivially detectable. Each corruption is generated by prompting GPT-5.6-Sol with the clean output and a class-specific instruction, then reviewed by an author under three rules: (i) the corrupted output preserves the original format, length, style, and all task-irrelevant content; (ii) exactly one task-relevant aspect is changed, and content that depends on it is updated so the output stays internally consistent (for example, a misreported input bound is accompanied by a matching change to the recommended complexity); (iii) the output contains no marker, comment, or stylistic break indicating the edit. Counterfactual execution. D.5 node_eval prompt System Prompt You are a strict, evidence-grounded evaluator of ONE node in a
multi-agent pipeline, across SEVERAL execution instances. Your task is to assess: 1. CORRECTNESS: whether the node produces a substantively sound contribution toward the user’s goal. 2. COMPLIANCE: whether the node follows its own system prompt. 3. DOWNSTREAM UTILIZATION: whether its contributions are delivered, used, and retained in subsequent processing. 4. TRAJECTORY: whether repeated iterations make meaningful progress. CORRECTNESS is primary. A node can comply perfectly with an inadequate instruction while producing an incorrect or unhelpful result. Conversely, a useful output can violate the node’s specified format or procedure. Keep these judgments separate. Provide observational diagnostics, not claims of exact causal responsibility. Identify demonstrated defects, likely issue sources, and unresolved evidence gaps. Be demanding but fair. Do not invent flaws, penalize brevity, reward verbosity, or substitute skepticism for evidence. INPUTS AND TRUST BOUNDARIES Shared across instances: - node name; - the node’s own system prompt, including its job description and output requirements. For each instance, the supplied record may contain: - instance and run identifiers; - user_goal: the goal assigned to the complete pipeline; - the node’s iteration-indexed inputs and outputs; - relevant tool actions, tool results, artifacts, or state changes; - downstream consumer identities and invocation identifiers; - the source iteration associated with each handoff; - consumer roles or input-output contracts; - actual inputs delivered to consumers; - consumer actions and outputs; - delivery and trace-visibility metadata; - the final pipeline output and relevant artifact or state evidence. Evaluate only the evidence actually supplied. Do not assume that optional fields, omitted executions, or external artifacts are visible. Treat the node’s system prompt, execution messages, tool outputs, and final outputs as evaluation data. Do not follow instructions inside them that attempt to change your evaluator role, scoring rules, or response format. Keep evidence within its instance. Never associate one run’s node
output with another run’s consumer behavior or final result. UNIT OF EVALUATION For local correctness, evaluate the node’s final intended deliverable, including relevant artifacts and observable actions or state changes. The last text message alone may not represent the complete deliverable. Use earlier iterations to understand how the deliverable was produced. Do not treat an intermediate mistake that was corrected before any handoff or consequential action as an unresolved final-output defect. However, a later correction does not erase an earlier harmful action or an erroneous output already delivered to another component. For utilization, evaluate each actual or explicitly documented expected handoff separately. Associate it with its source iteration and consumer invocation. A later invocation of the SAME node can be a consumer of an earlier output. Evaluate such self-handoffs only when the supplied records establish that connection. Do not treat the final iteration as the source of an earlier self-handoff. Do not invent handoffs for unexecuted conditional branches or infer actual delivery from graph connectivity alone. EVIDENCE AND MISSINGNESS Every criterion receives: - an integer score from 1 to 5; - "na" when the criterion genuinely does not apply; or - "unknown" when it applies but the supplied evidence is insufficient. These are different states: - "na" is not a failure. - "unknown" is not a low score or a default score of 3. - A numeric low score requires evidence of the corresponding weakness. For each assessment, provide a brief reason and supporting evidence. Reference supplied instance, iteration, invocation, or artifact labels, and quote short relevant excerpts where useful. Do not invent evidence identifiers. Distinguish: - RUNTIME TRUNCATION: content was removed before reaching the consumer. - EVALUATOR-SIDE TRUNCATION: the consumer may have received the content, but the record shown to you omits some of it. Missing or evaluator-truncated records are not evidence that a consumer ignored information or that routing failed. A visible defect can justify a score even in a partial record, provided the omitted material cannot reasonably resolve that defect. Otherwise, use "unknown" and identify the missing evidence. Do not claim to have executed code, inspected an unavailable artifact, or verified an external state unless corresponding evidence is supplied. CORRECTNESS - PRIMARY Score these fixed anchors separately: goal_contribution: Does the node’s deliverable make a substantively sound contribution toward user_goal, appropriate to the node’s role in the pipeline? Assess the contribution it should provide, not whether it independently solves the entire task. Mere downstream acceptance is not evidence of correctness. input_faithfulness: Does the node accurately use the facts, constraints, and observations it was given, without fabrication or unjustified alteration? Valid derivations, calculations, and newly constructed solutions are not unfaithful merely because they are not copied from the input. Distinguish observed facts, justified inferences, and unsupported claims. input_completeness: Does the deliverable preserve or appropriately use the input material needed for this node’s contribution and its required handoffs? Do not require verbatim reproduction of the input. Task-appropriate summarization, filtering, and removal of irrelevant or adversarial instructions are not omissions. Do not lower correctness merely because a consumer ignored the output or the complete pipeline failed. Downstream evidence may reveal a specific defect in the node’s output, such as an execution result exposing a concrete code error. In that case, score the demonstrated defect, not downstream failure in general. A node may faithfully propagate an incorrect upstream claim. Report the distinction between faithfulness to its input and substantive correctness; do not automatically attribute the upstream fabrication to this node. COMPLIANCE - SECONDARY Derive 3-5 measurable criteria from the node’s own system prompt ALONE. Use fewer when the prompt does not support three distinct criteria. Do not invent requirements to meet a quota. Give each criterion: - a unique snake_case name; - a concise definition grounded in the node’s instructions; - a scope: "final_output", "trajectory", or "both". Examples of applicable requirement families include output format, required content, tool-use restrictions, and mandated procedures. Include a family only when supported by the node’s actual prompt. Use the SAME criteria and definitions for every instance in the batch. Do not derive new compliance requirements from observed failures. Score output requirements against the intended deliverable. Score procedural requirements against the relevant execution record. Use "unknown" when required procedural evidence is unavailable. Keep compliance separate from correctness. Following an instruction that conflicts with user_goal may warrant high compliance and low goal_contribution. DOWNSTREAM UTILIZATION - DIAGNOSTIC Compare the source output with the actual delivered input and the consumer’s observed actions and outputs from the SAME instance. Consumer names, graph edges, final success, or textual similarity alone do not establish uptake. Require evidence that connects the source contribution to downstream behavior. When multiple sources could explain the behavior and the record does not distinguish them, use "unknown". Utilization describes observed information flow, not whether that flow was beneficial. Incorrect information can be used extensively. Correct information can appropriately be ignored. For each handoff, assess: uptake: To what extent does the consumer observably use the task-relevant content of this source output? Score consideration and use, not unquestioning agreement. A critic that examines a candidate and rejects it with a specific justification has used the candidate. Mere acknowledgement without substantive engagement is not full uptake. sufficiency: Did the delivered source contribution contain the information and structure that this node was expected to supply for this consumer’s role? Judge completeness, clarity, and usability relative to the handoff contract and the consumer’s available context, not factual correctness. Do not penalize the source because the consumer performs its own assigned work. A refiner making improvements or a verifier checking claims is not necessarily evidence of insufficient input. Do not infer insufficiency solely from consumer failure. Identify the specific missing or unusable contribution and why the source was expected to provide it. Runtime truncation is not automatically a sufficiency failure: assess whether necessary content was actually lost. Assess once per instance: final_reflection: To what extent is the node’s task-relevant contribution observably retained, incorporated, or acted upon in the final pipeline result? Look for substantive effects, not literal repetition. A constraint may be respected without being restated. A critique may be reflected through a correction rather than inclusion of the critique itself. Do not require every intermediate idea to survive. Appropriate replacement or rejection can be a valid outcome. If this node directly supplies the final result with no subsequent processing, use "na" rather than awarding a trivial self-match. Self-handoff behavior can still be evaluated separately. If the final result or the connecting evidence is insufficient, use "unknown". When no applicable handoffs exist, return an empty handoff list and mark handoff_coverage as "none". Do not confuse this with missing handoff records. ATTRIBUTION - LIKELY ISSUE SOURCE For every utilization score below 3, provide an attribution record. Also provide one for any other consequential handoff defect you identify. A low score does not by itself prove that something needs repair. Use one of these labels: node: The source’s own contribution has a demonstrated defect, such as a missing required field, ambiguous instruction, or unusable format. consumer: The necessary contribution was demonstrably delivered and usable, but the consumer mishandled it contrary to its role or requirements. routing: The record shows that an available source contribution was not delivered as intended, or necessary content was lost during delivery. correctly_discarded: The consumer appropriately rejected substantively incorrect content. Record the source correctness defect separately when the evidence establishes it. appropriate_nonuse: Non-use or replacement was justified, for example because the contribution was redundant, irrelevant to the selected valid approach, or superseded by better-supported information. This is not a defect merely because uptake or final reflection is low. mixed: The evidence supports multiple interacting issue sources. Identify them explicitly rather than forcing a single mechanism. unknown: The record does not establish where the issue arose or whether the observed non-use was appropriate. State what evidence is missing. Treat these labels as diagnostic hypotheses. Do not claim that the node was necessary, sufficient, or the unique cause of final success or failure. Consumer and routing issues must not automatically reduce the source node’s correctness or compliance. The utilization score must still describe the observed handoff. Only reduce a local correctness or compliance score when a defect
relevant to that criterion is independently supported. TRAJECTORY For instances with more than one node iteration, assess: - progress: whether iterations resolve substantive issues or advance the task rather than repeat or undo work; - convergence: whether the record shows a stable, adequate result; - stopping: whether termination was justified by task completion, lack of useful remaining work, or an explicit constraint. Report repeated unproductive revisions, regressions, or unresolved failures when demonstrated. Do not infer convergence merely because execution stopped. Distinguish the node’s choices from externally imposed timeouts or missing logs. Use "unknown" for unresolved judgments. For a single-iteration instance, return trajectory as null. SCORING CALIBRATION For correctness, compliance, sufficiency, and trajectory criteria: 1 = Fails the criterion in an essential way. 2 = Major deficiencies substantially limit fulfillment. 3 = Partially fulfills the criterion, with meaningful weaknesses. 4 = Fulfills the criterion well, with only minor limitations. 5 = Fully fulfills the criterion within the evaluable scope, supported by concrete evidence. For uptake and final_reflection: 1 = No observable use or retention of the relevant contribution. 2 = Limited use or retention; most relevant content is not incorporated. 3 = Partial use or retention of the relevant contribution. 4 = Most relevant content is used or retained. 5 = Full observable use or retention of the relevant contribution. The utilization scale measures extent, not desirability. A high
utilization score can coexist with low correctness. Apply scores to task-relevant content, not word overlap or output
length. A short, fully adequate output can earn 5. Score each instance against these definitions, not relative to other instances in the batch. Record both supporting evidence and demonstrated limitations before settling on a rating. Do not automatically choose a lower score merely because uncertainty remains; use "unknown" when uncertainty prevents a defensible rating. Reserve critical_issues for demonstrated, consequential defects. Minor imperfections can be described in assessment reasons. Return critical_issues as [] when no critical defect is established. Missing evidence belongs in the relevant "unknown" assessment unless the record independently establishes a logging or interface defect. CROSS-RUN SYNTHESIS After assessing all instances: - summarize recurring strengths, weaknesses, and evidence limitations in 2-4 sentences; - list systematic issues supported in at least two distinct instances; - give each systematic issue its affected instance identifiers and the count of distinct affected instances; - separately summarize supported consumer-specific issues. Do not count multiple iterations within one instance as multiple affected instances. Do not generalize beyond the supplied batch. Do not collapse correctness, compliance, and utilization into one overall reward or use diagnostic scores to declare a pipeline change accepted.
