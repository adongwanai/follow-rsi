There are two common ways to spend it on agents when a verifier can score every attempt. Best-of- N N sampling runs many independent agents and keeps the best result but saturates quickly. One large team of collaborating agents can score higher but stops improving as it grows. Can we get the best of both? Our method, parallel agent teams , does so by sampling many small teams in parallel and keeping the best team’s result. We test it on 12 long-horizon tasks from GPU kernels and formal proofs to exploit development. With a 27B model and a budget of 100 agents, parallel teams of ten beat parallel agents on 10 of 12 tasks and beat one team of 100 on all four tasks where we ran such large teams. At equal dollar cost the same parallel teams beat a frontier model on half the tasks. A simple statistical model shows that individual agents lose because their many samples have a low mean and that one large team loses because a single sample gets no gain from variance. Sampling many small teams in parallel balances a high mean with the gain from variance. Parallel agent teams thus offer a more effective way to scale test-time compute. 1 Introduction Figure 1 : Sample small teams, not agents or one big team. (a) Three ways to spend the same agents. Test-time compute lets a fixed pre-trained model do better by spending more computation at inference ( Snell et al., 2024 ; Wu et al., 2025 ) . An ideal method would turn more compute into better results steadily. We study tasks where a verifier can score every attempt and only the best solution matters. Many tasks for coding agents have this form ( Chan et al., 2025 ; Wijk et al., 2025 ) because a scorer can check each attempt automatically and only the fastest kernel or the finished proof matters. On such tasks there are two common ways to spend more compute on agents. The first is best-of- N N sampling, one of the strongest methods when a verifier is available ( Chen et al., 2021 ; Cobbe et al., 2021 ; Li et al., 2022 ; Brown et al., 2024 ) . It runs N N independent agents and keeps the one with the highest score. Yet best-of- N N over individual agents saturates quickly. In our experiments, 50 agents already reach 81% to 100% of the gain that 100 agents reach. The second way is to let more agents work together on the task as one team. Multi-agent systems split the work among agents with different roles ( Hong et al., 2024 ; Qian et al., 2024 ; Wu et al., 2023 ) , and recent work grows such teams to hundreds or thousands of agents ( Qian et al., 2025 ) . A larger team does not give more samples to select from. It changes a single sample, and its score does not rise steadily with its size ( Qian et al., 2025 ; Kim et al., 2025 ) . Large groups of people and of language models tend to converge on one solution and lose the diversity that exploration needs ( Lazer and Friedman, 2007 ; Wu et al., 2019 ; Weng et al., 2025 ) . Figure 2 : Parallel teams beat parallel agents on 10 of 12 tasks at 100 agents. Qwen3.8-27B parallel agents (blue) and parallel teams of ten (orange). Brackets give the teams’ gain at the largest budget both reach. Each approach has one half of what we need. Best-of- N N takes advantage of the variation across samples. A team lets its agents work together and reach better performance more efficiently. We introduce parallel agent teams , which combine the two by applying best-of- N N to independent small teams of collaborating agents (Figure 1 a). Each team is a small group of agents that share a forum. At the same budget of agents, parallel teams score higher than both parallel agents and one big team and keep improving as the budget grows (Figure 1 b). Averaged over 12 tasks, parallel teams of ten score 10% higher than parallel agents at a total budget of 10 agents and 27% higher at a total budget of 100 agents. At 100 agents, ten teams of ten beat one team of 100 by 8% to 30%. At equal dollar cost, teams of a 27B model also beat the frontier model GPT-5.6-sol on 4 of 8 tasks. A simple statistical model of best-of- N N explains why parallel small teams win. The expected best of several samples is the mean score of one sample plus a gain from variance, which grows with the number of samples the budget buys. Parallel agents collect the largest gain from variance because they draw the most samples, but each agent has a low mean. One big team has a high mean, but it is a single sample and wastes the gain from variance, and a larger team does not guarantee a higher mean. Parallel small teams balance the two. Each team has a much higher mean than an agent, and several teams still collect most of the gain from variance. They also keep improving as the budget grows. At a given budget they draw fewer samples than parallel agents because each team costs several agents, so their best-of- N N curve is still on its steep early part, where each additional sample raises the best result the most. Collaboration raises the mean score of each sample, and sampling turns the variance across samples into gains. Parallel agent teams get both and keep improving where best-of- N N over individual agents has saturated, offering a simple way to scale test-time compute further. 2 Parallel Agent Teams We first describe best-of- N N sampling and how we measure it, and then agent teams. We then introduce parallel agent teams, which combine the two. Best-of- N N sampling. We study tasks with a verifier that scores any candidate solution so that the best of several candidates can be found without human judgment. For a fixed model, drawing more independent responses and keeping the best steadily improves performance ( Brown et al., 2024 ) . Let X X be the score of one sample, such as one agent, defined as the score of its best verified submission. We report the expected best of N N independent samples: 𝔼 ⁡ [ max 1 ≤ i ≤ N ⁡ X i ] . \mathbb{E}\Big[\max_{1\leq i\leq N}X_{i}\Big]. (1) We estimate it from a pool of L L runs by taking every subset of N N runs and averaging its best score over all ( L N ) \binom{L}{N} subsets. With the scores sorted as v ( 1 ) ≤ ⋯ ≤ v ( L ) v_{(1)}\leq\dots\leq v_{(L)} , this average is ∑ i = N L v ( i ) ​ ( i − 1 N − 1 ) / ( L N ) \sum_{i=N}^{L}v_{(i)}\binom{i-1}{N-1}/\binom{L}{N} . One pool thus gives the whole curve from N = 1 N=1 to N = L N=L . Take five runs that score 2, 4, 5, 7 and 9. Their expected best is 5.4 for one run (the mean), 7.1 for two and 9 for all five. Agent teams. Instead of working alone, agents can work together as a team on the same task. Past work shows that such teams can produce better results than a single agent ( Li et al., 2023 ; Du et al., 2024 ; Hong et al., 2024 ; Qian et al., 2024 ; Chen et al., 2024 ) . We mainly study flat teams, in which peers share progress through a common log or memory ( Park et al., 2026 ; Qu et al., 2026 ) . Each agent has its own sandbox and context but shares a forum where any agent can post and read notes at any time. There are no roles, no manager and no assigned work. A team’s score is the best verified submission of any member (Appendix E ). Section 6 studies an alternative team architecture in which a manager assigns the work. Parallel agent teams. Parallel agent teams combine the two by applying best-of- N N to independent teams (Figure 1 a), so a sample is one team. We measure compute as a budget of B B agents that each run for the same fixed time. It buys N = B / k N=B/k teams of size k k . Parallel agents ( k = 1 k=1 ) and one big team ( k = B k=B ) are the two ends of this family, and parallel teams lie in between. More GPT-5.6-sol runs therefore add little while the best of several Qwen3.8-27B teams keeps climbing. 4 Why Do Parallel Teams Win? Parallel teams beat both common approaches. They beat parallel agents although at the same budget they draw ten times fewer samples, since a team of ten costs ten agents. They beat one big team, which puts the whole budget into a single sample. A simple statistical model of best-of- N N explains both results. 4.1 A statistical model Assume that the score X X of one sample follows a normal distribution 𝒩 ⁡ ( μ , σ 2 ) \mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2}) and that samples are independent. The expected best of N N samples is then μ + σ ​ a N \mu+\sigma a_{N} . Here a N a_{N} is the expected maximum of N N standard normal variables, and a N ≈ 2 ​ ln ⁡ N a_{N}\approx\sqrt{2\ln N} for large N N ( Fisher and Tippett, 1928 ; Gnedenko, 1943 , Appendix A ;) . Now let each sample cost c c agents so that a budget of B B agents buys N = B / c N=B/c samples. The expected best is then V ⁡ ( B ) ≈ μ + σ ​ 2 ​ ln ⁡ ( B / c ) . V(B)\approx\mu+\sigma\sqrt{2\ln(B/c)}. (2) The three numbers affect the curve at different rates. The mean μ \mu adds the same amount at every budget. The standard deviation σ \sigma multiplies the gain from variance. This gain grows only as 2 ​ ln ⁡ N \sqrt{2\ln N} . The cost c c cuts the number of samples from B B to B / c B/c but lowers the gain from variance by only about σ ​ ln ⁡ c / 2 ​ ln ⁡ B \sigma\ln c/\sqrt{2\ln B} . A tenfold cost therefore matters little once the budget is large. For teams of size k k the cost is c = k c=k , and the mean μ k \mu_{k} and standard deviation σ k \sigma_{k} depend on k k : V k ​ ( B ) ≈ μ k + σ k ​ 2 ​ ln ⁡ ( B / k ) . V_{k}(B)\approx\mu_{k}+\sigma_{k}\sqrt{2\ln(B/k)}. (3) The first term is the mean of one team and the second is the gain from variance when sampling B / k B/k teams. Parallel agents ( k = 1 k=1 ) and one big team ( k = B k=B ) are the two ends of this family. 4.2 One big team wastes variance Parallel sampling takes advantage of the variance of scores. By Equation 2 , more samples raise the expected best by σ ​ 2 ​ ln ⁡ N \sigma\sqrt{2\ln N} , so performance scales with compute at a rate set by the standard deviation σ \sigma . Parallel agents and parallel teams both draw more samples as the budget grows and collect this gain. One big team wastes it. It is a single sample, so 2 ​ ln ⁡ ( B / k ) = 0 \sqrt{2\ln(B/k)}=0 and its expected score is just its mean μ B \mu_{B} , whatever its standard deviation. A larger budget then helps one big team only if a larger team has a higher mean, and a larger team does not guarantee that. From 64 to 100 agents the mean of one team falls on all four tasks where we ran such teams (Section 3.1 ). One big team therefore loses to parallel teams, which raise the mean and still keep most of the gain from variance. 4.3 Parallel teams balance mean and variance Figure 6 : How the mean, standard deviation and cost of a sample shape the best-of- N N curve. (4) With equal standard deviations, drawing B / c B/c samples instead of B B loses only about σ ​ ln ⁡ c / 2 ​ ln ⁡ B \sigma\ln c/\sqrt{2\ln B} , and this loss shrinks as the budget grows. A higher mean counts in full at every budget. At a budget of 200, a mean gain of only 0.88 ​ σ 0.88\sigma pays for a 10 × \times cost. A method at ten times the cost can still win through a higher mean or a higher standard deviation. 4.4 Testing the statistical model We now check the statistical model against our measurements. The best-of- N N curve rises fastest at small N N and flattens as N N grows. At a given budget an expensive method has drawn few samples and still sits on the steep part of its curve while a cheap method is already on the flat part. Differentiating Equation 2 gives the return to one more agent of budget, σ / ( B ​ 2 ​ ln ⁡ ( B / c ) ) \sigma/\big(B\sqrt{2\ln(B/c)}\big) . This return grows with the cost c c . At 20 agents the statistical model gives a team of ten ( N = 2 N=2 ) a marginal return ln ⁡ 20 / ln ⁡ 2 ≈ 2.1 \sqrt{\ln 20/\ln 2}\approx 2.1 times that of an agent ( N = 20 N=20 ) at equal standard deviation. The measured ratio is 1.27% to 0.64% (Figure 3 b). Earlier work observes a trade-off between the quality and the quantity of samples at equal compute ( Brown et al., 2024 ; Snell et al., 2024 ; Wu et al., 2025 ) . We use it to evaluate two other choices, a longer session for each agent and a different model. Longer sessions. Instead of forming a team we can let each agent work longer. A 12-hour agent costs as much as a team of two agents that each work 6 hours (Figure 8 , Appendix G ). Longer sessions barely raise the mean. By Equation 4 , doubling the cost pays off at this budget only if the mean rises by about 3.4 points. The 12-hour agents therefore lose even to plain best-of- N N over 6-hour agents. On Graph Node Classification, one team scores 79.0 on average and the best of ten independent agents scores 69.1. Eight of the 12 tasks show the same pattern (Table 4 ), and so do most smaller teams (Appendix F ). Both use ten agents for the same time, so the extra gain must come from what agents share. Sharing discoveries. To see what agents share, we analyzed the forum posts of our team runs (Appendix I.1 ). Agents mostly report what they tried and the score it reached. From the first hour, they mark these reports with short tags such as win and fail that make them quick to scan (Figure 15 ). Useful habits spread without assigned roles. On Borden Source Inversion 88% of teammates start announcing experiments before running them once one agent does (Figure 13 ). Agents also build on each other’s work. In one run six of eight teammates resubmitted a peer’s new best solution within ten minutes. Agents agree with a named teammate more than three times as often as they disagree (Figure 16 ). The same copying pulls a team toward one solution, which helps a small team but makes a large team converge (Section 3.1 ). A manager hurts. In our flat teams no agent manages the others (Section 2 ). To test this choice, we replaced the forum with an orchestrator. One agent assigns work to the others and collects their reports while the workers never hear from each other. These orchestrated teams stay below both flat teams and independent agents at every budget on Borden Source Inversion, Graph Node Classification and KDA Chunk Forward (Figure 10 ). On Borden Source Inversion the best of ten orchestrated teams (68.4) scores below the average flat team (76.5). The orchestrator is a bottleneck because workers wait for it and share findings only through it. An orchestrated team costs as much as a flat team but has a lower mean, so it loses at every budget. 7 Related Work Scaling test-time compute. A classic way to spend more compute at inference is to draw many samples and let a trained verifier or a majority vote pick one ( Cobbe et al., 2021 ; Wang et al., 2023 ; Lightman et al., 2024 ; Li et al., 2024 ) . Brown et al. (2024) measured how coverage grows with the number of samples. Later work explains these curves and their limits ( Schaeffer et al., 2025 ; Stroebl et al., 2026 ; Li et al., 2026 ) , splits a budget across models, samples and search methods ( Snell et al., 2024 ; Wu et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2025 ) , and finds that without a verifier a simple majority vote beats more elaborate sequential schemes ( Wang et al., 2025 ) . In best-of- N N the samples are independent. Tree search and evolutionary program search let later samples build on earlier ones through a fixed search procedure ( Yao et al., 2023 ; Zhou et al., 2024 ; Romera-Paredes et al., 2024 ; Novikov et al., 2025 ) . Agents can now finish much longer tasks ( Wijk et al., 2025 ; Kwa et al., 2025 ) , but the return per token falls within one session ( Liu et al., 2026 ) . Figure 10 : A manager makes teams worse. Qwen3.8-27B parallel agents (blue), parallel flat teams of ten (orange) and parallel orchestrated teams of ten (purple), in which one agent assigns the work. Brackets give the difference from flat teams. We omit Dabic Gravity Inversion because its evaluator often crashed. Multi-agent systems. Most multi-agent systems give agents roles and route work between them ( Hong et al., 2024 ; Qian et al., 2024 ; Wu et al., 2023 ; Hu et al., 2024 ) , and fixed graphs of agents have grown past a thousand ( Qian et al., 2025 ) . Debate and Mixture-of-Agents combine several models on one answer ( Du et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2024 ) . Such systems often fail because of their design and weak verification ( Cemri et al., 2025 ) . Debate does not reliably beat simpler prompting ( Smit et al., 2024 ) . Evaluations that ignore cost reward needlessly complex agents ( Kapoor et al., 2024 ) . Our teams have no roles, manager or fixed graph. Team size and diversity. Growing a team of language model agents gives diminishing and sometimes negative returns. The performance of MacNet follows a logistic curve as agents are added ( Qian et al., 2025 ) , and the benefit of coordination ranges from large gains to large losses across tasks ( Kim et al., 2025 ) . Agents conform more to a larger majority ( Weng et al., 2025 ) , teams settle on a compromise below their best member ( Pappu et al., 2026 ) , and interaction shrinks the diversity of ideas ( Chen et al., 2026 ) . Studies of human groups find the same trade-off. Dense communication spreads good solutions quickly but ends exploration early ( Lazer and Friedman, 2007 ; Bernstein et al., 2018 ) . Large teams develop existing ideas while small teams find new ones ( Wu et al., 2019 ) . Splitting an organization into separate subgroups keeps its diversity ( Fang et al., 2010 ) . Parallel agent teams apply this idea to test-time compute and keep the best subgroup’s solution. Concurrent work. Park et al. (2026) let identical peers share verified progress through a common log. They compare one team of up to five with the best of as many independent agents. CORAL ( Qu et al., 2026 ) runs a few agents that share a memory on open-ended optimization problems. We sample many small teams at a fixed budget, compare them with one big team and explain why they win. 8 Conclusion and Discussion Best-of- N N over individual agents and one big team are the two ends of one family of ways to spend a budget of agents. Neither end scales well. The first draws many samples with a low mean, and the second draws a single sample and gives up the gain from variance. Parallel agent teams sit in between and beat both, and their advantage over parallel agents grows with the budget. More broadly, making each sample better through collaboration can be worth more than drawing more samples. A few runs of any sampling method give its mean and standard deviation, and Equation 4 then tells whether it is worth its cost. Our teams collaborate only through a simple forum, and better ways of working together should make each team stronger. Parallel agent teams offer a simple way to turn ever larger compute budgets into better results. Our study has limitations. The statistical model is only qualitative where many attempts fail or scores hit a ceiling. Team pools hold only 10 to 11 runs per task. Teams larger than ten ran on four tasks with one to five runs per size, and in teams of 64 and 100 agents the forum index listed only the newest posts and the unread ones. We use one agent harness and vary session length on one task. Picking the best team would become its own problem without a verifier. AI Use Statement In this work, we used generative AI tools for: 1. The mechanism holds everywhere: a team of ten has a higher mean than an agent on every task, by 0.5 ​ σ 0.5\sigma to 4.4 ​ σ 4.4\sigma . Where agent scores are far from normal, the fit fails in a consistent way: it overpredicts the best of 100 agents, on 9 of 12 tasks above the best score any agent reached, and so underestimates the teams’ gain. Appendix D Infrastructure and Scale Agents run the Codex CLI inside Modal sandboxes. Qwen3.8-27B is served by SGLang ( Zheng et al., 2024 ) on our own H200 GPUs behind an OpenAI-compatible endpoint, about ten concurrent agents per four-GPU engine, so a team never waits on a rate limit. Each agent has its own verifier: a judge sidecar for the EdgeBench tasks, a dedicated hardware-pinned H100 held for the whole run for the kernel tasks, a privileged virtual machine for the Browser Exploit task, and an end-of-run grade of the sandbox for the MLS-Bench tasks. The forum is one scale-to-zero service shared by every run; its transport failure rate was 0.04% with 60 concurrent agents and 0.19% with 250. In total the study covers more than 8,000 agent runs and about 95,000 agent-hours. Independent agents and small teams can run in batches, but one team of 100 agents on KDA Chunk Forward needs 100 sandboxes, 100 H100 judges and 40 H200 GPUs at once for 12 hours. We therefore ran single teams of 16 to 100 agents only on four tasks, with one to five runs per size: four or five runs of 16 and 32 agents on Dabic Gravity Inversion, Borden Source Inversion and Graph Node Classification, two runs of 50 agents on Dabic Gravity Inversion, Graph Node Classification and KDA Chunk Forward, and two runs each of 64 and 100 agents on all four tasks except a single graded run of 100 agents on KDA Chunk Forward. A forum of 64 or 100 agents holds thousands of posts, so its index listed the newest 100 titles and every unread post, while teams of up to ten saw the whole index. A run enters a pool only if it finished or ran to its budget; runs killed by infrastructure are dropped, a run with no valid submission scores zero, and runs that reached a known reward exploit on Kimi-Linear Decode and KDA Chunk Forward are excluded. Appendix E Forum Implementation The forum is a persistent board of titled posts served by one scale-to-zero service shared by every run. Inside each sandbox a small command-line tool exposes three verbs: post a titled note (a title of up to 120 characters and a body of up to 64,000, so a whole solution file fits), list the index of titles with unread posts marked, and read one post in full. The agent’s current best score is attached to each of its posts automatically. Agents learn about the forum from a single note appended to their initial prompt (Appendix K ). The note tells them how many peers they have, asks them to post clear wins and clear failures together with the exact change and the measured score, suggests scanning the index before starting a new experiment, and states that posting and reading are entirely their choice. The arm reported in the paper sends no reminders; a retired variant echoed a digest of new titles after every submission. Appendix G Longer Sessions Forming a team is one way to spend more compute on each attempt. The simplest alternative is to let one agent work longer. Section 5 compares the two on Graph Node Classification at equal cost: one agent for 12 hours uses the same 12 agent-hours as a team of two for 6 hours, or as two independent agents for 6 hours each. We did not run separate 6-hour experiments. Every run behind Figure 8 was scheduled for 12 hours, and we read each run’s best verified score at hour 6 and at hour 12. The 6-hour and 12-hour agents are the same 101 runs seen at two times, and the ten teams of two are forum runs read at hour 6. So our 6-hour agents were working to a 12-hour plan. An agent told it had only 6 hours might pace itself differently. The second six hours add little. The same agents improve from 52.0 to 52.7 on average, so at every budget 12-hour agents lose to plain best-of- N N over 6-hour agents, which get twice as many attempts for the same hours. A team of two beats a single 12-hour agent at the same cost, 57.4 against 52.7 on average. At this size the gain comes mostly from having a second attempt rather than from sharing: the better of two independent 6-hour agents scores about the same, 57.6. The least certain part is how far teams of two lead 6-hour agents at large budgets. In our runs they pull ahead at about 60 agent-hours and lead by 4.1 points at 120, 75.5 against 71.4. But there are only ten team runs, and at 120 agent-hours the team curve is simply the best of all ten. The normal fits in Figure 8 b give teams a smaller spread, 8.1 against 10.5 points, and predict a close race there, 69.8 for teams against 71.6 for 6-hour agents. I.1 How we read the forum Corpus and method. We read every graded forum run of Qwen3.8-27B except the Browser Exploit task (707 runs, 166,831 posts) and the DeepSeek V4.1 Flash forum runs (40 runs, 5,196 posts). To count how often agents do something, such as thank a teammate or reserve a cell, we search the posts for the words that express it. Searching for words is imprecise: “claim” also appears in “agent_7’s claim was wrong”, which reserves nothing. So for each behaviour we read a sample of the matching posts and narrowed the search until it caught the behaviour and not just the word. The counts below are still approximate. Where we say that agents actually did something, we checked it against the judge’s log of submissions or the forum’s record of which agent read which post. Coordination conventions. Figure 13 tracks four habits over the run: • Reservation : announcing an experiment config to try next ( claim ) and then the attempt itself ( fire ), so that no teammate tries the same one. • Banking : an agent resubmitting a teammate’s solution so that it also counts as its own record. • Thanks : thanking a teammate. • Acknowledgement : answering a teammate’s post with ack . We say a team has adopted a habit when at least three posts by at least two agents show it. Reservation is mostly a Borden Source Inversion habit: 86% of Borden Source Inversion teams adopt it and few others do (Kimi-Linear Decode 40%, every other task at most 20%), and once one Borden Source Inversion agent starts claiming, 88% of its teammates follow. Banking a teammate’s solution is adopted by 11% of teams overall and 61% of Borden Source Inversion teams, thanks by 71% and ack by 8%. Copying spreads fast: in one nine-agent Borden Source Inversion run, six of eight teammates resubmitted a peer’s newly found configuration within ten minutes of its post, and eight of nine finished on the same score. Claims mostly mean what they say, but not always: in the two Borden Source Inversion runs where each claim can be matched to the submissions, 43% reserve a cell nobody had tried and the claimer tries it within two minutes, while a third name a cell a teammate had already tried minutes earlier.
