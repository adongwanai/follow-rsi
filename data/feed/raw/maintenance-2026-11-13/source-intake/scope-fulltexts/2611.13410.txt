deviation across four evaluation runs at each checkpoint. 1 Introduction Large language model (LLM) agents call tools and interact with their
environments over multiple turns to complete tasks [ 52 , 49 , 42 , 47 , 24 ] . Repeated model
calls make inference cost a central deployment concern [ 40 , 54 , 22 ] , and low-bit
quantization offers a practical way to reduce it [ 9 , 44 , 23 , 21 , 14 , 7 ] .
However, quantized agents often fall short of their full-precision
counterparts in task success. Examining failed rollouts, we find that quantized agents overlook task
requirements or environmental conditions, producing environment transitions
that differ from those of successful full-precision references
( Sections D.1 and D.2 ). We refer to this mismatch as behavioral divergence . For example, an agent tries
to read a file before entering its directory, although the available reading
tool only accesses files in the current directory
( Figures 1 and 11 ).
In another task, an agent creates the requested files but leaves some empty
instead of writing the specified contents ( Figure 6 ). However, identifying and correcting these behavioral divergences remain
challenging. Offline training and distillation [ 5 , 19 , 8 , 46 ] rely on data collected independently of the student’s interactions and may
therefore miss the contexts in which it diverges. On-policy rollouts [ 13 , 30 , 1 ] address this coverage
gap, but may provide few correct responses in those contexts. In our BFCL V4
multi-turn diagnostic, the student’s unguided retries yield a correct action
at only 9.6% of divergent turns. 1 1 1 We report turn-level Pass@4, the
fraction of selected turns with at least one correct action in four attempts.
Each attempt starts from the same pre-turn history and environment state. Contribution. We introduce SteerDistill , a quantization-aware distillation (QAD)
method for LLM agents ( Figure 1 ). The key idea is to construct
and internalize teacher-guided behavioral corrections. During training, a
full-precision teacher identifies the task requirements and environmental
conditions overlooked by the student and provides targeted guidance for
choosing an appropriate action. The student generates and executes a revised
response, and the teacher refines its guidance using execution feedback. We
call this process closed-loop teacher steering . By distilling these
teacher-guided behavioral corrections, we train the student to take
appropriate actions without teacher guidance at inference. Two designs implement this approach.
(i) Teacher-steered trajectory revision constructs behavioral
corrections by revising failed student rollouts through closed-loop teacher
steering. We use successful teacher references to locate behavioral
divergences. At each divergence, the student revises its response from the
restored pre-error context, and locally verified corrections are retained
for distillation. In the same diagnostic, teacher steering raises turn-level
Pass@4 from 9.6% to 82.3%.
(ii) Internalization of steered behavior distills the locally
verified corrections while retaining full-precision teacher supervision at
other turns. At corrected turns, the student learns from the frozen steered
student’s predictions without receiving the guidance. Without teacher guidance at inference, SteerDistill achieves the strongest
quantized performance in our comparisons, with lower training cost and
shorter successful trajectories.

( Table 2 ). 2 Related work Capability recovery for low-bit LLM quantization. To preserve model capabilities under low-bit quantization, one line of work
improves the fidelity of quantized representations through weight
reconstruction, scaling, and rotation [ 9 , 23 , 44 , 35 , 2 , 25 ] .
Complementary training-based approaches recover task performance using
hard token targets [ 5 , 19 ] , soft
distributions from full-precision teachers [ 8 , 46 , 30 ] , or verifiable
rewards [ 13 , 30 ] .
For quantized LLM agents, however, the available training data (including
offline data and on-policy rollouts) may lack effective corrections in
contexts where the student overlooks task requirements or environmental
conditions.
To address this, we extend quantization-aware
distillation with closed-loop teacher steering to construct and internalize
behavioral corrections ( Sections 3.1 and 3.2 ). Teacher guidance and context distillation for LLM agents. Teacher-generated responses [ 39 , 20 , 26 ] and rollouts conditioned on privileged context [ 18 , 45 ] give students access to improved
training trajectories. To assess the value of such assistance,
FutureBridge-OPD [ 4 ] evaluates teacher interventions through
the distillation signals in subsequent student rollouts.
OVCSD [ 43 ] instead uses task success to verify privileged
continuations from student failure states, providing outcome-grounded
corrective supervision. Our closed-loop teacher steering instead evaluates
student corrections at the divergent turn, checking their environment
transitions against the successful reference and using feedback from failed
attempts to refine guidance.
For quantized agents, this local loop targets overlooked task requirements
and environmental conditions. We internalize
these corrections through context distillation, following prior work on
transferring the benefits of privileged information to unguided learners [ 53 , 11 ] . 3 Method SteerDistill constructs and internalizes behavioral corrections in each

The updated student generates the next cycle’s rollouts. 3.1 Teacher-steered trajectory revision For each failed student rollout, we seek a successful teacher reference for
the same task and initial environment state. This reference remains fixed
throughout revision. Successful student rollouts and failed rollouts
without a successful reference are retained unchanged for distillation. Localizing an intervention turn. We align the student and reference trajectories by their environment
transitions, using only environment states to determine a match. Actions
that produce matching transitions are treated as equivalent. We intervene
at the first behavioral divergence, denoting the student turn by t ⋆ t^{\star} ( Table 2 , Q1). To revise this decision in its original
context, we restore the history and environment state before t ⋆ t^{\star} . Closed-loop teacher steering. The teacher uses the failed response and successful reference to identify
task requirements or environmental conditions overlooked by the student
( Figure 2 , i).
It provides a steering prompt G t ⋆ G_{t^{\star}} that explains the discrepancy,
the conditions under which the guidance applies, and a corrective rule
( Table 2 , Q3). Conditioned on G t ⋆ G_{t^{\star}} , the frozen
student generates a revised response y t ⋆ ∼ π θ k ( ⋅ ∣ h t ⋆ , G t ⋆ ) y_{t^{\star}}\sim\pi_{\theta_{k}}(\cdot\mid h_{t^{\star}},G_{t^{\star}}) and executes its action. We then locally verify the revised response by comparing the resulting
environment states and returned information with those of the reference.
If verification fails, the teacher refines its guidance using feedback from
the attempt, and the student retries from the same pre-error history and
state. The loop continues until a correction is locally verified or the
per-turn attempt budget is exhausted. We compare teacher steering with
unguided retries and teacher takeover in Table 2 (Q2). Retaining verified corrections. Once a revised response passes local verification, we retain it and its
steering prompt for distillation. The response replaces the failed turn,
and the student generates a new
continuation without guidance. If the task still fails, we realign the
revised rollout with the fixed reference to locate the next behavioral
divergence. Revision stops when the task succeeds, no further divergence
can be located, or an intervention exhausts its attempt budget. The current
trajectory and its verified corrections are retained for distillation even
if the task remains unsolved. Table 2 (Q4) evaluates both
local verification and retaining corrections without requiring terminal success. Algorithm 1 and implementation details
appear in Appendix A . 3.2 Internalization of steered behavior We train the student on the retained trajectories using two sources of

points over takeover. Q3 (Steering format and source): Use structured steering prompts generated by the teacher. We compare two formats for the teacher’s steering prompts: structured
prompts that separately state the discrepancy, applicable conditions, and
corrective rule, and free-form prompts without a prescribed structure.
With the intervention turn, reference, and verification fixed, the
structured format gains 2.06 points. This result is consistent with prior
findings on the benefits of specific, actionable feedback and feedback
organized around explicit requirements [ 27 , 6 ] . Keeping the
structured format and reference fixed, we obtain gains of 5.10 points over
the student and 1.57 points over GPT-5.6-Sol [ 31 ] by using
the full-precision teacher as the prompt source. Section D.3 compares the resulting prompts and student
continuations in a ticket-creation task. Q4 (Revision retention): Retain locally verified corrections even when the task fails. Removing local verification substantially lowers the score from 46.84 to
42.18. Once corrections are locally verified, additionally requiring
terminal task success yields a slightly lower score of 46.30. This filter
discards verified corrections from unsuccessful trajectories without
improving performance. Q5 (Distillation routing): Use forward KL at corrected turns and reverse KL elsewhere. We keep the retained trajectories, target models, and input contexts fixed
and vary only the KL direction. Using forward KL (FKL) at corrected turns
and reverse KL (RKL) elsewhere gains 1.55 points over RKL throughout and
2.26 points over FKL throughout. Reversing this assignment lowers the
score by 2.56 points. 5 Conclusion SteerDistill recovers the agentic capabilities of quantized LLMs through
closed-loop teacher steering and distillation. By targeting the task
requirements and environmental conditions overlooked at behavioral
divergences, the teacher guides the student to generate its own corrections
and refines its guidance using execution feedback. Distillation internalizes
the locally verified behavioral corrections for unassisted inference.
Across five benchmarks and three models, SteerDistill improves over quantized
baselines under W4A16 and stays within one point of BF16 in average score.
On τ 2 \tau^{2} -Bench, it outperforms QeRL by 1.84 percentage points in success
rate using 67% fewer training GPU hours and produces shorter successful

We align student and reference trajectories using environment states to
match their transitions. In AgentWorldModel, state is represented by
task-relevant database contents. ALFWorld uses world facts and available
actions. WebShop uses product selections, page type, and available choices.
Search QA uses retrieved evidence as its information state. Matching state transitions can result from different actions and occur at
different turn indices. We select the first divergent student turn t ⋆ t^{\star} for intervention and use the corresponding reference transition ρ T \rho^{T} to verify candidate revisions. Local verification. Local verification determines whether a candidate response can replace the
divergent turn. In AgentWorldModel, we require the database states before
and after execution to match those of the reference and reject responses
that cause environment errors. Read operations must also return matching
information. For a terminal response, we require the reference to terminate
as well and the candidate to pass the task verifier. In ALFWorld, WebShop, and Search QA, we accept a valid candidate if its
resulting environment state matches the reference or if both executions
achieve task success. This allows successful executions to end in different
states. Verification returns an acceptance flag v v and a report f f with
the result and, on rejection, the failure category used to refine steering. Steering prompts and feedback. The teacher forms a steering prompt G G from the aligned discrepancy and
memory ℳ \mathcal{M} . This memory stores the latest accepted steering prompt
within the current trajectory, allowing the teacher to incorporate earlier
guidance that remains applicable. The new prompt explains the discrepancy,
the conditions under which its corrective rule applies, the expected
transition, and how to verify it. We allow up to R R attempts at each intervention. Before each attempt, we
restore the same pre-error history and environment state. The frozen
student then generates a response conditioned on G G and executes its
action. If local verification rejects the attempt, we append its prompt,
candidate assistant content and tool calls, termination status, and
verification report f f to the feedback history F F . The teacher uses
summaries of F F , together with the original failed rollout, fixed
reference, and memory ℳ \mathcal{M} , to refine G G for another attempt.
Feedback history F F is reset at each new intervention, while memory ℳ \mathcal{M} carries accepted guidance across interventions. Continuation and retained data. Once a response passes local verification, it replaces the response at t ⋆ t^{\star} , and the old suffix is discarded. We remove correction records
at or after t ⋆ t^{\star} from ℐ \mathcal{I} and 𝒢 \mathcal{G} , then record the
corrected turn in ℐ \mathcal{I} and its steering prompt in 𝒢 \mathcal{G} .
This prompt also becomes the new memory ℳ \mathcal{M} . From the resulting
environment state and observation, the student generates a new continuation
without guidance. If the task still fails, we realign the revised trajectory
with the fixed reference to locate the next behavioral divergence. Revision stops when the task succeeds, all R R attempts at an intervention
fail local verification, or no further divergence can be aligned with the
reference. In all cases, we retain the current trajectory together with ℐ \mathcal{I} and 𝒢 \mathcal{G} for distillation, including accepted corrections in trajectories
that remain unsuccessful. If no correction was accepted, we retain the
original student rollout. A.2 Internalization of steered behavior The retained trajectories provide the histories and response tokens used
for distillation. At turns in ℐ \mathcal{I} , we use the corresponding prompts
in 𝒢 \mathcal{G} to compute targets from the steered student. At all other
turns, targets come from the full-precision teacher. The learner receives
no steering prompts in either case. Quantized generation and optimization. The teacher and student start from the same BF16 checkpoint. The teacher
stays frozen, while the student maintains trainable BF16 master weights.
In cycle k k , we use a frozen snapshot θ k \theta_{k} in the low-bit runtime
for rollout generation and revision. During distillation, we recompute the FKL target π θ k ( ⋅ ∣ c , G ) \pi_{\theta_{k}}(\cdot\mid c,G) at corrected turns using a frozen
fake-quantized copy of the same snapshot and the stored steering prompt G G . At all other turns, the full-precision teacher supplies the RKL target π T ( ⋅ ∣ c ) \pi_{T}(\cdot\mid c) . For both targets, c c is the same retained history
and response prefix used by the learner. The learner π θ ( ⋅ ∣ c ) \pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c) uses fake quantization in its forward
pass, with gradients propagated to its BF16 master weights through the
straight-through estimator. The updated master weights form snapshot θ k + 1 \theta_{k+1} , which is quantized for rollout generation and revision in
the next cycle. Loss computation and masking. For each retained assistant turn, we compute loss only on its response
tokens. These include assistant text and serialized tool calls, as well
as reasoning and termination tokens when present. System and user
messages, environment observations, and steering prompts are excluded
from the loss. Discarded responses and suffixes are excluded from training. We reuse the token IDs recorded during generation when available.
Otherwise, we tokenize the retained response and append an EOS token
unless generation was truncated by length. At each supervised response-token position, we compute KL over the full
vocabulary, using FKL for corrected turns and RKL for all other turns.
This assignment also applies to regenerated continuations. The objective
in Equation 3 averages over all supervised response
tokens, so corrected and other turns are weighted by their respective
token counts. Table 3 : Key notation for SteerDistill. The revision algorithm handles one

source affect the guidance the student receives
( Tables 2 and 12 ).
Although the trading session is already authenticated, creating a ticket
requires a separate login. Structured prompts from both the teacher and the
student identify this prerequisite, and both continuations create the
ticket after login. The free-form teacher prompt instead claims that the
ticket already exists; the student logs in but stops without creating one. The GPT-generated structured prompt also gives the correct login order and
instructs the student to keep credentials out of the ticket description.
The student logs in before creating the ticket but still includes the
credentials in its description. This partial adherence illustrates why
closed-loop teacher steering uses execution feedback to assess whether the
intended behavioral correction has occurred. Figure 12 : Guidance content and student execution in ticket creation.

from a login error. D.4 Recovery cases These cases illustrate an emergent behavior beyond the corrections

restoration of the pre-error history and environment state, and reliable
local verification. Without a successful reference, a failed rollout is
retained for distillation but receives no teacher-steered corrections.
Applicability to environments without state restoration or reliable local
verification remains untested. Moreover, local verification checks a revised
response’s effect in the current context; it does not guarantee eventual
task success or establish the correctness of every reasoning step in the
