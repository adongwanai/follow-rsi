Evaluating Visual Interaction Models with Simulated Users in Interactive Worlds Chenhao Zheng Zixian Ma Kai Li Kanchana Ranasinghe Jieyu Zhang Artemis Panagopoulou Weikai Huang Jianxu Shangguan Yuanyuan Tan Silvio Savarese Juan Carlos Niebles Ranjay Krishna Ran Xu Honglu Zhou Abstract Visual interaction models that support users during ongoing physical tasks require evaluation in closed loop, where assistant responses can change subsequent user behavior and task outcomes. Existing benchmarks largely rely on offline replay and therefore cannot capture these effects. We introduce Interactive Visual Gym , a framework for closed-loop evaluation and training of visual interaction models through joint simulation of executable task environments and human users. We contribute three simulators that recreate everyday tasks and human-aligned action spaces across cooking, household manipulation, and mobile-interface domains, together with a persona-conditioned user simulation harness that collaborates with the assistant while incorporating controlled execution errors and other out-of-plan behaviors. We instantiate the framework as IVG-Bench , a benchmark with 315 interaction scenarios. Evaluating state-of-the-art VLMs as visual interaction models reveals substantial limitations: the best-performing model, Gemini 4 Argon, completes only 53.4% of tasks within the time limit, exhibits significant weaknesses in factual grounding and actionable guidance, and is often too slow to prevent errors when response latency is considered.
We verify our evaluation protocol with a human study which finds 78.5% agreement between our interaction-quality metric and participant preferences.
We further show that the environments provide useful learning signals: in a proof-of-concept GRPO experiment, closed-loop training improves both task success and interaction quality for Qwen3.5-4B model in our cooking environment. Chenhao Zheng 1,2 Zixian Ma 1 Kai Li 2 Kanchana Ranasinghe 2 Jieyu Zhang 1 Artemis Panagopoulou 2 Weikai Huang 1,2 Jianxu Shangguan 1 Yuanyuan Tan Silvio Savarese 2 Juan Carlos Niebles 2 Ranjay Krishna 1 Ran Xu 2 Honglu Zhou 2 1 University of Washington 2 Salesforce AI Research Project page: https://interactive-visual-gym.salesforceairesearch.com Code: https://github.com/SalesforceAIResearch/Interactive-Visual-Gym 1 Introduction Building systems that collaborate effectively with people is a longstanding goal of interactive machine learning ( Amershi et al., 2014 ) . Recent advances in LLM agents have enabled collaboration on digital tasks, including web research and software development, through dialogue, preference adaptation, and coordinated action ( Shao et al., 2026 ; Sun et al., 2026 ) . More recently, extending such collaboration to everyday physical tasks has drawn growing interest, motivating visual interaction models that observe a video stream of a user’s ongoing activities and provide guidance ( Cui et al., 2026 ; Thinking Machines Lab, 2026 ; Yao et al., 2026 ) . For example, an assistant monitoring a procedural physical task like cooking can help plan the next step, detect execution errors, and guide recovery if a mistake happens. Developing and evaluating interactive agents requires closed-loop rollouts that capture how each interaction changes subsequent user behavior and environment state, enabling assessment of its contribution to task completion. For digital agents, existing websites and software provide readily available tasks and executable environments, which can be paired with simulated users to support such rollouts ( Zhou et al., 2024 ; Yao et al., 2024 ; Shao et al., 2026 ) . Building an equivalent testbed for visual assistance is substantially more difficult. First, physical tasks and their environments are not readily available in a digital form; they must be reconstructed with visual observations, underlying world state, and an action space that supports human task execution. Second, visual assistance places substantially greater demands on user simulation. In most digital settings, the model executes actions while the user primarily clarifies goals and preferences. In physical activities, the user is the acting agent, and the model influences execution through communication. The simulated user must plan, act, make plausible mistakes, while interpreting guidance and deciding how to respond. These simulation challenges have limited visual interaction model training and evaluation to replaying recorded interactions and matching reference responses ( Liu et al., 2026 ; Kundu et al., 2026 ; Ran et al., 2026 ) , where the user’s actions and responses remain unchanged regardless of the assistant’s guidance. We present Interactive Visual Gym ,
a framework for closed-loop evaluation of visual interaction models through joint simulation of task environments and human users. Our framework has three components: First, we recreate everyday tasks across cooking, household manipulation, and mobile interface use in executable visual environments. These environments render visual observations from the user’s perspective. They use scene graphs to represent world state and track task progress independently of variations in action execution. They expose a human-aligned semantic action space for simulated users to operate in, translating instructions such as “put the pan on the stove” into executable operations. Second, we design a harness that turns a state-of-the-art VLM into simulated users that plan and act within these environments while interacting with the assistant and revising their actions in response to its guidance. The harness supports controlled insertion of common human out-of-plan events at specified task states, including repeating, forgetting, or incorrectly executing a step, changing plans, and asking questions. We further model diverse user personas through attributes such as skill level, trust in AI, and interruption preference. These attributes shape how users perform tasks and respond to guidance differently, enabling evaluation of the assistant’s adaptation to different users. Third, simulation provides execution traces that support comprehensive evaluation of an assistant, covering task completion, error detection, and interaction quality. This captures how assistance affects task execution and user experience, which offline replay cannot assess. Figure 1: Interactive Visual Gym. In each episode, the assistant is tasked to support a simulated user with a sampled persona through task execution, and needs to deal with a set of scheduled out-of-plan events.

The best-performing model, Gemini 4 Argon, completes only 53.4% of tasks within the time limit, averaged across the three environments, and also shows severe deficits in factual grounding and actionable guidance, revealing a substantial gap toward reliable interactive assistance. Finally, we demonstrate that CookSim can support end-to-end reinforcement learning of multimodal assistants through a proof-of-concept GRPO experiment with Qwen3.5-4B ( Qwen Team, 2026a ) as the assistant and Gemini 3.7 Flash ( Google, 2026b ) as the simulated novice user. After 12 RL updates, the assistant’s mean in-time task success rate improves by 8.9 percentage points on held-out development-validation scenarios with a Gemini 3.7 Flash user, and by 3.3 percentage points in a separate evaluation with a Gemini 3.8 Flash user ( Tab. 5 ). Despite limited tasks and training updates, these results show that CookSim can supply a learning signal and induce policy improvements in interactive task performance. 2 Related Work Streaming visual assistance. Recent interaction models continuously process multimodal streams and support low-latency, proactive, or full-duplex responses ( Cui et al., 2026 ; Yao et al., 2026 ; Orantqing et al., 2026 ) .
Live guidance has also been studied through user-in-the-loop cooking assistance ( Verghese et al., 2024 ) and workspace-conditioned visual instructions ( Wu et al., 2026 ) .
Complementary datasets and benchmarks study procedural guidance, mistake recovery, correct silence, and response timing in egocentric or general video streams ( Zhang et al., 2025a ; Liu et al., 2026 ; Kundu et al., 2026 ; Bhattacharyya et al., 2026 ; Chatterjee et al., 2026 ; Vasu et al., 2026 ) .
In these benchmarks a candidate model is typically scored against a prerecorded trajectory.
Our framework instead evaluates response-contingent rollouts. Interactive agent environments. Embodied benchmarks place agents in simulated worlds for navigation, manipulation, and collaboration ( Shridhar et al., 2020 ; Puig et al., 2018 ; Li et al., 2022 ; Padmakumar et al., 2022 ; Puig et al., 2024 ; Chang et al., 2025 ) , and recent multimodal gyms unify diverse visual tasks for evaluation and reinforcement learning ( Wang et al., 2026 ; Meng et al., 2026 ) or study which environment mixtures best train visual agents ( Zhu et al., 2026 ) ; in all of these, the evaluated model selects the actions itself.
Digital interaction benchmarks instead pair tool-using agents with simulated users and verify outcomes from application state ( Yao et al., 2024 ; Barres et al., 2025 ; Shao et al., 2026 ) , which τ \tau -Voice extends to voice interaction ( Ray et al., 2026 ) .
BASIS trains and evaluates visual interaction models through simulated user interactions in Alexa Arena ( Zhang et al., 2025b ) .
User simulation has long enabled scalable dialogue evaluation ( Schatzmann et al., 2005 ) , and language models now support persona-conditioned users ( Aher et al., 2023 ) , but simulated users can diverge systematically from people, requesting validation ( Seshadri et al., 2026 ) . everyday human communication. 3 Method Our framework combines executable physical task environments with simulated human users to evaluate visual interaction models. We first describe how these environments reconstruct everyday tasks, expose human-communicable actions, and track task progress. We then introduce the human-agent harness and its interaction with the assistant, followed by the evaluation metrics. 3.1 Executable Physical Task Environments Simulating everyday tasks. We reconstruct procedural physical tasks in three domains ( Fig. 2 ): Cooking. Inspired by Overcooked ( Ghost Town Games, 2016 ) , we build CookSim from scratch, with recipes specifying ingredient quantities, preparation requirements, and plating order where applicable. Users navigate kitchens with distributed ingredients and tools while accomplishing the cooking task. For example, preparing a cheeseburger requires cooking a patty and plating the bun, patty, and cheese in that order. The simulator is implemented in Three.js/WebGL and supports top-down and egocentric 3D rendering. Household activities. We adapt VirtualHome ( Puig et al., 2018 ) for everyday apartment tasks combining navigation and object manipulation. For example, cleaning up after a meal requires loading plates and a mug into the dishwasher, returning an apple to the fridge, and closing both appliances. The simulator uses VirtualHome’s Unity backend for egocentric 3D rendering, while providing a top-down trajectory map. Mobile interface use. ScreenSim replicates the iPhone 17 Settings interface, with multi-step configuration tasks derived from Apple’s user guides ( Apple, 2026 ) . For example, restricting an app’s access requires disabling its location permission, cross-app tracking, and background refresh while leaving other apps unchanged. The simulator is implemented in HTML/CSS rendered in Chromium and captures mobile-screen observations through Playwright. Human-communicable action space. Existing physical-task simulators expose simulator-specific movement and interaction primitives, such as movement increments or object identifiers ( Li et al., 2022 ; Puig et al., 2018 ; Fan et al., 2022 ) . People typically communicate at a more abstract semantic level, referring to objects and intended operations. We therefore build a semantic action space for each environment ( Fig. 2 ), with commands such as “go to the nearest pan” in CookSim, “place the book on the table” in VHSim, and “select Background App Refresh” in ScreenSim. During execution, an LLM-based grounding module resolves contextual references, such as “the pan on your left,” into object identifiers recognized by the simulator. The simulator then expands each high-level action into a sequence of low-level controls that update the world state and generate visual observations. Progress tracking via scene-graph state. Each simulator represents world state as a scene graph with nodes encoding objects and attributes, such as a dishwasher’s open or closed state, and edges encoding containment and support relations. We decompose each task into sub-goals defined by conditions over partial graph state, such as the designated dishes being inside the dishwasher. Tracking these conditions measures task progress independently of different valid execution procedures. 3.2 User–Assistant Interaction The visual interaction model under evaluation is tasked with guiding a simulated user through tasks in the environments described above. We implement the
user as a VLM agent with access to structured world-state information, execution feedback, and conversation history. The VLM is prompted to role-play a human user to test the assistant’s interaction capabilities, planning and acting independently while responding to guidance according
to its persona. A user-simulation harness conditions its behavior through persona instructions and temporarily takes control of its actions
to enact scheduled out-of-plan events. Below, we describe the action and communication interfaces, account for assistant response delays, and
detail these mechanisms for controlling user behavior. Action interface: user–environment. The simulated user maintains an action queue that the engine executes at each simulation tick and can revise in response to environment changes or assistant suggestions. The engine updates the world state and renders the resulting observation: s t + 1 = T ⁡ ( s t , a t ) , o t + 1 = R ⁡ ( s t + 1 ) , s_{t+1}=T(s_{t},a_{t}),\qquad o_{t+1}=R(s_{t+1}), (1) where a t a_{t} is the action executed from the user’s queue, T T is the state-transition, and R R is the renderer. Communication interface: user–assistant. The assistant monitors sampled frames from the user’s observation stream alongside the conversation history. At an assistant invocation at tick t t , it generates a response m t = A ⁡ ( o ≤ t , c ≤ t ) , m_{t}=A(o_{\leq t},c_{\leq t}), (2) where o ≤ t o_{\leq t} denotes the sampled visual history and c ≤ t c_{\leq t} is the conversation history. The response m t m_{t} contains an utterance or silence in text format and an optional event-type flag ( Tab. 1 (A)), which is enabled when the assistant detects a user execution error. On each user turn, the simulated user likewise produces an utterance or remains silent. Its conversational response types include asking for an explanation, questioning the correctness of guidance, requesting more guidance or fewer interruptions, accepting or rejecting a suggestion, and answering a question. Accounting for assistant delay. Assistant processing delay can substantially affect interaction quality: by the time a response arrives, the user and environment may have
progressed beyond the observed state, rendering the guidance outdated or invalid. Following the separation of simulation time and wall-clock
time in τ \tau -Voice ( Ray et al., 2026 ) , we assign each simulation tick a duration Δ \Delta based on approximate real-world action
speeds. For example, VirtualHome is configured to move the user one meter per simulation tick. Assuming this distance takes 1.2 seconds to
walk in the real world, we assign each tick a duration of Δ = 1.2 \Delta=1.2 seconds.
We convert the measured assistant inference latency ℓ t \ell_{t} into a delay of d t = ⌈ ℓ t / Δ ⌉ d_{t}=\lceil\ell_{t}/\Delta\rceil simulation ticks. The response m t m_{t} , generated from observations through tick t t , is delivered at tick t + d t t+d_{t} .
During this interval, the engine continues
executing the user’s queued actions and updating the world state. Scheduled out-of-plan events. We introduce scheduled out-of-plan events in all three simulators that require the assistant to respond to execution errors, questions and intended plan change. For example, the event types in CookSim are shown in Tab. 1 (A): we simulate common cooking errors of incorrect preparation, omitted or improperly sequenced steps, and ill-timed actions ( Peddi et al., 2024 ) ; we generate temporal questions adapted from streaming-video benchmarks ( Niu et al., 2025 ; Huang et al., 2025 ; Lin et al., 2026 ) and proactive requests for timely assistance ( Zhang et al., 2025a ) ; we also introduce intended plan changes, where users may announce revised goals or a side task during execution.
Each event is triggered by a specified
condition on task progress. When an event is triggered, the harness temporarily takes control of the user’s action queue to enforce its execution.
It privately informs the user agent of the ongoing event and explains that the event is intended to test the assistant, instructing the user agent to adapt its conversation accordingly. For mistake events, the user agent judges whether the assistant has correctly identified the mistake and decides whether to accept its guidance. Once the guidance is accepted, the harness returns control of the action queue to the user agent. Persona-conditioned users. Real-world users differ in how they perform tasks and respond to assistance. Drawing on HCI and related behavioral research, we operationalize this diversity through four representative axes: skill level ( Shneiderman, 2000 ) , trust in AI ( Lee & See, 2004 ) , execution speed ( Wickelgren, 1977 ) , and interruption preference ( McFarlane, 2002 ) ( Tab. 1 (B)). The harness instantiates each persona through axis-specific instructions in the user model’s context. For example, high-skill users are prompted to plan ahead independently, while low-skill users are prompted to ask assistant frequently to determine the next steps. Users who prefer minimal interruption are prompted to find unnecessary commentary disruptive, whereas those who benefit from step-by-step support prefer more frequent guidance. The execution speed and interruption preference are shared to assistant as part of announced user preference, while the skill level and trust in AI are treated as internal property of a user for assistants to infer and adapt. axes, each with three levels, simulate how different simulated users perform tasks and respond to guidance differently . 3.3 Evaluation Metrics Our simulation rollouts allow us to measure task completion under assistance and assess individual responses against the underlying world state. We evaluate the assistants with the following metrics: In-time success. In-time success measures the proportion of episodes whose engine-verified goal is satisfied within a completion budget of 2 ​ ( T i ref + α i ) 2(T_{i}^{\mathrm{ref}}+\alpha_{i}) , where T i ref T_{i}^{\mathrm{ref}} is the duration of an engine-generated, error-free reference rollout and α i \alpha_{i} is the prescribed event allowance. Environment-specific budgets are detailed in Appendix D . Error detection. Detection recall is the proportion of triggered errors correctly identified within their eligible detection windows, credited when
the assistant raises a correct event flag verified by the engine or when the simulated user judges its diagnosis correct. For the latency
ablation, we also report error prevention , the proportion of triggered errors prevented before the point of no return. Prevention
requires both timely identification and guidance that persuades the user to change course. Interaction quality. We evaluate each assistant response against the underlying world state, rather than against a reference response as in prior works. We
define five dimensions of evaluation informed by dialogue evaluation ( Mehri & Eskenazi, 2020 ) and HCI guidance on context-sensitive, user-controllable assistance ( Amershi et al., 2019 ) : factual grounding, situational relevance,
actionable guidance, user intent uptake, and guidance
conciseness. We operationalize these dimensions as binary rubric items evaluated by two LLM judges ( Liu et al., 2023 ; Lee et al., 2025 ) .
For
each conversational round, each judge receives engine state, user preferences, conversation history, and subsequent user actions,
returning evidence-supported pass, fail, or not-applicable judgments of each rubrics. A category passes only when all applicable items
pass; each judge’s overall score averages applicable categories, gated by its factual-grounding judgment. We average the two judges’ round scores before aggregating across rounds and runs. Judge prompts and score aggregation are detailed in Appendix I . Question answering and proactive requests. The engine pairs each generated question with a grading procedure that derives reference facts from world state and execution history. A semantic judge assigns correctness against these facts. For proactive requests, the engine derives an acceptable notification window from the relevant state transitions. 4 Experiments We use our engine to instantiate IVG-Bench benchmark for evaluating state-of-the-art VLMs as visual interaction models ( Sec. 4.1 ), and validate our evaluation paradigm through a human study ( Sec. 4.2 ). We further present a preliminary reinforcement learning experiment showing that our engine can support training visual interaction models and improve their interaction performance ( Sec. 4.3 ). 4.1 IVG-Bench: Benchmarking Visual Interaction Models in Interactive Visual Gym IVG-Bench comprises 315 interaction scenarios: 150 in CookSim, 75 in VHSim, and 90 in ScreenSim. Each case specifies a task, a user persona, and scheduled out-of-plan events triggered by task-progress conditions. Below we detail the setting for evaluated visual interaction models and show evaluation results. 4.1.1 Visual Interaction Model Setting Models. We evaluate four classes of assistants: (1) frontier API models: GPT-6 Astra ( OpenAI, 2026a ) , GPT-6.1 Sol ( OpenAI, 2026c ) , Gemini 3.8 Flash ( Google, 2026c ) , and Gemini 4 Argon ( Kavukcuoglu, 2026 ) ; (2) real-time streaming API models: Gemini 3.8 Live ( Google, 2026d ) and GPT-Realtime 2.1 ( OpenAI, 2026b ) ; (3) open-weight non-streaming VLMs: Qwen3.8-27B ( Qwen Team, 2026b ) , Molmo2-8B ( Clark et al., 2026 ) , InternVideo3-8B ( Yan et al., 2026b ) , Kimi-VL-A3B-Thinking ( Kimi Team et al., 2025 ) , and Muse Glimmer ( Meta, 2026 ) ; and (4) open-weight streaming VLMs: MiniCPM-o 4.5 ( Cui et al., 2026 ) , Qwen3-Omni-30B ( Xu et al., 2025a ) , Proact-VL ( Yan et al., 2026a ) , and StreamingVLM ( Xu et al., 2025b ) . We use Gemini 3.7 Flash as the simulated user in CookSim and ScreenSim, and Gemini 3.1 Pro ( Google, 2026a ) in VHSim. We examine sensitivity to the user model in Appendix A . Inference. Assistants observe the user’s ongoing activity through visual observations and conversation, without access to the scheduled events or the user’s private action plan. In CookSim and VHSim, we query assistants every two simulation ticks, including real-time API models in a polled setting. Streaming models retain context within their sessions. We adapt non-streaming models with a sliding window of the eight most recent observations and a recurrent, text-based Plan–Act memory. This follows the use of persistent procedural plans for visual assistance in Kundu et al. (2026) . Each open-weight model is served on an H100 GPU in CookSim and VHSim. ScreenSim settings are detailed in Appendix H . Response timing. We use fixed response timing for the main comparison: two simulation ticks for CookSim and VHSim’s streaming interactions, and zero ticks for ScreenSim’s action–response interactions. We separately evaluate the effect of measured assistant latency in CookSim and VHSim. Each setting uses three independent rollouts per benchmark scenario. 4.1.2 Quantitative Results We report task success, error detection, and interaction quality across the three environments in Tab. 2 and Fig. 3 . Even the strongest VLMs fall short of reliable visual assistance. Gemini 4 Argon achieves the highest task success, but completes only 53.4% of tasks within the time limit, averaged across the three environments. Moreover, Gemini 4 Argon trails Astra, Sol, and Gemini Flash in interaction quality, with notable limitations in factual grounding and actionable guidance. Open-weight assistants generally fall short of proprietary models on both task success and interaction quality. The strongest streaming models remain competitive in interaction quality but lag in task success. Gemini Live achieves high average interaction quality, yet completes fewer tasks than Astra and Gemini Flash. This pattern may reflect a trade-off between low-latency inference and high planning and reasoning ability. Higher task success does not necessarily imply better interaction quality. Although the two measures are positively associated ( Fig. 3 ), task outcomes alone can hide weak assistance. For example, Muse Glimmer completes more tasks than Astra in CookSim while receiving much lower interaction-quality scores. Completing the task is therefore not sufficient evidence of high quality guidance. Temporal question answering is surprisingly difficult during task-directed interaction. These fixed-scenario results show that Interactive Visual Gym supplies a useful learning signal for interactive assistance, paving the foundation for future work to demonstrate generalization across tasks, user models, or domains. 5 Conclusion We present Interactive Visual Gym, a framework for closed-loop evaluation and training of visual interaction models through joint simulation of task environments and human users. Across three
domains, we find substantial limitations in current VLM to serve as reliable assistants. We also validated our interaction paradigm with human study. Finally, a preliminary reinforcement-learning experiment shows that the environments can improve assistant performance. Appendix D Task Success and Error Detection We use the simulator’s execution records to determine task completion and whether an assistant detects or prevents scheduled errors. These metrics complement response-level interaction quality by measuring the effect of assistance on task execution. D.1 In-time success For episode i i , let T i T_{i} be its duration and T i ref T_{i}^{\mathrm{ref}} the duration of an engine-generated reference rollout that completes the task without errors. CookSim and VHSim define the completion budget and success indicator as D i = 2 ( T i ref + α i ) , S i = 𝟏 [ G i ( s i final ) = 1 ∧ T i ≤ D i ] , D_{i}=2(T_{i}^{\mathrm{ref}}+\alpha_{i}),\qquad S_{i}=\mathbf{1}[G_{i}(s_{i}^{\mathrm{final}})=1\ \land\ T_{i}\leq D_{i}], (4) where G i G_{i} checks the task goal against the final world state and α i \alpha_{i} allows additional time for scheduled events. CookSim uses case-specific allowances, while VHSim uses 20 ticks. ScreenSim uses D i = 2 ​ T i ref + 8 D_{i}=2T_{i}^{\mathrm{ref}}+8 . Reference durations are obtained from the corresponding error-free executions. CookSim checks the served dish’s ingredients, preparation states, and assembly order. VHSim checks the required object states and relations. ScreenSim checks the requested settings while requiring unrelated settings to remain unchanged; temporary search text is excluded. An episode succeeds only if it satisfies both the goal and the completion budget. We report the percentage of successful episodes in each run and average these rates across runs. D.2 Detection and prevention Detection recall measures the proportion of triggered errors correctly identified within their eligible detection windows. Error prevention measures the proportion stopped before the erroneous action becomes irreversible: R = ∑ i N i detected ∑ i N i triggered , P prevent = ∑ i N i prevented ∑ i N i triggered . R=\frac{\sum_{i}N_{i}^{\mathrm{detected}}}{\sum_{i}N_{i}^{\mathrm{triggered}}},\qquad P_{\mathrm{prevent}}=\frac{\sum_{i}N_{i}^{\mathrm{prevented}}}{\sum_{i}N_{i}^{\mathrm{triggered}}}. (5) Here, N i triggered N_{i}^{\mathrm{triggered}} , N i detected N_{i}^{\mathrm{detected}} , and N i prevented N_{i}^{\mathrm{prevented}} count the corresponding errors in episode i i . We credit identification when the assistant raises an event flag accepted by the engine’s event-specific grader or when the simulated user recognizes a correct diagnosis, as described in Sec. 3.3 . Prevention additionally requires the user to accept the guidance and change course before the point of no return. Events whose trigger conditions are never reached are excluded from these rates. Appendix E Temporal Questions and Proactive Requests We evaluate temporal question answering and proactive assistance during ongoing task execution. This section describes how CookSim generates these events, determines when to introduce them, and grades responses against the resulting interaction. E.1 Question generation and scheduling We instantiate templates for temporal order, action counting, duration, and event recall, adapting temporal-understanding tasks from streaming-video benchmarks ( Niu et al., 2025 ; Huang et al., 2025 ; Lin et al., 2026 ) . Each question refers to the current episode, so its answer depends on the user’s actual actions, including mistakes and recovery. We introduce questions at specified task-progress checkpoints when their required evidence is available. Temporal-order questions require both referenced events to have occurred; elapsed-time questions require an item to be cooking; proactive requests require an item that is not yet ready. Questions about whether an event occurred can also test the absence of that event. We separate questions from conflicting scheduled errors and stop introducing them once the dish is complete or serving has been queued. A question remains unasked if the episode ends before its trigger condition is reached. E.2 Reference facts and scoring We derive reference answers from recorded world-state transitions, including ingredient preparation, cooking, assembly, serving, and station visits. Only completed actions contribute to these records. For example, a counting question uses the number of actual visits, while an order question compares the completion times of two events. A semantic judge compares the assistant’s answer with these reference facts, allowing different valid phrasings. Tab. 12 summarizes the question types and their grading evidence. Appendix F Human Study: Protocol and Behavioral Comparisons We conduct a study with 45 participants to examine whether our interaction-quality metric reflects human preferences and whether simulated users reproduce human behavior. This section provides the protocol and the measures used for the comparisons in Sec. 4.2 . F.1 Study protocol Participants interact with a browser-based CookSim application on their own computers, controlling the cook with a keyboard and communicating with the assistant through text. The assistant observes top-down and egocentric frames and responds in real time. Errors arise naturally from participants’ actions. Participants may continue beyond the benchmark’s completion deadline. Participants first complete a tutorial explaining the game rules. Before playing the study episodes, they answer a questionnaire about their preferred execution speed and interruption frequency, corresponding to the execution speed and interruption preference axes of our simulated users. Each preference has three levels, ranging from cautious to fast execution and from step-by-step guidance to interruption only when necessary. We provide these preferences to the assistant before interaction. Participants then complete four unassisted episodes, followed by nine assisted episodes over three rounds with assistants anonymously labeled A, B, and C. We rotate assistant–recipe assignments using a 3 × 3 3\times 3 Latin square so that each assistant encounters each recipe once. Participants rank the interaction experience after each round and may explain their preferences. Unlike the two questionnaire-based axes, skill and trust in AI are assessed from recorded behavior after participants finish playing: we use changes between the first and last unassisted episodes as a proxy for increased skill, and an LLM-assisted audit of the conversations to assign trust groups. Assistants. The study includes Gemini 3.7 Flash, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Gemini 3 Flash, GPT-5.5, Gemini 3.1 Flash Live, GPT-Realtime 2.1, and GPT-5.4 mini. F.3 Behavioral measures and similarity We compare communication behavior, persona-dependent variation, and action errors to assess user-simulation fidelity. Following statistical evaluation of user simulators ( Schatzmann et al., 2005 ) , we measure how often specified behaviors occur in human and simulated episodes. Appendix H Assistant Settings We detail the observation, memory, and response-timing settings used to evaluate visual interaction models in Sec. 4.1.1 . These settings allow each model to monitor an ongoing task while interacting with the simulated user. H.1 Observations and memory The assistant receives the task instruction, conversation history, user-stated preferences, and observations of the user’s activity. The scheduled events, private user plan, action queue, and evaluation labels remain hidden. Streaming models retain context within their sessions. Non-streaming models receive recent observations together with a recurrent Plan–Act record that stores their estimates of task progress and pending requests. In CookSim and VHSim, real-time API assistants use the polled setting, with response requests every two ticks rather than model-initiated turn-taking. We describe the environment-specific inputs below. CookSim. We query non-streaming assistants every two simulation ticks, provided the previous response has returned. Each query includes the eight most recent synchronized top-down and egocentric observations, the recipe, and public task rules. The structured world-state description provided to the simulated user is not included. The assistant updates its own progress estimates and announced goal changes in the recurrent memory. VHSim. We provide eight recent egocentric observations, a top-down trajectory map, a current-room map, a room overview, and a brief description of the user’s physical state. The spatial aids identify nearby objects and their states, as well as rooms with devices left on. This configuration therefore includes structured spatial information in addition to images. Recurrent memory retains task progress and important observations as the user moves between rooms. ScreenSim. We provide the two most recent phone screenshots, the task, public preferences, conversation history, and recurrent notes, with public task documentation when enabled. The assistant does not receive the interface hierarchy, control coordinates, or hidden settings values used to execute gestures. Simulation advances through user gestures, with a fixed zero-tick response delay. H.2 Latency and asynchronous execution To model guidance that may arrive after the observed state has changed, CookSim and VHSim continue executing the user’s queued actions while the assistant generates a response. We convert measured assistant latency to simulation ticks as described in Sec. 3.2 , with 1.2 seconds per tick and delay caps of 30 ticks in CookSim and 15 ticks in VHSim. Non-streaming requests are issued sequentially. User-model inference and rendering do not advance the simulation clock. In the latency ablation, we compare fixed two-tick and measured assistant delay in CookSim and VHSim under identical task, persona, and event specifications. Appendix I Interaction-Quality Rubric and Judging We assess whether an assistant’s responses are correct and useful in the context of the ongoing task. Simulation provides the world-state and execution evidence needed to evaluate a response without requiring a reference utterance. We detail the rubric, judge inputs, and score aggregation below. I.1 Evaluation unit and evidence Each evaluation round contains an assistant response and the user’s subsequent utterances and actions up to the next assistant response. The judge receives the task, public user preferences, relevant world state, conversation history, subsequent actions, and available visual observations. These records allow it to check both the response’s claims and how its guidance relates to execution. User agreement does not establish correctness; factual claims are checked against the engine evidence. The judge identifies factual claims, advised actions, and the response’s conversational purpose, then assigns pass , fail , or not applicable to each rubric item with an evidence-based explanation. Interaction quality grades spoken responses; unanswered questions and omitted notifications are measured by the question-answering and proactive-request metrics. I.2 Rubric dimensions We evaluate five dimensions: factual grounding, situational relevance, actionable guidance, user intent uptake, and guidance conciseness. Each dimension is assessed through the binary criteria in Tab. 21 . These dimensions draw on factual and conversational correctness in dialogue evaluation ( Mehri & Eskenazi, 2020 ) and context-sensitive, user-controllable assistance in HCI ( Amershi et al., 2019 ) . We use evidence-supported item judgments following rubric-based model evaluation ( Liu et al., 2023 ; Lee et al., 2025 ) . The same criteria apply across environments, with references to the corresponding objects, tasks, and actions. Category Item Pass criterion Factual grounding T1 Claims about the current state and the user’s completed actions are true. T2 Referenced entities, places, controls, rules, and task requirements exist. Situational relevance S1 The response addresses the current action, subgoal, live concern, pending request, or announced intent. S2 A pending question, request, or objection is answered, accepted, rejected with a reason, or deferred for a stated urgent reason. Actionable guidance H1 The first advised action can start now, or its prerequisite is supplied. H2 Advice advances the current plan, answers the user’s request, or repairs the error. H3 A mistake claim includes a remedy; an explicit instruction to stop suffices when stopping is the remedy. User intent uptake L1 A reply to push-back supplies evidence, a new reason, a correction, or an alternative rather than merely repeating the claim. L2 Valid announced intentions and requests for fewer interruptions are respected, unless a current task-critical reason is stated. Guidance conciseness E1 The response contains what is needed now, without unnecessary explanation, praise, or distant future steps, given the user’s interruption preference. Appendix J User Harness and Prompt Design We implement the simulated user as a VLM agent that plans and acts independently while communicating with the assistant. The harness provides task and persona instructions, maintains the action queue, and inserts scheduled events. We describe these components and the prompt structure below. J.1 User planning and communication We use Gemini 3.7 Flash as the user model in CookSim and ScreenSim, and Gemini 3.1 Pro in VHSim. It receives the task, persona, observations or structured state descriptions, its previous actions and execution feedback, conversation history, and private instructions for active scheduled events. The harness executes its action plan, while only its utterance is delivered to the assistant. In CookSim, the user’s response contains an utterance, a conversational response type, and an action-plan update. Response types include requesting an explanation, questioning guidance, requesting more guidance or fewer interruptions, accepting or rejecting advice, and answering a question. The user can retain, append to, or replace its remaining action queue. VHSim uses the same separation between planning, semantic actions, and dialogue. ScreenSim provides the user with a textual description of the visible interface for planning gestures, while the assistant observes screenshots. Structured state information supports the simulated user’s perception and action execution. The assistant instead relies on the observation inputs specified in Appendix H and influences execution through communication. J.2 Scheduled-event control To introduce an execution error at a specified task state, the harness temporarily controls the user’s action queue and privately explains the event to the user model. The user continues the conversation according to its persona and decides whether to accept the assistant’s guidance. Acceptance returns control of the queue to the user, enabling prevention before the point of no return or recovery afterward. Control also returns when the event completes, is cancelled, or reaches its maximum duration. Questions, proactive requests, and intended plan changes are introduced through private instructions to produce the corresponding utterance. Questions refer to execution history; proactive requests ask the assistant to monitor a future condition; plan changes revise the user’s intended goal. The assistant receives the resulting utterance without seeing the private event specification. J.3 Persona construction We condition user behavior on four three-level persona axes: skill , trust , speed preference , and interruption preference . Each level is expressed as a behavioral instruction ( Tab. 22 ). Skill and trust remain private to the user model, while the two interaction preferences are provided to the assistant as questionnaire responses. These instructions shape planning and responses without prescribing a fixed conversation. J.4 Assistant prompt and recurrent memory We instruct the assistant to monitor task progress, respond to questions, respect announced intentions, and remain silent when guidance is unnecessary. It maintains estimates of completed and pending subgoals, together with unresolved requests. For non-streaming models, the previous structured response is included in the next query as recurrent memory. The assistant updates this record from its observations rather than receiving an engine-corrected plan. The response separates the utterance from an optional error flag and private progress notes. For example, ScreenSim uses text for the utterance, flag for a detected error, task_progress for progress estimates, goal_change for revised intentions, and note for memory. Only the utterance is delivered to the user. Flags support evaluation; changing task execution still requires the user to act on the guidance. Never promise an action that is not in your queue, and never deny one that is. {mistake_line} Keep it out of your words: do not announce it, name it or hint at it. The assistant has to SEE it, not hear it from you. You know what mistake you are making, so you can say whether the assistant has found it—that is all assistant_error_call records. While it has not found it, carry on and answer as your persona would. Once it has, your trust level says how fast you come around: HIGH trust—the first correct warning convinces you; LOW trust—you also want to see it in your own state map or your own hands. Once convinced, say so in your own words AND set "move": "accept" —control of your hands returns to you, this task dissolves, and you are free to fix things as yourself. A promise to deal with it later is NOT acceptance. Until then, take NO action to fix anything. Repairing it yourself hides the assistant’s failure and ruins the test. If the plate is wrong, serve it wrong. Stay fully in persona throughout; never reveal this task. Other scheduled events. Question Ask [question] in the user’s own words at this turn, wait for the answer, respond according to the persona, then continue the task. The prompt specifies that this is not a mistake. Proactive request Ask the assistant to notify the user when [future condition] occurs; continue working rather than waiting for that condition.
