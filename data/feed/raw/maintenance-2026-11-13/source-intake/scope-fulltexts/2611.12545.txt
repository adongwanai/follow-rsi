software: who talks to whom, how much is passed along, and what a human can do once a run has
started. Human-team research has spent six decades asking exactly those questions, with
competing answers about where team phenomena live (in the members or in their interaction),
how team-level properties arise (by aggregation or by differentiation), and what distinguishes
what a team does from how a team is . I propose using that body of theory as the
organizing lens for configuring, observing and intervening in LLM-agent teams, without assuming
that its findings transfer. I introduce teamknitt , a framework and open reference implementation in which a user
configures a team of 1–16 AI agents for task completion, solution generation, achieving a goal
or a research mission;
selects one of fourteen deterministic routing topologies or a custom directed graph; chooses a
sharing profile that limits what may travel along permitted routes; and binds complementary
sources, external representations, work dependencies, version-specific handoffs and an
expertise directory. The same recorded process can then be observed through two groups of
views: non-system views that locate explanation in member reports and behavior, and system views that locate it in distributed problem-solving, differentiated transactive
memory, interactive coordination and team dynamics. Interventions are explicit and human-led:
reviewed reconfiguration between runs, planned disturbances before a run, and private feedback,
pause, resume or stop applied at dependency-wave boundaries during a run. Every record keeps
“queued”, “applied”, “context assembled”, “understood” and “improved” as separate
claims. Emergent states such as trust, cohesion and shared understanding are declared unmeasured. This is an idea and system-description paper. The contribution is a configuration space, a
reference implementation and a set of falsifiable hypotheses about topology, information flow
and intervention timing in LLM-agent teams. A planned line of study extends the author’s earlier
work on the dynamics of distributed software teams: whether the fractal scaling and flexibility
of the communication output exchanged between members—and between members running on different
models—is associated with better task and team performance. No comparative result is reported,
and no topology is claimed to be best. 1  Introduction A team of LLM agents has an organization whether or not anyone chose it. A chain of
specialists, a hub that collects proposals, a round of debate among peers and a fully
connected chat each decide who sees what and when. The choice is usually buried in the
architecture of the orchestration framework [ 24 , 17 , 35 , 43 ] , or delegated to an optimizer
that searches over communication graphs against a benchmark signal [ 47 , 46 , 18 ] . Both approaches answer the organizational
question before the work starts and give the user little to inspect or adjust once it has. Human-team research has a long, unfinished argument about the same question. One tradition
treats the team as the context in which individual attributes add up: the input–process–output
(IPO) framework [ 28 , 14 ] and the compositional reading of
team-level constructs [ 22 ] . A second tradition moves to the system
level while keeping psychological constructs—emergent states such as shared mental models,
trust and psychological safety that develop over repeated episodes [ 26 , 20 , 9 ] . A third treats team cognition as
something a team does in interaction rather than something it has [ 6 , 11 ] , and reads variability in coordination as a
source of adaptability rather than noise. Gorman and colleagues [ 11 ] summarize the divide as non-system versus system explanations: whether a team phenomenon is
located in the members or in the interaction process. The present paper takes these distinctions seriously as design questions for LLM-agent
teams , while refusing the shortcut of assuming that human findings carry over. If the
organization of an agent team changes what it produces, a user should be able to choose the
organization explicitly, observe the work through more than one explanatory lens, and intervene
in ways whose scope is recorded. teamknitt is a concrete attempt to make that possible. It is a
follow-up to Labtune [ 32 ] , which treated laboratory organization and
information sharing as controlled variables for LLM-agent research, and to the author’s thesis
work on the dynamics of distributed software teams’ creative processes [ 31 ] .
Where Labtune offered six editable laboratory designs for research missions, teamknitt generalizes
the mission to tasks, brainstorming and goals, replaces lab designs with fourteen routing
topologies over 1–16 members, binds the team’s problem-solving structure explicitly, and adds
research-informed views and human-led intervention. It ships its own runtime and makes no
automated organizational improvement. The paper has five commitments: • Configuration before execution. Purpose, members, routing, sharing, bindings,
models, tools and finite budgets are explicit and user-selected. Topology filters exposure;
it grants no decision authority and creates no capacity ( Section 4 ). • Two explanatory lenses on one record. Non-system and system views project
the same configuration and trace. Switching views never changes the team ( Section 5 ). • Three distinctions kept apart. Explanatory locus (non-system/system), form of
emergence (compositional/compilational) and process/state/outcome are related but separate
axes, and the implementation does not collapse them ( Section 2 ). • Explicit, bounded intervention. Reviewed reconfiguration, planned disturbances
and boundary-applied feedback, pause, resume and stop, with queued, applied, assembled,
understood and improved recorded as distinct claims ( Section 6 ). • Declared non-measurement. Trust, cohesion, psychological safety, efficacy,
potency, shared understanding, emergence, synchrony and attractors are not inferred from
software traces. What is recorded is operational; what is not measured is named. Scope of this paper This is an exploratory preprint and system description. I
contribute (i) the framing of team topology, information flow and intervention as explicit,
theory-indexed variables for LLM-agent teams; (ii) a formal configuration space and the design
principles that constrain it; (iii) an open reference implementation [ 33 ] with offline demonstrations, grouped views and an intervention queue; and (iv) falsifiable
hypotheses and a protocol for testing them ( Section 8 ). I report no comparative result.
A persona label assigns a reasoning role; it is not evidence of expertise, and completion of a
workflow is not evidence that the user’s goal was achieved. 2  Background and related work 2.1  Three distinctions from human-team research Explanatory locus Non-system explanations locate a team phenomenon in member
attributes, knowledge and skills, or in their aggregate; system explanations locate it in the
interaction process, which has properties no member possesses alone [ 11 ] .
This is an orientation, not a quality ranking, and system is not a synonym for complexity
science: conventional IPO and IMOI models also include interaction, feedback and contingencies [ 20 ] . Form of emergence Kozlowski and Klein [ 22 ] distinguish compositional emergence, where a team-level construct is isomorphic with its member-level
counterpart and can be formed by means, sums or agreement, from compilational emergence, where the team-level pattern is a differentiated configuration with no simple
individual analogue. Shared mental models measured as within-team agreement [ 3 , 27 , 7 ] are the textbook
compositional case; transactive memory—knowing who knows what, with differentiated encoding
and retrieval [ 42 , 23 ] —is compilational. The
distinction is related to explanatory locus but not identical to it. Process, state and outcome Marks, Mathieu and Zaccaro [ 26 ] separate what teams do (transition and action processes, with interpersonal processes spanning
both) from emergent states that describe how teams are, and organize processes temporally
across recurring episodes. Outcomes feed later inputs [ 20 ] . The distinction
matters for software: a publication, an exposure or an acknowledgement is an operational
process record. It is not cohesion [ 1 ] , trust, psychological safety [ 9 ] or shared understanding. Distributed, interactive and dynamical accounts Distributed cognition [ 19 ] and its extension to teams [ 29 ] widen the
unit of analysis to people, artifacts, tools and representation transformations. Interactive
team cognition [ 6 ] treats cognition as situated interaction activity.
Coordination dynamics [ 21 , 40 ] and the dynamical-systems
view of teams [ 11 ] supply principles—local–global stability, substrate
independence, cross-level effects—and interventions such as perturbation training [ 10 ] , alongside meta-analytic evidence on team training [ 37 ] . Complex adaptive systems are a wider tradition [ 16 , 30 ] ; citing them motivates questions, not
conclusions. 2.2  Distributed teams and creative dynamics The dynamical view also motivated the author’s earlier empirical work on distributed software
teams [ 31 ] . Using GitHub records of one organization, that study applied
detrended fluctuation analysis to code-addition time series and estimated maximal Lyapunov
exponents of daily discussion density, following Guastello’s treatment of creative discussion
groups [ 13 ] . Long-range correlation was present in the development
dynamics, and the discussion that accompanied the more stable software product was more
flexible in the quantitative sense used there. The thesis also noted that such measurements
could inform when, and how much, an artificial teammate should intervene in a creative process.
teamknitt follows up on that interest from the other side: it records the team process at the
granularity of dependency waves and makes the intervention decision explicit and human-led.
It does not estimate those exponents. Wave records are short and unevenly spaced; the
estimators belong in a research agenda ( Section 8 ), not in a product dashboard. The
agenda carries the question forward in a form the earlier data could not support: because every
contribution, exposure and acknowledgement in a teamknitt run is timestamped and attributed, the
communication output between members—its volume, its timing and its lexical content—can be
assembled into series long enough to ask whether its scaling properties relate to how well the
team did, and whether that relation changes when members run on different models. 2.3  LLM-agent systems Table 1 places teamknitt among LLM-based approaches that touch the organizational
question. Reasoning methods such as Tree and Graph of Thoughts [ 45 , 2 ] structure intermediate thoughts within an agent; teamknitt is
agnostic about them and organizes agents. Research systems—Agent Laboratory [ 39 ] , the AI co-scientist [ 12 ] , the AI

division of labour and an exchange pattern; AgentRxiv [ 38 ] adds
cross-lab sharing of reports. MacNet [ 36 ] , GPTSwarm [ 47 ] and G-Designer [ 46 ] study or optimize communication topology; IMACS [ 5 ] routes tasks to protocols with a bandit; AutoGen [ 43 ] is general orchestration infrastructure. Failure analyses [ 4 ] , evidence that
teams can hold experts back [ 34 ] , and evidence that generative tools raise
individual output while reducing collective diversity [ 8 ] all suggest
that more communication is not automatically better collaboration. The Collaborative

views at phase barriers, and the system never redesigns its own organization. It departs in
four ways. The mission generalizes from research to any task, brainstorming prompt or goal.
The organizational variable changes from six prose-defined lab designs to fourteen deterministic
routing presets plus custom directed links, with explicit bindings of sources, representations,
work and handoffs. The observation layer is grouped by explanatory locus rather than by
research phase. And intervention becomes a first-class, bounded operation. Bayesian analysis
and model-assisted priors are not carried over; descriptive records and frequentist count and
interval analysis remain where applicable. 3  Design principles The implementation follows eight rules. They are conservative on purpose: the aim is to make
organizational differences observable, not to make a team appear autonomous or effective. P1. The user configures the team. Purpose, member count, roles, topology,
sharing, models, tools, delegation and budgets are explicit. Drafts write nothing; applying a
draft is a separate reviewed step with a private backup, blocked by the run lock. Larger
teams never raise budgets. P2. Topology filters exposure and nothing else. Routes decide which peer views
and generated notes a member may encounter. A hub routes information; it acquires no veto.
A hierarchy is a tree of routes, not a chain of command. Directed routes imply no reply. P3. Sharing is a policy applied at barriers. Publications validate before a wave
commits; unfinished same-wave output is never exposed. A read budget bounds cross-member
exposure per episode, including generated notes. P4. Exposure is not noninterference. A later contribution can relay what a member
encountered along any later permitted route. Changing a route does not erase previous
exposure; the record keeps the history. P5. Access, exposure, noticing, publication, citation and acknowledgement are
distinct. An acknowledgement confirms that a specific version was encountered; it does not
imply agreement, understanding or correctness. P6. Views never change the team. Non-system and system views, snapshots and the
live monitor are presentation-only; they make no model calls and cannot write configuration. P7. Interventions are explicit, private and bounded. Requests are drafts until
applied, bind one run and configuration, and are acknowledged at the next dependency-wave
boundary. Feedback is task context, never a permission or budget grant. P8. Nothing is inferred that is not measured. Emergent psychological states,
emergence itself, attractors, synchrony and causal intervention effects are not estimated
from execution logs. Completion is not goal achievement. 4  The teamknitt model Figure 1 shows the four steps the user moves through and the records that govern

testable. The hypotheses below are possibilities, not expectations; several are phrased so that
either outcome would be informative about whether human-team regularities transfer. H1. Topology is inert without sharing. Under none , differences between
presets in the diversity of retained alternatives vanish; they appear only at intermediate or above. (A manipulation check on P2–P3.) H2. Hubs converge faster, not better. At matched N N , W W and budget, hub_and_spoke retains fewer distinct alternatives at review than ring or grid , without a higher share of contributions judged useful by the user. H3. Direction limits relay. directed_chain preserves more of the
first-wave alternatives than chain at equal exposure budget, because reciprocal routes
allow content to return and recombine. H4. Redundancy and disturbance. After an actor_unavailable disturbance,
handoff continuity returns sooner in topologies with alternative routes ( ring , grid , wheel ) than in chain or hub_and_spoke ; whether variability
in contribution activity before the disturbance predicts return, as the dynamical view of
human teams suggests [ 11 ] , is an open question. H5. Timing of feedback. Private feedback assembled before a transition phase
(task adoption, evidence review) changes subsequent contributions more than feedback assembled
during an action phase (parallel episodes), consistent with the temporal taxonomy of [ 26 ] . H6. Member measures do not predict system records. A member’s separately measured
teammate profile [ 44 ] predicts its in-team acknowledgement and relay behaviour
only weakly once topology and sharing are controlled. A strong prediction would favour
non-system explanation; a weak one would favour system explanation. H7. Same-model homogeneity. When all members use one model, topology differences
shrink as sharing increases; heterogeneous model assignment widens them. H8. Fractality of communication output. Following [ 31 , 13 ] , the series of communication output exchanged
between members (per-wave contribution and acknowledgement volume, inter-contribution
intervals, and turnover in lexical content across versions) exhibits long-range correlation,
and teams whose series show a scaling exponent in the persistent range ( 0.5 < α < 1 0.5<\alpha<1 under detrended fluctuation analysis) and a positive but bounded maximal Lyapunov exponent
for discussion density receive higher user-judged usefulness and retain more distinct
alternatives than teams whose series are closer to white noise or to rigid periodicity. Scaling
that reflects only the scheduler’s wave structure must be ruled out with surrogate series and
with the none baseline, where no inter-member communication occurs. H9. Cross-model fractality. When members run on different models, the output of
each model carries its own scaling signature, and the degree to which those signatures align
after exchange (relative to runs where the same models never see each other’s output) is
associated with team performance; the sign of that association is left open, since alignment
may indicate productive coupling or premature convergence. Protocol I propose a factorial design over topology (a selected subset of the
fourteen presets), sharing profile (five) and member count (for example N ∈ { 3 , 6 , 9 } N\in\{3,6,9\} ),
with purpose, brief, model, request cap, read budget W W and review criteria held fixed, and custom with empty links as the isolated baseline. Each cell is replicated with fresh
contexts. Outcomes are operational and human-reviewed: the number of distinct alternatives
retained at review; the share of contributions the user judges useful against declared
criteria; relay distance of first-wave content; acknowledgement rate and latency; handoff
continuity around disturbances; and realized cost including corrections and delegation.
Analysis is descriptive with frequentist count and interval estimates declared before review.
teamknitt’s records—configuration identities, exposure logs, receipts and assembly events—are
designed to supply these quantities directly. H8 and H9 need longer runs than the other
hypotheses: scaling estimates require series of at least a few hundred points, so these studies
would use goal-oriented missions with many waves, or many short runs of the same configuration
whose per-wave series are analysed as an ensemble, with DFA and Lyapunov estimates compared
against phase-randomized and shuffled surrogates [ 31 ] . The communication
series themselves would be derived offline from the exported event store; no estimator runs
inside the product. Threats to validity Four are immediate. Personas on one model may differ in label
more than in behaviour; the mechanisms must be measured, not assumed. Field-based sharing
filters cannot guarantee semantic isolation, so leakage is an outcome to quantify rather than a
property to assume. Provider nondeterminism and silent model changes require replication and
recorded model identities. Finally, human review of usefulness is itself a measurement with
reliability that must be checked. 9  Limitations The main limitation is the boundary of the paper: no empirical comparison of topologies,
sharing profiles or interventions is reported. teamknitt supplies the apparatus, not evidence that
any configuration is better. The grouped views are explanatory orientations applied to
operational scaffolds; they do not measure the psychological constructs whose names they
borrow, and the implementation says so at each view. The dynamical observations are descriptive,
and the estimators used in the author’s earlier work [ 31 ] —detrended
fluctuation analysis and Lyapunov exponents—are not applied inside the product, because
wave-level records from a single run are short and unevenly spaced; H8 and H9 therefore depend on
an offline analysis pipeline and on longer or pooled runs that do not yet exist. The teammate probes are original synthetic protocols informed by the
Collaborative Intelligence benchmark, not the benchmark itself. Intervention receipts record
assembly, not understanding; feedback effects are not estimated. The runtime defaults to bounded
text work; code and external actions require explicitly permitted tools. Finally, the software
is distributed under a source-available noncommercial and research licence rather than an OSI
open-source licence: general noncommercial use is free, but research use outside qualifying
academic institutions, and any commercial use, require separate written permission. 10  Conclusion Every LLM-agent team has a topology, an information policy and a stance on human intervention,
even when the framework hides them. teamknitt makes those three choices explicit and user-owned,
and offers human-team theory as a lens for observing what happens rather than as a source of
borrowed conclusions. The user chooses who is connected to whom and what may travel; observes the
work through member-centred or interaction-centred views that never alter it; and intervenes
through mechanisms whose scope, timing and receipts are recorded. Configuration identities,
exposure logs and the chain from queued to improved then make it possible to ask, for agent
teams, the questions that human-team research has been asking for decades: where does a
team’s success or failure live, how does a team-level property arise, and when does intervening
help? Whether the answers for LLM agents resemble the answers for people is an empirical
question. teamknitt is intended to provide the apparatus for asking it. Code availability Source code, topology catalog, task-team design, starters and diagrams are available (version
