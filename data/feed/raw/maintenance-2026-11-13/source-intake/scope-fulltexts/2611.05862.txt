Yongliang Shen Affiliation: Zhejiang University Abstract Large language model (LLM) shopping assistants now recommend products in multi-turn conversations. However, reliable evaluation faces Data scarcity and Difficulty Standardizing Criteria . To address these obstacles, we introduce CARTBench for CART ( C omplex and A mbiguous R equests with T imelines). For Data scarcity , we curate cases from real commercial shopping needs across 17 retail domains, pairing timestamped dialogue with 1,603 product records. For Difficulty Standardizing Criteria , we develop case-specific rubrics guided by Ideal Response Principles , distilled from excellent replies manually selected from successfully completed orders. These principles also guide Principle-Guided CART Prompting , our structured recommendation strategy. The experimental benchmark contains 1,328 expert-annotated criteria across Core, Excellence, and Safety. To answer the CART question, we evaluate 7 flagship commercial API models, including GPT-6.1-Sol and Claude-Opus-5, and 35B+ parameters open-weight models. In the first full-suite evaluation under the frozen rubrics, 3 of 10 models exceed 90% on Core, while none exceeds 45% on Excellence. Therefore, we identify an understanding-to-execution gap between required behavior and high-quality purchase advice. To examine these limitations, we diagnose shared failures in product evidence and unresolved needs, linked to changing requirements and decisive unknowns. Matched colors link reply excerpts to their checks. 1 Introduction LLM shopping assistants help shoppers choose products for stated or implicit needs ( Mukherjee et al., 2025 ; Dammu et al., 2025 ; Dhole et al., 2025 ) . However, shopping needs can be complex or unclear and may expire over time (Figure 1 ). This raises the question: How well can LLMs handle C omplex and A mbiguous R equests with T imelines ( CART )? Existing shopping benchmarks do not explicitly report timeline inputs, and none credits replies that keep unconfirmed preferences and conditions open (Table 1 ). Without timelines, an evaluation cannot tell whether advice follows a need that has already expired; without credit for open unknowns, it cannot separate a lucky guess from a justified conditional answer. Further, identifying complex and ambiguous needs in confidential commercial conversations requires costly annotation. In addition, 2) Difficulty Standardizing Criteria : different products and user needs require different checks. For example, even within the same retail domain, such as clothing, “recommend an item of clothing” can imply different needs. These can depend on gender, age, style, occasion, and the intended gift recipient. Timeline information can also reveal expired needs; a gift intended for an earlier date may already have been given. A shared checklist cannot capture these differences, yet criteria written case by case without a common standard are hard to keep consistent across cases. To address these obstacles, we introduce CARTBench , a benchmark grounded in real shopping needs for evaluating CART advice. For Data scarcity , we curate CART cases from real commercial shopping needs.
For each case, timestamped dialogue retains changed needs and unanswered questions. Through stratified sampling, CARTBench covers 17 common retail domains and pairs dialogue with 1,603 product records.
For Difficulty Standardizing Criteria , we develop case-specific rubrics guided by Ideal Response Principles . Experts distill these principles from excellent replies manually selected from successfully completed orders, and instantiate them as case-specific rubrics. The principles keep criteria consistent across cases, and the rubrics adapt them to each case. The experimental benchmark contains 1,328 criteria across Core , which checks required behavior, Excellence , which rewards high-quality advice beyond it, and Safety , which checks case-specific risks. Models receive the same principles, but not the rubrics, through Principle-Guided CART Prompting . To answer the CART question, we evaluate 7 flagship commercial API models, including GPT-6.1-Sol and Claude-Opus-5 , and 35B+ parameters open-weight models. Under the frozen evaluation protocol, the first full-suite evaluation shows that 3 of 10 models exceed 90% on Core , while none exceeds 45% on Excellence . Therefore, we identify an understanding-to-execution gap . Here, meeting Core requirements does not ensure high-quality guidance for purchase decisions. To examine these limitations, further diagnosis reveals shared failures in product evidence and unresolved needs, linked to changing requirements and decisive unknowns. Our contributions are: • We introduce CARTBench , which evaluates CART advice on real commercial shopping needs with timestamped dialogue, product evidence, and case-specific rubrics. • We propose Ideal Response Principles , distilled from excellent replies, which keep case-specific rubrics consistent across cases and guide Principle-Guided CART Prompting . • We identify an understanding-to-execution gap . Excerpts show 3 checks. 2 Related Work 2.1 Shopping Recommendation Use cases and implicit needs guide recommendations without requiring exact product attributes ( Mukherjee et al., 2025 ; Dammu et al., 2025 ; Dhole et al., 2025 ) . Personalization draws on elicited or inferred preferences ( Lucas de Carvalho et al., 2025 ; Kostric et al., 2025 ; Kook et al., 2025 ) , profiles, and external knowledge ( Chien et al., 2026 ; Li et al., 2025 ) . Related benchmarks assess preference adherence ( Zhao et al., 2025b ; Zhao et al., 2025a ; Narasimhan and Narasimhan, 2026 ) , user states, and dialogue consistency ( Li et al., 2026 ; Sirdeshmukh et al., 2025 ) . However, data scarcity limits evaluation of complex and ambiguous commercial needs. To address this gap, we curate CART cases based on experts’ screening for interacting or unclear needs. This enables targeted CART evaluation, addressing the shortage of real commercial cases. 2.2 Shopping Evaluation Shopping evaluations assess actions and product selection ( Yao et al., 2022 ; Wang et al., 2026 ; Lyu et al., 2025 ) . Extensions test hidden preferences ( Du et al., 2026 ) and retrieval ( Hao et al., 2026 ) . Advice assessment adds factual comparisons ( Vedula et al., 2023 ) and expert rubrics ( Tou et al., 2025 ; Fan et al., 2026 ) . Evidence-grounded checks support judge evaluation ( Lyu et al., 2026 ) and rubric design ( Mei et al., 2026 ) . RealWorldShop tests changing, ambiguous needs in simulated sessions ( Yang et al., 2026 ) . However, CART evaluation requires case-specific checks with consistent standards. To overcome this limitation, we develop case-specific rubrics guided by our Ideal Response Principles . This enables systematic CART evaluation, aligning common standards with case-specific needs. 3 CARTBench To formalize CART advice, in CART Problem Formulation (Section 3.1 ), we define shopping inputs and outcomes. To address Data scarcity , in Data Collection (Section 3.2 ), experts select commercial cases and pair dialogue with product evidence. To address Difficulty Standardizing Criteria , experts instantiate shared Ideal Response Principles as case-specific rubrics. After case construction, in Recommendation Agent (Section 3.3 ), models generate replies from the selected dialogue, evidence, and shared principles. Rubrics remain hidden. Following generation, in Evidence-Grounded Rubric Judging (Section 3.4 ), we assign evidence-linked labels and aggregate expert-confirmed outcomes. We next construct these inputs and rubrics. 3.2 Data Collection To address data scarcity and standardize checks, we construct case inputs and expert-designed rubrics in four steps. Figure 3 shows how a case-specific rubric distinguishes stronger and weaker replies to the same request. Step 1: Sources and Case Selection. To capture CART needs, experts screen real commercial requests for interacting requirements, changes, unclear goals, and decisive unknowns.
To limit evaluation costs, rather than evaluating a large dataset at substantial time and financial cost, we use stratified sampling to select 169 representative cases across 17 retail domains.
At official list prices, one complete 169-case evaluation costs less than US$24 per API model for inference and judging, enabling cost-effective large-scale model selection.
Cases span eight intents; dates and intervals distinguish active, expired, and unconfirmed historical needs. Coverage. Retrieval establishes neither user endorsement nor suitability. Step 3: Ideal Response Principles. To standardize checks, experts distill four Ideal Response Principles. The source comprises excellent replies manually selected from successfully completed orders. To protect privacy, we do not share these replies.
These principles guide case-specific rubrics and Principle-Guided CART Prompting. Ideal Response Principles (Summary) 1. Address the current purchase decision. 2. Follow active needs; preserve unknowns. 3. Ground claims and explain trade-offs. 4. Give clear and useful decision help. Step 4: Case-Specific Rubric Design. To tailor checks, experts adapt shared principles to each case’s dialogue and evidence. The frozen experimental rubrics contain 1,328 criteria after expert review. Each criterion checks one observable behavior and accepts supported alternatives ( Mei et al., 2026 ) . The criteria form three business-oriented tiers: R = R Core ∪ R Excellence ∪ R Safety . R=R_{\mathrm{Core}}\cup R_{\mathrm{Excellence}}\cup R_{\mathrm{Safety}}. (2) Each criterion belongs to one tier. Core covers goal alignment, requirement adherence, uncertainty preservation, evidence fidelity, plan validity, and decision support. These criteria guide Evidence-Grounded Rubric Judging , our prompt for evidence-linked labels (Section 3.4 ). Principle-Guided CART Prompting. To ground recommendations, we apply shared principles to need analysis, product comparison, and justification. We next diagnose Core failures and examine where the same modes recur. 4.2 Failure Mode Analysis We first identify shared Core failure modes and locate the same errors across scenarios. Separately, we assess plan validity after resolved need and evidence checks pass. 4.2.1 Shared Failure Modes Unsupported product claims and unjustified suitability dominate the failure mix. These errors account for most pooled Core failures (Figure 5 ). F7: Product Evidence Overreach adds intrinsic product claims unsupported by supplied evidence. F8: Product Fit Leap turns stated attributes into unjustified user-specific suitability guarantees. F2: Shopping Uncertainty Collapse treats unknown preferences, possessions, purchase needs, or scenario conditions as settled. All three modes occur in every evaluated model, although cases differ. Thus, grounded shopping advice requires supported product facts and justified links to user needs. Diagnosis scope. Each failed Core response–criterion instance receives one primary AI-assisted diagnosis. Excellence and Safety failures are excluded; uncertain codes remain in the denominator. However, the global mix can hide errors concentrated in particular scenarios. We next examine these profiles. Figure 5: Core failure composition. Unsupported product claims (F7), unjustified suitability inference (F8), and premature need resolution (F2) recur in every model. Slices retain uncertain diagnoses. 4.2.2 Scenario-Specific Failures Context-state drift is minor overall but the most frequent diagnosed mode in task-switch cases. Following the shared-mode diagnosis, Figure 6 maps F2, F7, and F8 across scenarios alongside F1: context-state drift . F1 misuses a stated actor, requirement, or temporal state. Drift also appears when the shopping task continues. The continuity–F1 association remains positive after adjusting for overlapping mechanisms and rubric opportunities. Task continuity therefore does not guarantee correct state tracking. Even within one shopping goal, the relevant actor, constraints, or temporal state may change. Unlike the failure composition, scenario rates use all replies, with equal model and case weights. Groups and modes overlap. Cells use equal model and case weights; groups overlap. Decisive unknowns expose more evidence and fit errors than premature need resolution. In decisive-unknown scenarios, both F7 and F8 occur more often than F2. Thus, preserving unknowns also requires bounding product and suitability claims. Evidence checks ask whether product claims follow from the supplied records. Fit checks ask whether those supported attributes warrant a recommendation for the current user. Radius gives failure rates among all scenario replies (0–40%), equally weighting models and cases. 4.2.3 Shopping Feasibility Mirage Replies can meet assessed user requirements and pass product evidence checks yet fail to justify a workable recommendation. Eligible replies need resolved evidence and plan checks, plus a resolved goal, requirement, or uncertainty check. We then select replies passing all resolved goal, requirement, uncertainty, and evidence checks. Passing an uncertainty check can mean preserving an unknown, so selected replies need not settle every missing preference or fact. Among these replies, 13.0% fail a plan check. This estimate weights models equally (95% interval, 8.1–18.4). The analysis concerns plan validity within Core. 5 Conclusion CARTBench pairs timestamped dialogue and product evidence with case-specific rubrics, guided by Ideal Response Principles and Principle-Guided CART Prompting. Expert-confirmed results reveal an understanding-to-execution gap and shared failures in evidence, suitability, and unresolved needs. Even replies passing all resolved need and evidence checks can fail plan checks. Limitations First, although we cover common shopping scenarios such as gifting and travel clothing, specialized needs merit further study. Examples include historically accurate costumes for museum reenactments and equipment for extended polar expeditions.
Second, although we assess recommendations under changing needs, feedback from actual product use may introduce new requirements. How advice should adapt across longer purchase, return, and replacement cycles merits further study. Ethical Considerations CARTBench is provided solely for academic research and evaluation. Our cases are grounded in real commercial shopping needs. Product information is supplied by an internal product search platform. Internet searches were used only to confirm that corresponding products could be found online. Third-party content remains subject to the original rights and applicable terms. Experts annotate requirements from visible dialogue and retrieved evidence. Evaluation rewards grounded explanations and respects user choice.
