Abstract
Different optimizers impose different geometries on parameter updates. Adap-
tive methods such as Adam and AdamW use coordinate-wise updates, while
matrix-aware optimizers such as Muon utilize weight-matrix structure through or-
thogonalized updates. For Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), however, the effective
geometry of its weight matrices is shaped not only by its learned channel-mixing
weights, but also by the graph-propagated representations it acts on. Thus, even
if an update is well normalized as a matrix, its effect on the layer output can be
amplified when applied to graph-propagated features. To address this gap, we
introduce GraphMuon, a graph-aware optimizer that adapts Muon’s matrix up-
dates to the covariance structure of graph-propagated layer inputs. GraphMuon
performs orthogonalization in a metric defined by these propagated representa-
tions and normalizes step sizes according to the update’s induced effect on the
layer representation. We show that theoretically, vanilla Muon can suffer from
graph-induced update amplification, while our GraphMuon can mitigate this effect.
As such, our GraphMuon yields a graph-metric analogue of Muon’s polar update
step. Empirically, GraphMuon achieves 1.32×–2.42× validation-loss time-to-
target speedups over AdamW on three large-scale OGB benchmarks. Across 15
dataset–architecture configurations spanning node classification, inductive graph
classification, and graph regression, it achieves lower time-to-target in 13 cases,
while maintaining competitive or superior predictive performance. Code is avail-
able at https://github.com/guy-hadad/GraphMuon.

Introduction
Optimizers define the geometry in which neural-network updates are measured. Classical SGD
uses a global Euclidean scale [26], adaptive methods such as Adam and AdamW normalize updates
coordinate-wise [17, 20], and preconditioned methods such as Shampoo exploit matrix statistics to
approximate richer curvature information [10]. More recently, matrix-aware optimizers such as Muon
treat weight matrices as structured operators and replace raw momentum updates with approximately
orthogonalized matrix directions [16]. Muon has also been shown to improve compute efficiency
in large language-model training [19, 29, 30]. These developments suggest a broader principle: the
geometry used by an optimizer should depend not only on the shape of a parameter tensor, but also
on the module in which that parameter appears [1].

Graph neural networks provide a setting where this module dependence is especially important. In a
standard feed-forward layer, a weight matrix acts on representations produced by previous neural
layers. In a GNN layer, however, the trainable matrix acts on graph-propagated representations. For a
propagation operator S and node representation H, the effective input to the weight matrix is
Xg = SH.
(1)
Therefore, the effect of an update ∆W is governed by the induced representation-level perturbation
Xg∆W, rather than by the parameter-space norm of ∆W alone. Since Xg depends on the graph
topology, neighborhood aggregation, feature smoothing, and the current training state, two updates
with the same Euclidean matrix scale can induce very different changes in the layer output.
This observation exposes a gap in existing optimizers for GNNs. AdamW adapts updates through
coordinate-wise gradient moments, but does not explicitly distinguish directions that are amplified by
graph propagation from directions that are attenuated in the propagated feature distribution. Muon
improves over coordinate-wise updates by using the matrix structure of W, but its orthogonalization
is still input-agnostic: the same Euclidean matrix geometry is used whether the matrix acts on dense
features, tokens, or graph-propagated node representations. For GNNs, the relevant object is not only
the standalone matrix W, but the graph-conditioned map
W 7→XgW.

The geometry of this map is determined by the covariance of the propagated inputs,

Directions aligned with high-energy eigenspaces of Cg produce large representation-level perturba-
tions, while directions in low-energy eigenspaces have much smaller immediate effect [12]. Thus, an
optimizer for GNN weight matrices should be both matrix-aware and graph-aware: it should exploit
the matrix structure of W, while calibrating update direction and scale to the graph-induced geometry
of the representations on which W actually acts.
Inspired by this observation, we introduce GraphMuon, a graph-aware extension of Muon for GNN
optimization. GraphMuon adapts Muon’s matrix-orthogonalized updates to the layer-wise geometry
induced by graph-propagated inputs. It has two complementary components. First, graph-aware
orthogonalization applies the polar-style Muon update in a metric defined by the covariance of
Xg. Second, graph-aware step normalization scales each update by its induced representation-level
perturbation, rather than by its parameter-space magnitude alone. Together, these mechanisms align
the optimizer geometry with the actual forward map of a GNN layer.
Our analysis in Section 4.3 shows that this modification is principled: a vanilla Muon update, although
normalized in Euclidean matrix space, can still be amplified in representation space by the dominant
eigenspaces of Cg. In contrast, our GraphMuon directly controls the quantity ∥Xg∆W∥F /√n,
which is the perturbation induced in the pre-activation representation of the GNN layer. We further
show that the graph-aware orthogonalization step is the graph-metric analogue of Muon’s polar
update, rather than an ad hoc rescaling.
Figure 1 illustrates the resulting mismatch and our correction. A Muon update can be well normalized
as a matrix while still causing a large graph-propagated perturbation. GraphMuon addresses this by
using Cg to adapt both the update geometry and its scale.
We evaluate GraphMuon across graph-learning benchmarks spanning node classification, inductive
graph classification, and graph regression, using GCN, GAT, GINE, and GraphGPS architectures.
Under a fixed validation-loss time-to-target protocol, GraphMuon achieves 1.32×–2.42× wall-clock
speedups over AdamW on the three large-scale OGB benchmarks. Across a broader evaluation of
15 dataset–architecture configurations, GraphMuon improves epoch-level time-to-target in all cases
and achieves faster wall-clock convergence in 13 of 15, while maintaining competitive or superior
predictive performance.

Related Work
Graph neural networks. GNNs extend deep learning to graph-structured data by interleaving
learnable transformations with neighborhood aggregation [9, 27]. Scalable spatial methods, including
GCN [18], GraphSAGE [11], GAT [32], and GIN [33], have become the dominant paradigm, with
subsequent work extending these ideas to heterogeneous graphs [28] and heterophilous graphs where
connected nodes tend to differ in class [36]. Across these architectures, weight matrices act not on raw
node features but on representations smoothed and mixed by graph propagation. This distinguishes
GNN training from standard deep-network optimization: the geometry seen by each weight matrix is
shaped not only by network depth, but also by graph topology and its induced propagation operator,
which is precisely the mismatch GraphMuon is designed to address.
Matrix-aware optimization. Classical SGD and momentum update parameters in Euclidean space,
while AdaGrad, Adam, and AdamW normalize updates coordinate-wise using accumulated gradient
statistics [4, 17, 20]. Structured optimizers exploit richer geometry: K-FAC uses Kronecker-factored
curvature [22], Shampoo uses matrix preconditioners [10], and Muon applies momentum followed
by approximate matrix orthogonalization [16]. Muon is the closest predecessor, but remains module-
agnostic: it uses the same Euclidean matrix geometry for dense features, tokens, and graph-propagated
representations. GraphMuon instead tests whether update geometry should match the module-induced
3

operator, which for GNNs is the graph-conditioned map W 7→XgW, rather than the standalone
matrix W.
Graph-aware modules and optimization. A central theme in GNN research is adapting neural-
network components to graph structure: GCNs [18], GAT [32], and GIN [33] do so architecturally, as
do graph-specific normalization and modular designs [3, 25]. Izadi et al. [15] show that optimization
itself can be graph-aware by deriving natural-gradient updates for graph-based semi-supervised
learning. GraphMuon follows this optimizer-level view but targets a different setting: rather than
a natural-gradient method, it adapts Muon’s matrix-aware geometry to the layer-wise covariance
of graph-propagated representations, the optimizer-level analogue of graph-adaptive GNN design.
Additional related work is discussed in Appendix B.

Theoretical Properties
We summarize the main theoretical properties of GraphMuon: it controls the perturbation induced in
the representation space actually used by each GNN layer, and its graph-aware orthogonalization is
the natural graph-metric analogue of Muon’s polar update. All proofs are in Appendix E.
For the realized GNN module W 7→SHW, the covariance Cg = H⊤S⊤SH/n is the exact pullback
of the normalized output-space geometry, whereas CH = H⊤H/n describes the pre-propagation

underlying module-aware principle may also extend beyond GNNs; further theoretical motivation is
provided in Appendix E.

Experiments
In this section, we conduct an extensive set of experiments to demonstrate the effectiveness of
GRAPHMUON as an optimizer that adapts GNN updates to graph-propagated representations.
Concretely, our experiments address the following questions:
(Q1) Does graph-conditioned optimization improve over parameter-space optimization? We
compare GRAPHMUON with AdamW, Muon, Shampoo, and graph-aware natural-gradient baselines
to test whether adapting update direction and scale to the graph-propagated covariance improves
the main optimization outcome: quicker optimization that maintains competitive or superior test
accuracy. This question captures the central distinction of GRAPHMUON: it does not merely change
the optimizer family, but changes the geometry in which GNN weight updates are measured.
(Q2) Which component of GRAPHMUON is responsible for the gains? We ablate graph-aware
orthogonalization and graph-aware step normalization to determine if improvements come from
changing the update direction, controlling the representation-level step size, or their combination.
(Q3) Do empirical diagnostics support the theoretical motivation? We measure representation-
level perturbations and graph-induced spectral quantities to test whether Euclidean-normalized matrix
updates can indeed be amplified by graph-propagated covariance, and if GRAPHMUON controls it.
5.1
Experimental Setup
Datasets. We evaluate GraphMuon across nine graph-learning benchmarks, covering transductive
node classification, heterophilous node classification, inductive graph classification, and graph regres-
sion. The node-classification datasets include OGBN-ARXIV, OGBN-PRODUCTS, and OGBN-MAG [14],
together with QUESTIONS [24], Cora, and Citeseer. To assess generalization beyond transductive
node prediction, we additionally evaluate on the molecular graph-classification benchmarks OGBG-
MOLBACE and OGBG-MOLTOX21, and the graph-regression benchmark ZINC. Additional dataset
information is provided in Appendix G.1.
Backbones. We evaluate multiple GNN architectures, including GCN, GAT [31], GINE, and
GraphGPS [25]. GCN provides the primary comparison on the large-scale OGB node-classification
benchmarks. GAT and GraphGPS extend the evaluation to learned attention-based propagation,
while GINE is used for molecular graph-level tasks. Additional experiments examine GraphGCN
and deeper GNNs. Within each dataset–architecture configuration, all optimizers use the same
backbone, training budget, and evaluation protocol, isolating the optimizer as the varying component.
Architectural details are provided in Appendix G.2.

5.2
Main Results
For the primary comparison, we report validation-loss time-to-target across the large-scale OGB
benchmarks, heterophilous node classification, and inductive graph-level tasks. Table 1 shows that
GraphMuon reaches AdamW’s validation-loss target faster across all seven reported configurations.
On the three large-scale OGB benchmarks, GraphMuon achieves wall-clock speedups of 2.32× on
OGBN-ARXIV, 2.42× on OGBN-MAG, and 1.32× on OGBN-PRODUCTS. The corresponding 95%
bootstrap confidence intervals are [2.01, 2.69], [2.29, 2.53], and [1.19, 1.37], respectively. These
results demonstrate faster convergence to a fixed validation-loss target rather than to a potentially
variable accuracy threshold.
The improvements also extend beyond transductive GCNs. On QUESTIONS with GAT, GraphMuon
achieves a 2.14× wall-clock speedup. On the inductive molecular graph-classification benchmarks

Conclusions
We motivate our GraphMuon by a representation-space view of optimization in GNNs. For a layer
with graph-propagated input Xg = SH, the effective update size is not ∥∆W∥F , but the induced
perturbation ∥Xg∆W∥F /√n. Our analysis shows that a Euclidean-normalized Muon update can
still be amplified by the graph-induced covariance Cg = X⊤
g Xg/n, through its dominant eigenspaces.
GraphMuon addresses this by normalizing the update according to its induced representation-level
effect and by replacing Euclidean matrix geometry with a graph-metric analogue.
This theory also explains when GraphMuon may provide less benefit. The method is most useful when
Cg is stable, anisotropic, and informative. On very small graphs, however, Cg may be low-rank and
noisy, especially when the number of nodes is small relative to the hidden dimension. In this regime,
the graph-induced metric can be poorly estimated, and controlling ∥Xg∆W∥F does not necessarily
imply faster convergence or better generalization. AdamW can therefore remain competitive, since
its coordinate-wise moment estimates do not rely on estimating a graph-level covariance.
This limitation is particularly relevant for small inductive graph-level tasks, where each graph contains
few node representations. In such settings, the main optimization challenge may be dominated by
sparse local features, atom or bond types, and mini-batch variability rather than by graph-induced
update amplification. A natural direction for future work is to adapt the strength of graph awareness
to the reliability of Cg, for example, using effective rank, spectral anisotropy, or covariance stability
to interpolate between vanilla Muon and fully graph-aware updates.

Additional Related Work
Graph-adaptive neural components. A broad line of work adapts standard neural-network com-
ponents to graph-structured data. GCNs use normalized adjacency propagation [18], GAT adapts
attention to graph neighborhoods [32], and GIN studies aggregation from the perspective of expressive
power [33]. Graph-specific design has also been extended to normalization and modular architectures.
GraphNorm introduces graph-specific normalization [3], GRANOLA uses neighborhood-structure-
based normalization [5], GraphGPS combines local message passing with global attention and
positional encodings [25], GRAMA [6] learns a graph-adaptive autoregressive moving average filter-
ing technique, and DiGRAF learns graph-adaptive activation functions [21]. These works support the
broader principle that generic deep-learning components often benefit from graph-specific adaptation.
GraphMuon applies this principle to optimization rather than architecture.
GNN training dynamics. Several works study why GNN optimization differs from optimization in
standard Euclidean architectures. Natural-gradient methods exploit information-geometric structure
in graph-based semi-supervised learning [15]; skip connections, depth, and label distributions have
been shown to affect GNN convergence [34]; and recent training-dynamics analyses show that
gradient descent can implicitly exploit graph structure through kernel–graph alignment [35]. These
results motivate our representation-space view of optimization: the effect of a weight update in a
GNN is determined not only by the update matrix itself, but by the graph-propagated representations
on which it acts.

Additional GRAPHMUON Diagnostics
This appendix provides additional diagnostics for the graph-induced covariance used by GraphMuon.
These results are not required for the main correctness claims, but they help interpret when graph-
aware optimization is expected to matter and how the graph-awareness parameter τ affects the update

F.1
Effect of Graph-Awareness Strength
GraphMuon uses the interpolation parameter τ to control the strength of graph-aware orthogonal-
ization. When τ = 0, the covariance factors vanish and the update reduces to vanilla Muon. As τ
increases, the update direction increasingly respects the geometry induced by the graph-propagated
covariance.

vanilla Muon, while larger values place more emphasis on the graph-induced covariance structure.

validation accuracy over training.
Figure 7 shows that GraphMuon is reasonably robust to the choice of τ, although the best value
is somewhat dataset-dependent. On ogbn-arxiv, all tested values produce very similar final
validation accuracy, with only minor differences in early training speed. On ogbn-products,
moderate values (τ ∈[0.1, 0.3]) yield slightly stronger early progress and marginally better final
validation performance than the smallest tested value. On ogbn-mag, the effect of τ is more
pronounced: larger values generally improve optimization and lead to better validation accuracy,
while very small values converge more slowly and to slightly weaker solutions.
Overall, these results indicate that GraphMuon is not overly brittle to the choice of τ, but that tuning
τ can provide additional gains, especially on more challenging or structurally heterogeneous datasets
such as ogbn-mag. In practice, a small grid over τ is sufficient to identify a strong configuration.

G.4
Efficiency of the Graph-Aware Muon Implementation
We describe the implementation mechanisms used to keep the additional cost of GraphMuon modest
relative to vanilla Muon. The key design choice is that the optimizer reuses graph-propagated
activations already computed during the ordinary forward pass, and only estimates low-dimensional
covariance statistics from them.
Activation reuse. For a GCN layer, the forward pass computes the graph-propagated input
Hprop = ˆAH
before applying the learnable weight matrix. Our implementation stores a detached reference to
this tensor as cached_h_prop. The optimizer then reads this cached tensor at step time. Thus,
GraphMuon does not perform an additional forward pass and does not apply an additional graph
propagation inside the optimizer. The extra runtime cost at this stage is only the detach/reference
operation, while the extra memory cost is the lifetime of the cached Hprop tensor until the optimizer
step.

increasing peak CUDA memory, and incurs only 2.8% additional per-step runtime (1.411 versus
1.372 seconds). Thus, covariance sampling and eigendecomposition caching keep the additional
graph-aware optimization overhead modest in the evaluated settings.
G.5
Hardware, Software, and Baseline Implementations
All experiments were run on a single NVIDIA RTX 6000 GPU. GraphMuon was implemented
in PyTorch and integrated into a shared training pipeline used by all optimizers. Unless stated
otherwise, all methods use the same model architectures, dataset splits, input features, batching
scheme, evaluation protocol, and hyperparameter tuning budget.
For baselines, AdamW is the standard PyTorch implementation (torch.optim.AdamW). Muon
and GraphMuon are implemented directly in PyTorch within our training code. Shampoo is im-
plemented using the pytorch-optimizer library. For any remaining optimizer baselines, we
use the official or original reference implementations when available, adapting only the optimizer
interface required for compatibility with our training code. These adaptations do not change the
mathematical update rules of the baselines.
Runtime is measured on the same hardware for all methods. In time-to-target experiments, wall-clock
time is measured until the validation target is first reached. One-time dataset preprocessing is excluded
from the timing. We synchronize CUDA before recording timed intervals to avoid artifacts from
asynchronous GPU execution. All reported averages and standard deviations are computed over the
same set of random seeds.

G.8
Additional Architecture and Task Results
We extend the time-to-target evaluation across 15 configurations, covering GCN, GAT, GINE,
GraphGPS, and GraphGCN, as well as node classification, graph classification, and graph regression.
For each configuration, the target is the minimum mean validation loss attained by AdamW. We
report the first epoch and corresponding cumulative wall-clock time at which each optimizer reaches
this target.
Across all 15 configurations, GraphMuon reaches AdamW’s validation-loss target in fewer epochs,
and achieves the lowest wall-clock time-to-target in 13 of 15 configurations. It reaches the target in all
15 configurations, compared with 11 of 15 for Muon. The arithmetic-mean wall-clock speedup over
AdamW is 4.62× across all configurations. The strongest improvements include GAT on OGBN-MAG
(16.85×) and GraphGPS on OGBN-ARXIV (16.07×).
The gains are not universal in wall-clock time. On Citeseer with GCN and ZINC with GraphGCN,
GraphMuon requires fewer epochs but is slightly slower than AdamW in cumulative time. These
cases illustrate that improved epoch-level optimization does not always translate into wall-clock
acceleration, particularly when optimizer overhead is substantial relative to the model’s training cost.
