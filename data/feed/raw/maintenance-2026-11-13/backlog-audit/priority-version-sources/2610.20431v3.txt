Recursive self-improvement (RSI) in scientific research aims to improve agents’ research capabilities through their own experimental experience. Scientific agent swarms can pursue this goal when one agent’s findings and implementations advance another’s research. Two obstacles stand in the way. A failed experiment does not reveal whether its hypothesis or its implementation was at fault, and a program without overall gains can hide components that other agents need. We introduce SwarmRSI , a framework in which a swarm improves its research by evolving the research states its agents inherit, including hypotheses, programs and experimental evidence. Feedback-Guided Swarm Mutation uses other agents’ feedback to decide whether to revise a hypothesis or its program. Complementary Swarm Recombination tests retained hypotheses on other agents’ programs and combines complementary components. Recursive Swarm Evolution returns every outcome to a shared graph that guides which state each agent updates next. SwarmRSI achieves state-of-the-art performance across three benchmarks, scoring 76.2 on OpenProblems, attaining a 44.4% Match-SOTA rate on NatureBench, and earning medals on 81.8% of MLE-Bench Lite tasks. Across 90 NatureBench tasks, 77% of the best programs descend from feedback-guided mutation or recombination. Biologists further validate two generated programs in studies of intestinal and splenic immunity. The code is available 1 1 1 https://github.com/EvoScientist/SwarmRSI .

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) in scientific research aims to improve agents’ research capabilities through their own experimental experience ( Guo et al., 2026a ; Srikanth et al., 2026 ) . Scientific agent swarms offer a way to pursue this goal by coordinating idea generation, implementation, and evaluation across specialized agents ( Yang et al., 2025 ; Tang et al., 2025 ; Yamada et al., 2025 ; Lee et al., 2026 ) . Persistent memories and shared histories retain experimental experience for later research ( Lyu et al., 2026 ; Zhu et al., 2026 ; Du et al., 2026 ; Weng et al., 2026 ) , and accumulated histories can also support improvements to exploration strategies ( Zheng et al., 2026 ) . Together, these capabilities create an opportunity for agents exploring different directions to use one another’s findings and implementations in subsequent research.

However, sharing experience does not ensure that agents use it to make better research decisions. Program evolution refines executable candidates through execution feedback ( Jiang et al., 2025 ; Nam et al., 2025 ; Novikov et al., 2025 ; Liu et al., 2025 ) , while hypothesis evolution improves research directions through iterative generation and evaluation ( Gottweis et al., 2026 ; Wang et al., 2026a ; Liu et al., 2026 ; Jin et al., 2026 ) . As Figure 1 illustrates, promising hypotheses are abandoned after inadequate implementations, and useful components are overlooked in programs that perform poorly overall.

We hypothesize that these limitations arise from two interrelated dependencies in scientific research. The value of a hypothesis depends on how it is implemented and how rigorously it is tested. An unsuccessful experiment therefore leaves unclear whether the research direction or its implementation needs revision ( Ma et al., 2026 ; Koo et al., 2026 ) , and feedback from other agents identifies which changes are worth pursuing. Intermediate results also become stepping stones for other search paths ( Wang et al., 2019 ; Wang et al., 2020 ) , and their value depends on combinations with later findings or implementations. Their current performance thus underestimates their contribution to other agents’ future research. This leads to our central question of how scientific agent swarms can evolve toward recursive self-improvement.

To address this, we introduce SwarmRSI , a framework that evolves scientific agent swarms for recursive self-improvement. Each agent uses its hypotheses, programs, experimental evidence, and unresolved needs to plan further research, and a shared graph records these inheritable research states. In Feedback-Guided Swarm Mutation, agents revise hypotheses and programs using other agents’ experimental feedback. Performance differences inform hypothesis revision, and unmet research needs guide implementation changes. In Complementary Swarm Recombination, agents revisit retained hypotheses on other agents’ implementations and combine program components with complementary strengths. Recursive Swarm Evolution evaluates the resulting candidates, retains useful results, feedback and unresolved needs in the shared graph, and uses these states to decide which hypotheses agents revisit and which programs they refine. Each experiment thus updates the states that later agents start from.

We evaluate SwarmRSI on OpenProblems ( Luecken et al., 2025 ) , NatureBench ( Wang et al., 2026b ) and MLE-Bench Lite ( Chan et al., 2025 ) , 118 scientific coding and machine-learning engineering tasks, with two backbones. It achieves SOTA results on OpenProblems and NatureBench and outperforms hypothesis and program evolution agents with the same backbone. With DeepSeek-V4-Pro in one seed over all 90 NatureBench tasks, about 77% of its final best programs descend from a rebase, a crossover or an update triggered by feedback. Biologists reproduce two of its programs and use them in follow-up analyses of intestinal and splenic immunity.

Our main contributions are:

We formulate RSI for scientific agent swarms as evolving inheritable research states, so that each experiment updates the hypotheses, programs and evidence that later agents start from.

We introduce SwarmRSI , which updates these states through Feedback-Guided Swarm Mutation, Complementary Swarm Recombination and Recursive Swarm Evolution.

SwarmRSI achieves SOTA results on OpenProblems and NatureBench, most of its final NatureBench programs build on inherited states, and biologists reproduce its programs for immune analyses.

LLM agents automate scientific research through hypothesis generation, experiment implementation, and result evaluation ( Tang et al., 2025 ; Yamada et al., 2025 ) . Agents centred on programs search over or refine code with execution feedback ( Jiang et al., 2025 ; Nam et al., 2025 ) , R&D-Agent coordinates proposal generation and implementation through specialized roles ( Yang et al., 2025 ) , and systems with memory carry execution experience into later experiments ( Zhu et al., 2026 ; Du et al., 2026 ) . Closest to our setting, Arbor links explicit hypotheses to programs and experimental evidence in a persistent hypothesis tree ( Jin et al., 2026 ) , and SAGE evaluates competing failure explanations to direct interventions toward hypotheses, experimental design, or implementation ( Ma et al., 2026 ) . SwarmRSI likewise grounds hypotheses in experiments and lets agents use one another’s feedback and partial results.

Recursive self-improvement uses an agent’s own experience to improve the mechanisms that guide its subsequent activity. AIDE 2 retains accepted code modifications as the basis for further self-improvement ( Srikanth et al., 2026 ) , Dream-RSI refines exploration policies with replay simulators built from historical discovery trees ( Zheng et al., 2026 ) , MASS alternates workflow evolution with model fine-tuning ( Lee et al., 2026 ) , and AQuA uses recorded experiments to guide later research proposals ( Guo et al., 2026a ) . These methods improve the agent’s code, policy, workflow, or proposals. SwarmRSI instead evolves the research states that agents inherit, revising them with other agents’ feedback and reusing retained hypotheses and program components across agents.

SwarmRSI evolves scientific agent swarms by updating the research states agents inherit. Feedback-Guided Swarm Mutation uses other agents’ feedback to guide revisions, and Complementary Swarm Recombination reuses their hypotheses and program components. Recursive Swarm Evolution evaluates every candidate and retains its outcome, so each experiment informs subsequent research. Recursive Swarm Evolution decides which update to make next and returns every outcome to the shared graph. It maintains a hypothesis frontier ℱ H \mathcal{F}_{H} of active hypotheses and a program frontier ℱ P \mathcal{F}_{P} of valid program representatives. For H/P selection , which decides whether the next step revises a hypothesis (H) or a program (P), it scores each feasible frontier f ∈ { H , P } f\in\{H,P\} with a discounted-UCB index ( Auer et al., 2002 ; Garivier and Moulines, 2011 )

where μ ^ f \hat{\mu}_{f} is recent improvement per token cost, N f N_{f} is the discounted experiment count, and ψ f \psi_{f} is the saturated pressure from unresolved needs that f f can address. The weights β \beta and γ \gamma set exploration and request pressure. Untried frontiers and frontiers that have waited long take priority over the score.

Each candidate is executed and evaluated under the task protocol. Execution checks test completion and task compliance, and source and output comparisons check whether the intended change was implemented. The record ξ t \xi_{t} of every attempt, including failed ones and those without gain, updates the graph to 𝒢 t + 1 \mathcal{G}_{t+1} , from which the next acting agent assembles its state s t + 1 s_{t+1} . An implementation failure keeps the hypothesis and requests program repair, mixed gains and losses across instances request a revised hypothesis, and a new incumbent requests that retained hypotheses be realized on it. The loop repeats until budget B B is exhausted and returns the best valid program.

RQ3 : How do experimental feedback and reused intermediate results guide the swarm’s subsequent research within and across tasks? RQ4 : What are the main failure modes, and how frequent are they?

We evaluate SwarmRSI on scientific coding and machine-learning engineering tasks across three benchmarks. OpenProblems ( Luecken et al., 2025 ) contains six single-cell analysis tasks following TusoAI ( Turcan et al., 2026 ) . NatureBench ( Wang et al., 2026b ) contains all 90 scientific coding tasks across six domains. MLE-Bench Lite ( Chan et al., 2025 ) contains 22 competitions from its low-complexity split.

We compare against program evolution agents (AIDE and MLEvolve), hypothesis evolution agents (Arbor), domain-specific scientific agents (TusoAI and Biomni ( Huang et al., 2026 ) ), and general coding agents (Claude Code ( Anthropic, 2026 ) , Codex ( OpenAI, 2026a ) and Gemini CLI ( Google, 2026 ) ).

OpenProblems reports normalized task scores scaled by 100 and their unweighted mean. NatureBench reports Match-SOTA ( g ≥ 0 g\geq 0 ) and Surpass-SOTA ( g > 0.1 g>0.1 ) rates, where g g is the official direction-adjusted improvement over SOTA. MLE-Bench Lite reports Above Median and medal rates from official grading on the private test split. Rates retain all tasks in the denominator, including invalid or missing results. Match-SOTA comparisons with Arbor and AIDE on NatureBench use 95% paired bootstrap confidence intervals and McNemar tests, and other comparisons are descriptive. Token cost sums uncached input and output tokens over all LLM calls in a search.

SwarmRSI and rerun baselines use DeepSeek-V4-Pro ( DeepSeek, 2026 ) under matched settings for each benchmark. Results from our runs in Tables 2 – 3 are averaged over three random seeds. Published results keep the settings of their sources. We additionally evaluate SwarmRSI with Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview ( Google DeepMind, 2026 ) . In each run, all agents share one backbone and advance lineages that exchange results through the shared graph.

To answer our research questions, we compare benchmark performance, conduct component ablations, examine search trajectories, analyze annotated failures, and report biological case studies.

Tables 2 , 2 and 3 summarize benchmark performance, and we make three observations.

SwarmRSI outperforms domain-specific and general coding agents. With DeepSeek-V4-Pro, it reaches an OpenProblems average of 74.2 against 67.1 for TusoAI and exceeds Claude Code with the same backbone by 21.1 percentage points (pp) in NatureBench Match-SOTA.

SwarmRSI outperforms hypothesis and program evolution agents. With the same backbone, its NatureBench Match-SOTA rate exceeds Arbor by 7.8 pp and AIDE by 16.7 pp, both significantly. Its 81.8% Any-Medal rate on MLE-Bench Lite also exceeds Arbor and MLEvolve, and, as shown in Section B.6 , it uses fewer tokens than TusoAI and Arbor.

SwarmRSI remains strong across backbones. As shown in Figure 3 , with Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview it reaches the highest OpenProblems average of 76.2 and the same 44.4% NatureBench Match-SOTA rate as with DeepSeek-V4-Pro, which exceeds every compared method, and it keeps the 81.8% Any-Medal rate on MLE-Bench Lite.

Table 4 compares SwarmRSI with five variants on all 90 NatureBench tasks using DeepSeek-V4-Pro, grouped by the mechanism they remove and otherwise unchanged. w/o H-mutation only repairs programs under existing hypotheses, w/o P-mutation only revises hypotheses, w/o recombination disables rebase and crossover, w/o shared graph removes agents’ access to one another’s records, and w/o H/P selection replaces adaptive H/P selection with fixed H/P alternation. As shown in Table 6 , all variants lower mean g g with paired 95% CIs excluding zero, and we make three observations.

Feedback-Guided Swarm Mutation needs both revision modes. Removing H- or P-mutation lowers Match-SOTA by 10.0 and 13.3 pp, respectively. The two modes are complementary, and program repair contributes more.

Complementary Swarm Recombination improves solution quality. Disabling rebase and crossover lowers Match-SOTA by 6.7 pp and Surpass-SOTA from 15.6% to 7.8%, and its drop in mean g g excludes zero. RQ3 traces this gain to reused hypotheses and program components in most final programs.

Recursive Swarm Evolution depends on the shared graph and H/P selection. Match-SOTA drops by 7.8 pp without the shared graph, through which agents use other agents’ records, and by 8.9 pp without H/P selection, which also causes the largest drop in mean g g .

We examine how SwarmRSI uses feedback and retained research states during search. Figure 4 a,b compares diagnostics with AIDE and Arbor on all 90 NatureBench tasks, and Figure 4 c,d shows one run per method on two tasks.

SwarmRSI recovers more failed implementations and turns more of its hypotheses into improvements. Later refinement turns 35% of failed implementations into new best valid programs, compared with 12% for AIDE and 5% for Arbor. Of its proposed hypotheses, 62% lead to an improvement, against 17% for Arbor.

SwarmRSI revises with prior feedback. In one NatureBench seed with DeepSeek-V4-Pro, updates triggered by performance feedback or unresolved implementation requests reach a 28.4% rate of new bests, against 21.9% over all updates. On 00917, after nine candidates without improvement, two successive hypothesis mutations revise the program’s readout and reach Surpass-SOTA.

SwarmRSI reuses partial results across research trajectories. In the same seed, about 77% of final best programs descend from at least one rebase, crossover or update triggered by feedback, and these updates yield new bests more often than other updates at every stage of search. On 00639, crossover combines components from three programs to reach Match-SOTA, and a later rebase step activates retained message-passing and ranking-loss code that the program had not executed, further improving g g . On 00795, rebase tests an unrealized hypothesis on a later program, and crossover then reuses a component from a program that had not improved overall.

Inherited research states transfer to new tasks. As shown in Section C.2 , the state inherited after three generations of source tasks raises mean g g on 12 held-out NatureBench tasks from 12.01 to 21.89 and Match-SOTA from 41.7% to 86.1% over cold start.

We annotate 644 failed attempts from 35 SwarmRSI task trajectories that fall below Match-SOTA on NatureBench or earn no medal on MLE-Bench Lite. Two annotators judge implementation fidelity, execution success and improvement over the preceding best valid result, with Cohen’s κ = 0.92 \kappa=0.92 . Most failures are faithful interventions without gains. They account for 93.3% of these failures, and implementation or execution problems for 6.7%. We began with two obstacles for scientific agent swarms. A failed experiment does not show whether its hypothesis or its implementation was at fault, and a program without overall gains can still hold a component that another agent needs. SwarmRSI addresses both. Feedback-Guided Swarm Mutation uses other agents’ feedback to decide whether to revise a hypothesis or repair its program, and Complementary Swarm Recombination carries retained hypotheses and useful components into other agents’ work. Recursive Swarm Evolution returns every outcome to the shared graph, so each experiment changes the states that later agents start from. It achieves SOTA results on OpenProblems and NatureBench and outperforms baselines with the same backbone, most final NatureBench programs build on such reuse, and biologists reproduce its programs for follow-up analyses of intestinal and splenic immunity. A swarm thus improves its own research by evolving what its agents inherit.

This section supplements Section 3 with details of Recursive Swarm Evolution, execution feedback, and Complementary Swarm Recombination.

The execution harness and search controller perform the online audits automatically. The code audit uses execution status, artifact validity and evaluator feedback to check execution and the task contract. The scientific audit screens implementation evidence by comparing parent and offspring source code and recorded submitted outputs. It flags unchanged source or syntax trees, newly added functions without static name references, or identical submitted outputs when comparable output records are available. The controller uses these source/output flags to gate hypothesis credit. A valid scored candidate without such a flag receives hypothesis credit ( Potter and De Jong, 2000 ) . Flagged or unscored attempts retain program-level evidence and unresolved implementation requests. Hypothesis credit records a valid scored candidate passing the source/output checks. Improvement per cost drives H/P selection. Failed states and states without gain remain available in the graph together with their resulting programs, outcomes, and audit evidence.

An implementation failure retains the hypothesis and emits a deepen request for program refinement. Mixed per-instance gains and losses emit a broaden request for hypothesis exploration. An improved incumbent also creates deepen requests for hypotheses previously realized on older programs. These requests retain unresolved needs for later mutation or recombination.

We reviewed 35 failed SwarmRSI task trajectories across NatureBench and MLE-Bench Lite. The annotation unit was an individual attempt, assessed against the best preceding valid result using each benchmark’s metric direction. The final failure set comprised 644 attempts across these trajectories, after applying the exclusions below. OpenProblems reports continuous normalized scores without a binary success criterion, so no OpenProblems trajectories enter the failure set. The 35 trajectories comprise 32 NatureBench tasks below Match-SOTA and 3 MLE-Bench Lite competitions without a medal.

Annotators examined the proposed intervention, implementation and execution records, evaluator outputs, and preceding scores. Implementation or execution failure denotes an intervention that could not be reliably tested because of code or interface errors, execution interruptions, or proposal–implementation mismatches. No performance gain denotes a faithfully implemented and executed intervention with a valid evaluation that did not improve the preceding best valid result. Equal scores were treated as no gain. Recovered intermediate errors were judged by the final evaluated outcome. Successful verification or restoration of an existing result, and repetition of an attained metric ceiling, were excluded from the failure counts. Attempts with insufficient evidence to distinguish the two failure classes were also excluded.

Two annotators with PhDs in bioinformatics engineering reviewed the evidence using these criteria, with inter-rater agreement of Cohen’s κ = 0.92 \kappa=0.92 .

SwarmRSI requires an evaluator that can execute candidate programs, so research without executable feedback is outside its scope. Execution feedback does not always reveal whether the intended intervention was faithfully implemented, and performance gains alone do not establish its scientific mechanism. Rebase selects program bases by matching recorded implementation needs to program mechanism labels, a heuristic that does not measure what a program can do. Crossover applies only when a benchmark reports scores per instance. H/P selection adapts discounted UCB without inheriting its guarantees, and its benefit is established only empirically.

Evaluation covers comparisons under matched budgets on three benchmark families. The human failure analysis characterizes 644 annotated failed attempts from 35 task trajectories. Cross-task evidence comes from six source tasks, 12 held-out tasks and one backbone, and generations are confounded with the number of source tasks seen.

The evaluation does not separately assess literature synthesis ( Shi et al., 2025 ) , idea quality ( Sauter et al., 2026 ) , or peer-review quality ( Guo et al., 2026b ) . Generalization to complete research workflows ( Lu et al., 2024 ; Schmidgall et al., 2025 ; Lyu et al., 2026 ) and information-access agents ( Kanoulas et al., 2025 ; Cao et al., 2026 ) requires additional evaluation.

Other untested settings include legal reasoning ( Lyu et al., 2022 ; Lyu et al., 2023a ; Wu et al., 2023 ) , zero-shot entity extraction ( Wang et al., 2025 ) , conversational recommendation ( Zhang et al., 2024 ) , and shopping research ( Lyu et al., 2025c ) . These settings require task-specific objectives and evaluation protocols.

Benchmark gains do not establish factual reliability or alignment with human preferences, which require separate evaluation and optimization ( Lin et al., 2022 ; Lyu et al., 2024a ; Rafailov et al., 2023 ; Lyu et al., 2025b ) . We do not assess robustness to dataset shortcuts ( Lyu et al., 2023b ) , social stereotypes ( Parrish et al., 2022 ) , or cognitive biases ( Lyu et al., 2024b ; Lyu et al., 2025a ) , nor do we test harmful-content generation. Applications that affect people should preserve user agency and provide domain-appropriate human oversight ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) .

Generated programs and scientific interpretations require independent validation before use beyond the benchmark setting. The execution and scientific audits check task validity and implementation evidence; they do not certify security or scientific correctness. The biological case studies analyse existing datasets, and their associations do not establish causal mechanisms or clinical validity. Any deployment should restrict code execution, external-tool access and credentials to the approved scope, and review proposed uses for potential harm.

Data reuse should respect source licences, access restrictions and privacy requirements, with provenance and intended uses documented ( Gebru et al., 2021 ) . We report time budgets, hardware configurations and token consumption to support resource-aware comparisons ( Schwartz et al., 2020 ) . Token consumption is not a direct measurement of energy use or carbon emissions.
