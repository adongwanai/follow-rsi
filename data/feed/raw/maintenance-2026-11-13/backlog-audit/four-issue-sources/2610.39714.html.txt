MolRSI : A Recursive Self-Improving AI agent for Molecular Discovery Abstract Molecular discovery requires navigating vast combinatorial search spaces while balancing competing biological objectives. AI agents can automate expert-defined molecular design protocols, but automation alone does not address how to optimize workflows for new tasks or improve their development through experience. Here we introduce MolRSI , an AI agent that combines systematic optimization of workflows and model architectures with persistent cross-task memory to recursively improve its search policy for molecular discovery. Workflow performance, refinement outcomes, and execution feedback guide subsequent searches, allowing the agent to improve both its workflows and its strategy for developing them. We evaluate MolRSI in two challenging molecular discovery settings that require balancing competing properties. In antibiotic discovery, MolRSI developed a screening workflow balancing antibacterial activity, cytotoxicity and structural novelty, achieving an experimental hit rate of 15.9%, compared with 8.7% for a previous expert-led workflow using the same training data and screening library. In de novo peptide–MHC binder design, MolRSI developed workflows that jointly consider target binding and specificity, producing eight experimentally validated hits against MART-1 and SARS-CoV-1 pMHCs with preferential binding over tested off-targets while Claude Science completely failed. These findings demonstrate how experience-guided workflow optimization can address competing discovery objectives while improving the search strategies used in subsequent campaigns. Contact: Wengong Jin (wjin@broadinstitute.org). 1 Introduction Molecular discovery requires searching vast chemical and biological spaces for candidates that satisfy multiple competing objectives, including potency, binding specificity, toxicity, structural novelty, and stability [ ? ? ? ? ]. These demands have been traditionally addressed by experimental workflows comprised of library preparation, screening, counter-screening, and hit validation [ ? ? ]. Recently, computational workflows have shown great success in molecular design [ ? ? ? ]. They often involve different molecular representations, model architectures, training objectives, and optimization strategies with the available data [ ? ? ]. They must also be integrated with evaluation and selection procedures that reflect the discovery objectives [ ? ? ]. Virtual screening, for example, requires jointly considering predictions of activity [ ? ] and toxicity alongside structural constraints [ ? ? ], while protein design requires the coordination of target preparation, backbone generation [ ? ], sequence design [ ? ], and evaluation of binding and specificity [ ? ]. Decisions about both the models and their integration determine which candidates advance to experimental testing [ ? ? ], making task-specific model and workflow optimization central to successful discovery. Finding an effective workflow for a given task is a challenging search problem [ ? ? ]. The growing diversity of molecular representations, model architectures, training strategies, and optimization strategies creates a vast space of possible configurations whose performance varies across biological targets, available data, and desired properties (Figure 1a) [ ? ? ? ]. Navigating this space requires an effective search policy for designing and training models, evaluating candidate workflows, and deciding how to customize them [ ? ? ]. For human scientists, this policy is shaped by experience across projects. Previous successes and failures provide lessons in workflow and model engineering: which architectures suit the available data, which training strategies improve generalization, and which practical constraints must be addressed. These lessons help researchers identify strong starting points and prioritize beneficial changes (Figure 1b). As experience accumulates, scientists can adapt models and workflows to new problems with fewer rounds of trial and error. This expertise is particularly valuable, as the evaluation of each candidate requires expensive wet lab validation. Each project can contribute not only a workflow for a given task but also experience that improves the strategy used to develop future workflows. Recent advances in scientific agents have begun to automate aspects of model and workflow development that traditionally depend on human expertise [ ? ? ? ? ? ? ? ? ]. Biomni retrieves relevant tools, databases and software from a unified biomedical environment, then uses language model reasoning and code execution to compose them into workflows [ ? ]. In protein design, Claude Science has produced experimentally validated binders through campaigns guided by expert-written protocols specifying design objectives and evaluation criteria [ ? ? ]. Developing and executing these workflows produces evidence about which approaches succeed and which attempts fail [ ? ]. However, the reported studies do not establish how this experience improves workflow development in subsequent problems. When campaigns remain isolated, lessons from one project cannot inform the search policy for the next. Retaining and applying these lessons across tasks would allow agents to build on earlier work [ ? ? ] and develop new workflows with less repeated trial and error. Here we introduce MolRSI , a molecular discovery agent that couples model and workflow engineering with persistent cross-task memory to improve its search policy ( Figure 1 c). The agent converts scientific methods and associated code into validated, executable skills, then uses experience-guided Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) to assemble and refine candidate workflows, including their model architectures and training strategies. Inspired by how experienced researchers draw lessons from past projects, MolRSI records these choices and their outcomes in persistent memory together with the context of each task. Past workflow performance and modification outcomes guide selection and refinement on subsequent tasks, while records of execution failures and verified repairs help the agent resolve similar problems. Each search adds to this experience and changes how later models and workflows are developed, forming a cycle of recursive self-improvement (RSI). We evaluate MolRSI in virtual antibiotic screening and de novo peptide–MHC (pMHC) binder design, two challenging problems that require balancing competing objectives. These applications test our hypothesis that systematic optimization of models and workflows, guided by experience from previous tasks, can improve discovery under these constraints. In antibiotic discovery, MolRSI draws on experience from 25 molecular property prediction tasks to develop a customized screening workflow balancing antibacterial activity, cytotoxicity and structural novelty. Experimental testing against Staphylococcus aureus identifies 17 of 107 compounds with minimum inhibitory concentrations of 32 μ \mu g/mL or lower. The observed hit rate of 15.9% is substantially higher than the 8.7% achieved by our previous expert-driven workflow using the same training data and screening library [ ? ]. In pMHC binder design, MolRSI draws on experience from 14 minibinder design tasks to develop customized workflows for pMHCs. Experimental screening identifies eight hits against MART-1 and SARS-CoV-1 pMHCs, each showing preferential enrichment for its intended target over the tested off-targets, whereas the workflow developed by Claude Science yields no hits. Each task specifies a biological problem, available data, an evaluation criterion and a computational budget. An Explore Agent organizes task-relevant scientific papers and associated code, tools, benchmarks and algorithms into validated, executable skills. These skills form the resource library from which MolRSI constructs candidate workflows. MolRSI develops these workflows through experience-guided Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS), starting from configurations assembled from the resource library or retrieved from solution memory. Its search policy determines which workflows to explore, how to allocate search effort and how to refine and execute candidates.At each iteration, selection rules draw on workflows and scores in solution memory, combining performance on the current and related tasks with an exploration bonus [ ? ] for less-visited alternatives. Refinement memory supplies previous modifications and their outcomes to guide changes to the selected workflow. The agent then executes and evaluates the revised candidate, using execution memory to allocate resources and resolve errors. Search returns the workflow with the best development score within the task’s computational budget. Persistent memory carries the outcomes of these searches forward to guide later decisions. Each attempt updates the stored workflows, performance scores, modification outcomes and execution records. These updates change subsequent search decisions within the task and remain available for later tasks. Search experience can also lead to revised or additional skills. Searches on new tasks therefore use a policy informed by earlier outcomes and generate further experience that revises the policy applied to subsequent tasks, completing the RSI cycle (Methods and Sections S1.1 , S1.2 and S1.3 ). We assess the contribution of this cross-task policy adaptation by comparing MolRSI with and without RSI. Both settings use experiences obtained during the current task to guide search, whereas only MolRSI with RSI also draws on experience from previous tasks. Zero-search transfer tests direct reuse of previously developed workflow configurations without new search. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is one of the most pressing global public health threats, steadily eroding our ability to treat common bacterial infections and imposing a large and growing burden of mortality worldwide[ ? ]. The spread of antibiotic resistance creates an urgent need for compounds that inhibit bacterial growth while limiting toxicity to human cells. In our previous work [ ? ], we built an expert-driven virtual screening workflow that combined ensembles of Chemprop graph neural networks predicting activity against Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) with separate ensembles predicting cytotoxicity in human cell lines such as HepG2. We applied these models to more than 12 million compounds and used additional filters for undesirable substructures and similarity to known active compounds, followed by graph-based analyses that identified chemical substructures associated with predicted antibacterial activity. This workflow enabled the discovery of a new structural class of antibiotics. Developing such workflows requires navigating interdependent choices in molecular representation, model selection and training, screening criteria and candidate prioritization[ ? ? ]. We therefore use antibiotic discovery to assess whether MolRSI can autonomously find better virtual screening workflows and learn from its own searches to improve the search policy applied to subsequent tasks. We first benchmark its workflow search and then experimentally test compounds selected by the resulting virtual screening workflow. MolRSI outperforms existing agents in antibacterial activity prediction benchmarks. Accurate prediction of antibacterial activity is central to prioritizing compounds for experimental testing. We therefore benchmarked MolRSI and existing agents on six antibacterial activity prediction tasks, each corresponding to a different bacterial species ( Figure 2 a). We compared MolRSI with the scientific research agents Biomni, Claude Science and STELLA and general-purpose coding agents Codex and Claude Code [ ? ]. For this comparison, we first evaluated MolRSI without RSI, starting each task without experience from previous tasks. This establishes a baseline for assessing the contribution of RSI. MolRSI develops workflows by selecting and refining combinations of models and methods from the full resource library of 110 reusable skills, comprising 55 models, 48 methods and seven tools or resources ( Figure 2 b). All agents used the same five similarity-based data splits. Mean validation area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC) guides workflow selection, and mean held-out test AUPRC measures predictive performance (Methods and Sections S3.1 and S2 ). MolRSI achieves the highest mean test AUPRC among the evaluated agents, ranking first on four tasks and second on the remaining two. MolRSI ’s performance and search efficiency improves as it accumulates experience from more tasks. We next tested whether experience from other molecular prediction tasks improves the search policy used to develop antibacterial workflows. MolRSI first searched independently on 25 source tasks, comprising 22 Therapeutics Data Commons (TDC) [ ? ] tasks and three cytotoxicity tasks for HepG2, HSkMC and IMR-90 cells[ ? ] ( Table S3 ). The six antibacterial benchmark tasks were excluded from this experience pool ( Figure 2 c). MolRSI used workflow performance, refinement outcomes and execution feedback from these searches to guide workflow selection, refinement and execution on the antibacterial tasks (Methods and Section S3.2 ). Under the same search budget, MolRSI generally achieves higher mean test AUPRC than the baseline agents across the search trajectories ( Figure 2 d). On E. coli and N. gonorrhoeae , its first evaluated workflow already outperforms the final workflows selected by every baseline agent after 20 search iterations. To assess the contribution of RSI, we compared searches with and without experience from previous tasks using the same initial resource library, backbone model and search budget ( Figure 2 e). RSI improves mean test AUPRC on all six antibacterial tasks. We also included zero-search transfer to test whether previously developed workflows can be applied without further workflow optimization. This setting retains model fitting and validation on target data ( Section S3.3 ). Zero-search transfer outperforms search without RSI on S. aureus , A. baumannii and K. pneumoniae , achieving the highest mean test AUPRC among the three conditions on K. pneumoniae . Experience therefore improves subsequent search and, on several targets, provides workflows that outperform search from scratch without further optimization. We further examined whether the benefits of accumulated experience increase as more tasks contribute to it ( Figure 2 f). We increased the number of source tasks contributing experience from 2 to 25 and assessed workflow transfer to the six antibacterial targets. We averaged performance across all combinations containing the same number of source tasks to ensure that the observed trend is not driven by a particular task order. The mean test AUPRC of the transferred workflows increases from approximately 0.32 with two source tasks to approximately 0.37 with all 25. With experience from all 25 tasks, this performance exceeds the mean AUPRC of 0.361 achieved by search without RSI after 20 iterations. These results show that accumulating experience across more tasks makes increasingly effective workflows available for subsequent tasks, extending the benefits of RSI from improved search to better performance ( Section S3.2 ). We next evaluated whether the screening workflow developed by MolRSI can identify antibacterial compounds while balancing activity, cytotoxicity and structural novelty. We used the same training datasets and screening library of approximately 12.1 million compounds as in our previous expert-driven study [ ? ]. The workflow integrates optimized antibacterial activity and cytotoxicity predictors with structural filtering and diversity-based selection. Structural filters exclude specified antibiotic cores and compounds similar to known antibiotics, while cytotoxicity predictions for HepG2, HSkMC and IMR-90 cells inform candidate prioritization. Antibacterial activity ranking followed by selection across structural clusters yields 107 compounds for experimental testing ( Figure 3 a; Methods; Sections S3.4 and S3.4 ). Experimental validation identifies 17 compounds with minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of 32 μ \mu g/mL or lower against methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA). The hits occupy multiple regions of the chemical space ( Figure 3 b). Among these hits, C3 and C32 also exhibit potent activity against methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), with MICs of 1 and 8 μ \mu g/mL, respectively. C3 and C32 are ranked at 28th and 5th by predicted antibacterial activity among the tested compounds ( Figure 3 c). Both compounds inhibit MRSA at concentrations that do not cause cytotoxicity in each of the three types of human cells. Their half-maximal cytotoxic concentrations ( CC 50 \mathrm{CC}_{50} ) range from 6.4 to 17.5 μ \mu g/mL for C3 and from 24.2 to 54.7 μ \mu g/mL for C32. These measurements establish a separation between antibacterial potency and mammalian cytotoxicity under the tested conditions ( Figure 3 d,g; Section S3.6 ). MolRSI improves experimental hit rate and antibacterial activity prediction over the previous expert-driven workflow. The 17 hits among 107 tested compounds correspond to an experimental hit rate of 15.9%, compared with 8.7% (21 of 241 compounds) in the previous expert-driven study at the same MSSA MIC threshold [ ? ]. Thus, using the same training datasets and screening library, the workflow developed by MolRSI achieves an approximately 82% relative increase in observed hit rate ( Figure 3 e; Section S3.6.3 ). To assess predictive performance independently of differences in compound selection, we compared the antibacterial activity predictors on a shared held-out set of 271 compounds experimentally evaluated in the previous study ( Section S3.1 ). MolRSI achieves an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.828, compared with 0.590 for the previous predictor, and an area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC) of 0.300, compared with 0.090 ( Figure 3 f). Class I major histocompatibility complex (MHC-I) molecules display intracellular peptides on cell surfaces, providing access to disease-associated antigens for engineered cell therapies and protein therapeutics[ ? ? ]. Designing binders for these pMHC complexes requires recognition of the exposed peptide while avoiding the same MHC presenting peptides from healthy cells. This task is demanding because target and off-target complexes share the large MHC scaffold and may differ only in a few exposed peptide residues. In our previous work, an expert-driven workflow combined RFdiffusion [ ? ], ProteinMPNN [ ? ] and a pMHC-specialized AlphaFold2 model [ ? ? ] to generate small proteins that contact the presented peptide [ ? ]. The workflow produced binders that distinguished target pMHCs from closely related peptide-swapped off-targets and supported antigen-dependent T-cell activation when used in chimeric antigen receptors. Reaching this outcome required experts to choose peptide hotspots, generation strategies and criteria for both target binding and off-target discrimination. Here, we use this application to test whether MolRSI can automate and improve this process in three stages: benchmarking binder prioritization across protein targets, applying the accumulated experience to pMHC ranking and prospective design, and experimentally screening the resulting candidates. MolRSI improves binder prioritization than existing agents on a 14 target benchmark. Accurate candidate prioritization is central to recovering experimental hits from protein-design campaigns [ ? ]. Across 14 miniprotein targets, MolRSI more effectively prioritizes experimentally confirmed binders than the comparison agents. We asked MolRSI and four leading scientific or general-purpose agents [ ? ? ? ? ] to rank candidates in experimentally annotated tasks assembled from previous studies [ ? ? ? ]. Each task represents a different protein target, and all agents received the same inputs, objective and computational budget ( Sections 4.3.2 and S2.3 ). We first evaluated MolRSI without drawing on experience from previous tasks, while retaining access to the full resource library of 65 executable skills spanning candidate generation, structure prediction and scoring ( Figure 4 b). We assessed how effectively each workflow prioritizes experimentally confirmed binders using the Enrichment Factor (EF) at the top-ranked 1% and 2% of candidates ( Section 4.3.1 ). MolRSI achieved the highest macro EF at both cutoffs: 8.01 at 1%, compared with 7.62 for the next-best agent, and 7.29 at 2%, compared with 6.71 for the next-best agent ( Figure 4 a). Thus, even without prior-task memory, MolRSI develops a stronger binder-ranking workflow than the comparison agents. Additional benchmark results and comparisons with fixed expert-defined objectives are reported in Sections S4.3 and S4.2 . Cross-task experience helps MolRSI develop better pMHC binder design workflows. Drawing on experience from the miniprotein tasks, MolRSI achieves better candidate ranking on a new pMHC task. MolRSI developed and validated the pMHC ranking workflow using two gp100 datasets. MART-1 was reserved to test whether the resulting workflow generalizes to an unseen pMHC target ( Sections 4.3.1 and S4.1 ). We compared the resulting workflow with those developed by the four agent baselines. MolRSI achieves the highest MART-1 test enrichment, with EF@1% and EF@2% of 3.65 and 5.55, respectively ( Figure 4 c). None of the comparison agents identifies an experimentally confirmed binder among the top-ranked 1% of candidates, and the highest baseline EF@2% is 1.85. To isolate the contribution of RSI, we also compared against an otherwise identical MolRSI workflow search without access to accumulated experience from the 14 miniprotein tasks. Without this experience, the same system achieves an EF of 0.00 at both cutoffs. Additional AUPRC and EF results are reported in Section S4.3 . We next used the ranking workflow developed with accumulated experience to guide pMHC design workflow optimization for MART-1 and SARS-CoV-1. For each target, a peptide-swapped off-target retains the same MHC molecule, making peptide discrimination part of the design setting ( Sections 4.3.3 and S4.4 ). MolRSI kept the ranking procedure fixed while conducting a 30-round search over generation methods and settings, using the mean score of the top-ranked 5% of candidates as the search objective. The best observed objective reaches 6.76 for MART-1 and 7.56 for SARS-CoV-1, improvements of 66% and 28% over the initial configurations, respectively ( Figure 4 d). We then compared the resulting pMHC design pipelines with Claude Science under the same target and off-target specifications, design constraints and computational budget. We selected Claude Science for this comparison because it achieves the highest average macro EF among the agent baselines on the miniprotein benchmark and previously demonstrated autonomous design of experimentally validated protein binders [ ? ]. Each agent retained its own design workflow ( Sections 4.3.3 , S4.5 and S4.5 ). To compare their outputs, we evaluated the top 500 selected candidates using the fixed MolRSI ranking score and the sum of ipSAE min z-scores [ ? ] from the three prediction models used by Claude Science. Z-scores were calculated separately for each target and prediction model, pooling candidates from both design methods. By both measures, MolRSI designs have higher median on-target scores than Claude Science designs for MART-1 and SARS-CoV-1 ( Figure 4 e). This difference emerged within a broadly shared structural landscape: designs from all three methods occupied overlapping regions of the structural-similarity map, while the recovered hits spanned multiple structural neighborhoods ( Figure 5 b). These models are consistent with the peptide-engaging recognition mode sought by the workflow, while the screening results establish experimental enrichment and target preference for designs recovered by MolRSI . 3 Discussion MolRSI improves molecular-discovery workflows through recursive self-improvement of the search policy used to develop them. Performance on related tasks changes the selection scores that determine where the agent directs further exploration. Current-task evaluations revise these priorities, while earlier modifications and execution outcomes guide refinement and repair. The policy therefore changes throughout workflow development and carries the effects of earlier searches into subsequent tasks. Those searches produce new results that revise later decisions, completing the recursive process. This links two levels of improvement. At the task level, the agent selects and refines computational methods to produce an effective workflow. Across tasks, the results of these attempts change how it approaches the next search. The computational comparisons show that adapting this policy helps the agent make better use of a fixed search budget. Under matched conditions, incorporating prior-task results improves the workflows selected for antibacterial prediction and pMHC ranking. The antibacterial search trajectories also show that effective workflows can be found early, sometimes exceeding the final performance of baseline searches at the first evaluation. The resulting workflows can also remain useful beyond their original tasks. Direct transfer outperforms search from scratch on several antibacterial targets, providing an additional benefit of earlier workflow development. The retrospective pool analysis further identifies better available workflows as experience covers more source tasks. Together, these findings show how earlier searches can supply effective configurations and improve the policy used to develop new ones. The experimental studies demonstrate why improved workflow development matters for molecular discovery. In antibiotic discovery, the MolRSI workflow achieves a higher observed hit rate than the previous expert-driven workflow using the same training data and screening library [ ? ]. Evaluation on a shared held-out compound set supports the predictive value of the workflow alongside its ability to prioritize active compounds for testing. The pMHC application extends the framework to design, where selecting candidates requires considering both target binding and discrimination against related off-targets. The ranking workflow developed with prior experience guides subsequent generation and selection, yielding binders with preferential binding to their intended complexes over tested off-targets. The workflow developed by Claude Science yields no hits under the same screening conditions. These experiments assess complete workflows that coordinate several computational methods. Their outcomes show the practical value of improving how such methods are selected, combined and refined before candidates advance to experimental testing. These results also suggest how scientific agents that improve through use should be assessed. A successful final workflow establishes performance on a particular biological problem. Policy improvement concerns how the agent conducts subsequent searches, including which alternatives it explores and how effectively it uses the available budget. Comparisons between search with prior experience, search from scratch and direct transfer help distinguish the value of inherited configurations from the benefit of further workflow development. This makes the decisions involved in developing workflows open to systematic evaluation. Which methods are combined, which modifications are pursued and which attempts fail all provide evidence that can change later search priorities. Such evidence complements the scientific knowledge embodied in existing models and tools. Future benchmarks could assess how effectively an agent adapts its search policy over successive tasks. Further progress depends on whether policy updates remain useful as biological targets and evaluation criteria change. The variation among experience pools highlights the importance of the contributing tasks, while current-task evaluation provides a way to reassess priorities inherited from earlier searches. Biological validation remains necessary to establish whether better computational scores lead to more effective molecular candidates. Future systems could incorporate experimental outcomes into policy updates, allowing laboratory successes and failures to influence which workflows receive further exploration. This would connect the improvement of search strategies more closely to the biological goals of discovery. More broadly, MolRSI shows that scientific experience can be transferred between related tasks to improve the policy that guides workflow development. This provides a basis for discovery systems that refine their search strategies through use, with each project contributing to how future problems are approached. 4 Methods 4.1 MolRSI framework 4.1.1 Resource library construction MolRSI uses an Explore Agent to build the resource library. The agent searches for scientific papers and their associated code repositories, tools, benchmarks and algorithms relevant to the task. It uses the task description and data characteristics to formulate queries about data representations, model architectures, training objectives and domain constraints. For each retrieved resource, the Explore Agent creates a reusable skill describing how to use it. Each skill specifies the resource’s purpose, software dependencies, input and output requirements and execution steps, with references to available code, documentation and usage examples. The agent checks each skill’s syntax, dependencies and interfaces during library construction. When a newly constructed skill is first used for a task, the agent runs a usage example in an isolated environment. If execution fails, it uses the error messages and repository information to revise the code, dependencies or environment settings, then reruns the example. Skills that pass execution validation are tagged as validated and retained for reuse. Skills that still fail after repair are removed, so that only validated skills are available for subsequent tasks. Full implementation details are given in Section S1.1 . 4.1.2 Persistent memory MolRSI retains experience from workflow development and evaluation in solution, refinement and execution memory. These memories are updated during each task and retained for use in later tasks. MolRSI uses the accumulated experience to guide later iterations of the same search and searches on new tasks. Experience from previous tasks could also be used to select an existing workflow without a new search, a mode referred to as zero-search transfer. Solution memory. Solution memory stores each workflow’s executable code, configuration, execution status and any available development scores. Development scores are calculated using the task-specific evaluation criterion to compare workflows during search. The configuration specifies the component methods, data processing, model architecture, parameter settings and training or generation procedures. Each score is stored with the task, dataset, evaluation metric, and data split and random seed identifiers where applicable. MolRSI uses the stored workflows and scores to select workflows for further refinement on the current task or transfer to a new task. Refinement memory. Refinement memory stores the original and revised workflows, the changes made and the reasons for proposing them. For revisions that complete evaluation, it also stores the development scores before and after the change, measured under the same task-specific evaluation protocol, and their difference. Modifications are retained whether performance improves, declines or remains unchanged. Revisions that fail during execution are stored with their failure status. MolRSI uses these previous modifications and their outcomes when proposing further changes on the current task or related tasks. Execution memory. Execution memory stores the computational requirements of workflows and the skills available in the resource library. Runtime, GPU time, processing speed and GPU memory use are stored together with the dataset, dataset size, hardware configuration and software environment. MolRSI uses these measurements and their associated execution conditions to plan computing resources for later runs. When execution fails, execution memory stores the stage at which the failure occurs, the error messages and logs, and the cause when identified. It also stores each attempted repair, including changes to code, dependencies, environment settings or resource allocation, and whether the repair succeeds. When similar errors occur in the same or later tasks, MolRSI retrieves the previous repairs and their outcomes to guide further debugging. Details of memory storage and updates are given in Section S1.2 . 4.1.3 Recursive self-improvement of the search policy MolRSI uses experience from its own workflow searches to improve its search policy. Evaluation results from the current task and experience retained in persistent memory guide which workflows to explore, which modifications to pursue and how to resolve execution failures. Each attempt provides further evidence about workflow performance, the effects of modifications and execution requirements. This experience informs subsequent search decisions within and across tasks, completing the feedback loop through which recursive self-improvement occurs. Workflow search uses experience-guided Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) [ ? ]. Initial workflows form the roots of separate trees, with each node representing a workflow. A workflow selected for modification is the parent, and a revised version is its child. Workflows based on a common model or computational approach are grouped into families. Each search iteration comprises the following five steps: Step 1: Selecting workflow families. MolRSI selects a workflow family using current task performance, an exploration bonus and a historical transfer score. For families already evaluated on the current task, the performance term uses the best development score obtained in earlier iterations. The exploration bonus [ ? ] is larger for less visited families. The historical transfer score summarizes the family’s performance on related tasks using solution memory. Historical experience guides initial exploration, and current task scores are incorporated as evaluations become available. The family with the highest combined score is selected. The selection rule and historical transfer score calculations are given in Section S1.3 . Step 2: Selecting parent workflows. MolRSI samples a parent workflow from those already evaluated on the current task. Sampling favours better development performance, penalizes workflows that have already produced more candidates, and incorporates historical transfer scores from solution memory. The sampling rule and eligibility criteria are given in Section S1.3 . Step 3: Proposing workflow modifications. MolRSI retrieves previous modifications and their outcomes on related tasks from refinement memory. It uses these modifications and outcomes to propose changes to the selected parent workflow, such as replacing a method, changing how components are combined or adjusting parameter settings. Each proposal specifies the changes and their rationale. Before execution, MolRSI checks that the proposed workflow follows the required format, does not duplicate an existing candidate and is feasible to run. Step 4: Executing and evaluating workflows. Using the resource requirements stored in execution memory, MolRSI configures the runtime environment and allocates computing resources. It then runs the revised workflow and assesses its performance using the task-specific evaluation procedure. If execution fails, MolRSI first searches execution memory for similar errors and the repairs that have resolved them. When relevant experience is available, it uses the previous repair to guide changes to the code, dependencies or environment settings. Otherwise, it diagnoses the problem from the current error messages and makes the necessary changes. The workflow is rerun after each repair. If execution still fails, MolRSI uses the new error messages to guide further changes, up to a predefined limit on repair attempts and within the task’s computational limits. Successful evaluations provide development scores for subsequent search iterations. Step 5: Updating search policy to guide subsequent searches. After each execution attempt, MolRSI stores the workflow, its execution status and any available development score in solution memory. It adds the modification and its outcome to refinement memory and stores runtime, resource use, errors and repair outcomes in execution memory. MolRSI then uses the updated memories to select families and parents, propose modifications, allocate resources and repair execution failures in later iterations (Steps 1–4). We denote the effective policy governing the decisions in Steps 1–4 by π t ​ ( a ∣ s ) \pi_{t}(a\mid s) , the distribution over search actions a a for the current task and search state s s . Let M t M_{t} denote persistent memory after t t updates and E t E_{t} the new execution and evaluation experience. The policy before and after a memory update is expressed as π t ​ ( a ∣ s ) \displaystyle\pi_{t}(a\mid s) = π ⁡ ( a ∣ s ; M t ) , \displaystyle=\pi(a\mid s;M_{t}), (1) M t + 1 \displaystyle M_{t+1} = Update ⁡ ( M t , E t ) , \displaystyle=\operatorname{Update}(M_{t},E_{t}), π t + 1 ​ ( a ∣ s ) \displaystyle\pi_{t+1}(a\mid s) = π ⁡ ( a ∣ s ; M t + 1 ) . \displaystyle=\pi(a\mid s;M_{t+1}). The updated policy guides further searches, whose results supply experience for the next memory update ( Figure 1 ). This feedback loop implements recursive self-improvement of the effective search policy. MolRSI uses the outcomes of its own searches to focus exploration on promising workflows, reduce repeated unsuccessful modifications and reuse effective repairs. The same process operates within a task and across tasks as experience accumulates. Search stops when the task-specific limit on evaluation attempts or computation time is reached. MolRSI returns the successfully evaluated workflow with the best development score on the current task. MolRSI uses experience from previous tasks to select a workflow for direct transfer or as the starting point for a new search. It first identifies the best matching previously completed task, referred to as the source task, using task type, evaluation metric, dataset size and task description similarity. It then retrieves the workflow with the best development score on that source task from solution memory. In zero-search mode, the retrieved configuration is applied to the new task without searching over alternative workflows. Model parameters are fitted using the new task’s training data where required. Alternatively, the retrieved workflow serves as the starting point for further search using Steps 1–5, within the task-specific limits on evaluation attempts or computation time. 4.2 MolRSI for antibiotic discovery 4.2.1 Datasets and benchmark evaluation Source tasks for experience accumulation. We used 25 source tasks to accumulate experience for antibiotic workflow development: 22 absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion and toxicity tasks from the Therapeutics Data Commons (TDC) [ ? ], together with three cytotoxicity tasks for HepG2, HSkMC and IMR-90 cells from our previous study[ ? ]. The TDC tasks used the official benchmark partitions and task-specific evaluation metrics. The cytotoxicity datasets were obtained from our previous study, with the same compound structures and experimental measurements. Each cytotoxicity dataset was divided into training, validation and test sets at proportions of 8:1:1 using molecular similarity, with a Tanimoto threshold of 0.40. Five splits were generated using different random seeds. Antibacterial target tasks. We evaluated MolRSI on six antibacterial activity prediction tasks for Staphylococcus aureus , Pseudomonas aeruginosa , Acinetobacter baumannii , Escherichia coli , Klebsiella pneumoniae and Neisseria gonorrhoeae . These tasks were excluded from the source experience pool. The datasets were obtained from our previous study, with the same compound structures and experimental measurements. Each dataset used five similarity-based training, validation and test splits with a Tanimoto threshold of 0.40, proportions of 8:1:1 and the same five random seeds used for the cytotoxicity tasks. All methods used identical splits. Each task involved a single workflow search, during which every candidate was fitted and evaluated across all five splits. Mean validation AUPRC guided the search, and the workflow with the highest value was selected. Test AUPRC is reported as the mean and sample standard deviation across the five splits. Test outcomes were not used to guide workflow search. Activity S. aureus held-out test set. For the predictive comparison in Figure 3 f, we used a held-out set of 271 compounds from our previous study [ ? ]. This set comprised 241 candidate compounds and 30 low predicted activity controls; the 12 known antibiotic class positive controls were excluded. MolRSI and the published antibacterial activity model were evaluated on the same compounds using the same experimental activity labels. AUROC and AUPRC are reported for both models. Dataset sizes and evaluation metrics for all datasets are provided in Section S3.1 , together with the split sizes and random seeds used for workflow development. We benchmarked MolRSI with RSI and MolRSI without RSI against Biomni, Claude Science, STELLA, Codex and Claude Code on the six antibacterial target tasks ( Figure 2 ). All agents received the same task prompt, datasets and development objective, with up to 20 attempted workflow evaluations per task. Each agent retained its native tools and workflow development procedures. MolRSI and all agent baselines except Codex used Claude Sonnet 5; Codex used GPT-5.5. MolRSI accessed its backbone model through the Anthropic API, whereas Biomni ran on its own platform. Complete task prompts, model identifiers, API settings and baseline configurations are provided in Section S2.2 . 4.2.2 Evaluation of recursive self-improvement MolRSI searched for workflows independently on the 25 source tasks described in Section 4.2.1 . Experience from these searches was pooled in persistent memory before evaluation on the antibacterial target tasks. We compared MolRSI with and without RSI. MolRSI with RSI began with the accumulated source memory, whereas MolRSI without RSI began without experience from previous tasks. Both conditions used the same resource library and backbone model, together with the data partitions, evaluation procedure and workflow evaluation budget specified in Section 4.2.1 . Memory was updated during each search to guide subsequent iterations. Experience generated on one antibacterial task was not passed to the other antibacterial tasks. For the search trajectories in Figure 2 d, we identified the workflow with the highest mean validation AUPRC obtained so far at each iteration. Its test performance was calculated after the search to construct the plotted trajectory. For zero-search transfer ( Figure 2 e), MolRSI first identified the best matching source task using task type, evaluation metric, dataset size and task description similarity. It then selected the workflow with the best validation performance on that source task. Both selections were made before evaluating the workflow on the antibacterial task. Model parameters were fitted using the target task’s training data where required, and the transferred workflow was evaluated without further workflow search. One workflow from each of the 25 source tasks was transferred to each antibacterial task, yielding 150 source target evaluations. Experience pools contained 2–25 source tasks. For each pool, the transfer test AUPRC on each antibacterial task was identified retrospectively, and these values were averaged across the six tasks. The mean was calculated over all source task combinations of each size, with variation among combinations estimated from 2,000 random orderings of the source tasks. The transfer evaluations and calculation of means and percentile ranges are described in Sections S3.2 and S3.2 . 4.2.3 Virtual screening and compound selection MolRSI uses the experience accumulated across source tasks to guide the development of a virtual screening workflow for identifying candidate antibiotics against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). The workflow combines antibacterial activity prediction with cytotoxicity prediction for HepG2, HSkMC and IMR-90 cells. The activity models were trained on methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA; strain RN4220). All activity and cytotoxicity models used the same training datasets as our previous study [ ? ] and both studies used the same screening library. Screening began with structural filtering. Compounds with a maximum Tanimoto similarity of 0.40 or greater to the antibacterial reference set were excluded. Structural filtering also removed compounds matching PAINS [ ? ] or Brenk alerts [ ? ] and specified antibiotic cores. Cytotoxicity was then predicted separately for the three cell types. The HepG2 and IMR-90 predictors combined MiniMol embeddings [ ? ] with Morgan fingerprints [ ? ] and RDKit descriptors [ ? ], whereas the HSkMC predictor used MiniMol embeddings. Cytotoxicity filtering used a score threshold of 0.40 for each cell type. Model architectures, reference compounds and filter settings are described in Sections S3.4 and S3.4 . The remaining compounds were ranked by a weighted combination of predictions from two MiniMol-based variants and a LANTERN–RADR ensemble, with weights of 0.55, 0.30 and 0.15, respectively. Each component prediction was averaged across five fitted models. Butina clustering [ ? ] grouped structurally similar compounds, and candidates were selected across clusters to retain chemical diversity. We selected and experimentally tested 107 compounds. Activity against MRSA was assessed during experimental validation. Compound identities, prediction scores and sourcing details, including supplier substitutions and exceptions to the cytotoxicity criterion, are provided in Section S3.4 . 4.2.4 Experimental validation Antibacterial activity assays. First, ranking workflow development on 14 miniprotein-binder tasks supplied protein-domain source experience. Second, MolRSI used the resulting experience memory to develop workflows for one pMHC binder design task. Third, the selected pMHC workflow guided prospective binder design workflow optimization for MART-1 and SARS-CoV-1 pMHC complexes. Miniprotein-binder tasks. We assembled experimentally annotated de novo miniprotein-binder datasets for 14 protein targets from three published studies [ ? ? ? ]. Each target defined a task using a leave-one-target-out partition: workflow search used the other 13 targets, and the selected workflow was then evaluated on the held-out target. We retained all 1,565 positive designs and sampled up to ten negatives per positive within each target, yielding 17,215 designs. Target identities, sample counts and label definitions are reported in Section S4.1 . pMHC task. The pMHC benchmark comprised three target datasets: two for gp100 and one for MART-1. Both gp100 datasets were used directly for workflow search, while MART-1 was reserved for testing. Each candidate was represented as a four-chain complex comprising the HLA heavy chain, β 2 \beta_{2} -microglobulin, the presented peptide and the designed binder. The datasets were generated using the experimental screening procedure described by Liu et al. [ ? ]. Peptide identities, dataset sizes and label definitions are reported in Section S4.1 . MolRSI selected and configured components from the protein resource library, which contained 65 executable skills comprising 28 models and 37 registered scoring terms ( Figure 4 b). For ranking workflow development, these components covered input and feature preparation, complex-structure prediction, sequence and interface scoring, and score aggregation. Each candidate workflow mapped a target–binder pair to a scalar ranking score. AUPRC on development data from the 13 non-held-out targets guided workflow search and selection within each miniprotein-binder task, whereas AUROC guided search and selection for the single pMHC task. Benchmark baselines and budget. We compared MolRSI with Biomni [ ? ], Claude Science [ ? ], STELLA [ ? ] and Codex [ ? ]. For each benchmark task, all agents received the same task inputs, data partitions, and evaluation objective, with a budget of 300 A100 GPU-hours and 20 attempted workflow evaluations. Each agent retained its native tools and workflow-development procedures. Folded structures and the scores produced by each model were cached and reused across workflow evaluations to avoid repeated computation. Task instructions and baseline configurations are provided in Supplementary Information S2 and S2.3 . Memory ablation. We compared pMHC workflow search initialized with pooled experience from the 14 miniprotein-binder tasks against search without this prior experience. The initial memory contained no pMHC or prospective design outcomes. Both conditions used identical resource libraries, candidate data, search controllers, evaluation objectives and procedures, and the per-task budget specified above. Memory was updated during search in both conditions, so the comparison isolated the contribution of prior cross-task experience. 4.3.3 Binder generation and candidate selection Generation-workflow optimization. The prospective pipeline combined candidate generation, hotspot-contact filtering, inverse folding and ranking. MolRSI optimized generation methods and configurations separately for each target, using RFDiffusion3 [ ? ], Proteina-Complexa [ ? ], PXDesign [ ? ], BoltzGen [ ? ] and the MOSAIC [ ? ] simplex accelerated proximal gradient method (APGM). Each target-specific search received up to 30 attempted rounds and 1,000 A100 GPU-hours. The searches ran in parallel without sharing evaluations or search records. For each evaluated configuration, we generated 20 backbones with binder lengths of 80–120 residues. SolubleMPNN [ ? ] generated eight sequences per retained backbone, with cysteine excluded from the sequence alphabet. The ranking procedure selected in the memory-guided pMHC search remained fixed throughout prospective search and candidate selection. The mean score among the top-ranked 5% of generated candidates served as the configuration-selection objective. Final candidate generation and selection. After each target-specific search, the three highest-scoring workflows each generated 8,000 additional designs. Candidates from the search and expansion stages were pooled and deduplicated. The Explore Agent generates task-specific queries about scientific methods, software and benchmark evaluation using the task description and available dataset information. It searches literature and web sources, removes duplicate results and ranks the remaining resources by relevance to the task. The retrieved material is summarized to identify models, methods, tools, benchmarks and associated implementations for skill construction. Resource analysis and skill construction. The Explore Agent examines the retrieved resources and their available code, documentation and usage examples. It prioritizes resources for skill construction according to task relevance and the requested skill categories. When repository code is available, static analysis of Python abstract syntax trees recovers public interfaces and their arguments. These interfaces, together with model definitions and training or inference examples, inform the skill instructions. Additional searches supply supporting repositories or documentation when the initial material is incomplete. Library organization and skill contents. A shared index lists each skill’s name, category and purpose. Each skill describes how to use the resource and links these instructions to available implementations, dependencies, usage examples and source references. Where relevant, it also specifies feature representations and whether computed features could be reused. The contents are summarized in Table S1 . Checks during construction. The Explore Agent checks package identity and examines Python usage examples for syntax and import-name inconsistencies. When an execution environment is available, it also checks dependency availability and selected interfaces. Detected problems prompt revisions to installation instructions, dependencies or usage examples. These checks precede execution validation when a skill is first used. Execution validation and reuse. When a newly constructed skill is first used, MolRSI runs a usage example in an isolated environment. Execution errors guide repairs to the code, dependencies or environment settings. The example is rerun until it completes successfully or reaches the repair-attempt limit. Skills that pass are tagged as validated and retained in the resource library for reuse. Skills that remain unsuccessful are removed. Incremental expansion. The Explore Agent could expand an existing resource library by retrieving additional resources and identifying skills not already present. Existing skills are retained. New skills undergo the same construction checks and first-use execution validation, and the shared index is updated to include them. Table S1: Contents of the resource library. S1.2 Persistent memory Workflow development generates experience about candidate performance, proposed modifications and execution. MolRSI retains this experience in solution, refinement and execution memory, together with the tasks and conditions under which it was obtained. These memories guide later search decisions within and across tasks ( Figure S1 ). Solution memory. For each evaluated workflow, solution memory stores the task description, task type, dataset and dataset size, together with the workflow’s executable code, configuration and execution status. The configuration specifies the workflow family, component models and methods, architecture, hyperparameters, data processing, training objective and training or generation procedure. Development scores are retained with their evaluation metric and, where applicable, data split and random seed. A workflow that failed before evaluation retains its failure status without receiving a development score. Scores from completed workflows provide evidence about which families performed well on related tasks and which individual workflows are suitable for further refinement or transfer. The historical score normalization, family selection and parent selection procedures are specified in Section S1.3 . Refinement memory. Refinement memory stores the original workflow, the revised workflow, the task and the proposed changes. Changes are described by the affected component and its settings, such as an architecture change, the use of parameter-efficient fine-tuning, a different loss function, altered data processing or a change in learning rate. The agent’s reason for proposing a change is retained alongside the result of testing it. When the original and revised workflows are evaluated under the same protocol, both development scores and their difference are stored. Improvements, declines, unchanged scores and execution failures are all retained. If a proposal changes several components, its outcome is associated with the combined modification. For a subsequent proposal, the agent retrieves relevant earlier changes and their outcomes, including previous attempts derived from the selected parent workflow. Execution memory. Execution memory stores the execution history of workflows and the usable skills in the resource library. For each attempt, it retains the dataset and its size, hardware and software environment, execution status and available runtime measurements. Resource measurements include elapsed time, GPU time, processing speed and GPU memory use. Where measured, training time per sample per epoch relates runtime to dataset size and training duration. These measurements inform later resource allocation and timeout settings. For each failure, the memory retains the failed stage, error messages, execution logs and the cause when identified. It also stores each attempted repair, including changes to code, dependencies or environment settings, and whether re-execution succeeded. This experience guides repairs when similar errors recur. Updates within and across tasks. Memory is updated after each execution attempt and used to guide subsequent iterations. Experience retained from completed tasks could also guide later searches or direct workflow transfer. When an evaluation used fixed source-task experience, each target began with the same experience, and updates from one target were not shared with other targets. Application-specific experience pools are described in Sections S3.2 and S4.1 . Held-out test scores are excluded from memory updates and search decisions. Section S1.3 describes how the updated memory guides the search policy. S1.3 Recursive self-improvement of the search policy Search representation. MolRSI uses experience-guided Monte Carlo tree search over a forest of executable workflows. Initial workflows form the roots of separate trees, and each node represents a concrete workflow with its specification, execution status, development scores, resource use, logs and generated artifacts. A workflow selected for modification is the parent, and the revised workflow is its child. An edge represents the proposed changes, such as a different representation, architecture, objective, data-processing procedure or composition of methods. Workflows based on a common model or computational approach are grouped into families. The five steps below specify how current-task observations and historical experience guide search decisions. Step 1: Selecting workflow families. Family selection combines the best available current-task development performance, an exploration bonus and a historical transfer score. Historical workflow scores are first standardized within each source task and then aggregated using task-similarity weights. The family-level score enters selection as an additive term. The normalized sampler requires non-negative weights and a positive total weight. Step 3: Proposing workflow modifications. The agent retrieves previous modifications, their rationales and their outcomes from refinement memory to guide changes to the selected parent workflow. Each proposal specifies the changes and their rationale. The proposed workflow is checked for the required format, duplication of an existing candidate and feasibility of execution. Malformed, duplicate or infeasible proposals do not become completed workflow nodes. The proposed modification and its observed outcome are added to refinement memory after execution, including changes that reduced performance or failed to execute. Step 4: Executing and evaluating workflows. The proposed child workflow is implemented and run in an isolated environment using the task-specific evaluation procedure. Execution memory informs environment settings and resource allocation. If setup or execution fails, the agent first searches execution memory for similar errors and successful repairs. Relevant experience guides changes to the code, dependencies or environment settings. If no suitable repair is available or the problem persists, the agent diagnoses the current failure and proposes further changes. The workflow is rerun after each repair, within the repair-attempt limit and computational budget. Successful evaluations supply development scores; all attempts retain their execution status, logs, resource measurements and repair outcomes. Failed evaluations count toward the workflow-evaluation allowance, but repairs of the same candidate do not consume additional evaluations. Step 5: Updating search policy to guide subsequent searches. After each attempt, MolRSI adds the workflow and its development score or failure status to solution memory. It stores the proposed modification and its outcome in refinement memory, and runtime, resource use, errors and repair outcomes in execution memory. The corresponding node and edge in the search forest are also updated. The updated memories guide subsequent family selection, parent sampling, modification proposals and execution. These updates change the effective search policy π t \pi_{t} , whose subsequent evaluations generate experience for the next memory update. This feedback implements the RSI loop expressed by M t M_{t} , E t E_{t} and π t \pi_{t} in Equation 1 . Historical and current task updates. Current task executions update development scores, visit counts and the experience used for subsequent refinement and execution. When the source memory was fixed for an evaluation, its historical transfer scores remained unchanged during that target-task search. Under continued use across tasks, experience from newly completed tasks could contribute to transfer estimates for later searches. The bounded transfer scores serve as empirical selection priors; their boundedness alone does not establish a standard target-task or node-level regret guarantee. Search termination and returned workflow. Search stops when the task-specific limit on evaluation attempts or computation time is reached. MolRSI returns the successfully evaluated workflow with the best current-task development score. This is the best evaluated workflow under the stated criterion and budget, rather than a global optimum. The evaluation unit and accounting for failed attempts and repairs are specified in Supplementary Information S2 . Workflow transfer with and without further search. MolRSI could use experience from previous tasks to retrieve a workflow for direct transfer or initialize a new search. It first identifies the best matching previously completed task using task type, evaluation metric, dataset size and task description similarity. It then retrieves that source task’s workflow with the best development score from solution memory. This source task routing is distinct from the family- and workflow-level transfer scores used in Steps 1 and 2. In zero-search mode, the retrieved configuration is applied to the new task without proposing or comparing alternative workflows. Model parameters are fitted using the new task’s training data where required, and fitting and execution still incur computational cost. Alternatively, the retrieved workflow becomes a starting point for Steps 1–5 under the new task’s limits on evaluation attempts and computation time. Appendix S2 Comparison with baseline agents S2.1 Shared evaluation protocol Agents and comparison settings. We compared MolRSI with Biomni [ ? ], Claude Science [ ? ], STELLA [ ? ] and Codex [ ? ] on antibacterial prediction and protein binder ranking workflow development. The antibacterial benchmark additionally included Claude Code [ ? ]. Within each benchmark, agents received the same task inputs, permitted development data, evaluation objective and workflow-evaluation allowance. Each agent used its native tools and workflow development procedures without a common predefined workflow search space. The task-specific instructions and permitted tools are described below. Task instructions and permitted tools. For baseline searches in antibacterial prediction and protein ranking, prompts specified the task inputs, fixed data partitions, development objective and metric, evaluation allowance, required outputs and permitted tools. No starting prediction model or ranking workflow was prescribed. The instructions prohibited changes to the fixed data, partitions, and data-loading or evaluation code, hidden candidate evaluations, and the use of held-out labels or performance for workflow development or selection. Independent baseline runs also prohibited reuse of results, code or model choices from another baseline run. Search permissions and execution records. These baseline agents could search using their native capabilities, and local agents could install software packages. Search, installation, environment inspection and deterministic feature caching independent of the labels did not consume candidate evaluations. Candidates were evaluated sequentially, with subsequent choices based on the preceding development results. Each candidate’s code, outputs, execution status, logs, runtime, development scores and held-out test predictions were retained. Evaluation allowance and repair. A candidate counted as one workflow evaluation once its evaluation began, including evaluations that failed or timed out. Repair attempts for the same candidate did not count as additional workflow evaluations. The authors supplied the task inputs and evaluation interfaces without selecting workflow components, repairing candidates or granting additional retries during the runs. LLM backbones. MolRSI used Claude Sonnet 5 throughout the antibacterial and protein studies. In the antibacterial prediction and protein ranking benchmarks, the baseline agents also used Claude Sonnet 5, except Codex, which used GPT-5.5. Claude Science used Claude Opus 5 for prospective binder design. API access and agent interfaces. MolRSI accessed its model through the Anthropic API. Biomni ran on its own platform and used its platform-provided environment for workflow planning and execution. Its outputs were not independently reproduced outside that platform. Claude Science used its scientific workflow-development interface. STELLA ran in self-evolve mode using the implementation available at https://github.com/asfarasimconcerned/STELLA . Codex and Claude Code [ ? ] used their native coding-agent interfaces, with writable workflow directories and fixed data and evaluation helpers. Computational resources. Across antibacterial prediction, protein ranking and prospective binder design, the authors provided GPUs to agent baselines, and each agent controlled job scheduling and GPU use within its task-specific budget. Their prompts imposed no hard wall-clock limit. These hardware assignments describe the four local baselines and do not specify the resources provided by the Biomni platform. S3.2 Evaluation of recursive self-improvement Source experience. MolRSI developed workflows independently on each of the 25 source tasks and pooled the resulting experience in solution, refinement and execution memory. Each antibacterial target began with the same pooled source experience. Experience generated during a target search guided later iterations of that search and was not shared with the other targets. Resource library and RSI conditions. The small-molecule resource library contained 110 reusable skills: 55 models, 48 methods and seven tools or resources. These covered molecular standardization, feature construction, predictive models, class-imbalance handling, calibration, stacking and ensembling. MolRSI with RSI and MolRSI without RSI used the same resource library, backbone model, data partitions, evaluation procedure and 20-attempt search allowance. MolRSI with RSI began with the pooled source experience; MolRSI without RSI began without experience from previous tasks. In both conditions, memory was updated during the current task to guide later iterations. Search trajectories. At each iteration, we identified the completed workflow with the highest mean validation AUPRC obtained so far. Its test performance was calculated after workflow search to construct the trajectory in Figure 2 d. Test scores were not returned to the agent as feedback and were excluded from memory updates. Zero-search transfer. For Figure 2 e, MolRSI identified the best-matching source task using task type, evaluation metric, dataset size and task-description similarity, and then selected the workflow with the validation performance on that source task. Both selections preceded evaluation on the antibacterial target. Model parameters were fitted on the target training data where required, but no alternative target-workflow configurations were searched. Resource accounting. One workflow-search iteration comprised construction, execution and validation evaluation of a candidate under the accounting rules in Section S2.2 . Zero-search transfer omitted target-workflow search but retained any required model fitting and inference. Skill construction, experience accumulation on source tasks, target search, final fitting and screening-library inference were separate computational stages. Experience-pool-size analysis. We transferred one workflow from each source task to each of the six antibacterial targets, obtaining a matrix of 150 source–target evaluations. For each subset of 2–25 source tasks, we retrospectively selected the transfer test AUPRC available within that subset separately for each target, then averaged these six task performances. The mean curve in Figure 2 f is the analytical expectation of this quantity over all equally sized source-task subsets. To estimate the percentile bands, we generated 2,000 random permutations of the 25 source tasks using numpy.random.default_rng with seed 20260901. For pool size Z Z , the first Z Z tasks in each permutation defined a sampled pool, producing nested pools across sizes. We calculated the interquartile and 5th–95th percentile ranges of the six-target mean across these pools. The seed affected these sampled bands, not the analytical mean or the underlying source–target scores. The bands describe variation in pool composition, not independent search replicates or confidence intervals for the routing method. At size 25, only the complete pool was available. S3.3 Case studies of experience-guided search Experience-guided workflow reuse for A. baumannii . We examined how a workflow developed on AMES supported workflow selection for A. baumannii . Solution memory retained an equal-weight ensemble of LANTERN–RADR, MiniMol [ ? ], ADMETrix and MapLight GNN, together with their component configurations. MiniMol used IA3-based adaptation [ ? ] of the prediction head, a gated multilayer perceptron and class-balanced sampling. MapLight GNN used a Set2Set readout [ ? ] with three processing steps. The four components achieved AMES AUROCs of 0.8480–0.8633, and their ensemble achieved 0.8731 ( Table S6 ). These configurations provided previously evaluated alternatives for the antibacterial task. We transferred the components and their ensemble without changing their architectures or training settings. Model parameters were fitted on the A. baumannii training data, and target validation performance selected among the five inherited configurations before test evaluation. This selected LANTERN–RADR, which achieved a mean test AUPRC of 0.5356. The unchanged four-component ensemble achieved 0.4742, and the other individual components ranged from 0.3235 to 0.3863. Thus, the ensemble that performed best on AMES did not provide the best transfer performance. Retaining its component configurations allowed target validation to identify a more effective predictor without generating or refining a new workflow. Search without experience from previous tasks evaluated 20 workflows on A. baumannii , comprising five initial model evaluations, six refinements and nine ensemble evaluations ( Table S7 ). The refinements changed molecular features, prediction heads, class-imbalance handling and training procedures. For example, ADMETTBoost–XGBoost [ ? ] incorporated Morgan-fingerprint features, focal loss [ ? ] and bagging. Euclia-ML variants used gradient-boosting heads, class-balanced focal loss and feature selection. Ensemble evaluations combined configurations evaluated earlier in the search. The highest observed test AUPRC increased from 0.3716 at step 1 to 0.4134 after the ADMETTBoost–XGBoost refinement at step 3. It reached 0.4335 after the Euclia-ML refinement at step 5. Ensembles then achieved 0.4572 at step 6 and 0.4988 at step 7. The remaining 13 evaluations explored additional predictors, training modifications and ensembles but did not exceed 0.4988. These evaluations included gated fusion of molecular descriptors, cosine warm restarts [ ? ], stochastic weight averaging [ ? ] and label smoothing. Additional ensemble configurations achieved test AUPRCs of 0.4005–0.4684, showing that further combinations of the evaluated models did not improve the best observed result in this run. The validation-selected LANTERN–RADR configuration exceeded the best observed test AUPRC in the 20-step search by 0.0368, corresponding to a relative improvement of 7.4%. This advantage was obtained without further workflow refinement, while retaining target-task model fitting and validation-based selection. Within the RSI framework, solution memory contributed to the effective search policy by supplying previously evaluated configurations for the new task. In this case, selecting and reusing one of these configurations produced a better predictor than the 20-step search. The unchanged source ensemble did not exceed that search, emphasizing the contribution of selecting a suitable configuration from the accumulated experience. The prospective activity and cytotoxicity models used the same training datasets as our previous study [ ? ]. The activity models were trained on methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA; strain RN4220), and cytotoxicity was predicted for HepG2, HSkMC and IMR-90 cells. Both studies screened the same library of 12,076,365 compounds, comprising 11,277,225 entries from the Mcule purchasable database and 799,140 entries from the Broad Institute collection. Selected screening models. The MolRSI workflow used separately optimized predictors for activity and cytotoxicity ( Figure S2 ). HepG2 and IMR-90 cytotoxicity predictors combined MiniMol embeddings with Morgan fingerprints and RDKit descriptors and used residual multilayer-perceptron heads. The HSkMC predictor used MiniMol embeddings with gated multilayer-perceptron heads. Activity ranking combined two MiniMol-based variants and a LANTERN–RADR ensemble with weights of 0.55, 0.30 and 0.15, respectively. These weights combined the outputs of the three component-specific ensembles. Filtering, ranking and diversity selection. Screening proceeded through structural novelty filtering, cytotoxicity filtering, activity ranking, Butina clustering and diverse picking ( Figure S2 ). Structural filtering removed compounds matching PAINS or Brenk alerts [ ? ? ] or established antibiotic cores. It also required a maximum Tanimoto similarity below 0.40 to known antibiotics. The excluded core families were β \beta -lactams, quinolones, oxazolidinones, nitrofurans and sulfonamides. Cytotoxicity filtering required predicted scores of ≤ 0.40 \leq 0.40 for each of HepG2, HSkMC and IMR-90. The retained compounds were ranked by the weighted antibacterial-activity ensemble described above. Butina clustering [ ? ] grouped structurally similar candidates, and diverse picking selected compounds across clusters to retain chemical diversity. We selected and experimentally tested 107 compounds; the final selection included documented exceptions to the nominal cytotoxicity criterion. S3.5 Comparison with the previous expert-driven workflow Workflow differences. The workflows differed in their predictive models, filter order and thresholds, and compound-selection procedures ( Figure S2 ). The expert-driven workflow applied activity prediction before cytotoxicity and structural filtering. MolRSI applied structural filtering before cytotoxicity prediction and activity ranking. Training data and library composition are specified in Section S3.4 . Expert-developed predictors and filtering. The previous study used separate 20-member Chemprop ensembles [ ? ] with RDKit descriptors for S. aureus activity and for each of the three cytotoxicity tasks [ ? ]. Activity-score thresholds of > 0.4 >0.4 for Mcule and > 0.2 >0.2 for the Broad Institute collection retained 10,310 compounds. Requiring all three cytotoxicity scores to be < 0.2 <0.2 reduced this set to 3,646. PAINS/Brenk filtering retained 2,209 compounds, and similarity and antibiotic-core filtering retained 1,261. The similarity filter used a maximum Tanimoto similarity below 0.5 to training actives, and the excluded core families were β \beta -lactams and quinolones. Candidate selection. In the expert-driven workflow, Monte Carlo tree search identified substructures supporting antibacterial predictions and informed selection of compounds with or without such rationales. Compounds were sourced according to availability and purity greater than 90%, and 241 filtered candidates were tested. The MolRSI workflow instead ranked candidates with its weighted activity ensemble and selected structurally diverse compounds across Butina clusters, leading to 107 selected and tested compounds. The substructure search in the previous study served molecular interpretation and candidate selection; it was a different application of tree search from the workflow-development search performed by MolRSI . Comparison endpoints. Predictive discrimination was evaluated against shared labels, whereas experimental hit proportions were calculated within each campaign’s selected candidates. These analyses assess prediction and complete-workflow yield, respectively ( Sections S3.1 and S3.6.3 ). Passing designs were ranked by s8_on . S4.5 Comparison with reference design workflows Prospective agent comparison. We selected Claude Science as the prospective baseline because it achieved the highest performance on the miniprotein-binder benchmark and had previously demonstrated autonomous design of experimentally validated protein binders [ ? ]. The completed comparison comprised independent target-specific campaigns for MART-1 and SARS-CoV-1. The target and peptide-swapped off-target specifications, binder-length range, design objectives and total computational budget were held fixed, while each system retained its native generation, refinement and ranking procedure. Both systems received 1,000 A100 GPU-hours per target. The 30-round allowance applied to MolRSI ’s workflow search; Claude Science instead controlled job scheduling within the shared GPU-hour budget. Claude Science run configuration for prospective binder design. We initialized one independent Claude Science session per target, using Claude Opus 5 and an adaptation of Anthropic’s multi-target binder-design protocol [ ? ]. The prompt source is provided in Section S2.3 ; this section summarizes the study-specific adaptations and run configuration. We adapted the instructions to peptide-specific pMHC design and the available computing platform. Inputs comprised HLA heavy-chain and β 2 \beta_{2} -microglobulin sequences and MSAs, on- and off-target peptide sequences, an 80–120-residue binder range, epitope hints and novelty references. Every scoring prediction was required to include the HLA heavy chain, β 2 \beta_{2} -microglobulin, peptide and binder. Epitope positions were hints, not a mandatory hotspot-contact filter. The campaign was given a computational budget of 1,000 A100 GPU-hours per target, with job state recorded in files on a shared volume. SolubleMPNN [ ? ] and SolubleCaliby [ ? ] were named for designs selected for the final computational output, with their base variants permitted during backbone search. The specified pipeline placed checks for sequence and structural novelty, liabilities, monomer foldability, structural plausibility and redundancy before any complex prediction. The instructions required retained candidates to be scored against the on- and off-target complexes using ESMFold2-Fast, ESMFold2-Full [ ? ] and Protenix v2 [ ? ]. For each scoring model, the fixed criterion combined minimum ipSAE [ ? ], design-to-prediction scDockQ [ ? ] and the on-target-minus-off-target minimum-ipSAE difference. Scores were standardized within the candidate cohort and combined with relative weights of 4 : 1 : 4 4{:}1{:}4 . The minimum scDockQ across the scoring models used also entered the ranking key at a prespecified pose threshold of 0.23, ordering designs that met it above those that did not. At least two rounds of partial diffusion, prediction followed by redesign, or sequence resampling were required. The requested output was a ranked set of 500 designs per target, excluding exact and near duplicates and limiting representation from individual design lineages and structural clusters. At least three structure generators had to be represented, with no more than 50% of designs from one generator or two-thirds from one sequence-design method. The authors specified the target inputs, scoring criterion, output format, diversity limits and computational budget. Claude Science chose the generation strategy and parameters, sampling allocation, use of epitope hints, monomer and structural-plausibility thresholds, parent selection and refinement operations. The monomer and structural-plausibility thresholds were fixed after selection. The comparison therefore used its native binder-design protocol with pMHC- and platform-specific adaptations.
