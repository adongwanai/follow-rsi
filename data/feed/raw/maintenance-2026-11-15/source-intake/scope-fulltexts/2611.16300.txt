Abstract Closed-context question answering (QA) involves a system to generate an answer strictly supported by a supplied passage or to abstain when the passage lacks adequate evidence. The Stanford Question Answering Dataset (SQuAD 2.0) is a key benchmark widely used to evaluate machine reading comprehension under these constraints [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] . Traditionally, model performance on SQuAD 2.0 is measured via Exact Match (EM), which requires character-level agreement with human reference spans after lexical normalization. For generative large language models (LLMs), however, an EM failure does not necessarily indicate an erroneous response; it may instead reflect a valid paraphrase, an alternative passage-supported answer, or a hallucination. Prior results on the official leader board achieved a maximum EM of approximately 91% [ 3 ] but evaluating generative models requires separating lexical divergence from factual error. Our primary contribution of the paper is the introduction of SAGE-QA ( S emantic- A ware, G rounded E valuation for Q uestion A nswering), a multi-tiered evaluation framework that combines reference-based Semantic Equivalence Scoring (SES) and passage-based Re-Validation Scoring (RVS).
Across a fixed set of 10,000 SQuAD 2.0 validation examples, we compute baseline EM and decision-matrix F1 directly from True Positive (TP), True Negative (TN), False Positive (FP), and False Negative (FN) outcome counts across zero-shot, closed-weight frontier models and open-weight models (untuned zero-shot and fine-tuned versions).
In zero-shot evaluation, closed-weight frontier models Claude Opus 4.6 and Gemini 3.1 Pro achieve 85.21 and 86.66 EM, outperforming untuned open-weight models Llama 3 8B (56.88 EM), gpt-oss-20b (66.28 EM), and Llama 3.3 70B (71.39 EM). However, parameter-efficient fine-tuning yields gains of 21.19 to 34.06 EM points over untuned baselines, elevating fine-tuned Llama 3 8B, gpt-oss-20b, and Llama 3.3 70B to 90.94, 87.47, and 94.24 EM scores, respectively. Additionally, when we audit the zero-shot frontier models using SAGE-QA with an external LLM judge, the dataset-wide SES scores (on a 0–100 scale) are 92.81 for Claude Opus 4.6 and 91.67 for Gemini 3.1 Pro, while the corresponding hybrid dataset-wide RVS scores are 97.96 and 98.02. This combines benchmark-based credit with passage-based judge assessments. Our findings demonstrate that fine-tuning open-weight models perform the best to surpass previous best known EM benchmarks. I Introduction Closed-context question answering (QA) tasks a language model with answering a question using only the evidence provided in a companion passage, or returning an explicit abstention token ( [] ) when no answer is supported. We explore this question because QA systems needs to satisfy the information need expressed by the query while following the strict evidence boundary set by the source text. The Stanford Question Answering Dataset (SQuAD) [ 1 , 2 ] serves as the benchmark for this work, comprising comprehension questions authored by crowd workers over Wikipedia passages. SQuAD 2.0 extends the original 100,000 answerable questions of SQuAD 1.1 with over 50,000 adversarial, unanswerable questions designed to closely resemble answerable queries [ 1 ] . SQuAD 2.0 requires a solution that not only extracts or generates accurate answers when sufficient evidence is present, but also reliably abstains when the passage does not support a conclusion. For clarity, reference answers are to be considered as ground-truths because they are provided by human crowd workers. While traditional AI retrieval models select contiguous character spans from the passage, modern generative models frequently formulate complete phrases or valid paraphrases. Under standard Exact Match (EM) evaluation, any deviation from the exact wording or span boundaries chosen by crowd annotators is penalized as a failure. To examine disagreements between exact reference matching and LLM-based assessment, we propose the SAGE-QA evaluation framework, which extends standard confusion-matrix metrics to include two complementary LLM-as-a-judge diagnostics triggered whenever a prediction fails EM. 1. Semantic Equivalence Scoring (SES): SES uses evaluation on answerable questions where the model’s answer fails EM. SES employs an external judge LLM to assess whether the generated answer preserves the semantic meaning of at least one (ground-truth) reference answer. 2. Re-Validation Scoring (RVS): This evaluates on all predictions that fail EM (spanning both answerable and benchmark-unanswerable questions), a judge LLM is used to assess whether the candidate answer directly addresses the question and if it is fully grounded in the source passage. The judge receives no reference answers (including not answerable or [] type of answers). But scoring uses all of the reference answers to check against the model answer. To avoid direct self-evaluation that may be biased, we execute both SES and RVS using an external evaluator model ( gpt-6-luna [ 5 ] ) that does not participate as an answer generator in our benchmark. Architecturally, we maintain a strict separation between answer generation and answer evaluation. On the generation side, we investigate automated prompt optimization
for zero-shot, closed-weight frontier models (Claude Opus 4.6 and Gemini 3.1 Pro) and evaluate open-weight models (Llama 3 8B, Llama 3.3 70B, and gpt-oss-20b) in both
untuned, zero-shot and parameter-efficient fine-tuned (LoRA and QLoRA) configurations. This allows us to compare closed- and open-weight models zero-shot, while isolating the gain of fine-tuning. On the evaluation side, we retain strict lexical matching (EM and outcome-count F1) and add separate judge-based assessments of reference-meaning agreement (SES) and passage support (RVS). Our diagnostic contribution is to characterize which non-matching responses receive judge-assigned credit, where the two rubrics disagree, and how the evaluation criterion affects aggregate scores and model comparisons. II Background and Related Work II-A Benchmark Evolution and Extractive Baselines SQuAD 1.1 focused exclusively on span extraction for answerable queries [ 2 ] but SQuAD 2.0 introduced unanswerable questions to penalize models that rely on superficial keyword matching or heuristic guessing [ 1 ] . Early neural architectures, such as BiDAF [ 6 ] and discriminative pre-trained encoders based on BERT [ 7 ] and RoBERTa [ 8 ] , framed closed-context QA as a token-classification problem over passage start and end indices. Notice that the three human references differ only in whether they include the leading words ( “asking for a…” ) or the trailing constraint ( “whose total length is at most 10 km” ); consequently, any valid generative paraphrase or alternative span choice is penalized as a complete failure under surface-level lexical matching. II-B Semantic Equivalence and Passage Grounding Kamalloo et al. [ 9 ] demonstrated that lexical matching severely underestimates generative LLMs in open-domain QA because models frequently produce valid paraphrases or varied granularity spans. However, their analysis focused on always-answerable open-domain queries; SAGE-QA examines evaluation disagreement in closed-context QA with adversarial unanswerable questions, separating judge-assessed reference equivalence ( SES \mathrm{SES} on ℱ N ans \mathcal{F}_{N}^{\mathrm{ans}} ) from judge-assessed passage support ( RVS \mathrm{RVS} on ℱ P ∪ ℱ N ans \mathcal{F}_{P}\cup\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\mathrm{ans}} ), as defined precisely later. Embedding-based metrics such as BERTScore [ 13 ] compute contextual token similarity, but recent frameworks such as G-Eval [ 14 ] and MT-Bench [ 15 ] leverage LLMs directly as automated judges. Studies of annotation artifacts and label errors motivate closer examination of benchmark failures [ 16 , 17 ] ; they do not establish that disagreements observed under our judging protocol are annotation errors. Building toward semantic evaluation in QA, Bulian et al. [ 10 ] formulated question-aware answer-equivalence judgments and trained the learned BEM (BERT Matching) evaluator. Their asymmetric formulation can accept a candidate answer that expands upon or improves a reference, but our SES metric enforces a symmetric, scaled reference-meaning rubric anchored by strict mutual equivalence, in a logical sense. Complementing reference equivalence, Adlakha et al. [ 11 ] demonstrated the importance of separating reference correctness from context faithfulness in instruction-following QA. The RVS judge applies a passage-based rubric to assess this faithfulness criterion within a closed-context setting, without receiving reference answers. Directly relevant to our benchmark dataset, Bahak et al. [ 18 ] evaluated ChatGPT ( gpt-3.5-turbo ) as a closed-context QA system on SQuAD 1.1 and SQuAD 2.0 using standard character-normalized EM, Recall, and token-overlap F1. They found that ChatGPT trailed task-specific discriminative models by a wide margin, achieving only 44.4% EM (67% F1) on SQuAD 1.1 and plummeting to 18.12% EM (56% F1) on SQuAD 2.0. Bahak et al. attributed this collapse on SQuAD 2.0 to the model generating substantive responses on unanswerable questions, automatically classifying all such False Positives as model hallucinations. Our study examines three related aspects: first, programmatic prompt optimization and structured abstention schemas yield zero-shot, frontier EM above 85%–86%; second, the RVS judge accepts over 90% of benchmark False Positives in our frontier cohort, documenting disagreement with benchmark answerability labels, and third, parameter-efficient, fine-tuning enables open-weight generative models to surpass the 90.58%–90.94% EM ceiling from prior literature. II-C Prompt Optimization, Adaptation, and Evaluator Bias To obtain optimal performance from generative models, recent work has shifted from manual prompt engineering to programmatic prompt compilation using frameworks such as DSPy [ 19 ] , MIPROv2 [ 20 ] , GEPA [ 21 ] , and automated migration optimizers [ 22 ] . In parallel, parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques, notably LoRA [ 23 ] and QLoRA [ 24 ] , enable open-weight models to adapt rapidly to domain-specific output formatting and abstention calibration. When auditing model outputs with LLM judges, however, evaluators can exhibit self-preference bias by favoring generations produced by their own model family, and sensitivity to prompt structure and candidate ordering [ 4 , 25 ] . We avoid direct self-evaluation by employing a single, external judge model ( gpt-6-luna [ 5 ] ) that generates none of the candidate answers in our study, and enforce a uniform, blind input schema across all evaluated systems. III Definitions and The SAGE-QA Evaluation Framework We first define a partitioning of N N QA evaluation items into four mutually exclusive outcome sets which will be used throughout the rest of the paper. • True Positive ( 𝒯 P \mathcal{T}_{P} ): The question is answerable ( a i = 1 a_{i}=1 ), the model does not abstain ( z i = 0 z_{i}=0 ), and the normalized prediction matches a reference ( e i = 1 e_{i}=1 ). • True Negative ( 𝒯 N \mathcal{T}_{N} ): The question is unanswerable ( a i = 0 a_{i}=0 ) and the model abstains ( z i = 1 z_{i}=1 ). • False Positive ( ℱ P \mathcal{F}_{P} ): The question is unanswerable ( a i = 0 a_{i}=0 ), but the model supplies an answer ( z i = 0 z_{i}=0 ). • False Negative ( ℱ N \mathcal{F}_{N} ): The question is answerable ( a i = 1 a_{i}=1 ), but the model either abstains ( z i = 1 z_{i}=1 ) or supplies an answer that fails EM ( z i = 0 z_{i}=0 and e i = 0 e_{i}=0 ). To provide an end-to-end diagnostic of closed-context QA evaluation, we structure SAGE-QA around three sequential evaluation stages. First, we interpret each raw answer field and retain an abstention flag before applying lexical normalization for Exact Match. We then use the flag and the EM result to partition the N = 10,000 N=10{,}000 benchmark examples into correctness-aware lexical outcome categories ( 𝒯 P , 𝒯 N , ℱ P \mathcal{T}_{P},\mathcal{T}_{N},\mathcal{F}_{P} , and ℱ N \mathcal{F}_{N} , where ℱ N \mathcal{F}_{N} is further split into false abstentions ℱ N ∅ \mathcal{F}_{N}^{\emptyset} and answered non-matches ℱ N ans \mathcal{F}_{N}^{\mathrm{ans}} ). Second, we route eligible lexical failures concurrently to two separate LLM-as-a-judge diagnostics: Semantic Equivalence Scoring ( 𝒞 S = ℱ N ans \mathcal{C}_{S}=\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\mathrm{ans}} ) and Re-Validation Scoring ( 𝒞 R = ℱ P ∪ ℱ N ans \mathcal{C}_{R}=\mathcal{F}_{P}\cup\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\mathrm{ans}} ). These are consistent with the fact that the SES algorithm assigns a score of 0 to ℱ P \mathcal{F}_{P} and false abstentions, while RVS assigns a score of 0 to false abstentions but uses a scaled(gradually graded) approach for ℱ P \mathcal{F}_{P} and ℱ N ans \mathcal{F}_{N}^{\mathrm{ans}} . Third, we aggregate both eligible case-level means and dataset-wide scores that credit 𝒯 P \mathcal{T}_{P} and 𝒯 N \mathcal{T}_{N} automatically while avoiding any double-counting on the overlapping set ℱ N ans \mathcal{F}_{N}^{\mathrm{ans}} . These hybrid aggregates combine benchmark-matching credit with judge-assigned rubric scores. Table II summarizes the complete routing architecture. Throughout the RVS analysis, “accepted” means that a judge score meets the specified threshold τ = 0.95 \tau=0.95 . Scores below that threshold are described as “below threshold,” not automatically as “unsupported.” Because the rubric evaluates both passage support and completeness, a response can fall below the threshold for reasons other than lack of support. The 𝒯 P \mathcal{T}_{P} and 𝒯 N \mathcal{T}_{N} cases receive full credit without separate assessment by the RVS judge. This benchmark dependence also applies to the binary aggregate RVS all τ \mathrm{RVS}^{\tau}_{\mathrm{all}} ; excluding references from judge inputs does not make case selection or dataset-wide aggregation reference-free. IV Experimental Setup IV-A Dataset Partition and Model Cohorts We conduct our primary evaluation on a fixed subset of N = 10,000 N=10{,}000 examples from the public SQuAD 2.0 validation dataset [ 12 ] , comprising 4,990 answerable and 5,010 unanswerable questions. This held-out evaluation set is shared identically across all zero-shot and fine-tuned models. For our fine-tuning experiments, we train on a separate, balanced subset of 87,000 examples drawn exclusively from the official SQuAD 2.0 training data, ensuring zero data leakage into the 10,000-example evaluation set. We evaluate three distinct model cohorts: • Zero-Shot Frontier Group: Claude Opus 4.6 [ 27 ] and Gemini 3.1 Pro [ 28 ] . For this cohort, we retain full per-example prediction logs, FN subgroup partitions ( ℱ N ∅ \mathcal{F}_{N}^{\emptyset} vs. ℱ N ans \mathcal{F}_{N}^{\mathrm{ans}} ), and individual judge scores ( s i , r i s_{i},r_{i} ). • Zero-Shot Open-Weight Group (Untuned Baselines): Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct [ 29 ] , Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct [ 30 ] ,
and gpt-oss-20b [ 31 ] . Evaluating these models out-of-the-box establishes a direct zero-shot comparison against the closed-weight frontier models and
provides a matched untuned baseline for our fine-tuning experiments, retaining full outcome counts and FN subgroup splits ( ℱ N ∅ \mathcal{F}_{N}^{\emptyset} vs. ℱ N ans \mathcal{F}_{N}^{\mathrm{ans}} ) under reference-based
scoring. • Fine-Tuned Open-Weight Group: Llama 3 8B [ 29 ] , Llama 3.3 70B [ 30 ] , and gpt-oss-20b [ 31 ] . Because reference-based confusion-matrix counts suffice to quantify exact lexical adaptation gains over our matched untuned baselines, our analysis of the fine-tuned cohort focuses on reference-based metrics ( EM , F1 \mathrm{EM},\mathrm{F1} , and category error rates), whereas per-example SES and RVS judge audits are restricted to the zero-shot frontier group. IV-B Prompt Optimization and Zero-Shot Execution Protocol Prior to full-scale benchmarking, we conducted a preliminary prompt-optimization study comparing three human-authored prompts, an OpenAI-optimized prompt [ 22 ] , and prompts compiled programmatically via DSPy MIPROv2 [ 19 , 20 ] and GEPA [ 21 ] ; because the DSPy GEPA optimizer achieved the highest pilot performance (69.60 EM and 80.70 F1 reported \mathrm{F1}_{\mathrm{reported}} against SQuAD references), we standardized on this GEPA-compiled prompt across all subsequent zero-shot evaluations. In our main zero-shot benchmark, Claude Opus 4.6 and Gemini 3.1 Pro process all 10,000 passage-and-question pairs via their respective provider Batch APIs [ 32 , 33 ] . Similarly, the untuned open-weight models—Llama 3 8B Instruct (via Hugging Face/Featherless-AI), Llama 3.3 70B Instruct
(via Hugging Face/OVHCloud), and gpt-oss-20b (via Groq)—are evaluated zero-shot across the exact same 10,000 examples.
Each model is constrained via structured-output schemas to return a strict two-field JSON object containing the example identifier and the extracted answer (or [] ). All 10,000 requests per model across all five zero-shot runs succeeded and parsed cleanly. As an implementation-consistency check, we examined all 20,000 answers from the Claude Opus 4.6 and Gemini 3.1 Pro. Both scoring scripts determine abstention from the raw answer before applying normalization for EM, and the check found no misclassified abstentions or outcome assignments. An EM failure establishes that a response does not match the reference annotations, but does not explain why. By separating reference equivalence from passage support, SES and RVS distinguish how the judge assesses these responses under two different criteria. Their paired decisions (Table VII ) reveal distinctions that a lexical score alone cannot express. Model comparisons depend on what we measure. The ranking changes in Table V show that the evaluation criterion affects the comparison. Dataset-wide diagnostic scores also retain automatic credit for benchmark successes. Consequently, a higher score or a smaller gap under SES or RVS does not, by itself, establish greater answer correctness or equivalence between models. Disagreements identify cases for examination. We use these diagnostics to locate responses for closer review while preserving the original benchmark outcomes. Their practical value is to make disagreements visible and organize subsequent examination. VI-B Limitations of our Research Our semantic-equivalence and passage-support assessments rely on one external LLM judge ( gpt-6-luna ) and therefore we interpret SES and RVS as judge-based diagnostic scores rather than independently verified measures of answer correctness. Responses accepted despite failing benchmark criteria identify cases for further examination, but do not by themselves establish annotation errors or model hallucination rates. Using a judge that generated none of the evaluated responses avoids direct self-evaluation [ 4 , 25 ] . Moreover, while our untuned-versus-fine-tuned comparisons isolate the exact lexical gains of parameter-efficient adaptation across all three open-weight models, we executed per-example SES and RVS assessments only on the zero-shot closed-weight frontier cohort. Extending these diagnostics to the untuned open-weight models (particularly their 1,268–1,647 ℱ N ans \mathcal{F}_{N}^{\mathrm{ans}} cases and 1,447–2,558 ℱ P \mathcal{F}_{P} cases), together with independent human adjudication, would help examine the contributions of formatting divergence and comprehension errors to their lexical deficit that we defer to future studies as mentioned below. VII Conclusion In this paper, we presented SAGE-QA, a diagnostic evaluation framework for closed-context question answering that combines strict lexical matching ( EM \mathrm{EM} and
decision-matrix F1 \mathrm{F1} ) with judge-assessed reference-meaning agreement (SES) and passage-based assessment (RVS). Evaluating eight configurations across
10,000 SQuAD 2.0 validation examples, we demonstrated two complementary findings. First, under strict lexical evaluation, zero-shot closed-weight frontier
models (Gemini 3.1 Pro at 86.66 EM and Claude Opus 4.6 at 85.21 EM) outperform untuned open-weight models (56.88 to 71.39 EM) by 15 to 30 EM points
due to severe untuned over-answering on unanswerable queries; however, single-epoch parameter-efficient fine-tuning yields gains of +21.19 to +34.06 EM points, making the fine-tuned models excel in performance over prior results. Second, when we audit zero-shot frontier
EM failures with an external LLM judge under SAGE-QA, the graded dataset-wide SES scores (on a 0–100 scale) are 92.81 (Claude Opus 4.6) and 91.67 (Gemini 3.1 Pro), and the corresponding hybrid dataset-wide RVS scores are
97.96 and 98.02. These RVS aggregates combine benchmark-based full credit for 𝒯 P \mathcal{T}_{P} and 𝒯 N \mathcal{T}_{N} with passage-based judge scores on eligible cases. The graded RVS gap is 0.06 score points under this protocol, and the judge accepts over 90% of each model’s benchmark false positives. These results characterize how evaluation criteria affect scores, rankings, and assessments of non-matching responses while preserving the benchmark labels.
