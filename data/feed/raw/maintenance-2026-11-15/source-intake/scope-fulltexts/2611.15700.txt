Papka Argonne National Laboratory Lemont, IL, USA papka@anl.gov Rong Ge Clemson University Clemson, SC, USA rge@clemson.edu Huihuo Zheng † Argonne National Laboratory Lemont, IL, USA huihuo.zheng@anl.gov Abstract AI-based question-answering systems are increasingly used for user support, including at
high-performance computing (HPC) facilities. However, existing systems lack a systematic,
deployment-oriented approach for evaluating final-answer quality and attributing failures
to specific components. They also remain largely static after deployment, apart from
periodic knowledge-base updates. We present AskALCF, an agentic log-analysis and improvement pipeline for HPC user support.
AskALCF evaluates production interactions at a fine-grained level, identifies whether
failures arise from missing knowledge, retrieval, prompting, model behavior, or tool
execution, and routes each failure to an actionable repair. Its diagnostic core combines
answer-quality assessment with a two-scope sufficiency test that distinguishes information
missing from the documentation corpus from information present but not retrieved. We
further introduce a controlled harness-training process that uses expert references, null
edits, and explicit ship, revert, or decline decisions to improve the diagnostic pipeline
reliably. Across 2,174 support turns, AskALCF reduced unattributable failures from 41.4% to
14.2%, while harness training improved expert agreement from 108/150 to 123/150 turns.
These results demonstrate a governed form of self-evolution in which production evidence
drives targeted system improvements. By turning production interactions into fine-grained
diagnoses and actionable repairs, AskALCF provides a systematic foundation for
continuously improving deployed AI support systems beyond knowledge-base updates alone. Index Terms: high performance computing, artificial intelligence, natural language processing,
information retrieval, knowledge management 1 1 footnotetext: Joint first authors. 2 2 footnotetext: Corresponding author. I Introduction Leadership computing facilities support thousands of users across systems whose
hardware, software and operating procedures change continuously. New partitions
come online, programming environments evolve, and compiler, driver and library
versions change between maintenance cycles. At the same time, the information
users need is distributed across facility guides, vendor manuals, software
documentation, release notes and separate web portals. Finding the right
information is therefore difficult even when it has been documented. Together,
rapid change, fragmented documentation and growing demand create a persistent
support burden for both users and facility staff. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has become a promising way to make this
support more accessible. Systems such as AskHPC [ 1 ] and
chatHPC [ 2 ] , HyCE’s
HPC-oriented RAG architecture [ 3 ] , and recent
cross-facility studies [ 4 ] show that language models can
answer technical questions using facility documentation. These systems
represent an important advance over static documentation search: users can ask
questions in natural language and receive an immediate, context-aware response. However, current systems remain limited in two important ways. First, detecting
a poor answer does not reveal how to fix it. The same visible error may result
from missing documentation, failed retrieval, an inadequate prompt, model
behavior, or a broken tool call. These causes belong to different teams and
require different repairs. A single quality score can identify that an answer
is weak, but it cannot identify who should act or what they should change. Second, deployed assistants make little systematic use of the evidence they
accumulate. Every interaction records a real user need, the material retrieved
for it, and the answer produced. Support tickets provide another source of
operational knowledge: they capture problems that may require an existing
answer, reproduction on ALCF systems, or intervention by a system owner.
Yet chatbot logs and support tickets are typically handled as separate records
rather than as evidence for continuously improving the support system. We present AskALCF, an agentic support and improvement framework that connects
these two channels. Interactive questions are answered by a deployed RAG
service, while a diagnostic pipeline analyzes the resulting logs and routes
failures to the component that can address them. Support tickets are classified
by the type of resolution they require, and responses are drafted for expert
review. A shared knowledge-management process collects lessons from both
channels and turns them into reviewed updates to the documentation corpus.
Together, these four components allow the system to learn from production use,
improve its diagnostic rules and prompts, and accumulate reusable knowledge. We
refer to this controlled process as governed self-evolution : the system
proposes and evaluates improvements, while human experts retain authority over
responses and deployed changes. The central idea is to distinguish an answer’s visible symptom from its
underlying cause. In particular, AskALCF asks two separate questions: whether the
retrieved passages were sufficient to answer the user, and whether the required
information existed anywhere in the documentation corpus. This distinction,
inspired by sufficient-context evaluation [ 5 ] ,
separates missing knowledge from failed retrieval. Combined with evidence about
the answer, model and tool execution, it allows the system to route a failure
to one of five component owners rather than returning only a quality score. An
agent judge independently audits the route, while ALCF experts provide the
final reference for disputed or uncertain cases. This changes the role of an AI support assistant. Instead of treating each
interaction as an isolated question-answer exchange, AskALCF treats production
use as evidence for improving the support process itself. The result is a
governed loop from user request, to diagnosis, to repair, to reviewed knowledge.
The significance is not simply that the assistant can produce another answer;
it is that the system can identify an actionable repair, direct it to the right
owner, and preserve the resulting lesson for future users. We evaluate this approach on 2,174 production support turns. The diagnostic
pipeline reduces failures that cannot be assigned to an owner from 41.4% to
14.2%. A human-guided improvement process increases agreement with a frozen
three-expert reference from 108/150 to 123/150 turns. Controlled repair
experiments further show that the resulting diagnoses can guide effective
prompt and documentation changes. Together, these results demonstrate that
production support evidence can be converted into measurable and actionable
system improvements. Contributions. This paper makes four contributions: • An actionable diagnostic for AI-based HPC support (§ III , § III-B ). The diagnostic
distinguishes failures caused by knowledge, retrieval, prompting, model
behavior and tool execution, and routes each failure to the component owner
who can address it. • A governed agent judge for production diagnosis (§ III-B4 , § V-D ). The judge audits a deterministic
rule tree rather than replacing it, names the evidence behind a disagreement,
and can change a route only under explicitly evaluated rules. • A human-guided method for improving the diagnostic pipeline (§ IV-C ). We treat the rules, prompts and judge policy—rather
than model weights—as the object of improvement, and evaluate proposed
changes against fixed expert references and explicit controls. • An integrated system for governed self-evolution (§ III , § V-E ). By combining question
answering, log analysis, ticket resolution and knowledge management, AskALCF
converts production experience into proposed repairs, evaluates their effects,
and preserves approved lessons for future requests. Controlled prompt and
documentation experiments provide evidence that this loop can produce
effective improvements while experts retain authority over consequential
changes. Scope. The improvement loop is implemented and evaluated
through expert review. We diagnose production interactions, author targeted
repairs, and measure their effects in controlled experiments. No
CODEOWNERS-approved knowledge change has yet been merged into the deployed
corpus and re-indexed. We therefore report measured offline improvements, not a
claim that the production system has already modified itself autonomously. II Background and Related Work II-A HPC Assistants and RAG Diagnosis AskHPC and chatHPC [ 1 , 2 ] answer user questions from
facility documentation, with chatHPC also supporting script generation.
HyCE [ 3 ] extends retrieval to HPC-specific operational
data, while cross-facility studies show that support behavior does not transfer
cleanly between sites [ 4 ] . These systems demonstrate
the value of language models for HPC support, but their principal output is an
answer rather than a diagnosis or repair path. Existing RAG evaluations measure complementary aspects of retrieval and
generation. RAGAS [ 6 ] and RAGChecker [ 7 ] provide
multi-dimensional evaluation, while citation-based methods assess whether
claims are supported by retrieved evidence [ 8 ] . The Sufficient
Context test asks whether the passages returned for a question contain enough
information to answer it [ 5 ] . AskALCF extends this idea
to two scopes: the passages returned in production and the complete corpus.
The difference distinguishes a retrieval failure from a documentation gap and
therefore identifies the component owner who can act. Other work uses relevance or sufficiency judgments to adapt retrieval during a
turn [ 9 , 10 ] , and taxonomies describe recurring RAG
engineering failures [ 11 ] . These methods improve an answer
or characterize a failure, but do not connect production evidence to a facility
support workflow. Our contribution is an operational diagnosis: the
two-scope sufficiency test is combined with answer and tool evidence to assign
each production turn to a component-level route and a corresponding repair
owner. II-B Agentic Evaluation and Governance Agent-as-a-Judge systems use tools to acquire evidence before producing a
verdict [ 12 , 13 ] . This capability
is useful for failure attribution because an agent can inspect evidence that
was absent from the original interaction. However, LLM judges remain sensitive
to position, self-preference, and other systematic
biases [ 14 , 15 ] ; self-reported confidence
is also an unreliable indicator of correctness [ 16 ] .
Executable software agents introduce a related governance problem: their
localize–repair–validate workflows require bounded tools and explicit safety
controls [ 17 , 18 ] . Agentic failure-attribution systems similarly use tools to localize faults in
execution traces [ 19 , 20 ] . Existing governance
strategies commonly rely on confidence thresholds, escalation, or multiple
judges [ 21 , 22 ] . These strategies are difficult
to apply when confidence is not calibrated to correctness. AskALCF therefore combines deterministic and agentic evaluation. An ordered rule
tree assigns the initial failure route, an agent judge independently probes the
corpus, and a rule-conditioned gate limits when the judge may change the route.
Human review remains mandatory for ticket responses, knowledge changes, and
generated repairs. Our contribution is the division of authority: the
judge may overturn only decisions based on evidence it can independently test,
while experts retain control over every production-facing action. II-C Human-Guided Self-Improvement Self-improving agents commonly update an internal memory or skill library from
their own traces [ 23 , 24 ] , while systems such
as DSPy and GEPA optimize prompts or language-model programs against an
evaluation objective [ 25 , 26 ] . Recent systems also learn
skills from operational support traces or maintain documentation through
agentic feedback [ 27 , 28 ] . These
approaches differ primarily in what they modify and who determines whether a
change is acceptable. Pipeline optimizers can also propagate natural-language feedback through
prompts and agent trajectories [ 29 , 30 ] . However,
small prompt changes can interact with nondeterministic generation and produce
apparent gains that do not replicate [ 31 , 32 ] . A self-improvement claim therefore requires both a stable
reference and controls for run-to-run variation. In AskALCF, domain experts provide both corrected routes and written rationales.
These observations motivate bounded changes to diagnostic rules, judge
instructions, and ALCF knowledge. Each change is evaluated against a
frozen, session-disjoint expert reference before acceptance. The resulting
process improves the surrounding harness rather than model weights, while
human reviewers retain authority over every production-facing change. Our contribution is a governed self-evolution protocol that turns
expert explanations into testable harness changes, records accepted and
rejected arms, and promotes a change only when its target cases improve and its
controls remain stable. III System Design and Implementation At the Argonne Leadership Computing Facility (ALCF), users reach AskALCF through two channels. Short how-to questions arrive
through web chat or MCP [ 33 ] and need an immediate answer. The service
covers Polaris and Aurora and sends model requests through the ALCF Argo inference gateway. Problem
reports arrive by email and become support tickets. A ticket may be answered
from existing knowledge, sent to an agent to reproduce and repair the problem
on real HPC resources, or routed to the team responsible for a system-level
fix. These needs motivate four pillars. Pillar 1 answers chatbot questions, and
Pillar 3 analyzes the resulting logs to identify failures and improve the
service. Pillar 4 triages support tickets and directs each one to an appropriate
resolution path. Pillar 2 collects lessons from both channels and turns them
into reviewed knowledge updates. Together, the four pillars connect immediate
assistance, failure analysis, ticket resolution, and knowledge curation in a
single human-governed improvement loop. Because a ticket’s complete flag does not reliably indicate resolution (§ V-B ),
Pillar 4 re-triages every ticket from its content. corpus of facility user guides, vendor documentation and other operational
materials in Milvus [ 34 ] . For each question, it retrieves relevant
passages and sends them to a language model as context for answer generation.
It logs the question, answer, retrieved passages, tool calls and retrieval
score. Pillar 3 uses this record to evaluate what the production system
actually saw. Pillar 3 — Log analysis. Pillar 3 examines each failing Pillar 1
turn and assigns one of five causes: knowledge , retrieval , prompt , model or tools_mcp . A knowledge failure
means that the required information is absent from the indexed corpus, whereas
a retrieval failure means that the information exists but was not
returned to the model. A prompt failure occurs when the system
instructions induce inappropriate behavior, such as unnecessary clarification.
A model failure occurs when sufficient context and appropriate
instructions are available but the model produces an incorrect or unsupported
answer. A tools_mcp failure arises when an external tool, MCP service or
its invocation fails. We use no_failure when the evidence supports a
healthy response and unknown when the available evidence cannot support
an attribution. Knowledge failures go to Pillar 2; the others go to the teams
that own the affected components.
The deterministic rule tree makes the initial decision
(§ III-B ). LLMs provide evidence at fixed points in the tree,
and an agent judge audits the final route instead of replacing the tree
(§ III-B4 ). Pillar 4 — Ticket resolution. Pillar 4 assigns each ticket to one of
three resolution paths: answer it from existing knowledge, reproduce and repair
the reported problem by running a real job at the ALCF, or route a
system-level problem to the team that owns the affected service. It then drafts
a response explaining the answer, tested repair or handoff. Support staff at
the ALCF must review and approve the draft before it is sent to the user. Ticket ingestion, classification and response drafting are implemented. For a
reproducible problem, ingestion also produces a validated record containing the
failing job, error signature and rerun script. The agent that consumes this
record and runs the job on a live allocation has not yet been exercised. That
path uses Apptainer [ 35 ] , Spack [ 36 ] ,
CodeAct-style [ 17 ] functions and Parsl [ 37 ] , and is
limited to three build attempts, thirty minutes of wall time and one node-hour. Pillar 2 — Knowledge curation. Pillar 2 turns confirmed knowledge
gaps and reusable ticket lessons into updates to the corpus used by Pillar 1.
A writer drafts each change, and a reviewer either rejects it or prepares it as
a pull request. Drafting and dry-run application are operational. Opening a
pull request is marked □ \square because the service account cannot merge it.
Every change therefore requires CODEOWNERS approval before it can reach the
deployed corpus. No knowledge-writer pull request has yet passed that review, so
the merge-triggered Milvus re-indexing path has not fired. III-B Log Analysis and Failure Attribution Pillar 3 converts each logged interaction into a diagnosis and an actionable

rule tree combines this evidence and assigns the turn to a component owner. An
agent judge audits that decision, and a human expert reviews disputed or

tree and the judge skills. All entailment decisions use the same three-class judge
(entailment, neutral or contradiction), which keeps differences between signals
attributable to their evidence rather than to different judging functions. correctness measures the fraction of answer claims supported by evidence
retrieved separately for each claim. faithfulness applies the same test
to the context originally shown to the model. Their difference is therefore
diagnostic: an answer may contain correct information that was not supported by
its supplied context. grounding checks whether paths, modules and documentation URLs cited in
the answer exist. Following the fabricated-citation setting of
ALCE [ 8 ] , it returns found , mangled_real or fabricated . The middle value indicates that the artifact exists but was
damaged during chunking, which is a retrieval defect rather than a fabricated
citation. An answer with no such citation receives n/a , not zero. completeness follows the recall formulation of
RAGChecker [ 7 ] . Finally, facility_scope is a
deterministic lexicon check that rejects answers referring to another facility
or to an unsupported GPU stack. The abstention detector distinguishes full , partial and no abstention. It removes fenced code, references and clarifying
questions before counting substantive claims. Only a full abstention exempts
the answer-quality signals. Ambiguous cases are treated as partial abstentions,
because incorrectly exempting a substantive answer could hide a genuine
failure. III-B 2 Retrieval and Corpus Sufficiency (Layer B) Layer B separates two questions that are often combined. R R asks whether the
top- k k passages returned in production were sufficient. C C asks whether the
required information existed anywhere in the corpus. R R follows the
Sufficient Context test [ 5 ] ; C C extends that test to
a wider candidate set obtained from expanded dense queries and BM25 search. The two verdicts determine the responsible component. If R R is true, retrieval
was sufficient and the rule tree evaluates the generation-side evidence. If R R is false but C C is true, the corpus contains the answer and the failure is
assigned to retrieval . If both are false, the corpus lacks sufficient
documentation and the failure is assigned to knowledge . This distinction
turns a sufficiency judgment into an actionable work item. Two constraints preserve that interpretation. First, Layer B never sees the
generated answer; otherwise it could not distinguish insufficient retrieval
from poor answer generation. Second, R R and C C use different query policies. R R evaluates the query and passages used in production. C C may use rewritten,
goal-directed queries because it tests corpus coverage rather than production
retrieval. A relaxed condition is therefore applied to R R only when supported
by direct production evidence. For complex questions, C C is evaluated over answer requirements rather than by
asking whether a large collection of passages is sufficient as a whole. The
system decomposes the question into requirements, marks the essential ones as core , and checks each core requirement against the corpus. Core status is
assigned without access to coverage results, preventing a missing fact from
being reclassified as unimportant. If requirement extraction is malformed, all
requirements are treated as core, so the system fails conservatively. We use semantic core requirements rather than a fixed fractional coverage
threshold, which would treat requirements of unequal importance as
interchangeable. The materiality judgment preserves the meaning of corpus
sufficiency across questions with different structures. III-B 3 Cause Attribution with an Ordered Rule Tree The router is an ordered list of 28 rules with first-match semantics. A predicate
over a missing signal evaluates to false, so absent evidence cannot trigger a
route. The ordering in Fig. 2 reflects four priorities. Service
and tool failures are checked first, because a backend outage should not be
evaluated as a model-generated answer. Deterministic facility-scope and
clarification checks come next. The R × C R\times C sufficiency pivot then separates
retrieval and knowledge failures. Answer-quality signals are considered only
after retrieval has been shown to be sufficient. This ordering prevents answer-quality signals from absorbing retrieval errors.
When retrieval is insufficient, correctness and completeness are also likely to
be low. Evaluating those signals before the sufficiency pivot would therefore
assign retrieval failures to generation-side components. Sufficient retrieval
is treated as a prerequisite for attributing a failure to answer generation. On the generation side, the tree distinguishes how the answer used its context
from what the answer asserted. A fabricated citation or a contradiction of the
provided context is assigned to model , because the required evidence was
available but not used correctly. An answer that remains faithful to its context
but reaches an incorrect conclusion is also assigned to model , for a
different underlying reason. No answer-quality score directly produces a prompt route, because a low score alone cannot distinguish an inadequate
instruction from inadequate execution. Prompt routes arise from explicit policy
checks performed earlier in the tree. The terminal outcomes no_failure and unknown have different
meanings. no_failure requires positive evidence that retrieval was
sufficient and the answer was faithful and correct. unknown indicates
that no supported attribution could be made. Keeping these outcomes separate
distinguishes a verified healthy turn from one that could not be evaluated. If
a deterministic signal conflicts with an unreliable judged signal, the tree
escalates the turn for human review rather than guessing. Table I states the seven outcomes the tree can emit. Five are
failure modes, each naming the component that must change; two are terminal and
dispatch nobody. Every turn receives exactly one, so no turn leaves the pipeline

The judge does not replace the router. It checks whether the evidence used by
the matched rule supports the assigned cause. When it disagrees, it identifies
the disputed signal, producing a concrete item for review rather than only a
second route label. We selected this audit design instead of a second generative router because a
second router would consume the same potentially unreliable signals and would
not provide an independent check on them. The agent judge differs from the single-shot judges used to compute individual
signals. It runs a bounded probe loop over the corpus: it lists candidate URLs,
searches for evidence and reads selected chunks. It can therefore find evidence
that was absent from the production retrieval result. As evaluated in
§ V-D , this access is most useful when the rule tree reached a
decision from missing evidence. Every judge call is logged, observations are size-limited, and probe results are
content-addressed and pinned for replay (§ III-B5 ). These controls make
each conclusion traceable to evidence the agent actually inspected. Authority is configured separately from judgment. The agent judge may overturn
the tree only for seven rule classes whose inputs it can independently verify.
For all other classes, its disagreement is recorded for human review without
changing the route. The gate depends on the rule that fired, not on the judge’s
self-reported confidence. Section V-D evaluates this policy. III-B 5 Reproducibility Controls The corpus probe used to compute C C expands each question into sub-queries with
an LLM call requesting temperature=0 . The inference gateway did not
honor that setting: two otherwise identical runs produced different expansion
sets for all 78 tested questions, and the resulting retrieval differences
changed the final route for 16% of turns. We therefore cache each expansion under a hash of the query, model and prompt.
The cache is committed with the evaluation artifacts, making expansions
identical across runs and machines and reviewable as a diff. Every result in
this paper uses these pinned expansions. IV Human-Guided Self-Evolution Production evidence does not change the system automatically. Instead, experts
review diagnoses, record corrections and propose bounded changes to the rules,
prompts or knowledge base. Each proposal is evaluated against a fixed reference
before it is accepted. Figure 3 summarizes this cycle. We call
the process governed self-evolution because the system learns from its
operation while people retain authority over every consequential change. IV-A Expert Review and Reference Labels A diagnostic route is a proposal rather than an action. The review service
therefore presents the evidence behind each route before a human decision is
recorded. It provides two role-scoped workspaces behind ALCF single sign-on:
an administrative workspace for reviewing chatbot diagnoses and a staff
workspace for reviewing ticket responses. Both read immutable analysis
artifacts and write decisions to a separate append-only store. For each chatbot turn, the administrative workspace shows the answer-quality
signals, the retrieval and corpus-sufficiency verdicts, the matched rule, and
the agent judge’s independent assessment. Reviewers can accept the route,
provide a corrected route and explanation, or mark the sample as unsuitable for
evaluation. An unanswered item remains uncertain ; it is never interpreted
as agreement. These decisions form the expert reference used for harness
training. IV-B Ticket Feedback and Knowledge Accumulation The staff workspace applies the same approval principle to support tickets. It
re-triages each ticket, retrieves supporting documentation and drafts a response
with citations derived from the retrieved sources. A support expert reviews the
draft before exporting it to the expert’s mail client; the system does not send

weights. The editable harness consists of the rule tree, the judge skills and
the override gate. A human reviewer identifies a recurring error, proposes one
bounded change and names the turns that should improve and the control turns
that should remain unchanged. One complete proposal-and-evaluation cycle is an arm . As Fig. 3 shows, each arm follows four steps: diagnose an error,
pre-register its target and control sets, run the revised harness, and either

First,
retrieval should be evaluated at both the production-result and corpus levels;
the distinction determines whether a retrieval engineer or documentation owner
can act on the failure. Second, answer-quality scores should not be treated as
repair decisions. A visible symptom does not uniquely identify the component
that caused it, and the reliability of attribution is bounded by the evidence
detectors that feed the policy. Third, a repair evaluation should measure both
the target behavior and its inverse. The prompt repair reduced unnecessary
clarification but also increased under-clarification, a tradeoff that a
single-direction metric would miss. The study has several limitations. All labelled data come from the ALCF,
and approximately two thirds of the 150-turn reference received only one expert
label. The class distribution is unbalanced, making per-route estimates
unreliable for rare model , unknown , and tools_mcp cases.
We report exact agreement without a chance-corrected statistic, and the held-out
split contains only 52 turns. The gate-policy comparison in
Table III is the only agentic-versus-deterministic control. Finally,
each repair study represents one experimental campaign. Null arms estimate
run-to-run variation but do not substitute for repeated independent trials. The implemented system also stops short of fully autonomous repair. Three of
the five failure routes have not been exercised through an end-to-end repair
path, and no generated knowledge change has been merged and re-indexed in the
deployed corpus. The remaining errors, rejected harness arms, and finding that
76% of missing retrieved facts already exist in the corpus all identify
retrieval quality as the highest-priority next step. VII Conclusion Deploying a RAG assistant creates a corresponding operational challenge:
determining why an answer failed and who can repair it. AskALCF addresses this
challenge by evaluating answer quality separately from retrieval sufficiency
and by testing retrieval at both the production-result and corpus levels. This
distinction separates missing documentation from failed retrieval and converts
quality evidence into a component-level work item. Across 2,174 production turns, the router assigned a failure mode to 1,295
turns and explicitly left 309 unattributed. Human-guided harness training
increased agreement with a 150-turn expert reference from 108 to 123 turns,
including an increase from 67% to 77% on a session-disjoint evaluation split.
An agent judge provided useful independent evidence when the rule tree reasoned
from missing information; a rule-conditioned gate bounded its authority more
effectively than a confidence threshold. The expert-review interface is central to this improvement process. It produces
the reference labels used for evaluation and captures rationales that motivate
bounded changes to rules, prompts, and knowledge. Controlled repair studies
showed that diagnoses can guide prompt and documentation changes, while also
revealing adverse effects that require expert judgment. The system therefore demonstrates a governed path from support interactions to
measured improvement, but not a fully closed autonomous loop. Candidate repairs
have been evaluated offline; generated knowledge changes have not yet been
merged and re-indexed in production. Completing and evaluating that final stage
is future work. Acknowledgment This research used resources of the Argonne Leadership Computing Facility, a U.S. Department of
