The ability to generate a plausible modification, however, does not establish that it should become persistent system state. Candidate selection is adaptive, evaluators may be imperfect or manipulable, and repeated local improvements can conceal degradation relative to an earlier operating baseline. This paper develops Evidence-Governed Evolution (EGE), a statistical and operational framework for governing persistent adoption of self-generated agent modifications. EGE separates candidate generation from confirmatory adoption, evaluates frozen candidates on fresh paired audit samples, requires practically meaningful improvement on a prespecified primary objective, protects declared capabilities through reference-relative non-inferiority tests, and applies mandatory governance constraints before bounded canary deployment. The statistical contribution is an evaluator-calibrated simultaneous lower-bound construction for a countable sequence of adaptively generated candidates. It combines candidate-wise Hoeffding bounds with independent, candidate-specific calibration samples that compare the operational evaluator with a prespecified reference evaluation procedure. A second result formalises reference replacement through explicit bridge tests and derives a cumulative degradation bound along a versioned reference lineage. We also give a conservative audit-planning condition that links effect-size margin, evaluation sample size, calibration sample size, and a lower bound on acceptance probability. A reproducible Monte Carlo stress test illustrates the importance of calibration under candidate-dependent evaluator optimism; it is explicitly a stylised statistical experiment, not an evaluation of a deployed agent. The paper does not claim empirical validation on a real autonomous-agent system. All guarantees are conditional on bounded paired scores, fresh sampling, a valid reference-evaluation construct, and the declared evaluation distributions; they do not establish universal agent safety or robustness to arbitrary distribution shift. † † footnotetext: The author used ChatGPT as an assistive tool for research planning, literature discovery, conceptual organization, drafting and revision of explanatory text, and language refinement. Keywords: autonomous agents; self-evolving agents; adaptive data analysis; sequential inference; evaluator calibration; non-inferiority; AI governance. 1 Introduction Autonomous agents are systems whose behaviour depends not only on foundation-model weights but also on prompts, memory, tools, workflows, planning procedures, routing policies, and execution constraints. An agent can therefore change its behaviour without modifying its underlying model. Candidate changes may be generated from execution traces, user feedback, self-reflection, search, or optimisation. A candidate that performs well on recently encountered tasks is not necessarily a reliable improvement. It may overfit to development data, reduce performance on previously supported tasks, increase latency or resource use, or violate operational constraints. The risk is amplified when an agent repeatedly proposes and deploys changes with limited human intervention. Prior work studies reflection, iterative refinement, and skill acquisition in language-model agents ( Shinn et al., 2023 ; Madaan et al., 2023 ; Wang et al., 2023 ) . Automated prompt optimisation and evolutionary search provide additional candidate-generation mechanisms ( Guo et al., 2024 ) . These methods motivate the ability to generate changes; they do not by themselves settle whether a proposed change should become persistent system state. Broader work on AI safety identifies specification failures, distributional failures, and unsafe exploration as recurring challenges ( Amodei et al., 2016 ; García and Fernández, 2015 ) . The adoption problem has several properties that make it different from ordinary offline model selection. First, candidate proposals are often adaptive: the next proposal may depend on previous successes, failures, evaluator feedback, or deployment traces. Second, the candidate can modify the mechanism that will later produce evidence, for example by changing a prompt, retrieval policy, tool-selection rule, or evaluator-facing output format. Third, the system may optimise a narrow objective while being expected to preserve a much broader capability profile. Fourth, deployment is not a purely statistical event. A candidate can pass a benchmark and still fail under production load, interact unexpectedly with tools, or create external side effects that cannot be undone by restoring an earlier configuration. These properties motivate a layered adoption design. The proposal mechanism is permitted to explore the design space, but it does not control the confirmatory audit process. The audit process measures a frozen candidate against prespecified comparators and margins. A governance layer enforces requirements that must not be traded against average task performance. Finally, an operational deployment layer limits exposure while collecting evidence about behaviour in the target environment. The layers are related but not interchangeable: a confidence bound cannot replace a permission boundary, and a canary cannot repair an invalid statistical comparison. The scope of EGE is deliberately broader than parameter-level continual learning. A candidate may change a prompt, memory schema, retrieval strategy, tool interface, workflow, routing rule, or orchestration policy. The same principles can be applied to model-weight changes, but those changes may require additional controls for training-data provenance, model integrity, supply-chain security, and compatibility. EGE does not specify a universal metric set; metric selection remains an application-specific modelling and governance decision. This paper addresses the adoption decision through Evidence-Governed Evolution (EGE). EGE separates candidate generation from evidence-based adoption and combines: (i) fresh audit data collected after a candidate is frozen; (ii) meaningful improvement on at least one prespecified primary objective; (iii) non-inferiority on every prespecified protected capability relative to a stable reference; (iv) mandatory governance checks; and (v) bounded canary deployment before persistent commitment. The paper makes four bounded contributions. First, it formalises persistent adoption as a distinct decision problem for adaptively generated agent configurations, with separate development, calibration, confirmatory-audit, and deployment stages. Second, it gives a simultaneous lower-confidence-bound construction that accounts for candidate-dependent evaluator discrepancy using independent calibration data rather than assuming a known calibration allowance. Third, it formalises reference replacement through bridge comparisons and derives a cumulative capability-degradation bound along the reference lineage; this result makes explicit the statistical price of changing the protected baseline. Fourth, it provides an audit-planning inequality and a reproducible Monte Carlo stress test that exposes a failure mode of evaluator-naive acceptance. The Monte Carlo experiment is not presented as evidence about a real agent system. A real-agent evaluation remains necessary to establish operational usefulness and is identified as an outstanding empirical requirement. The distinction from prior work is deliberately narrow. EGE does not introduce a new concentration inequality, a new general-purpose sequential-testing principle, or a complete agent safety architecture. Its contribution is a specific composition and formalisation for persistent agent-update adoption: candidate-wise confirmatory testing, candidate-specific evaluator calibration, protected-capability reference lineage, and an operational commitment protocol. The novelty claim is therefore about this problem formulation and the resulting guarantees, not about the underlying Hoeffding or union-bound tools. The results are conditional statistical guarantees, not a proof of universal agent safety. In particular, rollback restores configuration but cannot reverse irreversible external actions. 2 Related Work 2.1 Reflection, Refinement, and Persistent Skills Reflexion uses verbal feedback and episodic memory to improve subsequent agent attempts ( Shinn et al., 2023 ) . Self-Refine iteratively critiques and revises model outputs ( Madaan et al., 2023 ) . Voyager accumulates reusable skills through interaction with an environment ( Wang et al., 2023 ) . EGE is complementary: these methods can generate candidate changes, whereas EGE governs whether a candidate is retained persistently. EvoPrompt connects language models with evolutionary algorithms for discrete prompt optimisation ( Guo et al., 2024 ) . Such optimisation makes the distinction between development evidence and final audit evidence especially important: the data used to select a candidate should not also be treated as an untouched confirmation sample. Recent surveys organise self-evolving agents by the components and stages of evolution, examine self-improvement in modern agentic systems, and study endogenous observations generated within agent interaction loops ( Gao et al., 2026a ; Ren et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026 ) . These works motivate reliability concerns around self-generated learning signals; EGE focuses on the statistical and operational gate at adoption time. 2.2 Adaptive Data Analysis and Sequential Inference Hoeffding’s inequality gives finite-sample concentration bounds for independent bounded observations ( Hoeffding, 1963 ) . Repeated adaptive reuse of a holdout can invalidate ordinary fixed-sample inference; reusable-holdout methods address this problem through controlled information release ( Dwork et al., 2015 ) . Confidence sequences provide time-uniform inference when data are monitored repeatedly ( Howard et al., 2021 ) . Classical group-sequential designs and alpha-spending methods likewise address repeated interim testing ( Lan and DeMets, 1983 ; DeMets and Lan, 1994 ) . Conformal risk control offers a complementary approach to controlling expected monotone losses under its own exchangeability and procedure-specific assumptions ( Angelopoulos et al., 2024 ) . EGE’s simple construction is a union-bound allocation across candidate indices and metrics. It is valid when each candidate is frozen before a fresh audit sample is collected and the stated conditional sampling assumptions hold. It does not permit unrestricted reuse or repeated optional inspection of a fixed sample. 2.3 Safe Adaptation and Risk Constraints Safe policy improvement studies evidence-supported policy changes relative to a baseline ( Laroche et al., 2019 ) . Broader safe reinforcement-learning research considers performance and safety constraints during learning and deployment ( García and Fernández, 2015 ; Chow et al., 2018 ) . EGE extends the governance perspective to heterogeneous agent configuration changes and multiple protected capabilities. Its non-inferiority tests do not imply that every possible failure mode is controlled. 2.4 Agent Self-Evolution Evaluation and the Adoption Gap Recent work has begun to study self-evolving agents as a distinct research area. Gao et al. organise the field around what, when, and how an agent evolves, while Ren et al. survey self-improvement mechanisms in modern agentic systems ( Gao et al., 2026a ; Ren et al., 2026 ) . Zhang et al. analyse endogenous observations as learning signals generated by an agent’s own generation, evaluation, and interaction processes ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) . Benchmarking work such as EvoAgentBench evaluates ability transfer during agent self-evolution ( Gao et al., 2026b ) . Lin et al. distinguish the ability to produce useful harness updates from the ability of a task-solving agent to benefit from those updates, underscoring that update generation and update utility are separate capabilities ( Lin et al., 2026 ) . These studies strengthen the motivation for evaluating agent evolution directly. EGE addresses a different stage: after a candidate update has been generated, what evidence should be required before it is allowed to alter persistent system state? Unlike a fixed, unexplained allowance, EGE estimates the discrepancy for each frozen candidate using an independent calibration sample drawn from the same declared metric distribution. This is intentionally demanding: a candidate-specific calibration set is needed because an update may change output style or behaviour in ways that affect evaluator agreement. A global calibration result can replace this design only if a separate uniform-generalisation argument covers the complete candidate class and adaptive selection process. The calibration procedure does not eliminate construct-validity concerns. If the reference evaluator is systematically wrong, the resulting guarantee is only relative to that reference procedure. If human adjudication is used, the paper’s target is the expected adjudicated score under the stated protocol; inter-rater disagreement and adjudication reliability must be reported. The reference procedure, rubric, blinding, version, and sampling frame must be fixed before confirmatory results are examined. 4.2 Evidence Partitioning and Audit Integrity A practical implementation should distinguish at least three information partitions. The development partition is available to the candidate generator and may be used for prompt iteration, debugging, reflection, and search. The validation partition may be used to compare alternative candidate designs during development, but repeated adaptive use means it should not be treated as a final confirmatory sample. The audit partition is reserved for the frozen candidate and is revealed only after the candidate, comparator, metrics, margins, and analysis procedure have been committed. The audit partition should be controlled as an operational resource. Access logs should record which candidate was evaluated, which audit instances were used, the evaluator version, the scoring rubric, and whether any audit result was exposed to the candidate generator. If an audit result is used to modify the candidate, that sample is no longer fresh for the modified candidate. The modified candidate must receive a new confirmatory sample or be evaluated with a method that explicitly accounts for adaptive reuse. This requirement is stronger than merely labelling a dataset as a test set: the information flow from audit results back into candidate construction must also be controlled. Where tasks are clustered by user, session, domain, or environment, sampling individual rows independently may be inappropriate. The unit of sampling should reflect the dependence structure. For example, if several test cases arise from one user session, treating them as independent can understate uncertainty. A cluster-level design, a concentration inequality for dependent observations, or an appropriately conservative aggregation is then required. EGE therefore separates statistical acceptance from persistent commitment. 7.1 Two-Phase Commitment The operational state machine has four states: (1) Candidate , where a proposed modification is frozen; (2) Audit-passed , where statistical and governance requirements pass; (3) Canary , where the candidate runs under bounded traffic, permissions, workload, or execution scope; and (4) Committed , where it becomes the persistent configuration after prespecified canary criteria pass. A candidate failing offline evaluation is rejected. A candidate failing canary criteria is contained and the incumbent configuration is restored. Configuration rollback does not reverse irreversible external side effects; systems with consequential tool access need transaction controls and compensating-action procedures. The canary scope can be limited along several dimensions: the fraction of eligible requests routed to the candidate, the set of users or tenants exposed, the classes of tools enabled, the maximum number of tool calls, the permitted data domains, the execution time, and the maximum resource budget. The appropriate controls depend on the agent’s action space. For a read-only summarisation agent, a traffic-split canary may be sufficient. For an agent that can modify records or trigger external workflows, a shadow mode or approval-gated mode may be more appropriate than live execution. The canary must have an explicit stop condition and an explicit authority to stop. A useful operational record includes the candidate hash, incumbent hash, canary start and end times, exposure volume, metric values, alert events, rollback reason, and any compensating actions. The candidate should not be permitted to change itself during the canary; otherwise, the object being monitored is no longer the object that passed the offline audit. If adaptation during canary operation is required, each resulting version should be treated as a new candidate or covered by a separately justified online-learning protocol. 7.2 Operational Monitoring Canary criteria should be application-specific and fixed before deployment. They may include task success, tool-error rate, latency, resource consumption, policy violations, escalation rate, and user-impact indicators. Repeated monitoring requires sequentially valid procedures or deterministic operational limits; a fixed-sample confidence interval must not be repeatedly inspected as if it were time-uniform. 7.3 Risk-Tiered Deployment A practical deployment controller can assign candidate changes to risk tiers before evaluation. A low-impact change might alter wording or reorder a non-critical retrieval preference while preserving permissions and tool interfaces. A medium-impact change might modify memory retrieval, task routing, or multi-step workflow logic. A high-impact change might modify access to sensitive data, external-action permissions, financial or operational workflows, or safety-relevant control logic. These examples are illustrative; the organisation must define its own classification. The risk tier should determine the required canary scope, duration, approval level, and rollback authority. It should not change the statistical meaning of the audit results after they have been observed. Higher-risk changes may require stricter margins, more protected metrics, deterministic checks, or mandatory human approval. Such requirements should be encoded before the candidate is evaluated. This preserves the separation between evidence and policy while allowing governance controls to scale with consequence. 7.4 Rollback and Recovery Rollback should be implemented as a tested recovery procedure rather than an informal instruction to restore a prior prompt. A complete recovery package may include the prior configuration, memory-schema compatibility information, tool and API versions, feature flags, routing state, and any migration steps required to restore service. If a candidate changes persistent memory, rollback may require restoring a snapshot or applying a reversible migration. If it writes to external systems, recovery may require compensating transactions and an incident process. Some side effects cannot be reversed; the system should identify those actions before enabling the candidate and apply stricter controls accordingly. The study should use a reproducible agent whose editable harness is limited to declared components such as prompts, retrieval settings, tool-routing rules, or workflow configuration. Candidate generation must be separated from audit and reference-evaluation services. Each candidate should be frozen and content-hashed before independent audit and calibration samples are drawn. Tasks and metrics. Use a task suite with deterministic verifiers wherever possible; benchmark design and red-teaming studies can inform breadth and adversarial coverage ( Hendrycks et al., 2021 ; Perez et al., 2022 ) . Include at least one primary objective and multiple protected capabilities chosen before the experiment. Candidate and comparator should be evaluated on the same task instances when pairing is valid. For stochastic agents, record model version, decoding settings, tool versions, seeds, timeouts, and retry rules. If tasks are clustered by user/session or share state, use cluster-level sampling or a concentration result appropriate to the dependence structure. Do not apply the independent-observation Hoeffding bound to correlated traces without justification. Candidate generation and baselines. Use the same candidate proposal stream for all adoption strategies where feasible. Compare (i) greedy observed-score acceptance, (ii) fixed observed-mean thresholds, (iii) confidence-bound acceptance without evaluator calibration, (iv) calibrated EGE without reference protection, (v) calibrated EGE with incumbent-only protection, and (vi) full EGE. Report candidate counts, accepted candidates, harmful acceptances, false rejections, inconclusive decisions, audit observations, calibration observations, elapsed time, and canary exposure. Ablations and stress conditions. Vary candidate index, audit size, calibration size, evaluator optimism, evaluator drift, protected-capability regression, and task-distribution shift. Include an evaluator-manipulation condition in which candidate changes alter output style or structure while leaving task success unchanged. Report evaluator/reference disagreement by candidate class and style. Use blinded human adjudication or deterministic verifiers as the reference procedure; if human ratings are used, report the rubric, rater training, agreement, adjudication, and sampling frame. The reference procedure must not be described as ground truth unless that claim is justified. Statistical reporting. Report campaign-level false-acceptance rates with exact or otherwise justified uncertainty intervals, acceptance power under prespecified effect sizes, per-capability regression rates, and audit cost. Report simulation seeds and number of repetitions. For agent experiments, report uncertainty across independent update episodes and avoid treating multiple tasks from one shared history as independent episodes. Separate offline false acceptance from canary failure: the former is controlled only under the theorem’s assumptions, whereas the latter depends on deployment representativeness and monitoring design. Canary study. A candidate that passes offline evaluation should enter a bounded, immutable canary. Predeclare exposure limits, permitted tools, monitoring metrics, stop rules, and rollback authority. If canary metrics are inspected repeatedly, use a sequentially valid procedure or deterministic stop thresholds; do not repeatedly inspect a fixed-sample interval as if it were time-uniform. Record irreversible side effects and compensating actions. The canary stage must not be described as statistically guaranteed by the offline theorem. 8.4 Reproducibility and Availability The supplementary simulation script specifies the random seed, data-generating process, allocation, sample sizes, thresholds, and outcome calculations. A real-agent reproduction package should additionally include candidate hashes, incumbent and reference versions, task provenance, evaluator prompts and versions, calibration records, raw aggregate scores, exclusion rules, and canary logs. Privacy- or security-sensitive task content may be withheld, but the paper should explain the resulting reproducibility limitation. No real-agent code or dataset is claimed to accompany this version. 9 Discussion 9.1 Generation Is Not Adoption Candidate generators optimise the production of plausible modifications. Adoption mechanisms decide whether evidence justifies changing persistent state. If the same agent proposes, evaluates, and endorses changes using overlapping evidence, a self-confirming loop can arise. EGE makes evidence separation explicit without requiring human approval for every low-impact update. Human review remains appropriate for high-impact changes or uncertain evaluators. This separation also clarifies accountability: the candidate generator is responsible for proposing a change, the evaluation service is responsible for producing a measurement under a fixed protocol, and the governance authority is responsible for defining which risks and trade-offs are acceptable. These responsibilities may be implemented by separate services or teams, but they should remain distinguishable in logs and incident reviews. 9.2 Evaluator Validity Fresh audit data do not guarantee evaluator correctness. The candidate-specific calibration construction accounts for sampling uncertainty in the observed discrepancy, but it does not repair an invalid reference construct or a calibration sample drawn from the wrong population. Candidate-specific calibration is expensive and may itself be vulnerable if the reference procedure is not blinded or independent. A model-based judge may favour a candidate’s style or exploit a scoring rubric. Deterministic verifiers and blinded human adjudication are preferable where feasible. For subjective tasks, inter-rater agreement and construct validity matter as much as nominal sample size. A numerically precise interval around a systematically invalid construct is not evidence that the intended capability has been measured. 9.3 Distribution Shift and Unmeasured Failure The statistical result concerns the declared distribution 𝒟 r \mathcal{D}_{r} . It does not automatically transfer to shifted deployment tasks. Monitoring, re-audit, and suspension of automatic adoption may be needed when representativeness deteriorates. No finite metric set can guarantee preservation of capabilities that are not measured. Distribution monitoring should therefore be treated as a trigger for reassessment, not as proof that the original guarantees remain valid. A shift detector can itself produce false alarms or miss subtle changes, so its thresholds and response policy should be documented. When a material shift is detected, the organisation may suspend automatic adoption, collect a new representative audit set, revise the target distributions, or require additional human review. Any change to the reference distribution or protected metric definitions should be versioned. 9.4 Conservatism and Cost The allocation in ( 17 ) is intentionally conservative: later candidate indices receive smaller per-test error levels. This can require larger samples. Alternatives include a finite prespecified candidate horizon, confidence sequences, or more efficient concentration bounds. Any replacement must preserve the relevant family-wise guarantee under its own assumptions. There is also a distinction between the error budget and the desired power of the procedure. The alpha-spending schedule controls false statistical acceptance, but it does not guarantee that beneficial candidates will be accepted. Later candidates receive smaller error allocations, which can increase the required sample size and the probability of an inconclusive outcome at a fixed sample size. A deployment study should therefore report both error control and power or acceptance probability under specified effect sizes. The system owner must choose an acceptable balance between audit cost, decision delay, and missed improvements. 10 Limitations and Future Work The guarantee assumes bounded paired observations and conditionally independent fresh confirmatory and calibration samples. Real agent tasks may be dependent, nonstationary, or affected by shared infrastructure. The reference-evaluation procedure must be sufficiently valid for the intended construct; the theorem only controls discrepancy relative to that procedure. Candidate-specific calibration is sample-intensive, and the Monte Carlo study is a stylised stress test rather than a real-agent evaluation. Extensions to clustered sampling, martingale differences, nonstationary evaluation, and efficient uniform calibration over candidate classes are important. A reference can become obsolete; the bridge-test result controls only declared metrics on a common distribution. Configuration rollback cannot undo external actions. The immediate empirical priority is to implement the protocol in a representative agent system and measure both audit cost and operational outcomes. A further limitation concerns metric multiplicity within a capability. A single aggregate score can conceal subgroup regressions, while a large collection of subgroup metrics increases the number of simultaneous tests and therefore the required error allocation. Hierarchical testing or structured metric families may improve efficiency, but they require a prespecified hierarchy and a valid multiplicity procedure. Another limitation is that bounded normalised scores can discard information about severity. For example, a rare catastrophic event may be poorly represented by an average score in [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] . Such outcomes should be represented by separate hard constraints, tail-risk measures, or event-rate requirements rather than being diluted in an average capability score. The framework also assumes that the comparator can be evaluated on the same instance as the candidate. This paired design is attractive because it removes some task-to-task variation, but it may not be feasible when candidate changes alter task eligibility, tool availability, or execution pathways. In such cases, the experiment may require matched cohorts, randomised assignment, or a different estimand. The resulting inference must reflect the actual design; the paired Hoeffding formula should not be applied mechanically. Finally, EGE is a governance framework, not a complete agent security architecture. It does not by itself prevent prompt injection, data exfiltration, supply-chain compromise, malicious tool responses, or failures in the underlying model. Permission isolation, secure execution, logging, identity management, and incident response remain necessary. The contribution is narrower: EGE specifies a statistically explicit gate for persistent adoption and a deployment protocol that complements those controls. 11 Conclusion This paper developed Evidence-Governed Evolution for the persistent adoption of self-generated modifications to autonomous-agent configurations. The framework separates candidate generation from confirmatory adoption, requires fresh paired evaluation, calibrates the operational evaluator against an independently governed reference procedure, protects declared capabilities relative to a versioned reference, and places accepted candidates behind bounded canary deployment. Under explicit conditional sampling assumptions, the evaluator-calibrated lower bounds provide simultaneous coverage over a countable sequence of adaptively generated candidates. A reference-lineage result makes the cost of replacing a protected baseline explicit, while an audit-planning inequality relates the effect-size margin to confirmatory and calibration sample requirements. The Monte Carlo stress test demonstrates one narrow point: candidate-dependent evaluator optimism can make an evaluator-naive gate accept harmful updates, while candidate-specific calibration can prevent those acceptances in a stylised setting. It does not establish that EGE improves real-agent performance, is operationally efficient across realistic workloads, or remains valid under arbitrary dependence and distribution shift. Those questions require a controlled agent-task study and careful validation of the reference evaluator. EGE should therefore be viewed as a formal adoption protocol with testable assumptions, not as a proof of universal agent safety. The central principle remains that generating a plausible update is not evidence that it should become persistent system state. References Agrawal et al. (2024) A. Agrawal, N. Kedia, A. Panwar, J.
