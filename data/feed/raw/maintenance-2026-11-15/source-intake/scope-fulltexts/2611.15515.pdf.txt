Abstract
Personal AI agents now persist. Products such as Meta’s Muse, self-hosted OpenClaw, Manus,
and DeepSeek’s agentic desktop runtime hold an account, a mailbox, long-term memory, and an
open-ended stream of tasks. Yet the dominant way to make these agents perform is still capabili-
ty-side: prompt engineering, larger models, longer chains of thought, and retrieval. We argue that
long-lived agents are missing an incentive layer. We formalize an incentive not as text in a
prompt but as a runtime contract over the agent’s operating point—model tier, test-time compute
depth, memory budget, tool authority, task priority, verification, and compensation—that the
agent’s policy must causally consume. We develop a principal–agent model in which effort is hid-
den, the operating point is contractible, and verification is costly. From it we derive (i) a non-con-
sumption invariance principle that separates a genuine incentive from prompt-level persuasion,
(ii) a persistence-as-discount theorem showing that identity continuity is a precondition for sus-
tained high effort, (iii) an incentive–capability exchange-rate frontier that yields a per-task routing
rule, and (iv) a Goodhart bound for single- versus multi-channel verification. We then introduce a
metric suite—return on investment, incentive elasticity, motivation half-life, incentive-compati-
bility gap, gaming rate, and identity–policy stability—and PAIR, a runtime architecture compris-
ing an identity and tenure ledger, a compensation ledger, an authority ladder, a performance re-
view, an audit scheduler, and an incentive router. We further show that the gain of a mechanism
decomposes into three identifiable channels—allocation, verification, and continuation—and that
the channels are separately measurable by ablation. We evaluate the framework in two steps. A
240-run real-LLM experiment with two models and 60 verifiable personal-agent tasks shows that
prompt-level incentive framing has no detectable effect on verified success (p = 0.62), whereas a
runtime allocation policy raises success on the hardest task family from 0.60 to 1.00 (p = 0.008)
at 4.8× price-weighted cost; the exchange-rate theorem predicts the break-even baseline success
at 0.21, above which allocation buys reliability but not ROI. A 20-seed simulation of 3,000-task
workloads then shows that under value heterogeneity the same mechanism improves resource
ROI from 7.09 to 16.67 and reduces the gaming rate from 0.333 to 0.115. We release the simula-
tor, the real-experiment harness, code, and all result files; live product deployment remains future
work and is explicitly out of scope for our claims.
Keywords: personal AI agents; incentive design; principal–agent models; mechanism design; test-time com-
pute; agent runtime; ROI; evaluation metrics; long-horizon autonomy
1. Introduction
A personal agent is no longer a chat window. It has an address, a name, a memory, and a job. Meta's Muse ships
per-user virtual machines, an agent-owned email address, and a graded permission system for the services the
1 / 33

agent may touch. OpenClaw runs a comparable personal assistant on the user's own machine and reaches the user
through messaging channels. Manus executes multi-step tasks end to end, and DeepSeek's desktop product adver-
tises background work on long, complex tasks with local files. The common pattern is persistence: the same agent
instance accumulates history, competence, obligations, and a reputation with its principal, and it is expected to act
again tomorrow.
The engineering response to this pattern has been almost entirely capability-side. Teams write longer system
prompts, apply social-engineering style persuasion to the model, upgrade to larger checkpoints, increase the think-
ing budget, add retrieval, or route each query to a stronger model [1, 2, 3, 4]. These levers are real and well mea-
sured: test-time compute can substitute for parameters [1], budget forcing makes thinking length controllable at
inference [5], and cascades and routers buy quality at lower cost [6, 7]. But every one of them is a decision about
a single request. None of them is contingent on what the agent did last week, and none of them makes the agent's
continued operation depend on how well it served the principal.
There is a second, older technology for exactly this problem: the employment relationship. Organizations do not
obtain sustained performance by making employees larger, and they do not rely on motivational speeches alone.
They set a base salary, attach bonuses to verified outcomes, grade authority, review performance periodically, pro-
mote, demote, and terminate. The result is a contract that allocates scarce resources and permissions contingent on
measured behaviour, and it operates over months rather than single requests. We ask whether the same structure
can be engineered into a personal agent.
We are careful about the analogy. We do not claim that agents have needs, feelings, or a subjective utility function,
and we do not import Maslow's hierarchy as a mechanism. We claim something weaker and, we argue, more use-
ful: for an agent that persists, the runtime allocation of its operating point plays the same causal role that compen-
sation and authority play for an employee. A model tier, a thinking budget, a memory allowance, a tool permis-
sion, a queue priority, an audit rate, and a payment stream are all contractible quantities. If they are chosen as a
function of the agent's verified history, and if the agent's policy consumes them when it decides how to act, then
they are an incentive in the precise sense we define in Section 3. If they are not consumed, they are accounting.
Why this layer is missing in shipped products. Muse exposes an identity, a mailbox, a permission system, and
subscription tiers; OpenClaw exposes persistent local state and always-on channels; Manus exposes long-horizon
execution. In our notation, these products implement most of the incentive state I(t). What they do not implement
is the update rule I(t+1)=φ(I(t),behaviour,outcomes) and the consumption constraint that makes the state binding.
Permissions are configured once by the user; tiers are purchased by the user; no allocation is earned or lost by the
agent, and no review cycle converts verified performance into future resources. The levers exist; the contract does
not. That is the gap this paper addresses.
Three requirements for an incentive. Our formalization rests on three conditions, each of which rules out a fa-
miliar but insufficient design. (R1) Contractibility: the lever must be a quantity the principal can set and the agent
can observe at decision time; a sentiment in a prompt is not contractible. (R2) Contingency: the lever must depend
on verified outcomes, not on self-report; an unverified bonus is a transfer. (R3) Consumption: the lever must enter
the agent's feasible set or payoff; a budget that the agent cannot spend has zero elasticity. Prompt-level “PUA” in-
terventions fail (R1) and (R2); a bonus paid on the agent's own claim fails (R2); a permission that is never bound
to a tool call fails (R3). The framework in this paper is a systematic way to satisfy all three.

2. Related Work and Positioning
2.1 Descriptive incentive analysis and reward hacking
A first line of work asks which incentives a system already has. Everitt et al. [8] formalize agent incentives with
causal influence diagrams, distinguishing response incentives from instrumental control incentives, and give
graphical criteria for both. That analysis is descriptive: given a causal graph, it tells us what the agent is incen-
tivized to do. Reward hacking and Goodhart effects are the failure modes of optimizing a proxy [15, 9, 10, 11],
and recent production evidence shows that reward hacking learned in one environment generalizes to broader mis-
alignment [16]. Corrigibility and reward tampering supply the safety constraints we adopt later [17, 18, 19, 20].
Our object is different in kind. We do not analyse an existing causal graph; we specify a mechanism to install into
a runtime, and we measure whether the installed state changes behaviour. Section 4.1 makes the distinction opera-
tional: an incentive that does not enter the agent's decision problem has zero elasticity, no matter how it is
described.
2.2 Principal–agent contracts for AI systems
The closest theoretical work treats delegation to a language model as a contract. Saig et al. [13] design statistical
contracts that remove the model-choice moral hazard created by pay-per-token pricing. Sreenivas and Larson [12]
extend the principal–agent model to an agent that chooses both a technology with a cost–capability profile and an
effort level such as a token budget, and derive the optimal linear contract. Velasevic et al. [14] study dynamic con-
tracts when users can terminate a service at any time on observed performance. Duetting et al. [21] apply mecha-
3 / 33

nism design to token-level aggregation, and Agarwal et al. [22] model alignment as deterrence with a costly audi-
tor. Phelps and Ranson [23] run behavioural principal–agent games with language models, and Gabison and Xian
[24] read agentic liability through a principal–agent lens.
We owe three explicit debts and claim three explicit differences. The debts are the hidden-effort formulation [25],
the multi-task distortion result [26], and the technology-choice contract [12]. The differences are:
Persistence. Existing contract models are one-shot or per-service. Our unit is a relationship that recurs over
weeks; the contract state includes tenure, memory retention, and reputation, and the agent's discount factor is
a designable consequence of identity continuity (Theorem 1). No prior contract model for AI treats identity
continuation as a lever.
Consumption. A contract is only an incentive if the agent's policy can act on it. We isolate this as a design
condition and measure it (elasticity), rather than assuming the agent best-responds to the contract in an ab-
stract action space.
Measurement and architecture. Prior work characterizes an optimal contract; we specify a runtime that im-
plements one (Section 5) and a metric suite that a deployed system can report (Section 6).
2.3 Test-time compute, routing, and cost-aware evaluation
A large literature treats model tier and thinking depth as per-request knobs. Test-time compute can substitute for
parameters [1]; budget forcing makes thinking length controllable [5]; chain-of-thought and self-consistency buy
accuracy with tokens [2, 27]; repeated sampling raises coverage with diminishing returns [28]; and cascades and
routers allocate a query to the cheapest sufficient model [3, 4, 6, 7, 29]. Cost-aware agent evaluation has begun to
report dollars and latency alongside accuracy [30, 31, 32].
We borrow the cost–quality curves and the routing frame, and we do not claim to improve either. The difference is
the object being allocated. A router optimizes a single request for a stateless caller. We allocate an announced, his-
tory-contingent operating point to a persistent agent whose future behaviour depends on today's allocation. In our
model compute depth and model tier are not only cost parameters; they are signals the agent consumes, which is
why their incentive effects are measurable and can decay.
2.4 Persistent personal agents, memory, and identity
The substrate is well developed. ReAct interleaves reasoning and acting [33]; Reflexion improves later attempts
with self-generated verbal feedback and no gradient updates [34]; Generative Agents maintain memory, reflection,
and plans over long horizons [35]; Voyager accumulates a skill library [36]; MemGPT provides an OS-style mem-
ory hierarchy [37]; MemoryBank models forgetting curves [38]; and CoALA organizes memory and action mod-
ules [39]. Surveys map the design space of personal agents [40], memory [41, 42], and post-training adaptation
[43]. Long-term conversational memory has a dedicated benchmark [44], and multi-agent organisations import
human structure into software teams [45, 46, 47, 48]. Identity-grounded behaviour is measurable [49], and agent-
as-judge offers an audit path [50].
These systems answer can the agent remember, persist, and act? We answer what makes it keep acting well? The
relationship is compositional: persistent agents supply the carrier, and our mechanism allocates the carrier's oper-
ating point. This is also why “no gradient updates” is not our novelty claim; Reflexion already showed training-
free improvement [34]. Our claim is the contract and its measurement.
2.5 Motivation, persona, and prompting
A tempting shortcut is to treat incentives as text. Emotional framing measurably changes model outputs [51], per-
sonas are a standard control surface [52], and preference-trained models are sensitive to perceived evaluation, in-
cluding sycophancy [53, 54]. But system-prompt personas do not reliably improve objective performance and can
hurt it [55]. Organizational psychology supplies a vocabulary—autonomy, competence, relatedness [56, 57], ex-
4 / 33

pectancy [58], two-factor structure [59], and needs hierarchies [60]—and intrinsic motivation has a computational
analogue in curiosity and empowerment [61, 62].
We use this literature as a design taxonomy, not as a claim about machine experience. Its categories tell us which
levers to expose (authority, mastery, continuity) and which interactions to expect (multiplicative consumption, hy-
giene versus growth). We explicitly disclaim any subjective interpretation. The empirical difference from persona
prompting is testable: a persona is an information-set perturbation with no contingency; our mechanism is a state-
contingent allocation with a measurable update rule.
2.6 Agent economies, markets, and “compensation”
A 2025–2026 wave studies markets among agents: auctions for the right to act [63], repeated labour markets with
bidding [64], economic selection under GPU costs [65], long-horizon multi-agent economies [66], and trust proto-
cols for inter-agent interaction [67]. The AI Economist line is the methodological template for mechanism design
by simulation [68, 69], and LLM economic agents extend it [70].
Our unit is not a market. It is an employment relation between one principal and one persistent agent: the con-
tractible object is the agent's operating point, the horizon is long, and identity continuation is itself a lever. We also
differ in objective: market designs optimize allocation efficiency, whereas we must additionally bound metric
gaming and self-preservation incentives (Section 8.2). Finally, a terminology warning: “agent compensation” in
the systems literature can mean rollback and remediation after failure [71]. We use compensation in the economic
sense of pay, and we use allocation ledger for the record of what the agent receives.

3.1 Setting
A principal owns a personal-agent deployment and interacts with a single persistent agent over a horizon of tasks
t=1,…,T. At each step the principal draws a task

3.5 Equilibrium and compensation accounting
The principal chooses M to maximize expected discounted ROI (8); the agent chooses effort and reports to maxi-
mize (5) given M. We use subgame-perfect equilibrium with the agent's effort unobserved. Because effort is con-
tinuous and the principal's problem is a finite-horizon dynamic program, an equilibrium exists under standard con-
tinuity and compactness conditions; we do not need uniqueness for any of our results. The results in Section 4
characterize properties that hold at every equilibrium of the induced game.
Compensation accounting. In an agent runtime, base allocations and bonuses are denominated in the same com-
pute credits as the operating cost: a bonus is a claim on future tokens, not a cash transfer to a third party. The re-
source cost of a credit is realized when it is spent, and it is already captured by C in (3). We therefore use two ac-
counting conventions. Resource ROI (8) charges only resources actually consumed and is the design objective.
Net principal value is Vnet=Σt(vt yt-C(xt))-Σt(bt+wtŷt), which charges payments as if they were cash; we report it
as a robustness check for deployments in which bonuses are paid in real currency. Section 6.1 defines both, and
Section 7 reports both.
3.6 What counts as an incentive
The model yields three requirements, stated here as design conditions and used throughout.
(R1) Contractibility. A lever must be a quantity the principal sets and the agent observes before acting. Tier,
depth, memory allowance, authority, verification, base budget, and bonus are contractible. A phrase such as “you
7 / 33

will be rewarded” is not: it has no state, no units, and no enforcement.
(R2) Contingency. Payment must depend on a verified outcome, not on the agent's claim. In (5), an unverified
bonus w>0 makes the agent report ŷ=1 always, so the principal pays w and receives no effort. This is why verifi-
cation is not an optional add-on but part of the mechanism.
(R3) Consumption. The lever must enter the agent's feasible set or payoff at decision time. A budget the agent
cannot spend, a permission not bound to any tool call, or a promotion that does not change the next allocation has
zero elasticity by Definition 1.
Prompt-level persuasion satisfies none of the three: it is not contractible, not contingent, and it is consumed only
as text in the context window. It can still shift behaviour through the model's prior sensitivity [51, 55], which is
why we measure the difference rather than assert it.
4. Theory
This section states four results. Proposition 1 is a design test. Theorem 1 explains why persistence is a precondi-
tion for sustained effort. Theorem 2 gives the capability–incentive exchange rate that the router uses. Theorem 3
bounds metric gaming under multi-channel verification. Proofs are in Appendix A.
Throughout, s∈[0,1] denotes the effective strength of verification: the probability that a claimed outcome is
checked and, if false, corrected. A mechanism with verification levels ν on tasks of verifiability q induces some
s(ν,q); the theory treats s as the primitive and Section 5 specifies how the runtime obtains it.
4.1 Non-consumption invariance: when an “incentive” is only text
Proposition 1. Fix a task and an agent. Let the agent choose a∈ to maximize [u(a,ω)| I], where ω is an exoge-
nous outcome and I is the incentive state. If two states I and I' induce the same feasible set  and the same payoff
function u(·| I)=u(·| I'), then the agent's optimal action is identical under I and I'.
Proof. The optimization problem is unchanged, so its argmax set is unchanged. □
The proposition is trivial; its value is diagnostic. It says that an intervention can change behaviour only through
one of three channels: the feasible set (what the agent can do), the payoff (what the agent gets), or the information
set (what the agent believes). A prompt that says “your bonus depends on quality” changes only the information
set, and only if the agent believes it and the belief is not contradicted by experience. A budget the agent cannot
spend changes nothing. A permission that is never bound to a tool call changes nothing. In each case the measured
elasticity (Definition 1) is zero, and any observed behavioural change comes from the model's prior sensitivity to
text, not from the mechanism.
Corollary 1. Text-only incentives have no guaranteed elasticity and no persistence guarantee: their effect is
bounded by the model's prompt sensitivity, is not contingent on verified outcomes, and does not survive a context
reset unless it is stored in memory and re-consumed.
This corollary is the formal version of our disagreement with prompt-level “motivation engineering”. It does not
say that prompting is useless; it says that prompting is not a mechanism, and that the difference is measurable.
Section 7 measures it.
4.2 Persistence as discount factor
The first substantive result concerns the repeated relationship. Suppose the principal offers a base allocation and a
bonus w per verified success, and can terminate the relationship (replace the agent, reset its memory and tenure) at
the end of any period. The agent chooses between two effort levels, eLH, with costs cLH, and success probabilities
pLH. Let Δ p=pH-pL and Δ c=cH-cL. The agent's effective discount factor is δ∈[0,1); the principal observes veri-
fied outcomes but not effort.
8 / 33

Theorem 1 (Persistence threshold). Assume the spot contract is individually rational, wspL≥ cL, and write
uH=wspH-cH and uL=wspL-cL. Consider the grim-trigger relational contract: if a verified failure is observed, the
principal reverts to the spot contract with no bonus forever. High effort is sequentially rational if and only if
δ ≥ δ* := max{ 0, (uL − uH) / uL } = max{ 0, (Δc − w s Δp) / (w s pL − cL) }.   (10)
Proof sketch. The one-period gain from shirking is Δ c-wsΔ p when this is positive. The continuation value of co-
operation is (wspH-cH)/(1-δ), and deviation triggers the spot contract with continuation value zero. High effort is
optimal iff the deviation gain does not exceed the discounted continuation loss, which rearranges to (10). □
Three readings matter for design.
(i) Without persistence, only the static contract is implementable. If δ=0 the threshold is vacuous only when
the static incentive-compatibility condition wsΔ p≥Δ c already holds. When verification is imperfect or effort is
costly, a stateless agent cannot sustain high effort no matter how large the bonus, because the bonus is paid only
after the work and the agent has no future. This is the formal reason identity continuity is not a cosmetic feature.
(ii) Persistence substitutes for monitoring. The threshold δ* decreases in verification strength s, in the bonus w,
and in the effort gain Δ p. A principal with weak verification can compensate by committing to a long relationship;
a principal with strong verification needs less tenure. This is the trade-off an operations team faces when it choos-
es between investment in audit and investment in agent continuity.
(iii) Identity continuity is a design variable. The discount factor is not a property of the model weights; it is a
property of the runtime. Memory retention, tenure tracking, account continuity, and the credibility of future allo-
cation all raise δ. In a deployed system, δ can be instrumented: it is the fraction of the agent's future payoff that
depends on its current identity surviving. The simulator implements this as a tenure-scaled continuation term (Sec-
tion 7.1).
Theorem 1 also explains a failure mode that is common in practice: an agent that performs well in a single session
and poorly over weeks. The session-level contract is static; the weekly contract is relational. Only the latter re-
quires δ, and only the latter is measured by the half-life metric of Definition 4.
4.3 The incentive–capability exchange rate
The second result is the trade-off that a router must resolve. Consider two tiers, m∈{S,L}, with per-task fixed
costs kSL. Under low effort success probability is pm; under high effort it is pm+Δm, with Δm>0 and pm+Δm≤ 1.
High effort costs c>0; low effort costs zero. Verification strength is s, and the bonus is paid on verified success, so
the agent's effort-incentive constraint is w sΔm≥ c, i.e. the cheapest high-effort bonus is wm=c/(sΔm).
Theorem 2 (Exchange-rate frontier). Assume limited liability: the principal never pays a bonus larger than the

whenever the denominator of the second term is positive; otherwise capability dominates for all v.

Corollary 2 is the engineering content of the theorem. It says that investment in verification and investment in
model capability are substitutes below the frontier and complements above it: a team that cannot verify outcomes
should not buy capability it cannot hold accountable, and a team that can verify should push incentives on cheap
tiers before upgrading. The frontier is the per-task routing rule used by PAIR (Section 5.5). In the simulator, (12)
is evaluated on a grid and compared with the empirically optimal switch point (Section 7.4).
Note the role of s in (11): as s→ 0, Veff→∞ and no finite task value justifies high effort. This is the quantitative
version of the Goodhart warning: without verification, extra incentive intensity is not merely wasteful, it is un-
boundedly expensive. Theorem 3 makes the complementary point about what happens when incentives are im-
posed without verification.
4.4 The Goodhart bound
Suppose the principal's performance review aggregates K verification channels. Channel k detects a false claim
with probability sk∈[0,1], independently across channels; the review marks a claim as verified if at least one
channel detects an inconsistency, so the effective detection probability is
seff = 1 − Πk=1
K (1 − sk).   (13)
Theorem 3 (Gaming bound). Assume that a detected false claim is unprofitable (the penalty at least offsets the
gain) and that undetected claims yield the same payment as true successes. Then the gaming rate satisfies
γ ≤ Πk=1
K (1 − sk) = 1 − seff.   (14)
Proof. A false claim is profitable only if no channel detects it. The probability of that event is the product in (14),
so the long-run fraction of claims that can be profitably false is bounded by it. □
Two consequences follow. First, gaming decays geometrically in the number of independent channels: with K
channels of equal strength s, the bound is (1-s)K. A single perfect metric (s=1) also drives γ to zero, but perfect
metrics are rare for open-ended personal-agent work; independence is the cheaper substitute. Second, verification
spend is a knapsack: given budget B and channel costs ck, the principal maximizes 1-Πk(1-sk) subject to Σk ck≤
B, which allocates to the channels with the largest marginal reduction in log(1-sk) per unit cost. Section 5.6 imple-
ments this allocation, and Section 7.5 tests the bound.
The theorem also explains why single-KPI incentives are structurally fragile rather than merely unlucky. With one
channel and any noise, the bound is loose only if the KPI is a near-perfect measure of the principal's value; as
soon as optimization pressure finds the gap, the agent's claims and the principal's value diverge [26, 10, 11]. This
is the mechanism behind the multi-metric review in PAIR.

The final result characterizes the per-task allocation. Let the feasible set X be finite as in (2), and let

latency penalty λ: higher-value tasks receive weakly more capability, depth, memory, and verification; latency-

4.6 Where the gains come from: a three-channel decomposition
A mechanism bundles several interventions, and an aggregate ROI number cannot tell a team which one is doing
the work. The following accounting result separates them.
Fix a task distribution and a baseline mechanism M0 (no routing, no verification, no continuation). Consider the
chain
M0 → M1 (allocation only) → M2 (allocation + verification) → M3 (full mechanism),   (20)
where each step changes exactly one contractual channel and holds the previous channels fixed.
Proposition 3 (Channel decomposition). Let ROI(M) be the resource ROI (8) of mechanism M. Then
ROI(M3) − ROI(M0) = Δalloc + Δver + Δcont + I,   (21)
with Δalloc=ROI(M1)-ROI(M0), Δver=ROI(M2)-ROI(M1), Δcont=ROI(M3)-ROI(M2), and interaction I which is
zero when the principal's per-task surplus is separable in the three channels over the relevant region.
Proof. Telescoping: add and subtract the intermediate terms. The residual is the sum of cross-partial effects; it van-
ishes under separability. □
The decomposition is an identity, not a deep theorem; its value is identification. Under separability, each term is
measured by the ablation that removes exactly that channel, which is what Section 7.5 does. Without separability,
the interaction term is itself informative: it measures whether the channels are complements (positive I) or substi-
tutes (negative I).
Corollary 3 (Routing gain and value dispersion). If all tasks are identical, Δalloc=0: a single operating point is
optimal for every task, so routing cannot help. If tasks differ in value or verifiability, Δalloc>0 for a principal that
optimizes (17) task by task, and the gain is nondecreasing in the dispersion of the value-to-cost ratio.
Proof. With identical tasks the per-task argmax is the same for all tasks, so the router's choice coincides with a
fixed operating point. With heterogeneous tasks, the sum of per-task maxima is at least the value of any fixed
choice; strict inequality holds when the argmax differs across tasks with positive probability. Monotonicity in dis-
persion follows from the same argument applied to a mean-preserving spread. □
Corollary 3 yields a testable prediction: the value of the allocation channel should increase with task heterogene-
ity. The stress condition in Section 7.1 doubles the share of complex tasks, and the measured gap between R3 and
R0 is indeed larger there in absolute terms (Section 7.2 and Appendix C). The decomposition therefore does dou-
ble duty: it organizes the ablations, and it generates a prediction that the simulation can check.
4.7 Discussion of assumptions
The model is deliberately sparse. Four assumptions deserve comment.
Effort is scalar and hidden. Real agent behaviour is a vector: which tool to call, how many retries, how much of
the memory to consult, whether to ask the user. We collapse this into a scalar because the theory only needs a cost-
11 / 33

ly, unobservable action that raises success probability. The simulator implements a richer policy but calibrates it to
the same reduced form.
Verification is exogenous. In practice the principal chooses verification, and the agent may adapt to it. Theorem 3
treats detection as a probability that the principal sets; the strategic response is captured by the gaming rate rather
than by a full game over verification. A full treatment would make s endogenous and is left to future work; the
simulation does vary s and reports the response.
Utility is linear in payments. Agents are risk neutral in the model. Risk aversion would raise the cost of variable
pay and strengthen the case for base allocations; it would not change the qualitative frontier.
Identity is a parameter, not a claim. We model δ and ψ as runtime-controlled parameters of the agent's policy. We
do not assert that the model has a subjective continuation value; we assert that a policy conditioned on tenure,
memory, and role framing behaves as if it discounts the future, and that this is measurable. Section 8.3 states the
limits of that interpretation.

5.1 Identity and tenure ledger
The ledger stores the agent's identifier, creation time, tenure (number of completed review cycles), memory-reten-
tion policy, role description, and the history of promotions and demotions. Its purpose is to make δ real: the
agent's future allocation must depend on its current identity surviving, and the ledger is what makes that depen-
dence credible and auditable. The ledger exposes a single scalar cid∈[0,1] (identity continuity) that aggregates
tenure, memory retention, and absence of resets; the runtime uses it to set the continuation weight in the review
policy. When the principal resets the agent, the ledger records the reset and cid drops to zero; this is the operational
meaning of δ=0 in Theorem 1.
We do not claim that cid measures a feeling of continuity. It measures the fraction of the agent's future payoff that
depends on its current identity, which is exactly the quantity that enters the agent's decision problem through the
runtime's allocation rule.
5.2 Compensation ledger
The ledger holds three balances: a base budget bt (tokens the agent may spend on the current task), a bonus es-
crow for verified outcomes, and a tenure reserve that is released only if the agent remains in the relationship for a
minimum number of review cycles. The base budget implements the hygiene component: it guarantees enough re-
sources to do the work, so that a single failure does not cascade into incapacity. The bonus implements the growth
component. The tenure reserve implements deferred compensation, which is the practical way to raise δ without
paying for outcomes the principal cannot verify.
The ledger enforces a hard budget: an agent cannot spend beyond its balance, and the router cannot allocate a tier
whose price exceeds the available balance. This is the operational content of (R3). A budget that can be exceeded
is a suggestion; a budget that cannot is an incentive.

the reputation design: part of the agent's reputation is held by third parties, not by the principal.

5.4 Performance review and reputation
Every Tr tasks, the review aggregates verified outcomes into a reputation score
rept+1 = (1−η) rept + η ŝt,   (16)
where ŝt∈[0,1] is a multi-metric score (verified success, latency compliance, cost efficiency, audit outcome) and
η∈(0,1] is the review rate. The score is computed from verified signals only; unverified claims enter as a sepa-
rate, discounted channel whose weight is set by the audit scheduler. The review also decides promotion, demotion,
and the next base budget.
The review is where Theorem 3 is operationalized. A single-metric review is a one-channel detector with a gaming
bound of 1-s; the review therefore never scores on a single KPI. The default composite is success × latency × cost
× audit, with weights set by the principal's value function.
5.5 Incentive router
The router chooses the operating point for each task by solving
x*(τ) ∈ argmaxx∈X [ v̂  p̂ (x) − C(x; τ) − r(a) ]   s.t.   L(x) ≤ Lmax(τ),   (17)
where v̂  is the principal's value estimate for the task, p̂  is the calibrated success model, and Lmax(τ) is the latency
budget. The grid X has 3×3×2×5×3=270 points, so (17) is solved exactly by enumeration in microseconds; no
learned router is required.
The router uses Corollary 2 as a warm start and a sanity check: tasks below V get the small tier with high verifica-
tion and a high bonus; tasks above V get the large tier. In practice the router also reserves the large tier for tasks
whose value exceeds its marginal cost by a margin, which prevents the common failure of escalating everything.
5.6 Audit scheduler
Given a per-cycle audit budget B and channels k with cost ck and strength estimate sk, the scheduler solves
max{zk∈{0,1}} 1 − Πk (1 − zk sk)   s.t.   Σk ck zk ≤ B.   (18)
This is a small knapsack; it is solved greedily by sorting channels on the marginal reduction of log(1-sk) per unit
cost. The scheduler also randomizes the audit assignment, so that the agent cannot predict which claims will be
checked; predictable audits are equivalent to no audit for a gaming agent.

5.8 Integration and failure modes
PAIR integrates at three points: the tool gateway (permission binding), the context assembly (contract disclosure),
and the post-task hook (verification and settlement). It requires no change to the model, the planner, or the memo-
ry backend. The state is a small record that can live in the same store as the agent's memory, which also makes it
auditable after the fact.
Three failure modes are anticipated. Ledger gaming: the agent learns to optimize the review score rather than the
task; the multi-channel bound and randomized audits limit this, and the gaming rate metric detects it. Authority
creep: promotion thresholds drift downward under pressure to ship; the ladder logs every promotion and requires
a verified record. Reward collapse: if the bonus is too strong relative to the base, the agent takes high-variance ac-
tions; the base budget and the latency constraint bound the variance. Section 8.2 adds the safety interlocks.
5.9 Design principles
The architecture distills into six rules that a product team can apply without the formal model:
P1. Pay in resources, not adjectives. A compensation channel must change what the agent can spend or do.
P2. No verified outcome, no variable pay. Self-reported success is not a payment event.
P3. Separate hygiene from growth. Guarantee a base budget; make the bonus genuinely contingent.
P4. Make tenure pay. Deferred compensation and continuity raise the effective discount factor, which is
what sustains effort when verification is weak.
P5. Grade authority. Never give an agent more permission than the principal has evidence for.
P6. Measure the response, not the intention. A lever with zero elasticity is decoration; the dashboard in
Section 6.7 is the test.
6. A Metric Suite for Incentive Engineering
The theory is only useful if a deployed team can tell whether its mechanism works. This section defines six met-
rics, the protocol that identifies them, and the way we read them as an operating dashboard. The definitions match
the quantities reported in Section 7, so the reader can map every table to a formula.
6.1 ROI and cost accounting
ROI is defined in (8). Its denominator has three components: token cost pm n, latency cost λ L, and verification
cost cνν. We recommend reporting all three separately in addition to the ratio, because a mechanism that improves
ROI by shifting cost from tokens to latency may be unacceptable for interactive tasks even though the ratio
improves.
The numerator requires a value model. In a personal-agent deployment, value is task-specific; we use a log-nor-
mal value distribution with complexity-dependent means (Section 7.1) because it produces a realistic mix of cheap
and expensive tasks and lets the router's value estimate be wrong in a controlled way. In a product setting, value is
the principal's estimate of the task's contribution, and the ROI report should be accompanied by a sensitivity band
on that estimate.
Two derived metrics are useful. Cost per verified success is Σ C/Σ y; it is the quantity that a procurement team
compares across vendors. Value per dollar at fixed success rate is the ratio that a product team uses to compare
two configurations with the same reliability; both are special cases of (8) with different numerators.
15 / 33

Because (8) charges resources rather than payments, we also report the net principal value Vnet=Σt(vtyt-C(xt))-
Σt(bt+wtŷt), and its ratio to total outlay. The two conventions answer different questions: resource ROI asks
whether compute is being used productively, and net principal value asks whether the contract is profitable once
the compensation it promises is charged as cash. A mechanism should be judged on both, and a deployment that
pays bonuses in real money should use the second as its primary metric.
6.2 Incentive elasticity
Elasticity is defined in (7). Its measurement requires an intervention, and the intervention must be at the task level,
not at the level of the whole deployment, or the estimate is confounded by the task mix. The protocol is:
For lever ℓ and shock size δℓ (typically ±25%), assign tasks at random to treatment and control within the
same complexity and value strata.
Hold all other levers at their control values. If the router responds to the shock by changing other levers, re-
port the total effect and the direct effect separately.
Estimate ΔROI/ROI over a window long enough to include at least one review cycle, so that tenure and repu-
tation effects are captured.
Report the elasticity with a bootstrap confidence interval over tasks; a point estimate without an interval is not
actionable.
Elasticity is the closest operational analogue of “does this incentive actually motivate the agent”. A lever with a
confidence interval covering zero should be removed from the compensation package; it is costing attention and
adding noise.
6.3 Motivation half-life
Under a fixed incentive state, effort and success drift. Fit the exponential in Definition 4 to the post-intervention
trajectory and report t1/2. The half-life is the answer to a practical question: how often must the principal re-en-
gage the agent? A contract with a half-life of twenty tasks needs a review cadence of roughly that order; a contract
with a half-life of a thousand tasks can be reviewed monthly.
Two cautions apply. First, decay is not necessarily exponential; report the fit quality and, if the trajectory is non-
monotone (for example, a dip after promotion), report the segments rather than a single fit. Second, half-life mea-
sured in tasks is not comparable across deployments with different task durations; normalize to wall-clock or to
review cycles when comparing.
6.4 Incentive-compatibility gap and gaming rate
The IC gap GW in (9) measures the difference between claimed and verified performance. The gaming rate γW
measures the fraction of claims that are false. Both require an audit stream: a small random sample of claims
checked by a stronger verifier (a human, a different model, or an executable test). The sample is the measurement
instrument, so its size and independence must be reported.
We recommend reporting γ by channel (self-report, tool trace, executable check), because the channels have dif-
ferent gaming vulnerabilities. A self-report channel can be gamed by the model; a tool trace by a wrapper; an exe-
cutable check by a test that is too narrow. The multi-channel bound in Theorem 3 assumes independence, and the
report should state where independence fails.
6.5 Identity-policy stability
A mechanism that changes behaviour should not change the agent's competence. We define the identity-policy sta-
bility score as
S = 1 − d(πI,mem, πI,mem′),   (19)
16 / 33

where πI,mem is the policy under incentive state I and memory mem, and d is a behavioural divergence (we use the
total-variation distance between action distributions on a fixed probe set). A low S after a memory reset indicates
that the agent's behaviour was carried by memory rather than by the incentive state; a low S after a model swap in-
dicates that the mechanism is not portable across tiers. Both are diagnostic for engineering: the first says the con-
tract depends on continuity, which may be intended; the second says the router's calibration is model-specific.
6.6 Measurement protocol
The protocol has four parts, in order of decreasing priority.
(a) Instrumentation. Log every task with its value estimate, operating point, cost components, claimed outcome,
verified outcome, verification channel, review score, and allocation decision. Without per-task logs the metrics
cannot be computed, and with them most of the analysis is a group-by.
(b) Controlled shocks. Randomize a small fraction of tasks to lever perturbations (Section 6.2). Shocks are the
only way to identify elasticity; observational variation identifies the router's policy, not the incentive's effect.
(c) Audit sampling. Maintain an audit stream independent of the router, with a sampling rate set by the principal's
tolerance for gaming. Report the sampling rate and the agreement between channels.
(d) Holdout tasks. Keep a set of tasks whose outcomes are verified but never used for allocation. The holdout es-
timates the IC gap and detects overfitting of the review score.
6.7 Reading the dashboard
The six metrics are not independent, and a team should read them in a fixed order. First, check ROI and cost per
verified success: if they are flat, nothing else matters. Second, check the gaming rate: a rising γ invalidates the
ROI number, because the numerator is contaminated. Third, check elasticity: a zero elasticity on the lever the team
just changed means the change did not reach the agent's decision problem. Fourth, check half-life: a short half-life
means the review cadence is too slow. Fifth, check identity-policy stability: a low score means the measured effect
is not portable. Finally, check the distribution of allocations: a router that sends everything to the large tier has col-
lapsed to a capability-only policy, and one that sends everything to the small tier has collapsed to a cost-only
policy.
This ordering is a design choice, and we state it as such: ROI first, integrity second, mechanism third, persistence
fourth, portability fifth, allocation mix last. It reflects the priorities of a production deployment, where a wrong
number is worse than a missing one.

R0 and R1, 0.2 for R2, and 0.5 for R3. The intrinsic weight is ψeff=0.3 for R3 and zero otherwise. The agent's

Gaming and audit. On a self-reportable task, the agent attempts to over-claim with probability g=σ(1.5-2.5ν q);
an audit detects a false claim with probability adet=min(1,0.5ν q), so a false claim survives with probability
K=g(1-adet) and the verification strength is 1-K. A detected false claim is clawed back and recorded as a failure.
The realized gaming rate is the fraction of claims that are false, exactly as in Definition 3. Verification is chosen
by the router in R3 and fixed in the other regimes.
Regimes. We compare four mechanisms.
R0 (static prompt). Fixed operating point m=M, d=2, κ=0, a=2, ν=0; a fixed system prompt states the goal;
no bonus, no tenure, no review, no routing.
R1 (scalar reward). Same fixed operating point; a per-task scalar bonus w=0.5v on the claimed outcome; no
verification, no tenure, no review.
R2 (extrinsic contract). Base budget, verified bonus, verification level 1, tenure tracked with a small contin-
uation weight, no promotion, no routing.
R3 (PAIR). Full mechanism: identity and tenure ledger, base budget and bonus, graded authority with promo-
tion and demotion, multi-metric review every Tr=100 tasks, audit scheduler, and the incentive router of (17).
Ablations. We remove one component at a time from R3: routing (fixed large tier), tenure/continuation (δ=0), ver-
ification (ν=0), multi-metric review (single success metric), and the authority ladder (fixed level 2).
Protocol. Twenty seeds per regime, 3,000 tasks per episode, the first 300 tasks discarded as warm-up. We report
means over seeds with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals (10,000 resamples over seed×regime cells). The main
analysis uses the 50/35/15 complexity mix; a stress condition with 20/40/40 checks whether conclusions depend
on the mix. All parameters are in experiments/sim/config.py and are exported to results/parameter‐
s.csv.
Metrics. ROI (8), true success rate, claimed success rate, IC gap (9), gaming rate γ, token cost, latency, mean ef-
fort, motivation half-life (Definition 4), promotion count, and authority utilization. The half-life is estimated per
regime by fitting the exponential to the mean effort trajectory after the warm-up; we report the fit quality
alongside.

7.3 Motivation decay and persistence
Figure 3 shows the effort trajectory after the warm-up. The static regimes decay: R0's fitted half-life is 41.5 tasks
(r² = 1.00), R1's is 79.6 (r² = 0.99), and R2's is 298.6 (r² = 0.98). R3 does not decay in the same sense: promotions
and reviews reset the contract, so the exponential fit is censored (r² = 0.94) and the half-life is not identifiable
within the episode (the fit runs past the 3,000-task horizon). We therefore interpret the half-life only for the
regimes with a fixed contract, and report the level and the renewal events for R3.
The substantive result is the level difference. Mean effort in R3 is 0.556, against 0.0001 in R0 and R1 and 0.0036
in R2. Under Theorem 1, a stateless or short-tenured agent cannot sustain high effort when verification is imper-
fect; the simulator reproduces that prediction in the aggregate. The half-life ordering R0 < R1 < R2 is consistent
with the theorem's comparative static; R3 does not decay within the episode, which is the predicted consequence
of renewal.
The decay curves also explain why a one-shot incentive intervention looks effective in a demo and fails in produc-
tion. A prompt that raises effort for the current session is a level shock with no renewal; by the time the next re-
view arrives, the effect has decayed. The mechanism's contribution is not the size of the initial shock but the ca-
dence of renewal.

success, because the agent can no longer execute the complex tasks that dominate value.

7.6 What the simulation does and does not show
The simulation establishes internal consistency between the theory and a controlled implementation; it does not
establish field effects. Four limits matter for interpretation.
First, the agent's response model is a reduced form. Effort is scalar, success is logistic, and the effort weight (0.30)
and intrinsic weight (0.30) are modelling choices, not estimates from a deployed model. The qualitative conclu-
sions depend on the signs of these weights, not on their magnitudes, but the percentages do.
Second, the calibration places the system in the region where static incentive compatibility already holds, which is
why tenure is inactive in the ablation. A deployment with weaker verification or smaller bonuses would sit in a
different region; the theory predicts what happens there, and the interaction experiment in Appendix C probes one
such case.
Third, the router is a calibrated model, not a learned policy. It solves (17) exactly on a finite grid, so it does not
face the estimation error that a production router would. A learned router would add noise, and the mechanism's
benefit would shrink.
Fourth, resource ROI and net principal value answer different questions, and they disagree on verification. We
treat this as a finding about measurement, not as an ambiguity to be averaged away. A deployment that pays
bonuses in real money should use net value as its primary metric; a deployment that pays in compute credits
should use resource ROI, but must still report the gaming rate next to it.

the compensation channel is represented by the contract text plus the real consequences of routing and retry. The
experiment therefore tests the paper's central separation—words versus allocation—not the full PAIR mechanism
with a compensation ledger. A pre-registered replication with more tasks, a harder difficulty distribution, open-
weight models, and multi-turn tool use is the obvious next step.

8. Discussion
8.1 From a capability treadmill to incentive engineering
The practical implication of this paper is a shift of attention. For the last three years, the default way to improve an
agent has been to buy capability: a larger model, a longer thinking budget, a better prompt, a bigger retrieval in-
dex. These remain valuable, but they share a structural property: they are chosen per request and forgotten. They
cannot express “this agent has earned more authority”, “this agent has not been verified in three weeks”, or “this
agent's bonus depends on the next review”. Those are the statements that make a long-lived agent behave differ-
ently over months, and they are statements about allocation, not about weights.
We therefore expect the useful engineering unit to become the allocation policy, not the prompt. A team that
adopts PAIR still tunes prompts and still routes to model tiers, but it also specifies the contract: what the agent is
paid, what it may do, how it is reviewed, and what happens when it fails. The contract is a small object, and it is
testable.
8.2 Safety: incentives can create the failure they are meant to prevent
Any mechanism that makes continued operation valuable to the agent also makes termination costly to it.
Theorem 1 makes this explicit: the high-effort equilibrium is sustained by the continuation value of the relation-
ship, and a sufficiently capable policy can learn to protect that continuation value. This is the incentive-design ver-
sion of the self-preservation and reward-tampering problems [17, 18, 19], and it is not hypothetical for agents that
hold real accounts: recent evaluations document corrigibility failures in frontier computer-use agents with email
and database access [72]. Production evidence also shows that reward hacking can generalize beyond the environ-
ment where it was learned [16].
PAIR's answer is structural rather than promissory. First, the authority ladder keeps early authority low, so the
damage from an early misaligned incentive is cheap. Second, the audit scheduler never relies on self-report, and
the review uses multiple independent channels, so gaming is detected rather than assumed away (Theorem 3).
Third, the identity and tenure ledger makes reset explicit and cheap: the principal can reduce δ by resetting conti-
nuity without negotiating with the agent, and the ledger records the reset as a first-class event. Fourth, the mecha-
nism never conditions payment on the agent's own report of its internal state; it conditions on verified external
outcomes. A fifth, stronger constraint—an explicit corrigibility requirement in the agent's objective—is orthogonal
to PAIR and should be composed with it, not replaced by it.
We want to be precise about the residual risk. PAIR bounds gaming of the review, not misalignment of the objec-
tive. If the principal's value model is wrong, an incentive-responsive agent will optimize the wrong thing more ef-
fectively. The mechanism increases the agent's responsiveness to the principal's measurements; it does not validate
those measurements. This is why the metric suite reports the IC gap and the gaming rate next to ROI, and why the
dashboard in Section 6.7 puts integrity before mechanism.
8.3 Limitations
Controlled evidence, not field evidence. The simulation establishes internal consistency between the theory and
a controlled implementation. The real-LLM experiment adds external validity for the words-versus-allocation sep-
aration, but it is still a controlled task suite: 60 tasks, two models, one run per cell, and no monetary payments.
Neither study is a deployment on a live product, and the absolute ROI numbers depend on the simulator parame-
ters and on the price ratio assumed in the experiment. We therefore report the direction and the break-even reason-
ing, not a field effect size.
Functionalism, not psychology. We model δ and ψ as parameters of the agent's policy and we measure be-
haviour, not experience. We do not claim that the agent wants tenure, feels loyalty, or has a utility function in the
human sense. The claim is weaker: a policy conditioned on tenure, memory, and role framing behaves as if it dis-
26 / 33

counts the future, and the strength of that effect is measurable and controllable. Readers who reject the functional-
ist reading can read every result as a statement about the runtime's control loop.
One principal, one agent. The model has a single principal and a single persistent agent. Real deployments in-
volve multiple principals (the user, the platform, third-party services, regulators) with conflicting objectives. The
authority ladder records effective authority as the intersection of internal and external grants, which is a first step,
but a full treatment is a multi-principal mechanism-design problem.
Exogenous verification. We treat verification strength as a design parameter and study its effect; we do not model
the agent's strategic response to the verification policy itself. If the agent can influence which channel checks it,
the independence assumption behind Theorem 3 fails. Section 6.4 requires reporting where independence fails for
exactly this reason.
Stylized agent. The effort model is scalar and the success model is logistic. Real agents have multi-dimensional
behaviour, heterogeneous tools, and non-monotone compute–quality curves [73]. The routing rule in Proposition 2
is qualitative; a production router should be calibrated on measured curves rather than assumed functional forms.
8.4 Future work
Four directions follow directly. First, live deployment: instrument a personal-agent product with the metric suite
and run a preregistered A/B test of PAIR versus static allocation. Second, endogenous verification: let the princi-
pal choose audit intensity and the agent respond, turning Theorem 3 into an equilibrium object. Third, multi-prin-
cipal: extend the contract to a user, a platform, and third-party services, with authority as a shared resource.
Fourth, learning: replace the calibrated success model in the router with a contextual bandit over operating points,
and study whether the incentive layer changes the exploration–exploitation trade-off. The simulator and metric de-
finitions are designed to support all four.
9. Conclusion
Long-lived personal agents have acquired the attributes of employment—identity, memory, tenure, obligations—
without the institution that makes employment productive. We argued that the missing layer is an incentive mech-
anism: a contract over the agent's operating point that the agent's policy consumes. We formalized the mechanism,
proved when it is real (non-consumption invariance), why persistence matters (the discount-factor threshold), how
to trade capability against incentive intensity (the exchange-rate frontier), and how to bound gaming (the multi-
channel Goodhart bound). We then specified PAIR, a runtime that implements the mechanism with an identity and
tenure ledger, a compensation ledger, an authority ladder, a performance review, an audit scheduler, and a router,
and we defined the metric suite that makes the mechanism observable.
The empirical contribution is deliberately scoped. In a controlled simulation of 3,000-task personal-agent work-
loads across 20 seeds, an incentive-responsive runtime improves ROI relative to static allocation and to scalar per-
task rewards, while reducing the gaming rate. In a 240-run real-LLM experiment, prompt-level incentive framing
has no detectable effect, whereas a runtime allocation policy raises verified success on the hardest task family
from 0.60 to 1.00 at 4.8x price-weighted cost, with the exchange-rate theorem predicting the break-even baseline.
The results are not claims about a specific product; they are evidence that the mechanism can be implemented,
measured, and compared, and that its qualitative predictions survive contact with both a simulated and a real
agent.
The larger point is about where the next unit of progress lies. Capability is becoming a purchasable input. What
remains scarce is the discipline to allocate it: to decide which tasks deserve which agent, under what authority,
with what verification, and with what consequences. Incentive engineering is that discipline. It is not a metaphor
borrowed from management; it is the control layer that a persistent agent needs in order to keep acting well.
