This raises the question of whether they can also carry out control system design. In this paper, we
consider control system design as a task for LLM agents and grade the designed controller as control
engineers do: the controller is given in a single Python file mapping measurements to actions,
evaluated on held-out seeds against an ordered set of requirements, under specified physical
perturbations and structural certificates, without using neural networks. We formulate the task as a
chance-constrained feasibility problem and instantiate it in ControlHarbor, a benchmark of 44 tasks over
13 plants, which includes network consensus, vehicle platooning, the MuJoCo hopper, walker and humanoid.
The tasks are packaged in the Harbor format, so that any agent harness can be run against them, and
the same environments also serve learning algorithms other than LLMs, such as reinforcement learning
(RL), under the same evaluation. With ControlHarbor, we observe that the controllers designed by three
frontier coding agents, Claude Opus 5.5, Fable 5.1 and Sonnet 5.5, reach the RL return on every parity
task, but only in a minority of sessions. We also observe that the controllers certified for return
alone usually fail under perturbations. When robustness is part of the specification, a structured
design meets it at the RL return on the humanoid task, where domain-randomised RL does not; under large
pushes, domain-randomised RL succeeds and no designed controller does. The benchmark, the controllers,
and their grades are released. 1 1 1 Tasks, controllers, grades and scripts: https://github.com/jq-wei/hl-control . keywords LLM agents, control synthesis, benchmarks, reinforcement learning, legged locomotion 1 Introduction Coding agents, i.e., language models operating a terminal, now solve a growing share of the
software-engineering tasks of benchmarks such as SWE-bench and Terminal-Bench [ 14 , 20 ] , and the harnesses built for those benchmarks, i.e., the
programs that call the model in a loop, give it tools and record its actions, run any such
agent in a container against a test it cannot see [ 13 ] . Control design is also done in
code, as a feedback law and its evaluation in simulation, so a natural question is whether LLM
agents can design controllers, and how a controller designed this way compares with a
reinforcement-learning solution, trained on the same plant . In this paper we consider control design as a task for a
coding agent and grade it as control engineers do: one file, evaluated on seeds the agent never sees
against an ordered set of requirements, under physical perturbations and structural certificates,
within one hour, and without using neural networks or gradient-based learning. 2 2 2 Both are excluded
so that the controller stays a white-box program whose structure an engineer can read, check and
modify; the RL solutions supply the black-box comparison. The controller is given in a single
Python file that maps measurements to actions: the file is the whole artefact, so it can be read,
re-run and graded as it stands, and it cannot rely on trained weights or outside services. For each
plant, successive task tiers introduce one additional challenge at a time, which are model mismatch,
sensing delay and noise, heterogeneity, coupling and disturbances. On the hopper, walker, humanoid, and
lunar lander, the designed controllers are compared with the reference RL policies under the same
plant-specific perturbations on the robust tiers. On the three locomotion plants, we also test nominal
parity by requiring controllers to meet a distance target and achieve at least 90 % of the reference
policy’s mean return. Together, these tests show which performance and robustness requirements each
approach meets on each plant. The locomotion plants serve as stress tests: they are hard, far from saturated,
and carry strong RL solutions, and they are not a model of how legged robots are controlled in
deployment. (a) Humanoid (b) Walker (c) Hopper (d) Lunar lander (e) Vehicle platoon (f) Cart-link swing-up (g) Two-link arm (h) SO(3) synchronisation Figure 1: The benchmark spans networked systems, manipulators and legged robots, and every plant shown is

or tunes textbook control: ControlBench [ 15 ] grades frontier models on
undergraduate problems, ControlAgent and AgenticControl [ 11 , 21 ] tune classical compensators on linear plants, and CoDyControlBench [ 37 ] asks
six models to design feedback for 132 coupled systems; these check the specification on the nominal
model and put no RL solution beside the designed one. In the second, the model writes an executable
policy and a search loop scores it: Code as Policies and Voyager [ 17 , 32 ] write robot programs, Eureka [ 18 ] writes the reward, and FunSearch, AlphaEvolve and GEPA [ 27 , 22 , 1 ] search over programs or prompts with a
scorer in the loop. With a coding agent maintaining a programmatic policy, the pattern has been called
heuristic learning [ 34 , 33 ] ; it has been applied to the lunar lander [ 10 ] , to steel rolling [ 29 ] , to manipulation [ 6 ] and, independently, to active flow control, where it matches or beats deep
reinforcement learning on 10 of 13 benchmarks [ 9 ] . Closest to us, Ahmed et al. [2] let the model write the controller’s structure and a derivative-free
search fit its parameters, reaching PPO-level return on MuJoCo. Each of these works uses its own search
loop and its own scorer, compares with reinforcement learning on its own terms, tests robustness after the fact if at
all, and offers no task format that other agents can be run against. Closer to deployment, language-model
agents now act as the policy itself on generalist manipulation benchmarks such as RoboDojo [ 4 ] . In one harness the agent calls motion, perception and state tools from Python, with
inverse kinematics and motion execution supplied below that interface, and the remaining failures are
dominated by grasps that slip, which originate below the interface [ 8 ] ; the developer
of Claude Fable 5.2 (released on 2026-11-09) reports an average success of 64.9 % in simulation, against 27.6 % for Fable 5.1,
from its own internal runs [ 3 ] . In these works the low-level feedback is
supplied by the platform; here it is the object the agent is asked to write. The third group grades RL solutions and designed controllers on the same plants: the MuJoCo suite and its public RL solutions [ 30 , 31 , 24 , 12 , 7 , 28 ] ,
the linear policies of Mania et al. [19] , to which our results return, the control view of
reinforcement learning [ 26 ] and of data-driven design [ 5 ] ,
against the designed side of SIMBICON, Raibert hopping and capture-point walking [ 36 , 25 , 23 ] ; the two sides are rarely graded by one gate
on one set of seeds. We make three contributions. First, we formulate controller design as a chance-constrained feasibility
problem and use held-out seeds to test whether a controller meets the specified requirements on nominal
and perturbed plants (Section 2.1 ). Second, we introduce ControlHarbor, a benchmark of 44
tasks across 13 plants (Figure 1 ), and release the tasks, the passing controllers and

Section 5 describes the sessions, and Section 6 concludes. 2 The benchmark A task asks for one Python file exporting one function 4 4 4 The function controller(obs) in the code. from the plant’s output to an action array, starting from a file
that applies zero action. Two rules hold: (1) no neural networks and no gradient-based learning, so that
the artefact stays a white-box program an engineer can read, and (2) a budget of one hour of wall-clock
time, with a submission below the gate scoring zero. The agent
evaluates its controller on visible seeds and has a parameter tuner that searches a declared
block of parameters; grading runs on held-out seeds in a separate container. Each
task is packaged as a Harbor task [ 13 , 20 ] , so that any agent harness runs
against it. Since it carries a verifiable scalar reward, the proposed ControlHarbor can also serve as an
environment for post-training language models. The RL solutions of Section 4 and the
coding agent act at different levels: one learns the controller from millions of plant steps, the other
writes the controller as code after a few hundred plant episodes. Both levels are graded by the same
gate using the same seeds. The task
format, the sandbox and the task generator are described in Appendix A ,

A controller is a feedback law π : y ↦ u \pi:y\mapsto u that does not use a neural network. We denote the
set of such laws by Π \Pi .

agent evaluates on. Implementation. Each plant’s evaluator rolls the controller out on a list of seeds and returns one record of metrics per
seed, and a generic grader composes the gate ( 1 ) from these records. The platoon rungs
are safe , spacing , string_1_35 , string_1_0 ; the locomotion tasks use 40 held-out seeds; siblings are e.g. torso mass + 20 % +20\,\% , friction − 30 % -30\,\% , motors − 20 % -20\,\% , gravity + 10 % +10\,\% or a 75 N push on the humanoid; and the platoon
certificate requires a frequency sweep of a sinusoidal leader whose amplitude ratio along the string
must not exceed one. The grader writes a reward of one or zero and a record
with the failed seeds and the metrics that explain them, which is what the agent reads on the visible
seeds. One variant moves the tuner into the grader, so that the agent is judged on the structure it
wrote rather than on its numbers. Thresholds come from physics or from a frozen baseline, not from
what an agent reached. A parity bound is 90 % 90\,\% of the mean return of a public RL solution, or, on the
humanoid, of an RL solution we trained, measured on the grader’s seeds. The humanoid robust-parity suite
keeps the perturbations this RL solution still clears on 0.9 of the seeds. The grader is an empirical test on simulated episodes. It does not give a proof: the platoon
certificate is a frequency sweep, not a proof of string stability, and no rung carries a Lyapunov
argument, a reachability analysis or a test on hardware. Any of these can be added later as a further
component of the gate. 2.2 Plants and tasks The 13 plants are listed with their ladders in Table 4 of the
appendix. The difficult ones are a platoon of 20 vehicles with delayed, noisy local sensing, and the
MuJoCo hopper, walker and humanoid. Within a plant, the 44 tasks vary
the dimensions named in the introduction: on the platoon, delay and noise, heterogeneity, a strict
string-stability rung and the certificate; on the lander, 14 physics scenarios; on the bipeds, the

The task puts no constraints on control law. The agent may write any function from the
observation to an action, except for a neural network or gradient-based learning. Each submitted file was
read once afterwards by a language model and assigned a design family. The files are textbook methods for the plant. On the consensus, formation and attitude plants the
controllers are the standard consensus protocol, 18 of 18 passing; on the pendulum and the cart-link an
energy-based swing-up with a linear-quadratic catch, 12 of 12; on the arm and the platoon feedback
linearisation, 12 of 12, and on the platoon the string-stable cooperative cruise controller, 47 of 53;
on the crowd, barrier functions and social forces, 9 of 9. On these plants the agents stay with that one
method. On the hopper, the walker and the humanoid the method changes from session to session. In 199 sessions
SIMBICON was written 71 times and passed 43, Raibert-style foot placement 42 and 20, a clocked pattern
generator 29 and 8, a linear policy with searched gains 13 and 7, capture-point walking 7 and 4, and
sampling model-predictive control 14 times with no pass, each stopped by the verifier’s budget. When a
design stalled, the agent switched to another of these. No submitted controller fell outside them.
Of the 14 model-predictive attempts, 11 are Opus’s; most SIMBICON passes are Fable’s and
Sonnet’s. Guarantees. No task asks the agent for a proof and the grader checks none. On the light plants
the chosen families carry the textbook guarantees and the agents argue from them: Laplacian feedback on the ring, a linear-quadratic catch under an energy-shaping swing-up, platoon gains set to the
string-stability condition the certificate measures. That guarantee applies to the idealised model.
The grader accepts or rejects the submitted file by the held-out seeds. On the locomotion plants no such argument exists: stability results cover particular gaits on reduced models, the Raibert hopper [ 16 ] and hybrid zero dynamics [ 35 ] , not SIMBICON,
foot-placement heuristics or searched linear policies or RL solutions, and none was expected. How sessions fail. Of the 115 sessions that did not pass, 57 fell, i.e. cleared the rung on
fewer than half of the held-out seeds, 19 were marginal, 20 cleared the nominal plant and missed one
sibling scenario, 5 fell short of the return bound only, and 14 timed out. In 42 of these 115 the agent’s
last run on the five visible seeds reached 0.9, and the 40 held-out seeds did not. Five seeds are too
few to tell a gait that stands on every seed from one that stands on only some of them. The passes are the short sessions: a passing session ran the grader three times, 23
programs of its own, and 25 minutes at the median; a failing one, twice, 40 programs and 51 minutes. The
agents do not stop when they are losing; they search until the hour ends. The tuner made no difference,
45 passes in the 71 sessions that invoked it against 170 in the 259 that did not. Carrying a controller forward. The humanoid brisk and fast tiers were run twice. One
task starts from the zero-torque file. The other starts from a controller that passed the tier below.
From the zero file, brisk passed in 4 of 9 sessions and fast in 2 of 10. From the previous controller,
brisk passed in 7 of 8 and fast in 7 of 9. That file was not written by the model that continued the
task. Every other session in this paper starts from the zero file, and the sessions do not see one
another. A plant outside the sweep. We also experimented with a plant not commonly seen in the control
textbook, a hanging two-segment pneumatic soft arm with creep, hysteresis and pressure lag, seen by a tip
camera, in three tasks: reach and hold, the same under four perturbations, and a circle to trace
(Appendix F ). All 27 sessions passed, with the
one architecture of the soft-robotics literature: the nominal static map inverted for a feedforward and the
camera error fed back through a damped inverse of its Jacobian. The robust siblings made seven of nine
sessions identify a gain per chamber, three circle sessions added repetitive control over the lap, and six of
nine reached the fine rung our oracle had not. The recipe exists in soft robotics, and the agents found it. 6 Conclusion We set out to test what current frontier coding agents can do when asked to design a controller. For that
test we propose a Harbor task format: 44 tasks over 13 plants, so that any agent harness
runs against them unchanged. A task grades one file as control engineers grade, on held-out seeds,
against a ladder of named requirements, under perturbation suites and certificates, within an hour.
Under one protocol, three frontier models designed controllers that reach
the return of an RL solution on every plant where the gate asks for it, even though in a minority of sessions; the
controllers are programs of tens to hundreds of lines that an engineer can read. On the four plants
that carry an RL solution, one evaluation of the reference controller is 2 to 40 times faster than one
evaluation of the RL network, measured on CPU. Robustness appeared
only when the specification demanded it, and it was then within reach of a textbook design, on the
humanoid at the RL solution’s own return, where a domain-randomised RL solution falls short; designs
whose gains came from in-session search were as brittle as the RL solutions they match. Two tasks
remain open for the designed route: the humanoid under 100 N pushes, and the same plant under those
pushes together with torso mass + 20 % +20\,\% , friction − 30 % -30\,\% , motors − 20 % -20\,\% , gravity + 10 % +10\,\% ,
measurement noise and one-step command delay. The second remains open for the RL route as well. The limits of the study are those stated along the way. The three models are from one vendor, and the
earlier cross-vendor sweep is not comparable. Three sessions per cell give counts, not rates. The grader
is an empirical test on simulated episodes, below an industrial certificate (e.g., formal verification), and the RL solutions
are single checkpoints of single seeds. Some future directions follow directly. The first is whether the open tasks mark a limit of capability,
or of one hour from a zero controller: the handoff tiers showed that one controller carried forward doubles the pass count, and a
board of prior attempts, built from visible-seed results so that nothing held out flows forward, is the
natural next experiment; if the push tasks stay open under accumulated attempts, the frontier is a hard
one. The second is to replace some of the present components with the methods used in industrial
practice, such as reachability and formal verification. Generative AI Use Generative AI tools were used for coding and experimental implementation, and for writing and editing.

operating a terminal, its actions are shell commands and file edits, an episode is a session with a

is learned inside this loop: the session trains no neural network and runs no reinforcement-learning
algorithm. It leaves a controller file behind. The RL solution of Section 4 and the coding
agent thus act at different levels, one learning the controller from millions of plant steps, the other
writing it as code after a few hundred plant episodes, and both are graded by the same inner-loop gate
on the same seeds. A Harbor task carries a verifiable scalar reward, thus
the tasks can also be used as reinforcement learning environments for post-training of language models. A.2 The artefact and the sandbox The artefact is one Python file exporting one function, controller(obs) , from the plant’s
output to an action array, and the starting file applies zero action. The instruction states the plant,
its observation and action, the ladder of requirements and the
grader’s flags, and imposes two rules: no neural networks and no gradient-based learning, so that the
artefact stays a program an engineer can read, and a budget of one hour from the agent’s first command,
with a submission below the gate scoring zero. The sandbox is a container with the plant package
installed and no network; the hidden reference controllers are stripped from the image. The agent has
the evaluator itself as a tool, on visible seeds disjoint from the held-out ones, and a parameter tuner
that searches a declared PARAMS block with a declared budget. It has no clock other than the
shell and is told to run date , which gives it a precise reading of the time. Grading runs in a separate container on the submitted file alone, with a
budget of 15 minutes. A.3 Registry and generated tasks Everything specific to a plant, its configuration, evaluator, rungs, failure keys, certificates
and container packages, is declared in one registry; the grader, the tuner and the task generator know
no plant by name. Tasks are generated from one specification, so that the flags the agent reads and the
flags the grader runs cannot drift apart, and a lint checks that no held-out seed appears outside the

gravity, with two segments each driven by an antagonistic pair of pressure chambers, i.e. four commands in [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] at 100 kPa full scale. A material layer adds creep (a standard linear solid), co-contraction
stiffening, a Bouc–Wen hysteresis loop on the actuation, a first-order pressure lag of 0.15 s with a slew
limit, and a draw of stiffness, damping, chamber gains and loop width per seed; a tip camera at 25 Hz with
1 mm noise is the only measurement of the shape, and the instruction provides the nominal arm’s static tip
map. The tasks and their gates: soft-arm-reach , six targets each held within 10 mm for 2 s (fine); soft-arm-reach-robust , the same on the nominal plant and under a 5 g payload, a 10 g hook attached
mid-episode, a stiffness drift and one chamber at 75 %; soft-arm-trace , a 3 cm circle with a
period of 8 s within 20 mm RMS (coarse), with the fine (10 mm) and fast (5 mm) rungs left open because the
oracle, the map inverse with a PI-D correction on the camera, tracked at 15 mm. The sessions ran under the
protocol of Section 3 after the main sweep and are not counted in its tables.

on each camera frame through a damped inverse of the map’s Jacobian; no session built a simulator of the arm.
