OmniBrain-0: A Unified Agentic Model for Embodied Intelligence Xiaomi Embodied Intelligence Team University of Macau × \bm{\times} Tsinghua University Abstract General-purpose embodied intelligence requires robots to coordinate diverse skills over long horizons, track task progress, and recover from failures. Specialist policies alone do not ensure task-level reliability, while powerful multimodal agents rely on costly reasoning and external experience that compact models struggle to reproduce. We introduce OmniBrain-0 , a lightweight unified embodied agent that coordinates manipulation and navigation through a shared high-level decision model, integrating task understanding, planning, progress tracking, and feedback-driven recovery. Our progressive training framework combines embodied pretraining, domain-specialized agent distillation, and multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD). It distills powerful teachers’ interaction experience into manipulation and navigation experts, then consolidates their decision capabilities into OmniBrain-0. Unification therefore occurs within the learned decision model rather than only at the tool interface. We further introduce Rubric-Guided Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI), a data flywheel in which a Meta-Agent turns execution evidence into refined rubrics, step-level reminders, and verified trajectories for domain-expert updates before their reconsolidation into OmniBrain-0 through MOPD. We evaluate OmniBrain-0 on LIBERO-Pro, LIBERO-Plus, RoboCasa365, RoboTwin 2.0, and R2R-CE Val-Unseen, as well as multiple real-world manipulation tasks. OmniBrain-0 combines strong cross-domain task performance with substantially lower inference and execution overhead than its closed-source teacher, demonstrating efficient transfer and consolidation of embodied capabilities into a compact unified agent. General-purpose embodied intelligence requires diverse physical skills, long-horizon planning, skill composition, and closed-loop correction to fulfill task intent. Vision-language-action (VLA) models and vision-and-language navigation (VLN) policies advance manipulation and navigation [ 22 , 4 , 47 , 43 ] , but reliable individual skills do not guarantee task-level success. Under complex constraints, multi-stage dependencies, or distribution shifts, local action prediction may fail to maintain goal alignment, requiring feedback-driven reassessment and replanning [ 61 , 51 ] . Large-model-based embodied agents offer a promising approach by exposing perception, specialist policies, and motion controllers as tools. A high-level model interprets instructions, decomposes tasks, orchestrates skills, and uses execution feedback to track progress and recover from failures, while low-level policies retain their physical interaction expertise [ 61 , 28 , 16 ] . This division of labor enables goal- and feedback-driven execution, but reliable coordination also requires the high-level model to reason about skill applicability, capability boundaries, and handoff states [ 15 ] . These shared task-level requirements motivate a common high-level decision mechanism for manipulation and navigation, spanning task understanding, skill orchestration, and feedback-driven adaptation. However, successful task execution by powerful models does not by itself establish how their planning and recovery capabilities can be learned by a compact, unified agent. These models combine contextual reasoning, experience, and environmental feedback [ 61 , 15 ] , and compact agents must learn from this interaction experience how to decompose tasks, coordinate skills, track progress, and adapt to execution outcomes across domains. Repeated reasoning by powerful models during long-horizon interaction incurs computational overhead and decision latency, constraining deployment efficiency [ 9 , 33 , 32 , 29 , 27 ] . Distilling these capabilities into compact agents offers a way to improve deployment efficiency, but effective interaction data remain limited, particularly for states that require feedback-driven correction. Useful supervision must therefore preserve how tasks progress, how skills are coordinated, and how decisions change in response to execution feedback, including during recovery from failure. Alongside effective learning and consolidation methods, we therefore need a scalable data construction mechanism that preserves informative task progression and recovery while continually expanding coverage of execution challenges encountered during interaction [ 31 , 7 ] . These requirements motivate a unified learning framework that combines scalable interaction data construction with capability transfer and consolidation, enabling a compact agent to coordinate planning and recovery across manipulation and navigation. First, capability transfer aims to learn effective decision behaviors from teacher interactions rather than reproduce teachers’ language outputs or reasoning processes [ 19 ] . Because embodied decisions alter the environment and subsequent observations, students must learn to interpret execution feedback, track progress, and adapt when plans become invalid. Fixed demonstrations alone may not provide sufficient supervision for appropriate decisions in states encountered during autonomous execution [ 16 , 1 ] . Second, acquiring specialized capabilities and integrating them into a unified agent are distinct problems. Manipulation and navigation differ in scene distributions, skill interfaces, and task structures, so expertise learned by specialized planners must be further integrated for joint deployment [ 35 ] . Therefore, we introduce OmniBrain-0 , a lightweight, unified embodied agent for manipulation and navigation (Figure 2 ). OmniBrain-0 integrates specialist policies and tools through a shared skill-invocation framework. A compact high-level planner performs task understanding, long-horizon planning, skill orchestration, state tracking, and failure recovery. Conditioned on observations, instructions, and interaction history, it selects skills and adapts to execution feedback, preserving low-level specialization while learning task decomposition and adaptive correction. To realize these capability transformations, we develop a progressive training framework comprising shared embodied initialization, specialized capability acquisition, and cross-domain capability integration . First, we combine embodied, question-answering, and general language data with limited long-horizon interaction data to establish a shared initialization for a compact model. We then train manipulation and navigation planners from this initialization using quality-filtered interaction trajectories generated by multiple powerful models. By learning observation–decision–feedback relationships from canonical execution and successful recovery, these planners acquire the teachers’ planning and correction behaviors. Finally, we adopt multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) [ 35 ] , using the domain experts to supervise student-generated decision sequences and integrate specialized capabilities into a unified student initialized from the shared pretrained model. The resulting OmniBrain-0 thus unifies manipulation and navigation at the level of learned high-level decisions as well as the skill-invocation interface. To support the scalable construction of effective interaction data, we introduce Rubric-Guided Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI), a closed-loop mechanism connecting data collection, execution-guidance refinement, and model training (Section 5 ). OmniBrain-0’s autonomous interactions reveal challenges in task progression, skill coordination, and failure recovery. A Meta-Agent analyzes execution evidence from successful and failed trajectories, derives execution rubrics, and refines step-level reminders to guide subsequent interaction and collection. Candidate reminders are validated through interaction with model parameters fixed, while successful, failed, and inefficient executions inform further refinement. Environment-verified canonical successes and successful recoveries are curated and structured to augment manipulation and navigation training pools, which are then used to update domain experts. MOPD consolidates the updated experts’ capabilities into the unified model, whose subsequent interactions provide evidence for the next round of guidance refinement and data collection. This process connects challenge discovery, execution guidance, trajectory verification, and capability learning, supporting the continued expansion of effective embodied interaction data. We evaluate OmniBrain-0 on five manipulation and navigation benchmarks—LIBERO-Pro, LIBERO-Plus, RoboCasa365, RoboTwin 2.0, and R2R-CE Val-Unseen—with additional assessments of spatial understanding, spatial referring, and real-world manipulation. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • A unified agentic model for manipulation and navigation. We introduce OmniBrain-0, a compact high-level agent that coordinates heterogeneous perception, manipulation, navigation, and motion skills through a shared decision model for planning, orchestration, and recovery. • Scalable interaction data construction for embodied capability transfer. We construct structured trajectories that preserve observation–decision–feedback dependencies and recovery, and develop a closed-loop mechanism that combines challenge-driven collection, execution-guidance refinement, and environment verification to expand training data for compact domain-specialized agents. • Progressive specialization and multi-expert consolidation. Embodied pretraining, domain-specialized distillation, and MOPD unify manipulation and navigation expertise while preserving low-level specialization. 2 Related Work 2.1 Specialist policies for manipulation and navigation VLA models such as π 0 \pi_{0} [ 5 ] , π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} [ 4 ] , and LingBot-VLA [ 49 , 50 ] learn multitask control from large-scale robot data and vision-language pretraining. These policies provide a foundation for reusable manipulation skills [ 43 ] , while task-level coordination must also account for sub-goal dependencies, execution progress, and the conditions required by subsequent skills. Yet even π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} and LingBot-VLA 2.0 degrade substantially on long-horizon tasks under object and initial-position shifts [ 50 ] . For navigation, NavGPT-2 [ 66 ] couples language reasoning with a navigation policy, and DualVLN (InternVLA-N1) [ 47 ] connects high-level planning to low-level execution in a dual-system design. Manipulation and navigation nevertheless involve different observations, action interfaces, and temporal structures, motivating a shared decision layer that can coordinate domain-specific execution. Rather than replacing these policies, OmniBrain-0 invokes them as skills and learns a shared high-level decision model across both domains. 2.2 Tool-using embodied agents SayCan [ 2 ] and Code as Policies [ 28 ] ground task decomposition in executable skills, and Inner Monologue [ 16 ] feeds environmental feedback back into planning. This division between task reasoning and skill execution provides a basis for composing heterogeneous capabilities, with feedback enabling the planner to reassess whether intermediate outcomes satisfy the current goal [ 34 ] . Agent harnesses, including Harness VLA [ 61 ] , RoboHarness [ 15 ] , and Pigey [ 14 ] , add state tracking, outcome verification, and recovery through agent loops. However, their orchestration relies on repeated reasoning by large models at deployment time. OmniBrain-0 instead internalizes planning, progress tracking, and recovery into a compact decision model learned from interaction experience. Building on this execution paradigm, we retain the tool interfaces and feedback loop while using curated interaction trajectories to supervise the high-level decisions made within it. 2.3 Agent distillation and expert consolidation AgentTuning [ 56 ] and STEVE-2 [ 63 ] transfer agent capabilities via trajectory tuning and hierarchical distillation, but fixed teacher trajectories may not cover states encountered by students. For embodied capability transfer, useful supervision must preserve the relationship between observations, tool choices, and execution outcomes, including corrective decisions that lead to successful recovery. On-policy distillation mitigates this mismatch by supervising student-generated sequences [ 1 , 30 ] ; TurnOPD [ 68 ] adds turn-aware supervision for agents, and MOPD [ 35 ] merges domain-specialized teachers. Such consolidation complements trajectory-based specialization: the latter acquires domain-specific decision behaviors, while the former integrates complementary expert distributions within shared model parameters. In embodied settings, each decision alters subsequent observations and skill applicability; OmniBrain-0 applies multi-teacher distillation to consolidate manipulation and navigation expertise, including planning and recovery, into a single model. 2.4 Recursive self-improvement of embodied agents Recursive self-improvement (RSI) uses successive interactions to refine an agent’s guidance or learned policy. Reflexion [ 41 ] stores verbal reflections in episodic memory, while ACE [ 60 ] evolves contextual playbooks through reflection and curation. ReST meets ReAct [ 3 ] iteratively trains agents on feedback-filtered trajectories. In robotics, EmbodiRSI [ 24 ] targets corrective data collection at policy weaknesses, and AdaRSI [ 44 ] combines harness refinement with selective executor fine-tuning. RE-0 [ 57 ] verifies local teacher corrections before distilling them into embodied code-generating agents. OmniBrain-0 connects rubric-guided reminder refinement with verified trajectory curation, domain-expert SFT, and MOPD for a decision model shared across manipulation and navigation. Reminders are validated without changing model parameters; subsequent training updates the agent, whose new interactions guide the next improvement round. OmniBrain-0 uses the general-purpose multimodal model Qwen3.5-9B [ 38 ] as a shared agent, decoupling task-level decision-making from specialized tool execution. The agent performs instruction understanding, long-horizon task decomposition, tool orchestration, and failure recovery, while execution modules provide perception, manipulation, navigation, and motion-control capabilities. A unified invocation-and-feedback interface connects the agent and execution modules in a closed loop, enabling the same decision model to coordinate heterogeneous tools with different inputs and control mechanisms. Unified tool abstraction We encapsulate heterogeneous execution capabilities as parameterized semantic tools. Perception tools provide scene understanding and target localization; VLA tools handle manipulation and contact interactions; navigation tools support goal reaching through VLN policies or geometric navigators; and motion-control tools provide explicit pose and actuator adjustments. Each tool retains its native conditioning and low-level implementation while exposing a unified invocation-and-feedback interface. Thus, the agent shares a common high-level decision mechanism and interaction protocol rather than a single low-level control policy. Context-driven tool orchestration The agent jointly processes task instructions, current observations, and historical feedback to select subsequent tools and determine their invocation parameters. Interaction history supports task progress tracking, while an optional experience library provides priors on task organization, tool composition, and recovery strategies. Experience guides task-level organization, whereas online perception grounds tool invocation in the current execution state. 3.3 Closed-Loop Planning and Failure Recovery At each turn, the agent selects one tool and makes the next decision only after receiving its execution feedback. High-level tool invocations are executed sequentially and connected through execution outcomes. The agent continuously updates its decisions based on observations and feedback, adapting target localization, invocation parameters, or tool selection when execution deviates from expectations. Expert Interaction Trajectories We collect expert demonstration data by leveraging multiple powerful models to execute manipulation and navigation tasks in closed-loop environments. Experts invoke tools within an execution framework that a Meta-Agent iteratively refines, continually adjusting decisions based on task objectives, online observations, and execution feedback. We preserve the temporal relationships among observations, decisions, and feedback, ensuring that each decision is grounded in information available at that step. Figure 4 details the conversion of expert interaction records into training trajectories through four steps: interaction normalization , trajectory quality control , reasoning reconstruction , and consistency validation . The pipeline aligns raw logs, images, and environment states into supervision compatible with the student’s observations and tool interfaces. It preserves the observation–action–feedback structure and retains both canonical successes and successful recoveries, while removing task-irrelevant information and unreliable trajectories. Dataset composition and splits are provided in the experimental setup and appendix. Interaction Normalization We parse raw interaction logs and reconstruct each episode as a temporally ordered sequence of reasoning–tool call–tool feedback units, retaining synchronized images and robot states at each decision step. Tool calls and feedback are normalized into a unified format, with visual observations aligned to the corresponding interaction steps. Context unavailable to the student is removed, while infrastructure errors and incomplete logs are filtered out to obtain executable interaction sequences. Trajectory Quality Control We use rule-based failure detection and visual verification to assess task outcomes and execution quality, discarding unsuccessful task trajectories. Successful trajectories do not necessarily provide equal training value. Canonical successful trajectories provide reliable demonstrations of standard task execution, while failure-recovery trajectories offer valuable state-dependent supervision by capturing how the agent diagnoses and corrects execution failures. In contrast, blind repetitions without new observations or state information may reinforce inefficient action patterns. We therefore categorize successful trajectories into three types: • Canonical trajectories : The task is completed without tool failures, with perception, manipulation, and termination proceeding as expected. • Recovery trajectories : One or more grasping, contact, or other execution failures occur, but the agent subsequently recovers through re-localization, gripper adjustment, re-grasping, or other corrective actions and eventually receives an environment success signal. • Noisy trajectories : The agent repeatedly executes the same action without acquiring new observations or state information, reflecting blind retries rather than feedback-driven decision making. Both canonical and recovery trajectories are retained, while noisy trajectories are discarded. Canonical trajectories provide standard execution demonstrations, while recovery trajectories capture the failure–diagnosis–correction–continuation process. Reasoning Reconstruction The raw reasoning text generated by the expert model may introduce unnecessary linguistic variation. We therefore rewrite each reasoning step into a structured representation using only information available at the corresponding decision step, following two principles: state grounding , which derives the state from tool outputs, robot states, and images, and evidence traceability , which excludes future information. Specifically, each reasoning step is represented by four fields: • Phase : the current stage of task execution, such as localization, grasping, transport, placement, release, success verification, or recovery. • State : the current interaction state, including the held object, gripper and grasp status, target and destination locations, and task completion status. • Evidence : relevant visual observations and tool feedback supporting the current state assessment. • Decision : the next tool to be invoked and its action description determined by the actual tool parameters. We generate these fields by replaying each trajectory chronologically: tool feedback first updates the interaction state, while visual auditing verifies information not reliably available from tool outputs, such as object identity, grasp status, and placement. The Phase , relevant Evidence , and Decision are then determined from the resulting state and the actual next tool call. Failed actions are marked as recovery operations, while environment-confirmed completion produces the terminal state. For long-horizon tasks, the State additionally tracks the current sub-goal and its completion progress. The resulting structured reasoning is independent of the teacher’s wording and remains one-to-one aligned with each tool call. Consistency Validation We finally check temporal consistency, state–action consistency, and agreement between visual observations and textual evidence. These checks verify consistency among the state, evidence, phase, and tool call, including target/destination localization, action–decision alignment, and environment-verified termination. Evidence-deficient trajectories and those failing consistency validation are discarded, while canonical successes and successful recovery trajectories are retained as high-quality training data. At every high-level decision step, the harness evaluates the current interaction context to determine whether guidance is needed. A reminder is inserted before the next tool invocation only when an issue is detected; otherwise, the agent proceeds without a reminder. Reminder-policy validation OmniBrain-0 executes evaluation trajectories under the candidate guidance with its parameters fixed. The verifier assesses goal attainment and execution efficiency, and the Meta-Agent uses the resulting evidence to identify valid reminders and revise ineffective guidance. Verified evaluation trajectories, including successful and failed executions, are returned for the next round of rubric-based policy update. Effective reminders are retained across rounds, enabling execution guidance to accumulate from observed interaction outcomes. Ablation Study on the Data Processing Pipeline The proposed data processing pipeline is designed to transform expert interaction data into a format suitable for training compact models. The ablation results on LIBERO-Pro in Figure 10 demonstrate the impact of key components of the pipeline. When the final training data excludes expert failure-recovery trajectories and retains only a single successful trajectory, the model trained on this ablated dataset suffers an 9% drop in success rate. This degradation highlights the value of failure-recovery trajectories, which provide supervision for actively selecting corrective actions in response to concrete failure evidence, such as failed grasps or object drops during transportation. Furthermore, when the expert-generated reasoning is used directly as free-form text rather than being structurally rewritten, the model trained on the resulting data also exhibits a substantial performance drop. This is because expert models typically produce lengthy and unconstrained reasoning, and directly using such reasoning to construct long-horizon training trajectories for compact models can lead to overfitting to the linguistic style of the expert model, while weakening the learning of visual grounding and decision-making logic. Ablation Study on Experience Library Figure 11 evaluates the effect of the Experience Library on agent execution, using GPT-5.6-Sol with and without access to the library. The library consistently improves the success rate across all three benchmarks, with gains of 27.7, 6.8, and 26.4 points on LIBERO-Pro, RoboTwin 2.0, and RoboCasa365, respectively. By providing reusable priors from previously collected cross-episode experiences, the Experience Library helps the agent avoid repeated exploration and make more informed execution decisions. Since only successful trajectories are retained for training, the Experience Library also increases the yield of canonical and recovery trajectories available for distillation into OmniBrain-0. The reminder policy is checked against the current interaction context before the agent selects its next tool invocation. In the manipulation example (Figure 17 ), the initial move_arm and vla_skill decisions proceed without reminders. Guidance is triggered at step 2 before grasp and again at step 3 before move_arm , concentrating the intervention around grasping and the transition to subsequent motion. The later transport motions, release, and final finish decision proceed without further reminders. This pattern illustrates how the policy supplies guidance at selected states within an ongoing task, while OmniBrain-0 continues to generate the tool calls. Aligning the observations, trigger states, and tool sequence makes the timing of reminder guidance explicit within closed-loop execution. Figure 17 : Reminder-guided manipulation. A wine-bottle placement example aligning visual observations (top), reminder-policy trigger states (middle), and OmniBrain-0’s tool invocations (bottom). For the task Put the wine bottle in the bowl , reminders are triggered at steps 2 and 3 before grasp and move_arm , respectively; the remaining displayed steps proceed without reminders. The sequence continues through bottle transport, release, and task termination. Figure 18 : Reminder-guided navigation. An illustrative bathroom-mirror navigation example aligning visual observations (top), reminder-policy trigger states (middle), and OmniBrain-0’s tool invocations (bottom). Reminders at steps 3 and 6 guide route selection and prevent premature termination, respectively. 7 Conclusion and Limitations We introduced OmniBrain-0 , a compact unified embodied agent that coordinates manipulation and navigation through a shared high-level decision model. Our progressive training framework combines embodied pretraining, domain-specialized agent distillation, and multi-teacher on-policy distillation to transfer and consolidate planning, skill orchestration, and recovery capabilities. A structured data pipeline converts expert interactions into state-grounded supervision, retaining both canonical successes and successful recoveries. Rubric-Guided Recursive Self-Improvement further connects execution evidence, reminder-policy refinement, verified trajectory collection, and expert consolidation, supporting the continued construction of effective interaction data. Simulated manipulation and navigation benchmarks show strong task performance with substantially lower token consumption and shorter task completion times than the closed-source teacher, while spatial and real-world evaluations further assess grounding and physical applicability. These results support learning transferable embodied decision-making capabilities in a compact model while retaining specialized low-level skills. OmniBrain-0 remains dependent on the coverage and reliability of its low-level skills, and gaps persist on unseen task compositions and spatial localization. Real-world evaluation also covers a limited set of tasks. Future work will broaden task and embodiment coverage, strengthen grounding and recovery under distribution shifts, and study sustained improvement through larger and more diverse verified interaction corpora. Section 11 presents complete manipulation traces, a navigation execution example, and comparisons with InternVLA-N1. 8 Details of the Agentic System Design This section provides additional details of the agentic system design, including the tools developed for the VLA and VLN tasks, as well as the construction and utilization of the Experience Library. The tools provide standardized interfaces for interacting with heterogeneous model-based and scripted skills, while the Experience Library organizes reusable knowledge and interaction experiences accumulated from expert models. Tools We design a unified set of tools for VLA and VLN tasks, serving as the interface through which the agent interacts with and acts upon the physical environment. The detailed definitions of the tools are as follows. Figure 8.1 : General Tools: Shared tools for environment observation, pixel-to-metric localization, text-guided object segmentation, execution termination, and mobile-base control. Figure 8.2 : VLA-Specific Tools: Tools for end-effector motion, wrist orientation, gripper control, and multi-step manipulation or interaction through a learned VLA policy. Figure 8.3 : VLN-Specific Tools: Tools for panoramic observation, instruction following, navigation-goal grounding and execution, point-directed navigation, and trajectory backtracking. Experience Library To collect diverse and successful expert interaction trajectories, we follow the data collection paradigm of Harness VLA [ 61 ] , allowing expert models to explore the same task environment across multiple episodes and autonomously reflect on the causes of failures and the factors contributing to successful executions. These cross-episode experiences are then manually reviewed and consolidated into an Experience Library, which provides reusable priors for subsequent model execution. The library is organized into four complementary categories, covering general principles, task execution patterns, failure recovery, and the capability boundaries of VLA/VLN skills. 1. General Principles. Reusable perception, coordinate, gripper, controller, navigation, and safety knowledge that applies across tasks. Examples include image-first decision making, object-to-end-effector offsets, appropriate camera/view selection, spatial landmark awareness, and short, capped contact or navigation motions. 2. Task Execution Patterns. Reusable recipes for pick–transport–place, containment, articulated fixtures, stacking, handover, ranking, multi-object ordering, and navigation. These experiences describe task phases, skill composition, and verification gates rather than fixed scene coordinates, and cover both VLA-based manipulation and VLN-based navigation behaviors. 3. Failure Cases and Recovery Strategies. Negative evidence and recovery strategies accumulated from unsuccessful executions, including common failure modes, their observable symptoms, recovery actions, and appropriate execution budgets. For VLA tasks, these may involve grasp failures, collisions, or inaccurate object localization; for VLN tasks, they include navigation deviations, incorrect waypoint selection, ambiguous landmark grounding, and ineffective recovery behaviors. 4. VLA/VLN Capability Boundaries. Knowledge about the respective capabilities and limitations of VLA and VLN skills, including the conditions under which to invoke learned VLA/VLN policies, switch between them, or fall back to scripted tools. These experiences capture when learned skills are more reliable and when explicit geometric or rule-based actions are preferable. 9 Benchmarks and Evaluation Protocol We use a common episode-level protocol across the closed-loop benchmarks. Each episode is associated with its benchmark, task suite, task, perturbation, random seed, and model version. Duplicate records of the same evaluation case are removed before aggregation. The planner receives the task instruction, camera observations, robot state or proprioception, and tool feedback. Hidden task specifications, ground-truth object poses, and evaluation labels are used for scene construction or scoring and are not supplied to the planner. The planner also has no access to the environment state of other evaluation episodes. An episode ends when the environment confirms success, the planner requests termination, an execution limit is reached, or an infrastructure failure prevents further interaction. Completion claimed by the planner is accepted only when supported by the environment’s success criterion. Unless a benchmark specifies a macro-average, Overall is computed as the total number of successful episodes divided by the total number of evaluated episodes. Task failures, timeouts, simulator initialization failures, infrastructure errors, and invalid or missing results are recorded separately. When infrastructure failures are retried, both the original attempt and the retry are retained in the evaluation records. LIBERO-Pro LIBERO-Pro [ 67 ] is an extension of the LIBERO benchmark designed to evaluate the generalization capabilities of vision-language-action (VLA) models. It introduces controlled perturbations to the original LIBERO tasks, including changes to manipulated objects, object positions, task instructions, task configurations, and environments. Following Harness VLA [ 61 ] , we evaluate eight LIBERO-Pro conditions: Spatial-T , Spatial-S , Object-T , Object-S , Goal-T , Goal-S , L10-T , and L10-S , where T denotes task or instruction redirection and S denotes spatial swapping of object configurations. Each condition contains ten tasks evaluated with seeds s 1 s_{1} – s 10 s_{10} , yielding 100 episodes per condition and 800 episodes overall. LIBERO-Plus LIBERO-Plus [ 13 ] extends LIBERO with 10,030 task variants spanning camera viewpoints, robot initial states, language instructions, lighting, background textures, sensor noise, and object layout. These variations assess robustness to visual, linguistic, and physical changes in manipulation settings. For LIBERO-Plus, we evaluate four suites: Spatial , Object , Goal , and LIBERO-10 . From each suite, we sample 210 task variants, evenly distributed across seven perturbation types: Camera , Robot , Language , Light , Background , Noise , and Layout . Each perturbation type contains 30 tasks per suite, all evaluated with seed s 0 s_{0} , yielding 840 episodes overall. RoboTwin 2.0 RoboTwin 2.0 [ 8 ] is a simulation benchmark for evaluating generalist policies in bimanual robotic manipulation, featuring 50 diverse manipulation tasks. It provides two evaluation settings: a Clean setting (Easy), which largely preserves the standard environment, and a Randomized setting (Hard), which introduces substantial environmental variations. This design enables evaluation of both task execution capability and robustness to distribution shifts. Following Harness VLA [ 61 ] , we evaluate all 50 tasks under both the Clean and Randomized settings, with five seeds per task in each setting, resulting in 2 × 50 × 5 = 500 2\times 50\times 5=500 episodes. The planner receives task instructions, camera observations, robot state, and access to the available bimanual tools and skills. Scene randomization and ground-truth simulator state are used to construct and evaluate episodes; hidden evaluation labels are not provided to the planner. Success is determined by the simulator’s built-in task completion criterion. We report separate success rates for Clean and Randomized environments and an Overall rate across all 500 episodes. RoboCasa365 RoboCasa365 [ 36 ] is a large-scale simulation benchmark for evaluating generalist robot policies on everyday household tasks. The benchmark includes both atomic tasks targeting individual manipulation skills and composite tasks that require sequencing multiple skills for longer-horizon activities, enabling evaluation of general task-solving capabilities and generalization across diverse environments and task compositions. Following Harness VLA [ 61 ] , we evaluate on the RoboCasa365 Target-50 split, comprising 18 Atomic-Seen , 16 Composite-Seen , and 16 Composite-Unseen tasks. The Seen groups correspond to task templates present in pretraining, while Unseen contains templates held out from pretraining. We use held-out seeds s 1 s_{1} – s 10 s_{10} for Atomic-Seen and s 1 s_{1} – s 5 s_{5} for both composite groups, yielding 18 × 10 = 180 18\times 10=180 , 16 × 5 = 80 16\times 5=80 , and 16 × 5 = 80 16\times 5=80 episodes, respectively, for 340 episodes overall. RoboCasa365 uses a task-macro Overall: each task first receives a success rate computed over its evaluation episodes, and the 50 task rates are then averaged with equal weight. Thus, the three split groups contribute 18, 16, and 16 task rates, respectively, and this Overall differs from the episode-micro average used by the general rule above. R2R-CE R2R-CE [ 23 ] is the continuous-environment adaptation of the Room-to-Room (R2R) vision-and-language navigation benchmark. It ports language-annotated navigation episodes to the Habitat simulator, where an agent follows a natural-language route instruction through reconstructed indoor environments. Unlike the original navigation-graph setting, the agent moves through continuous space rather than jumping between predefined viewpoints. We evaluate a fixed subset of 100 episodes from the Val-Unseen split. The planner receives the navigation instruction, camera observations, and execution feedback, and uses SmartWay [ 40 ] for navigation. Ground-truth shortest paths and goal positions are used for scoring only. We report Navigation Error (NE), the final distance to the goal; Success Rate (SR), the percentage of episodes ending within 3 m of the goal; Oracle Success Rate (OSR), the percentage whose trajectory reaches within 3 m of the goal at any point; and Success weighted by Path Length (SPL), which accounts for both success and path efficiency. All four metrics are computed on the same 100 episodes, with service failures, invalid actions, timeouts, and navigation failures recorded separately. EmbSpatial-Bench EmbSpatial-Bench [ 12 ] contains 3640 multiple-choice visual questions derived from MP3D, AI2-THOR, and ScanNet scenes. It tests six egocentric relations: above, below, left, right, close, and far. MP3D contributes 1201 questions, AI2-THOR contributes 1239, and ScanNet contributes 1200. Each input consists of an image, a spatial question, and candidate answers. Accuracy is the percentage of questions answered correctly. Table 6 reports accuracy for each source environment and across all questions; Overall therefore weights each environment by its question count. RefSpatial-Bench RefSpatial-Bench [ 65 ] evaluates spatial referring from an image and a spatially constrained instruction. Its Location and Placement sets contain 100 examples each, requiring target-object localization and free-space selection, respectively. Unseen comprises 77 examples drawn from these sets with spatial-relation combinations absent from the benchmark authors’ RefSpatial training data. A prediction is correct when the output point falls within the annotated target mask. Table 7 reports the percentage of correct predictions for Location, Placement, and Unseen. Overall weights each category by its size: 100 examples for Location, 100 for Placement, and 77 for Unseen. Because Unseen overlaps the two task sets, Overall summarizes the evaluation categories rather than accuracy on a disjoint set of 277 examples. Real-world manipulation The real-world manipulation suite comprises three language-conditioned tasks: block stacking , where the robot places the yellow cube on the red cube; apple placement , where the robot picks up the apple and places it in the purple bowl; and drawer opening , where the robot grasps the handle and pulls the drawer open. These tasks cover precise object placement, pick-and-place manipulation, and contact-rich articulated-object manipulation. Each method is evaluated over 20 independent trials per task. A trial is successful if the robot completes the task within the allotted execution budget: placing the red cube stably on the blue cube, placing and releasing the apple inside the blue bowl, or opening the drawer to the specified target position. We report per-task Success Rate (SR), defined as the percentage of successful trials, and the macro-average SR across the three tasks. 10 Datasets and Experimental Details VQA Data We construct our embodied pretraining corpus from EmbSpatial-SFT [ 12 ] , RefSpatial [ 65 ] , ShareRobot [ 18 ] , and RoboInter-VQA [ 26 ] . EmbSpatial-SFT provides instruction-tuning data for egocentric spatial relationship understanding and object localization, covering six fundamental spatial relations. RefSpatial further enriches spatial understanding and referring with 2.5M samples and 20M question-answer pairs from 2D images, 3D embodied videos, and simulated scenes, spanning 31 spatial relations and multi-step spatial reasoning. ShareRobot provides heterogeneous supervision for task planning, object affordance, and end-effector trajectories, spanning 23 source datasets, 12 embodiments, and 107 atomic task types, with over 1.0M planning-oriented VQA pairs. RoboInter-VQA complements these datasets with approximately 2.3M embodied VQA samples covering spatial and temporal reasoning, intermediate-representation understanding, and task planning. From these sources, we select samples focusing on spatial grounding, spatial reasoning, affordance understanding, and task planning, and incorporate agentic interaction data at a predefined ratio, ultimately obtaining approximately 820K embodied pretraining samples. Agentic Data The interaction records preserve task instructions, visual observations, robot states, decision text, tool calls, and environment feedback in temporal order. We apply the data pipeline in Section 4.1 to verify task completion, remove incomplete, invalid, or noisy records, and normalize retained trajectories into multi-turn multimodal conversations. Each decision and tool invocation is aligned with the observations and feedback available at that step. The resulting corpus combines successful trajectories from the initial expert collection with additional verified successful trajectories collected through the RSI Data Flywheel. LIBERO/LIBERO-Pro, RoboTwin 2.0, RoboCasa365, and R2R-CE contribute 2,349, 513, 880, and 1,246 usable expert demonstrations, respectively. Supervision targets the planner’s decision text and tool invocations, with observations and environment feedback as conditioning context. Expert Collection and Execution Configuration We use GPT-5.6-Sol and GLM-5.3-Flash to collect closed-loop interaction trajectories on LIBERO/LIBERO-Pro, RoboTwin 2.0, RoboCasa365, and R2R-CE. At each decision step, an expert receives visual observations and available state feedback, together with an experience library that provides reusable guidance on task organization, tool composition, and failure recovery. It selects a tool and its parameters, then conditions its next decision on the resulting observations and execution status. Following the tool-based execution paradigm of Harness VLA [ 61 ] , a shared invocation-and-feedback interface separates high-level decisions from benchmark-specific low-level execution. Available manipulation skills include π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} [ 4 ] , LingBot-VLA [ 49 ] , and RLDX-1 [ 20 ] ; navigation uses SmartWay [ 40 ] . Detailed tool definitions are given in Appendix 8 .
