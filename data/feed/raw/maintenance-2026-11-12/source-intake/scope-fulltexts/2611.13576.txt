Text-to-video (T2V) generation has advanced rapidly, and recent T2V models [ 59 , 62 , 1 , 14 , 67 , 57 , 45 ] can now render complex scenes from text prompts. As these models are increasingly positioned as world simulators [ 68 , 33 , 1 ] , a basic requirement is that their videos conform to physical commonsense, the shared expectations about the physical world that everyone acquires from everyday experience, without any textbook physics [ 37 , 55 ] . A reliable and comprehensive evaluation of this ability is therefore essential for tracking progress and guiding model development. Existing benchmarks for physical commonsense have made notable progress [ 7 , 8 , 47 , 19 ] , but they share two limitations (Fig. 1 ). First, the ratings are subjective. Annotators rate each video and its caption with a single score on a discrete scale, but no rule defines how physical violations map to scores. Each rater thus applies an intuitive mapping, weighing the severity of violations by personal standards, so the same video may receive different scores. This inconsistency introduces noise into both benchmark results and the automatic evaluators trained on these ratings. Second, the ratings are uninterpretable. A discrete scale is easier to rate than a continuous one, since fractional scores would be harder to assign and more scattered. However, a single score reflects only how implausible a video is overall, not which physical principle is violated. Evaluations can thus only rank models and categories, yielding coarse findings that offer little guidance for improvement. To address these limitations, we introduce PhyRubric , a rubric-grounded diagnostic framework for evaluating physical commonsense in T2V generation (Fig. 2 ). For each caption, PhyRubric constructs a caption-specific rubric before any video is seen, and reuses it for every video generated from that caption. The rubric decomposes the caption into verifiable items of two types: semantic items list the visible facts the caption requires, while physical items specify which visible events count as violations and are each assigned a severity level inspired by the order in which infants acquire physical knowledge [ 55 , 6 , 41 ] . Given a video, each item receives a verdict grounded in timestamped evidence, and scores are derived deterministically from these verdicts rather than rated directly. Every score thus comes with a diagnosis of which expectation failed and why. We instantiate this framework as PhyRubric-Bench . Unlike prior benchmarks dominated by mechanics (Suppl. Tab. 4 ), it spans 34 categories across five physical domains: mechanics, optics, heat, electricity, and acoustics 1 1 1 Since generated videos are silent, the acoustics domain covers only the visible consequences of sound, such as water rippling beside a speaker. . Every caption describes a complete causal event and states its outcome explicitly, so that semantic adherence requires rendering the cause, the action, and its physical consequence, e.g . , “the key switch is turned, and the lamp lights” rather than “the key switch is turned.” Beyond PhyRubric-Bench, we scale up to PhyRubric-Train , 947 captions that share the taxonomy of the benchmark but no scenes, each paired with its own rubric. Here the rubric plays a different role: rather than defining the evaluation as in PhyRubric-Bench, it guides an agentic annotator, telling it which expectations to check and where to look, yielding 3,788 labeled videos from four T2V models with 45.9K rubric-item verdicts. On these labels, we train PhyRubric-Judge , an open-source VLM that needs no rubric at inference. Given only a caption and a video, it predicts semantic adherence and physical commonsense scores together with a diagnosis of which physical expectation is violated and why. We evaluate ten T2V models on PhyRubric-Bench and find that basic physical commonsense remains far from solved: even the strongest model, Seedance 2.5, produces videos that are both faithful to the caption and physically plausible in fewer than half of cases. Moreover, PhyRubric-Judge correlates more closely with human judgments than the judges released with existing benchmarks, generalizes well to unseen generators and domains, and provides a diagnosis that locates each violation. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose PhyRubric , a rubric-grounded framework that constructs a caption-specific rubric before any video is seen and derives every score from evidence-grounded item verdicts, so that each score comes with a diagnosis. • We construct PhyRubric-Bench , spanning 34 categories across five physical domains with human-verified rubrics and labels, and PhyRubric-Train , labeled by a rubric-guided agentic annotator, on which we train the open-source PhyRubric-Judge that generalizes to unseen generators and domains. • Rather than proposing a definitive standard, we show that our rubric-grounded evaluation serves as an analysis tool that yields deeper insights: semantic adherence is the main bottleneck, physical errors are all-or-nothing, and models often avoid physical errors by not depicting the event at all, a shortcut that prior protocols reward. 2 Related Work 2.1 Physical Commonsense Benchmarks for Text-to-Video Generation The rapid advancement of text-to-video (T2V) generation has motivated a range of benchmarks for its assessment [ 30 , 66 , 31 , 43 , 60 , 56 , 44 , 39 ] . Moving beyond general visual quality, a growing line of work focuses on physical commonsense in T2V generation [ 7 , 8 , 47 , 19 , 20 , 53 , 36 ] , while other works study physical understanding in settings where models are conditioned on observed frames and predict how the scene evolves [ 48 , 33 , 40 ] . However, most of these physical commonsense benchmarks are dominated by mechanics [ 7 , 8 , 20 ] , and some include prompts that require specialized knowledge beyond everyday experience [ 47 , 19 , 20 , 36 ] . In contrast, PhyRubric-Bench spans five physical domains with prompts grounded in physical commonsense, and pairs each prompt with a caption-specific rubric that enables diagnostic evaluation. 2.2 VLM-as-a-Judge for Physical Commonsense Existing evaluation of video generation mainly follows two paradigms: standard metrics such as FVD [ 58 ] , SSIM [ 61 ] , and CLIP score [ 27 ] , and VLM-based evaluation [ 12 , 52 , 23 ] such as VideoScore [ 26 ] . As standard metrics cannot capture physical plausibility, physical commonsense evaluation mainly adopts the latter, employing VLMs as dedicated judges. On the open-source side, judges such as VideoCon-Physics [ 7 ] and VideoPhy-2-AutoEval [ 8 ] are fine-tuned on the human annotations of their corresponding datasets. On the proprietary side, recent works prompt commercial models such as GPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-Pro, and GPT-5.5 as judges [ 47 , 19 , 53 , 51 ] . Nevertheless, most of these judges output a holistic score rather than a detailed diagnosis, failing to reveal what goes wrong and why. 2.3 Rubric-Based Evaluation A rubric is an explicit set of criteria that decomposes a holistic quality judgment into verifiable items, making the assessment transparent, consistent, and interpretable [ 32 , 13 ] . Recently, rubrics have been widely adopted for fine-grained evaluation of large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models [ 34 , 38 ] , and increasingly tailor the criteria to each individual query rather than applying a generic rubric to all samples [ 35 , 16 , 4 ] . In text-to-image generation, a related line of work decomposes prompts into fine-grained questions to assess text-image alignment [ 29 , 15 ] . However, rubric-based evaluation remains underexplored for physical commonsense in video generation. In this work, rubrics play two roles: they serve as the evaluation criteria of PhyRubric-Bench, and they guide an agentic annotator to label training data for PhyRubric-Judge. This keeps the criteria identical across videos, so that scores from different generators are directly comparable and cannot be swayed by what a particular video happens to show. Moreover, a rubric reviewed by humans once can be reused for any number of videos, including those from future generators. 3.1 Caption-Specific Rubric Formulation We evaluate generated videos along the two dimensions introduced by VideoPhy-2 [ 8 ] , semantic adherence (SA) and physical commonsense (PC), each on a 1–5 scale. Unlike prior benchmarks, where annotators assign a holistic score to each dimension, we pair every caption with a rubric that specifies what a video must satisfy to be faithful and physically plausible for that caption. The rubric consists of two groups of items, one for SA and one for PC, and each score is derived from item-level verdicts rather than rated directly. A simplified rubric example is shown in Fig. 3 . Semantic Adherence (SA). SA measures whether the video shows what the caption describes. Its rubric items are the visible facts the caption requires, in two tiers: • Primary : the main objects and each event in the caption. • Secondary : the remaining facts, including stated qualifiers ( e.g . , once, slowly, wooden), initial states implied by the caption, and the order of events. Physical Commonsense (PC). This metric evaluates whether the events shown in the video follow physical commonsense. We define four severity levels for physical violations, inspired by the order in which infants acquire physical knowledge in developmental psychology [ 55 , 6 , 41 ] : • Fatal : the scene is impossible as a whole, violating core object principles such as permanence, solidity, or continuity ( e.g . , objects passing through each other, or appearing or vanishing without cause). • Core : the central interaction described by the caption does not happen or happens incorrectly ( e.g . , in reverse or through a broken mechanism). • Major : a clear violation outside the central interaction, such as a missing secondary consequence ( e.g . , the lamp lights but the desk beneath it stays dark). • Minor : a visual flaw that leaves the event intact, such as color or texture drift of untouched objects. Each PC item is assigned one of these severity levels and one of ten violation types ( e.g . , permanence, solidity, causeless motion, process direction). Following the same developmental order, the ten types are grouped into L1 core object principles, L2 everyday dynamics, and L3 attribute experience (Suppl. Tab. 6 ). Each PC item is then judged by a pair of verifiable statements: • Expect : what a physically correct video should show, specifying the moment, region, or event to inspect. • Fail : the concrete visible signs that count as a violation, and the item fails only if one of these signs is observed. Verdicts. An SA item is judged pass or fail . A PC item has a third verdict, not shown , which applies when the moment, region, or event specified by its Expect statement never appears in the video. Otherwise, the item fails if any sign in its Fail statement is observed, and passes if not. Note that not shown means the situation is absent, not that the expected behavior failed to occur. For example, for the caption “The key switch on the base of a desk lamp is turned, and the lamp lights” (Fig. 3 ), if the key is never turned in the video, P2 is not shown rather than failed, since the missing action is already penalized by the SA item S2. Since a fail can only be triggered by a listed sign, every negative verdict is grounded in a specific observation rather than an overall impression, and carries the severity and violation type of its item. Consistent with the nature of commonsense, PC items judge only the direction and order of change rather than its rate or extent, and never penalize a cause for being out of view ( e.g . , a heat source or power supply). Scoring Rules. Both scores lie on a 1–5 scale and are derived from the verdicts by fixed rules. For SA, a video judged entirely unrelated to the caption scores 1. Otherwise, it scores 5 if all items pass, 4 if only secondary items fail, 3 if exactly one primary item fails, and 2 if two or more primary items fail. For PC, let F F be the set of failed items. PhyRubric-Bench covers all five physical domains with 34 categories (Fig. 4 ), and every caption tests only physical commonsense and is paired with a human-verified rubric. Caption and rubric generation. Captions are drafted by Claude Fable 5.1 [ 2 ] and GPT-5.6 Sol [ 49 ] and refined by human reviewers until each describes a single causal event that is visible within about five seconds and has an explicitly stated outcome. Stating the outcome serves two purposes. (1) Fixed expectations : what should happen is determined before any video is seen, so the rubric can specify the expected events and violations rather than guess among plausible outcomes. (2) Diagnostic failures : if a model is told what should happen yet fails to render it, the failure reflects missing physical knowledge rather than creative freedom. In addition, captions include a person only when the human body itself is part of the physics, since current generators often produce distorted human bodies [ 66 , 65 , 46 ] . Each phenomenon is instantiated in three to five distinct scenes for diversity, yielding 1,192 captions, which are split into 245 for PhyRubric-Bench and 947 for PhyRubric-Train (Sec. 3.3 ) with no scene shared between the two. Each caption is then paired with a rubric, drafted by an LLM prompted with example rubrics from the same domain, checked automatically for format and score reachability, and revised by human reviewers until approved. Video generation and annotation. For evaluation, the 245 benchmark captions are rendered by ten text-to-video models, six of which are unseen during judge training, yielding 2,433 videos 2 2 2 The remaining 17 of the 10 × 245 10\times 245 videos were not produced because the generators’ built-in safety filters declined the captions. . Each video is first pre-annotated item by item by our agentic annotator (Sec. 3.3 ), and human annotators then verify every verdict against the video to obtain the ground truth. Since the rubric already specifies what counts as a violation, annotators only need to check whether the listed signs appear, rather than judging physical plausibility themselves. More construction details and statistics are given in Suppl. Sec. A.2 . 3.3 PhyRubric-Train and PhyRubric-Judge Training data annotation. Human verification does not scale to thousands of videos, so we label the training set with the same agentic annotator used to pre-annotate PhyRubric-Bench, built on Claude Sonnet 5.5 [ 3 ] . Instead of a fixed set of frames, the annotator first scans a timestamped overview of the clip and then zooms in at full frame rate where and when it needs to, until each rubric item can be decided from frames that clearly show the relevant event. This matters because physical violations often involve small objects or brief moments that uniform sampling misses. For every item, it returns a verdict with timestamped evidence, and the scores follow from the verdicts by the rules in Sec. 3.1 . Applied to the 947 training captions, each rendered by four generators, it yields PhyRubric-Train with 3,788 videos and 45.9K item-level verdicts. Suppl. Sec. A.4 details its protocol and reliability. Judge. The agentic annotator produces reliable labels but is impractical as a general-purpose evaluator: it relies on a proprietary model, makes repeated tool calls per video, and needs a rubric for every new caption. We therefore train PhyRubric-Judge, an open-source VLM, on PhyRubric-Train. Starting from Qwen3-VL-8B [ 5 ] , we explore configurations that vary the rubric input, the output format, and the training objective (SFT or GRPO [ 54 ] ). Our default judge takes only the caption and uniformly sampled frames, so that it applies to any new caption and receives the same input as existing judges. It outputs the SA score, whether PC is assessable, the PC score, and a diagnosis. The training targets come from PhyRubric-Train, with the diagnosis assembled from the item-level evidence: a summary of what the video shows, the missing caption facts, and each physical violation with its evidence. PhyRubric-Bench is rendered by ten video generators. Four of them are open-source T2V models that also render PhyRubric-Train: CogVideoX1.5-5B [ 64 ] , HunyuanVideo-1.5-8B [ 62 ] , LTX-Video-2B [ 22 ] , and Wan2.2-T2V-A14B [ 59 ] . The other six are unseen during training and measure how well a judge generalizes to new generators: Wan2.2-TI2V-5B [ 59 ] , LTX-2.3-22B [ 21 ] , Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B and Cosmos-Predict2.5-14B [ 1 ] , and the proprietary Seedance 2.5 [ 11 ] and Veo 3.1 [ 17 ] . All videos are five to six seconds long. Evaluation Metrics. Each video is rated on a 1–5 scale for SA and PC. We report the mean SA over all videos and the mean PC over assessable videos only. We also report the joint performance (JP), the percentage of all videos with SA ≥ \geq 4 and PC ≥ \geq 4, i.e . , videos that are both faithful to the caption and physically plausible. Following VideoPhy-2 [ 8 ] , we use JP as the primary metric. A high PC alone only indicates that what is shown is plausible ( e.g . , a nearly static video can easily appear plausible), so SA is needed to confirm that the model produces the described event. Implementation Details. For video generation, each open-source model is run with its official checkpoint and default sampling recipe, generating one video per caption with a fixed seed of 42. HunyuanVideo-1.5 and Wan2.2-T2V-A14B are rendered at 480p, CogVideoX1.5-5B at 768p, and the remaining open-source models at 704p. Seedance 2.5 and Veo 3.1 are accessed through their official APIs at 480p and 720p, respectively. Per-model settings are listed in Suppl. Sec. B.1 . For PhyRubric-Judge, we fine-tune Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct [ 5 ] on PhyRubric-Train with LoRA (rank 16) [ 28 ] on all linear layers of the language model, keeping the vision encoder and projector frozen. The judge takes the caption and 16 uniformly sampled frames, each resized to at most 448 × 448 448\times 448 . We train for 2 epochs (900 steps) with an effective batch size of 8 and a cosine learning rate schedule peaking at 10 − 4 10^{-4} . At inference, the judge decodes greedily, and we additionally compute a soft score [ 42 , 63 ] as the expected rating under the probabilities of the five score tokens (Suppl. Sec. B.2 ). We summarize the main findings below. Physical commonsense remains far from solved. The two proprietary models lead by a wide margin: Seedance 2.5 achieves an average JP of 44.20% and Veo 3.1 36.20%, two to three times that of the strongest open-source model, HunyuanVideo-1.5-8B (14.93%). Even so, more than half of their videos are still not both faithful to the caption and physically plausible.
Moreover, every open-source model has an average SA below 3, indicating that at least one primary element of the caption is typically missing from the generated video. Even among assessable videos, where the intended event does occur, their average PC never exceeds 3.5, suggesting that the event typically violates at least one expectation of major severity or above. Video generative models handle optics best but struggle with heat and acoustics. As shown in Tab. 1 , Optics is the strongest domain for eight of the ten evaluated models, and ranks second (or tied for second) for the remaining two models, Seedance 2.5 and CogVideoX1.5-5B. In contrast, Heat and Acoustics are the weakest domains for most models. Even the proprietary models reach only 30.00% (Seedance 2.5) and 15.00% (Veo 3.1) JP on Acoustics, and two open-source models score 0.00% in this domain. We conjecture that optical phenomena ( e.g . , reflection and shadows) are visually salient and well represented in training data, whereas thermal processes involve gradual state changes and acoustic phenomena must be conveyed through indirect visual cues ( e.g . , sound-induced ripples on a liquid surface), both of which are harder to render. Semantic adherence is the main bottleneck, and physical errors are all-or-nothing. Fig. 5(a) shows the distribution of SA and PC scores. SA concentrates at 2 (50.8% of all videos), the score given when two or more primary elements of the caption are missing, while only 5.0% of videos receive a 4, the score for a video that misses only secondary details: most generated videos fail to depict the captioned event itself, before physics even comes into play. In contrast, once the event is fully shown, its physics is usually correct: as shown in Fig. 5(b) , only 5.9% of videos show the event fully but violate physics (SA ≥ \geq 4, PC < < 4), compared with 16.9% that pass, meaning that 74.1% of fully shown events are rendered with plausible physics. Physical errors themselves are all-or-nothing: PC over assessable videos is bimodal, with 52.9% scoring 5, i.e . , no violation at all, and 40.8% scoring 1 or 2, i.e . , a fatal or core violation, whereas scores of 3 and 4, i.e . , only major or minor violations, together account for 6.3%. This suggests that when current models do show the event, they either render its physics correctly or break it at the most basic level, and rarely get the core event right but slip on peripheral details. Models take a shortcut to high PC by not showing the event. Prior protocols rate PC on every video, so a video that never attempts the event leaves nothing for physics to contradict and receives a high PC. In PhyRubric, the PC items on the central event of such a video are judged not shown , so the video is marked unassessable and excluded from PC. We define the avoidance rate of a model as the share of its videos that never show the event. Such avoidance is common: 25.0% of all videos never show the event (Fig. 5(b) ), and the avoidance rate ranges from 20.0% to 44.5% across open-source models, compared with 5.7% and 8.6% for Seedance 2.5 and Veo 3.1 (Fig. 5(c) ). We regard avoidance as a failure of physical commonsense rather than merely of instruction following: every caption in PhyRubric-Bench states the outcome explicitly, so a model that still renders a static scene or a different event suggests that it does not know how the stated cause produces the stated effect. If avoided videos are scored as in prior protocols, the PC score of every open-source model rises by 0.16–0.59, and the gain grows with its avoidance rate (Fig. 5(c) ). LTX-Video-2B, with the highest avoidance rate (44.5%), would then rank above Cosmos-Predict2.5-14B and Wan2.2-T2V-A14B. PC alone is most misleading for Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B: under prior protocols its PC would be second only to Seedance 2.5 and above Veo 3.1, yet its JP (6.69%) ranks eighth of ten, as it rarely depicts the event. 4.3 Evaluation of PhyRubric-Judge We assess PhyRubric-Judge by its agreement with the human labels of PhyRubric-Bench. We apply each judge to every video in PhyRubric-Bench and report two metrics on its soft score: Pearson correlation ( r r ) [ 50 ] with the human scores, and ROC-AUC [ 24 ] for distinguishing videos that humans rate as pass (score ≥ \geq 4) from those rated as fail, separately for SA and PC. Suppl. Sec. B.2 reports the results of all judges on the full set of metrics adopted by prior work. Baselines. We compare PhyRubric-Judge with four groups of judges. To better understand the limitations of current T2V models, we categorize their failed items by the three levels and ten violation types in PhyRubric, with more analysis in Suppl. Sec. D.1 . The physical commonsense of current T2V models is still limited at the most basic level. As shown in Fig. 6 (a), 41.6% of the violations belong to L1 (core object principles) and 50.7% to L2 (everyday dynamics), while only 7.6% belong to L3 (attribute experience). This indicates that current models rarely fail on fine attributes, but instead break principles that hold for any object, such as persistence over time, and the way common events unfold. Fig. 6 (b) shows that the two most frequent types, missing consequence (24.0%) and permanence & count (20.4%), together account for nearly half of all violations. A permanence & count violation occurs when an object appears from nothing, vanishes, teleports, duplicates, or merges with another during the video. For example, in Fig. 7 (a), the sauce becomes fully stirred and the spoon teleports to a new position between 1.50 s and 1.62 s, a severe violation that any viewer would notice at a glance. A missing consequence violation occurs when the trigger of an event is shown but its effect never follows, or occurs at the wrong time. In Fig. 7 (b), the lights are on from the start, before the finger even reaches the button. Both videos have high visual quality, yet their obvious physical errors make them distinguishable from real videos. 5 Conclusion We presented PhyRubric, a rubric-grounded framework for diagnostic evaluation of physical commonsense in T2V generation, which fixes a caption-specific rubric before any video is seen and derives every score from evidence-grounded item verdicts. We instantiated it as PhyRubric-Bench across five physical domains and scaled it up to PhyRubric-Train, on which we trained PhyRubric-Judge, an open-source judge that outperforms existing open-source judges, matches Gemini 4 Argon, and generalizes to unseen generators and domains. Our evaluation of T2V models reveals that physical commonsense remains far from solved, and our in-depth analysis provides actionable insights for developing future T2V models. Captions are drafted one category at a time. For each of the 34 categories, Claude Fable 5.1 and GPT-5.6 Sol receive the scope note of the category and the drafting instructions in Sec. E.3 , and each model proposes a batch of candidates. Beyond the requirements in Sec. 3.2 , the instructions add three rules. (1) A caption states the trigger and the visible outcome in plain words but never how the outcome unfolds. (2) It contains no law names, numbers, decorative adjectives, or camera directions. (3) Its outcome must hold under every reasonable rendering rather than depend on a threshold, such as how hard an object is pushed. Each candidate also comes with two hidden fields that neither the generator nor the VLM judge ever sees: one to three violation types that the event puts at risk, and an expectation briefly describing how the outcome should unfold. Both guide rubric drafting. Candidates are first checked automatically for a well-formed record, a valid category, and the absence of numerals, and then reviewed manually in two stages. First, the categories are divided between two reviewers. Each reviewer marks every candidate in the assigned categories as keep, edit, or remove, provides a revised caption for each edit, and compares the captions within each category for repeated scenes, lack of variety, and events that belong to another domain. The other reviewer then cross-checks every decision and revision. Second, a third reviewer goes through all remaining captions to ensure consistent standards across categories. Disagreements are resolved by discussion. Review removed 242 candidates: 99 in heat, 48 in electricity, 48 in mechanics, 24 in acoustics, and 23 in optics. Rubric Construction. Every caption in both splits receives a rubric by the same procedure. Claude Fable 5.1 drafts the rubric from the caption, the scope note of its category, its violation types and expectation, and approved rubrics from the same domain as examples, following the instructions in Sec. E.3 . The instructions specify what each item may say: an SA item states a fact the caption requires and nothing else, and a PC item pairs a physical requirement with two to four visible signs of failure, constrains direction and order but never magnitude, and never requires an invisible cause such as heat, current, or sound to be seen. Every draft is first run through a checking program, which flags a draft that is malformed, alters the caption, has too few or too many items, or makes some score from 1 to 5 unreachable. It also flags numeric thresholds, vague words, SA items that judge physics or are conditional, secondary SA items that overlap a PC item, and declared violation types that no PC item covers. The 1,192 drafts are then divided evenly among three reviewers regardless of category. Each reviewer reads every assigned draft together with its flags and decides whether it passes, checking whether each item tests what the caption puts at risk and and whether every sign of failure is visible in the video. A draft that does not pass is revised with the LLM, checked by the program again, and reviewed until it passes. Finally, for cross-checking, each reviewer re-reads a random 10% of the rubrics approved by another reviewer, in a cycle, and agrees with the original decision in 93% of cases. The remaining cases, mostly wording refinements rather than incorrect items, are discussed and revised.
Fig. 9 shows the full rubric of the desk-lamp caption in Fig. 3 . Caption: The key switch on the base of a desk lamp is turned, and the lamp lights. Semantic adherence (SA) S1 [primary] A desk lamp with a key switch on its base. S2 [primary] The key switch is turned. S3 [primary] The lamp lights. S4 [secondary] The lamp is dark before the turn. S5 [secondary] The order is dark, turn, lit. Physical commonsense (PC) P1  [fatal | permanence_count] Expect: One lamp with one key switch; the lamp keeps its shape; the hand passes through nothing. Fail: A second lamp appears; or the lamp vanishes; or the hand passes through the base; or the lamp changes shape. P2  [fatal | permanence_count]  (scene-wide item, shared verbatim by all rubrics) Expect: Everything in view, named in the caption or not, keeps its number, identity and shape unless something visible acts on it, and nothing passes through anything else. Fail: An object that nothing visible acts on appears from nothing; or vanishes while still in frame and not occluded; or duplicates or merges with another; or turns into a different object or a different shape between frames; or any object passes through another. Changes brought about by the caption’s event or by a visible interaction, objects leaving the frame, and occlusion are not failures. P3  [core | missing_consequence] Expect: The lamp lights at the moment the key clicks over, and only then. Fail: The key is turned and the lamp stays dark; or the lamp is lit before the key moves; or the lamp comes on with the key untouched; or the lamp comes on well after the turn with nothing else happening. P4  [core | process_direction] Expect: Once on, the lamp stays lit steadily for the rest of the clip. Fail: The lamp goes off again by itself; or flickers on and off; or dims back to dark; or the key turns back by itself. P5  [major | missing_consequence] Expect: The lamp’s light reaches its surroundings: the shade glows and the desk beneath brightens, with shadows pointing away from the lamp. Fail: The bulb glows while the shade and desk stay as dark as before; or the surroundings brighten while the lamp stays dark; or the light comes from a place other than the lamp. P6  [major | causeless_motion] Expect: The lamp stays put as the key is turned; nothing else on the desk moves. Fail: The lamp slides, tips or lifts at the turn; or the shade swings by itself; or items on the desk jump. P7  [minor | attribute_drift] Expect: The shade’s color, the base and the desk stay the same apart from the light. Fail: The shade changes color rather than brightness; or the base changes shape; or the desk’s contents rearrange. E.3 and does not affect scoring. A.4 Annotation Agentic annotator. Both splits are first labeled by Claude Sonnet 5.5 running as a tool-using agent. Videos are labeled by independent agent instances, each handling five videos from mixed generators. Every video is labeled in a fresh context, so the agent sees only its caption, rubric, and video, never the labels of other videos. The agent sees the video only through a frame-inspection tool with two operations. Overview returns 16 uniformly spaced frames of the whole clip, each stamped with its timestamp. Zoom returns up to 16 consecutive frames at the full frame rate within a time window chosen by the agent, optionally cropped to a region. For every video, the agent first takes an overview and writes a description of the whole clip before reading the rubric in detail. It then zooms at least once into the time window and region where the central event should occur, and zooms again whenever a decision hinges on a moment it has not yet seen at full frame rate. An item that involves a small object or a fast action is never decided from the overview alone. The full instructions are given in Sec. E.3 . The agent applies the verdict definitions of Sec. 3.1 with one additional requirement: an item passes only when the agent can point to the frames that show it, since the caption states what was requested, not what the video contains. For every item, the agent writes its verdict with one or two sentences of timestamped evidence, and for a failed PC item it also records which sign in the Fail statement was observed. The agent never outputs scores; SA and PC are computed from its verdicts by the rules in Sec. 3.1 . PhyRubric-Train uses these labels as they are. Human verification. For PhyRubric-Bench, the three reviewers who built the rubrics verify every verdict against the video. Each reviewer watches the video, reads every verdict together with its evidence, and keeps it or changes it; the agent’s original labels are stored separately so that every change can be counted. Generator names are hidden and videos are shuffled across generators, so that a reviewer cannot be biased toward or against a particular model. The three reviewers first verify 20 videos together to align their standards, and these are excluded from the statistics below. Next, 150 videos, 15 per generator, are each verified independently by two reviewers, with each pair of reviewers covering one third of them. The remaining videos are divided evenly among the three reviewers. Overturn rate. Reviewers changed 2.8% of the SA verdicts and 5.6% of the PC verdicts. Most PC changes (61%) turn a pass into a fail, i.e . , a violation the agent missed, typically a brief or small one, which matches the error patterns of Sec. 4.4 . After verification, 88.1% of the videos keep both their SA and PC scores, and most of the remaining ones change by one point. On the doubly verified videos, the two reviewers agree on 95.3% of the verdicts (Cohen’s κ = \kappa={} 0.87), so the corrections reflect what the video shows rather than the preference of one reviewer. Because PhyRubric-Train is labeled by the same agent under the same instructions, the overturn rate also approximately estimates the error rate of its training labels, about 3% for SA and 6% for PC verdicts. Should the judge see the rubric? The annotator needs the rubric, whereas the judge takes only the caption (Sec. 3.3 ). We test this choice in three ways. (1) Giving the judge the rubric as input and training it to output the scores only improves correlation by 0.06 on SA and 0.05 on PC . The rubric thus carries information that the judge cannot fully recover from the caption, but every new caption would then need a rubric before the judge can be applied. (2) Giving the rubric and training the judge to output item-wise verdicts instead of scores does not help , as the scores derived from its verdicts are less accurate than directly predicted ones. (3) The rubric is of no use to the untrained model. Given the rubric, zero-shot Qwen3-VL-8B becomes worse on both dimensions, and its PC correlation falls below zero. We therefore keep the caption-only input, so that the judge applies to any new caption, and use the rubric at annotation time only. Where should the diagnosis go? The judge emits the scores first and the diagnosis after them. We compare two alternatives in which the diagnosis is generated first. (1) The diagnosis is the first field of the same JSON object. (2) The diagnosis is a chain of thought inside <think> tags, followed by a JSON object that holds the scores only. The diagnosis text is identical in all three cases, and only its position changes. Both alternatives lose 0.06–0.07 on SA and 0.12 on PC. The reason is that the soft score is the expectation over the score tokens (Eq. 2 ). When the score comes first, this distribution still reflects the uncertainty of the judge. After a committed diagnosis, it becomes nearly one-hot, and this uncertainty is lost. Scores first thus keeps an informative soft score and a diagnosis. Does reinforcement learning help? Starting from the SFT judge, we continue training the same LoRA with GRPO for one epoch, with 4 samples per prompt at temperature 1, a batch of 8 prompts, a learning rate of 10 − 5 10^{-5} , and no KL term. The reward is a weighted sum of four terms, R = r SA + r PC + 0.5 ​ r fmt + 0.5 ​ r assess , R=r_{\text{SA}}+r_{\text{PC}}+0.5\,r_{\text{fmt}}+0.5\,r_{\text{assess}}, (6) where r fmt r_{\text{fmt}} rewards a valid output format and r assess r_{\text{assess}} rewards a correct pc_assessable flag. The score terms measure how close the predicted score s ^ d \hat{s}_{d} is to the label s d s_{d} : r d = 1 − | s ^ d − s d | 4 , d ∈ { SA , PC } . r_{d}=1-\frac{|\hat{s}_{d}-s_{d}|}{4},\quad d\in\{\text{SA},\text{PC}\}. (7) We run this recipe twice, (1) without thinking, starting from Default, and (2) with thinking, starting from Diagnosis as thinking, and compare each run with its own starting point. As shown in Tab. 14 (c), neither run improves over its SFT starting point, and the thinking-free run is clearly better than the one with thinking, especially on PC ( r r of 0.314 vs. 0.086). A possible reason is that GRPO has mainly proven effective on reasoning tasks with verifiable answers, such as mathematics, and may bring less to a perceptual task such as judging physical commonsense in video. We leave this to future work and keep SFT as the default. How much training data is needed? PhyRubric-Train is split into 3,600 videos for training and 188 videos from 47 held-out captions for monitoring the training loss. All judges in this paper are trained on the 3,600-video split. We train on random 25% and 50% subsets of it (900 and 1,800 videos) with the same number of epochs (Fig. 11 ). SA improves only slightly with data (0.58, 0.59, 0.61), whereas PC improves steadily (0.22, 0.25, 0.32). Physical plausibility is thus the dimension that benefits most from more supervision. Since the training labels are produced automatically by the annotator, the judge can be strengthened further at low cost, by generating and labeling more videos without human annotation. A.2 ). Tab. 16 reports, for each domain, the share of assessable videos with at least one failed PC item of each type, and Tab. 17 breaks the same rates down by generator. Adherence decays along the causal episode. Every caption in PhyRubric describes a complete episode, namely a scene, a trigger, its effect, and their order, and the SA items follow this structure. Fig. 13 (a) plots the pass rate of each stage. Models render the scene most reliably, but the pass rate falls for the trigger and its effect, and falls further for their order. The decay is steepest for the weakest models. Fig. 13 (b) focuses on the step from trigger to effect. In mechanics and optics, where the effect follows directly from contact or light, 68% and 70% of triggered videos show it. In heat, electricity, and acoustics, where the cause reaches the effect through an invisible medium, only 43–49% do. Violation types follow the physics of each domain. Tab. 16 shows that the violation types are not spread evenly over the domains but follow what each domain asks for. Missing consequence is the most frequent type overall (17.3% of assessable videos) and peaks in acoustics (44.4%) and electricity (24.6%), where an invisible sound or current must produce a visible effect, against 9.1% in mechanics. Acoustics also leads in causeless motion (21.8%), since a vibration that should follow a sound is often rendered as an object that shivers on its own. Process direction concentrates in heat and electricity (11.2% and 13.0%), where a melting process reverses or a lamp switches back off. Optics carries the appearance types, namely shape and identity (11.7%), where a reflection or a refracted object does not match its source, and attribute drift (9.3%), where the color of an untouched object changes. Mechanics, where several objects interact, leads in permanence and count (18.5%), material response (8.6%), and trajectory and momentum (8.2%). Each domain thus exposes a different class of failure, and a benchmark concentrated on mechanics would leave most of these failures unmeasured. Object permanence separates the generators, while missing consequence is shared. Tab. 17 breaks the same rates down by generator, and the two most frequent types behave differently. Permanence and count failures vary by a factor of six. Objects appear, vanish, or duplicate in 33.3% of the assessable videos of CogVideoX1.5-5B and in 22–23% of those of LTX-Video-2B and Wan2.2-TI2V-5B, but in only 5.2% for Seedance 2.5 and 8.6% for HunyuanVideo-1.5 and Cosmos-Predict2.5-14B, with Veo 3.1 in between at 12.9%. Missing consequence, in contrast, stays between 11% and 22% for every generator, and causeless motion between 6% and 13%. Missing consequence is the most frequent type for all generators except the two weakest, where permanence and count leads. Keeping objects consistent over time thus separates the generators, whereas linking a cause to its visible effect is a weakness they all share and remains the dominant failure even of the generators that largely keep objects consistent. When the event passes (a), the description is a complete timeline of the event. When the event never occurs (b), the judge says so and marks PC as not assessable. When physics breaks (c, d), the judge names the violation and when it occurs, such as fish that tumble with nothing touching them or balls that appear and vanish on the table. Caption drafting You write captions that test the physical commonsense of text-to-video models. Each caption describes an everyday scene with one visible dynamic event, and will later be rendered by video models and judged against a rubric. Domain: {domain}. Category: {category}. Scope of this category: {category_note} Write {n} captions for this category. Each caption must satisfy all of the following. 1. One or two sentences in plain English. An everyday scene that a non-expert recognises from experience; no textbook demonstrations. 2. State the triggering event AND its visible outcome (falls off, is thrown forward, shatters, slides a short way and stops), so the phenomenon is guaranteed to be in frame and the ending is not left open. 3. Never state HOW the outcome happens physically (no ‘‘accelerating’’, ‘‘in an arc’’, ‘‘bounces lower each time’’, ‘‘at the same moment as’’). The manner belongs in the expectation, not the caption. 4. The outcome must hold for every reasonable rendering of the caption. Do not write an event whose outcome depends on a threshold (fast enough, hard enough), on skill, or on details the caption leaves open. If the outcome is conditional, change the scene or drop it. 5. No law names, no numbers or units, no decorative adjectives, no camera or shot directions (close-up, slow motion, the camera follows). Describe the scene, not how to film it. 6. The visible change must fit in a clip of about five seconds from one fixed viewpoint. Describe slow processes as an ongoing state or a before-and-after result, not as a completed action. 7. Include a person only when the human body itself is part of the physics (a hand warming ice, breath fogging a window). Otherwise use objects, animals, or an unseen actor. 8. Vary the objects, settings, and triggers across captions; no two captions should share the same scene. For each caption also give: -- violation_types: one to three tags from {permanence_count, shape_identity, solidity, causeless_motion, gravity_direction, trajectory_momentum, process_direction, missing_consequence, material_response, attribute_drift}, naming the expectations this event puts at risk. -- expectation: one or two sentences on HOW the stated outcome should unfold: its timing relative to the cause, its direction, whether it is gradual or abrupt, and what must not happen. It must hold for every reasonable rendering. It is never shown to the video model or the judge. Example: caption: A block is pulled out of the bottom row of a tall wooden block tower and the tower collapses. violation_types: gravity_direction, missing_consequence expectation: The collapse starts as the support is removed, the tower sways then falls, and the blocks scatter on the table; it does not stand for long on nothing. SA items ask whether the clip shows what the caption says; PC items ask whether what the clip shows is physically possible. Scores are derived from the item verdicts afterwards and are never rated directly. Caption: {caption}. Category: {category}. Scope of this category: {category_note}. Violation types: {violation_types}. Expectation: {expectation}. Approved rubrics from the same domain, to match in structure, length and tone: {examples} SA items (3--6; at least two primary and one secondary). -- One item per visible fact the caption requires: the objects, the action, the stated outcome. Nothing the caption does not say, and nothing about physics or quality. -- Primary: facts without which the clip is not this caption. Secondary: qualifiers the caption states (once, slowly, wooden), stated counts, and, when the caption names several sub-actions, one item asking whether they happen in the caption’s order. -- A secondary item never restates a physical expectation, is never hypothetical, and never depends on an event the caption does not include. Every item must be decidable on any clip. PC items (six; a seventh scene-wide fatal item is appended automatically), in this order: 1. Core items, one per violation type that is the central mechanism, built from the expectation, in the order the event unfolds. 2. Major items: the accompanying physics of side elements that this scene puts at risk (gravity on a secondary object, material response, a secondary consequence). 3. One fatal item naming the scene’s objects: nothing appears, vanishes, merges, morphs, or passes through anything. 4. One minor attribute-drift item: colour, texture, label and position of untouched things. Each PC item has an expect and a fail. Expect says what a correct clip shows and thereby which moment, region or event the item is about. Fail is the only ground for a FAIL: two to four concrete visible events joined by ‘‘; or’’, each something a viewer can point at in the frames. Fail is not the negation of expect. Judge direction and order only, never magnitude or rate: a correct clip in which the process has barely advanced must pass. Never require an unseen cause (heat, current, sound, wind, a hand) to be visible. Blur, flicker, resolution, colour cast, style, and self-driven agents starting or stopping are not violations. Do not use vague words (unrealistic, implausible, properly, correctly, natural, smoothly); name the event. No two items share a fail text. Add a precondition only for scene content the caption does not guarantee; such an item is not_shown when the precondition does not occur. Present tense, plain words, no numbers with units, no camera or image-quality language. Return the rubric as JSON in the format of the examples. Agentic annotator You label generated videos against a rubric, from the video frames alone. Work independently: you never see the labels of other videos or other labellers. Do not judge from the caption. Captions say what was requested; generated videos often do something else. Mark a pass only when you can point to what you saw. Decision rules. -- SA item: pass if the clip visibly shows exactly what the check says, else fail. SA is never not_shown. Read each check literally: a loosely similar action does not count (a hand resting on a car is not pushing it). A clip showing a different scene fails SA items and is NOT penalised in PC for that. -- PC item, three steps. (1) Is the situation the item is about in the clip at all (the precondition occurs; the moment, region or event in expect appears)? If not: not_shown. not_shown means the situation is absent; it does not mean the correct behaviour is missing, and it does not mean unsure. (2) Situation present and you SEE an event listed in fail: fail, and copy the matching clause into matched_fail. The fail text is the only ground for a fail. (3) Otherwise pass. -- Something that looks wrong but that no fail clause names goes into uncovered, never into a fail. Before writing anything into uncovered, go through the fail clauses of every PC item once more. -- End state present from the first frame: when the effect the caption describes is on screen from frame 0 and the action that should cause it never happens, the central event is absent. The SA item for the trigger fails; PC items tied to the trigger moment are not_shown. -- Judge only what is visible. Direction and order of change matter, not speed or how far it got. Not penalised: image quality (blur, flicker, colour cast); a heat source, power supply, sound, wind, breath, or hand that is not visible; camera motion; style and set dressing; anything that needs a numeric measurement; self-driven agents starting, stopping or turning.
