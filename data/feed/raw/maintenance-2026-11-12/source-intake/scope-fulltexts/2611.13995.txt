We introduce LipsFlower to reduce this amplification. We derive
stability bounds for Flow-from- Y Y , which initializes the ordinary
differential equation from a noise-perturbed observation, as well as for
observation-conditioned posterior sampling, which conditions the
velocity field on the observation throughout integration.
Based on this analysis, we develop LipHDiT, a Lipschitz-aware
hourglass diffusion transformer that serves as the vector-field architecture of LipsFlower. LipHDiT incorporates stabilized approximate spectral normalization, bounded modulation, and convex residual mixing. We also use adversarial finite-radius gain regularization
at sampled interpolation states to penalize excessive sensitivity
to the state and, for conditional sampling, the observation.
We test both formulations on blind face restoration, measuring adversarial robustness and the perceptual quality of the reconstructions. LipsFlower reduces perturbation amplification while keeping restoration quality
competitive with the evaluated baselines. These results suggest that Lipschitz-aware flow matching can improve the stability of generative image restoration. 1 Introduction Image restoration seeks to recover clean images
from degraded observations. One particularly challenging case is
blind face restoration, which aims to recover a
high-quality face image from an observation affected by unknown
degradations, such as blur, noise, downsampling, and compression.
The problem is ill-posed: several clean faces may explain the
same degraded input. The output must therefore look realistic,
preserve facial identity and geometry, and stay consistent with
the observation. Recent generative methods use face priors to
synthesize plausible
details [ 49 , 14 , 60 , 56 ] .
But a convincing output can still be sensitive to small input
perturbations. Figure 1 : Robustness to observation perturbations. LipsFlower reduces output changes for both backbones under model-specific black-box SPSA attacks, with a larger reduction for Flow-from- Y Y .
The mean perturbation and output differences are amplified for visibility.
Blue boxes mark the largest above-threshold change regions,
with thresholds matched within each backbone pair. For human faces, even a slight change in the input
can cause an image restoration model to produce noticeably different facial features, potentially
changing the person’s apparent identity.
Metrics such as PSNR [ 20 ] ,
LPIPS [ 58 ] , and FID [ 17 ] assess reconstruction or distributional quality, but do not directly
measure input sensitivity.
A model can score well on these metrics
and still produce very different reconstructions from nearly
identical observations. Flow matching [ 30 ] learns a time-dependent
velocity field whose ordinary differential equation (ODE) transports
samples from a source distribution toward clean images. A common form of flow matching is rectified flow [ 31 ] ,
in which models are trained on straight-line interpolations
between source and target samples. Recent work shows that these models can produce
visually convincing restorations [ 31 , 38 ] . We consider two
formulations: Flow-from- Y Y [ 38 ] , which
starts the ODE from a noise-perturbed degraded observation, and observation-conditioned posterior sampling [ 61 ] , which starts from a simple base distribution (e.g., multivariate Gaussian) and conditions the velocity field on the observation throughout integration.
Neither formulation by itself guarantees that nearby observations
produce nearby reconstructions. In Flow-from- Y Y , an input
perturbation changes the initial state and propagates through the
dynamics. In conditional sampling, perturbing the observation directly
changes the velocity field. Our stability analysis in Sec. 4 relates these effects to the field’s Lipschitz behavior: the velocity field’s sensitivity to the evolving ODE state matters in both
formulations, while conditional sampling additionally
requires controlling the velocity field’s sensitivity
to the observation. Standard flow-matching regression does not directly constrain the field’s Lipschitz constants with respect to the ODE state or the observation.
Our goal is to reduce excessive amplification while preserving the capacity needed for realistic restoration. This motivates controlling selected sources of
amplification rather than imposing an excessively small global Lipschitz constant. The latter may restrict the nonlinear transformations needed to restore heavily degraded images and recover facial detail. We introduce LipsFlower , a flow-matching framework that
combines architectural controls with adversarial finite-radius gain
regularization to reduce input sensitivity. Its velocity network, LipHDiT , builds on the hourglass diffusion transformer
(HDiT) [ 8 ] . It uses stabilized approximate
spectral normalization, controlled normalization and time
modulation, and convex residual and skip mixing. Our architectural contribution lies in adapting
and integrating these controls within HDiT to improve restoration
stability. The conditional
variant of LipHDiT uses separate state and observation projection
stems with controlled fusion, targeting
the additional observation sensitivity identified by our analysis.
We also adapt adversarial Lipschitz
regularization [ 46 ] to velocity fields at
sampled flow-matching interpolation states. To do this, we search for local
state perturbations at several magnitude scales and use a separate
observation perturbation search to target conditional sensitivity.
A hinge penalty applies only when the finite-difference velocity
gain exceeds a prescribed threshold. Sampling across training
times extends regularization beyond the source and endpoint
without requiring full ODE rollouts. Together, these mechanisms
encourage empirical stability.
We evaluate both formulations on blind face restoration,
measuring reconstruction changes under adversarial input
perturbations, along with reconstruction and perceptual quality.
Our experiments show that architectural controls and gain
regularization reduce perturbation amplification while keeping
reconstruction fidelity and perceptual quality competitive with the evaluated baselines.
Our main contributions are: • We derive stability bounds for Flow-from- Y Y and
observation-conditioned posterior sampling, identifying the state and observation Lipschitz quantities that govern input
sensitivity. • We introduce LipsFlower, which combines a Lipschitz-aware
hourglass transformer with adversarial finite-radius
regularization of state sensitivity and, for conditional
sampling, observation sensitivity. • We evaluate both formulations on blind face restoration,
assessing restoration-map
sensitivity and reconstruction quality. The results
show that LipsFlower improves robustness to observation
perturbations, particularly in the Flow-from- Y Y formulation, while maintaining competitive
reconstruction quality. 2 Related Work Blind face image restoration. Early deep-learning methods for blind face restoration (BFR) used facial geometry, semantic parsing maps, or component dictionaries to reduce ambiguity [ 28 , 5 , 27 ] . More recent generative
methods use learned priors to synthesize realistic facial details.
GPEN [ 55 ] and GFP-GAN [ 49 ] incorporate
generative face priors into restoration networks.
VQFR [ 14 ] , RestoreFormer [ 52 ] , and
CodeFormer [ 60 ] use vector-quantized codebooks or
transformer-based retrieval of high-quality facial features.
These methods improve perceptual quality, but strong priors can also
generate details that the observation does not clearly support.
Diffusion models [ 18 ] have since been applied to BFR
through conditional generation, degradation removal, and identity-aware
guidance. DifFace [ 56 ] uses a pretrained diffusion prior
for posterior estimation, while DR2 [ 51 ] separates
degradation removal from content enhancement.
Region-adaptive conditioning [ 44 ] ,
frequency-domain restoration [ 36 ] , and one-step
diffusion models [ 48 ] further improve identity
preservation, efficiency, or the perception–distortion trade-off.
Evaluation still focuses mainly on the quality of restored images.
Their consistency under small changes to the degraded observation
has received less attention. Standard distortion and perceptual
metrics do not directly show whether nearby observations produce
nearby reconstructions. Our experiments show that
the evaluated baselines can amplify small observation perturbations
into larger changes in their reconstructions. Flow matching for image restoration. Flow-based restoration methods differ in how they use the degraded
observation. Plug-and-play methods such as
PnP-Flow [ 35 ] reuse a pretrained flow model
as an image prior within an iterative reconstruction algorithm
that promotes consistency with the degraded observation. Other methods transport degraded observations or preliminary
estimates toward the clean-image
distribution [ 39 , 11 ] . For example, ELIR [ 7 ] uses latent consistency flow matching to transport preliminary latent estimates with added Gaussian noise toward clean-image representations.
FlowIE [ 61 ] instead learns a conditional rectified flow from Gaussian noise to clean-image representations, using initial restorations of the degraded inputs as guidance.
PMRF [ 39 ] is closely related to our setting.
It transports an estimate of the posterior mean toward the clean-image
distribution, aiming for a better perception–distortion trade-off.
Its controlled study compares Flow-from- Y Y with two conditional
rectified-flow baselines whose velocity fields are conditioned on
either the degraded observation or its posterior-mean estimate. Unlike PMRF’s two-stage pipeline, our formulations
do not require a separate posterior-mean predictor.
Other recent methods use rectified flows to connect discriminative
and generative restoration or progressively refine images across
degradation levels [ 11 ] . These methods focus mainly on
restoration quality, sampling efficiency, and transport design.
We instead study how perturbations to the degraded observation propagate
through the learned dynamics. We consider two pathways: the initial
condition in Flow-from- Y Y and the observation supplied to the velocity
field in observation-conditioned posterior sampling. Lipschitz control and robustness of learned dynamics. Methods for controlling input sensitivity in neural networks include
spectral normalization of linear operators [ 37 ] ,
gradient penalties [ 15 ] , orthogonal or
norm-constrained parameterizations [ 6 ] , and non-expansive activations [ 1 ] . GroupSort networks
combine norm-constrained linear maps with gradient-norm-preserving
activations to retain expressivity under Lipschitz constraints [ 1 , 45 ] .
Standard dot-product self-attention is not globally Lipschitz on
an unbounded domain, which has motivated normalized and otherwise
controlled attention mechanisms [ 24 , 9 ] .
For neural ODEs, the regularity of the vector field governs the
existence, uniqueness, and sensitivity of trajectories [ 3 ] . This connection has been used to study
robustness, stability, and generalization in continuous-depth models [ 54 , 22 , 34 ] .
Recent work on plug-and-play flow restoration also links vector-field
Lipschitz regularity to numerical restoration error [ 21 ] .
Our analysis focuses on how perturbations to the degraded observation
affect the restoration map, both for flows initialized
from the observation and for flows conditioned on it. LipsFlower uses this analysis to guide two forms of control: architectural control and gain regularization.
First, LipHDiT limits amplification within the hourglass diffusion
transformer through controlled normalization and conditioning,
regularized linear projections, and scaled residual branches.
Second, since layer-wise global bounds can be
conservative and overly restrictive, we also regularize adversarial
finite-radius gain at sampled flow-matching interpolation states. This penalizes excessive local amplification across training times without imposing an overly small global Lipschitz constant on the whole model.
TVM [ 59 ] is the closest work architecturally.
It identifies the lack of Lipschitz continuity in standard diffusion
transformers as a source of training instability and introduces
semi-Lipschitz modifications based on normalized attention,
normalization, modulation, and initialization.
Under a Lipschitz assumption, it relates a terminal-velocity
objective to distributional error in one- and few-step image
generation. This result concerns the distance between generated and target image distributions, not the sensitivity of individual reconstructions to their degraded observations.
We bound how observation
perturbations affect the final reconstruction when introduced
through the initial state or the observation condition.
We also regularize finite-radius amplification
at sampled interpolation states.

produce correspondingly small reconstruction changes, with

formulations require control of the velocity field with respect to the evolving
state. Second , an observation-conditioned flow must additionally control its
dependence on the conditioning image. Third , control should apply throughout
the transport interval, because sensitivity accumulated at intermediate states
affects the terminal reconstruction.
The next section explains how LipsFlower addresses these three
requirements through architectural controls and gain regularization. 5 LipsFlower Guided by our stability analysis, LipsFlower combines a
Lipschitz-aware velocity network with finite-radius gain
regularization. We instantiate this framework for both
Flow-from- Y Y and observation-conditioned posterior sampling,
targeting state sensitivity in both formulations and
observation sensitivity in the conditional formulation. 5.1 Lipschitz-Aware Velocity Network Our velocity network, LipHDiT , keeps the overall
structure of HDiT [ 8 ] and adds controls
to limit amplification. Linear projections use approximate
spectral normalization [ 37 ] with a safety
margin and full-precision power iteration. We include safeguards
against degenerate iteration vectors, including for zero-initialized
weights. We add a denominator floor to RMSNorm [ 57 ] and bound the time-dependent modulation used for adaptive
conditioning [ 8 , 41 ] .
Our query-key normalization also uses a denominator floor,
building on normalized-attention
designs [ 16 , 8 ] .
To control amplification in the residual branches and decoder skip connections, we use convex
aggregation [ 6 ] .
For conditional sampling, separate
state and observation projection stems feed a controlled fusion
module before the shared backbone. These controls aim to reduce
amplification without requiring every component to be nonexpansive. More architectural details are given in Appendix B of the supplementary material. 5.2 Finite-Radius Gain Regularization Figure 3 : Finite-radius gain regularization. Adversarial searches perturb local state regions and,
for conditional sampling (marked in orange), the whole observation.
Squared hinge penalties on velocity gains above prescribed
thresholds are added to the flow-matching loss. We also adapt adversarial Lipschitz
regularization [ 46 ] to restoration
velocity fields. It searches for perturbations that cause large

Flowing directly from the observation offers a better balance between robustness and restoration quality, whereas conditioning on the observation reduces maximum amplification and perceptual drift at a moderately higher cost to perceptual fidelity. 7 Limitations Our results show that Lipschitz-aware flow matching can reduce perturbation amplification, although the effect depends on the restoration formulation. In particular, LipsFlower (w/ Flow-from- Y Y ) gives a better balance between robustness and reconstruction quality,
whereas observation-conditioned sampling loses more perceptual quality and shows less consistent robustness gains.
At the same time, the architectural controls and finite-radius regularization do not provide a global Lipschitz guarantee.
Therefore, the stability results still depend on the assumptions used in the analysis. In addition, our experiments are limited to blind face restoration.
Evaluating other inverse problems and stronger perturbations are good directions for future work. 8 Conclusion We introduced LipsFlower to make flow-based image restoration less sensitive to input perturbations.
Our method combines Lipschitz-aware architectural controls with finite-radius gain regularization, while our stability analysis explains how observation perturbations enter and propagate through two flow-matching formulations.
On blind face restoration, LipsFlower reduces perturbation amplification, with Flow-from- Y Y giving the better balance between robustness and reconstruction quality.

Appendix B LipsFlower: Architecture, Regularization Details, and Additional Results This section details the architectural controls and
finite-radius regularization used by LipsFlower.
Both Flow-from- Y Y and observation-conditioned posterior
sampling use the same core architectural modifications
and localized state regularizer. The conditional model
additionally uses separate input stems and an
observation-sensitivity penalty. B.1 LipHDiT Architecture Backbone. LipHDiT follows the hierarchical organization of
HDiT [ 8 ] . We use 4 × 4 4\times 4 input
patches, feature widths ( 256,512 , 1024 ) (256,512,1024) , and level depths ( 2 , 2 , 8 ) (2,2,8) . The first two levels use neighborhood attention

global attention. The attention head dimension is 64 64 ,
and the feed-forward hidden dimension is three times the

we control linear projections using an estimate of the

The matrix–vector products and spectral estimates are

disable subsequent spectral estimation.

LipsFlower and the inference procedures used for the
main comparisons. C.1 Training Data and Degradation Pipeline We train our restoration models on FFHQ images [ 23 ] at 512 × 512 512\times 512 resolution. Images are converted to
three-channel RGB tensors with values in [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] .
Degraded observations are generated by combining blur, resizing, Gaussian noise, and JPEG compression.
The pipeline samples isotropic or anisotropic Gaussian
blur with equal probability, using a 41 × 41 41\times 41 kernel and blur standard deviations in [ 0.1 , 15 ] [0.1,15] .
The resizing factor is sampled from [ 0.8 , 32 ] [0.8,32] ,
the Gaussian noise standard deviation from [ 0 , 20 / 255 ] [0,20/255] ,
and JPEG quality from [ 30,100 ] [30,100] .
The degraded image is subsequently resized back to 512 × 512 512\times 512 using bilinear interpolation.
Training also uses random horizontal flipping and
grayscale conversion with probability 0.01 0.01 ,
while retaining three channels.
The resulting observation is quantized to 1 / 255 1/255 increments.
Consequently, the clean image and degraded observation
are supplied in the same spatial and channel dimensions.
Downsampling and resizing back to the target resolution are part of observation generation, and the restoration model does not receive the sampled degradation parameters. C.2 Optimization Both LipsFlower formulations use AdamW [ 32 ] with a constant

and disabled for the raw weights of spectrally
controlled projections.
