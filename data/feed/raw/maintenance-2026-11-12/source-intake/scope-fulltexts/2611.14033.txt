Distribution Matching Distillation (DMD) supervises few-step generators with a separate fake-score network, which adds training memory and computation and must track the evolving generator.
We extend APEX to streaming video by using condition shifting to estimate the velocity field of the generated distribution within the generator.

This removes the independent fake network while retaining S2-Avatar’s frozen reference teacher, Self-Replay Forcing, and perceptual regularization.

Demo times are measured relative to the start of each recording. 1 Introduction Diffusion and flow models produce high-quality videos through repeated network evaluations ( Brooks et al., 2024 , Google DeepMind, 2025 , Wan et al., 2025 , Seedance et al., 2026 ) .
Streaming generation incurs this cost for every new block, making few-step sampling particularly valuable for long sequences.
Distillation reduces the number of evaluations ( Song et al., 2023 , Salimans and Ho, 2022 , Zheng et al., 2025b ) , while the resulting generator must preserve visual quality, maintain characters and scenes, and respond to changing instructions. Figure 1 shows APEX video examples of object interaction and storytelling. Distribution Matching Distillation (DMD) supervises generated samples through the difference between reference and generated-distribution scores ( Yin et al., 2024b , Yin et al., 2024a ) .
A frozen pretrained teacher supplies the reference estimate, while a separate fake-score network learns from current generator outputs.
For large video backbones, maintaining this auxiliary network adds training memory and computation.
Its estimate must also track the generator as the output distribution changes, requiring coordination of their optimization settings and updates.
This motivates learning the fake estimate within the generator. TwinFlow ( Cheng et al., 2026 ) and the original APEX ( Liu et al., 2026c ) show how generation and distribution estimation can share a network.
We extend APEX’s condition shifting to streaming video: the original text condition selects generation, and its shifted version selects a fake estimator trained on outputs for the same prompt.
The two roles share model parameters and, by default, optimizer states, while a separate frozen pretrained teacher provides the same reference guidance as in S2-Avatar. Streaming video requires the estimator to track outputs generated with a causal history.
We integrate condition shifting into S2-Avatar’s Self-Replay Forcing (SRF), which trains on replayed segments of the model’s own generated sequences ( Zhang et al., 2026c ) .
The shared fake estimator is fitted to these replay outputs, and its clean-latent predictions are compared with those of the frozen teacher to construct a detached correction for the generator.
We retain S2’s replay procedure and perceptual regularization, as described in Section 3 . We evaluate APEX on 180-second videos through a 70-case human study and all 320 StreamAV-Bench scenarios.
Relative to S2-Avatar, APEX receives positive net human preference for instruction following, consistency, and video quality, and achieves higher video instruction-fulfillment scores and visual aesthetics on both benchmark tracks.
A separate measurement under matched training settings shows 4.4 % 4.4\% lower peak allocated GPU memory and 9.2 % 9.2\% shorter mean iteration time than DMD.
Instruction-following preference also remains positive at 2 backbone NFE. Section 4 also reports audiovisual synchronization, motion quality, and usability. The paper makes three contributions to few-step streaming video generation: 1. Shared-network estimation through condition shifting. We learn the fake velocity within the generator while retaining a frozen reference teacher and removing the independent fake network. 2. Integration with Self-Replay Forcing. We apply condition-shifted estimation to the outputs of S2’s Self-Replay Forcing, retaining its replay procedure and perceptual regularization. 3. Video quality and training efficiency. Embedded Representation Warmup ( Liu et al., 2025a ) improves training efficiency by aligning early model layers with a pretrained encoder before full generative training.
Consistency and trajectory-based methods train the model to predict over a time interval, allowing generation with fewer network evaluations.
Consistency Models (CMs) ( Song et al., 2023 , Lu and Song, 2024 ) learn to map points on the same trajectory to a common endpoint.
Score-regularized continuous-time consistency (rCM) combines consistency training with score-based distribution matching for large-scale diffusion distillation ( Zheng et al., 2025b ) .
MeanFlow ( Geng et al., 2025 ) predicts the average velocity over an interval, while Transition Models (TiM) ( Wang et al., 2025 ) learn the transition between its endpoints.
DuMo ( Sun et al., 2026b ) jointly predicts local velocity and a flow map with two heads on a shared backbone.
UCGM ( Sun et al., 2025b ) interpolates between generative training objectives. A.2 Distribution Matching and Adversarial Training Few-step generation also benefits from direct feedback on the generated distribution. External correction signals can come from auxiliary score models, as in distribution matching ( Yin et al., 2024b , Yin et al., 2024a ) , or from discriminators used in adversarial refiners ( Kim et al., 2023 , Sauer et al., 2024 , Zheng et al., 2025a ) .
Additional networks increase memory use and update cost. Some early distillation methods also use precomputed teacher-student pairs for regularization, while DMD2 removes that paired-data requirement ( Yin et al., 2024a ) .
Direct Discriminative Optimization (DDO) ( Zheng et al., 2025a ) expresses the GAN discriminator as a likelihood ratio constructed from the target and a fixed reference model. TwinFlow ( Cheng et al., 2026 ) learns real and fake trajectories within a shared model and uses their velocity discrepancy to construct an internal correction signal.
LLaDA-Image Turbo ( Chen et al., 2026a ) adapts TwinFlow-based distillation to support image generation in two to four sampling steps. APEX ( Liu et al., 2026c ) follows this line and separates the branches through condition shifting. The shifted condition selects the fake estimator for samples generated with the original prompt. In Section 3.2 , our video formulation shares generator and fake parameters while retaining S2’s frozen reference teacher. Intrinsic Distribution Matching (IDM) ( Liu et al., 2026a ) adds feature mean and standard deviation matching to DMD using the generator’s exponential moving average (EMA) copy. Few-step score identity distillation (SiD) ( Zhou et al., 2025 ) also studies how conditioning affects distribution matching. Its Zero-CFG strategy removes text conditioning from the fake-score network, while Anti-CFG applies negative classifier-free guidance by combining conditional and unconditional predictions. These strategies modify the fake-score estimate. APEX fits the fake velocity under shifted text. Fake-Score-network-Free DMD (FSF-DMD) ( Kim et al., 2026a ) replaces the auxiliary fake-score network with a generator-induced endpoint pseudo-velocity. APEX instead learns the fake velocity through flow matching on generated samples under a shifted condition.
TBSM ( Sun et al., 2026a ) constructs regression targets from attraction to real samples and repulsion from generated samples under an energy-distance objective; its correction is defined by sample interactions rather than a real/fake velocity difference. A.3 Causal Streaming Video Autoregressive video diffusion has been studied through asynchronous generation ( Sun et al., 2025a ) , progressive prediction ( Xie et al., 2025 ) , and causal generation with cache sharing ( Gao et al., 2025a ) .
MAGI-1 studies autoregressive generation at scale ( Teng et al., 2025 ) , while SkyReels-V2 targets extended video generation ( Chen et al., 2025 ) .
FIFO-Diffusion ( Kim et al., 2024 ) and StreamingT2V ( Henschel et al., 2025 ) also investigate extending video generation beyond a short clip.
Later predictions depend on generated history. CausVid distills bidirectional video diffusion into an autoregressive model ( Yin et al., 2025 ) ; diagonal distillation provides another streaming autoregressive formulation ( Liu et al., 2026d ) .
Causal Forcing and Causal Forcing++ study few-step autoregressive distillation for real-time interaction ( Zhu et al., 2026a , Zhao et al., 2026b ) .
Causal-rCM unifies Teacher Forcing and Self Forcing in a streaming distillation recipe ( Zheng et al., 2026 ) .
LPM studies video-based character performance ( Zeng et al., 2026 ) , providing a setting for distribution matching in autoregressive models that animate characters. S2-Avatar ( Zhang et al., 2026c ) combines bidirectional reference-conditioned training, causal adaptation with Teacher and Diffusion Forcing, and Self-Replay Forcing.
Diffusion Forcing ( Chen et al., 2024 ) exposes the model to noisy historical states, while Self Forcing ( Huang et al., 2026a ) trains with autoregressive histories generated by the current model.
S2’s replay procedure retains cross-block gradients inside a sampled trajectory segment without retaining the full rollout graph.
APEX uses this video setting to learn the fake estimator within the generator while retaining S2’s causal replay procedure and reference teacher.
FlowCPO ( Han et al., 2026 ) studies offline preference alignment using fixed preferred and dispreferred samples.
Preference optimization and latent refinement are separate from the APEX correction. A.4 Historical Context and Long Video Generation History-Guided Video Diffusion studies conditioning on past video ( Song et al., 2025 ) , while Grounded Forcing separates persistent semantics from recent dynamics ( Chen et al., 2026b ) .
LongLive ( Yang et al., 2025a ) and Rolling Forcing ( Liu et al., 2025b ) target real-time long video generation; Infinity-RoPE investigates action control under autoregressive self-rollout ( Yesiltepe et al., 2026 ) .
UltraViCo ( Zhao et al., 2026a ) addresses length extrapolation by suppressing attention dispersion beyond the training window, providing a complementary approach to extending video duration.
These works motivate evaluating instruction response and motion quality alongside visual appearance as a stream grows longer. Memory design provides a complementary approach.
SWIFT uses prompt-adaptive memory ( Tan et al., 2026 ) , IAMFlow studies identity-aware memory ( Liu et al., 2026e ) , and FramePack compresses the input-frame context for next-frame prediction ( Zhang and Agrawala, 2025 ) .
Deep Forcing uses sink states and compression ( Yi et al., 2025 ) ; Rolling Sink studies the gap between limited training horizons and open-ended testing ( Li et al., 2026b ) .
MemRoPE uses evolving memory tokens for extended generation ( Kim et al., 2026b ) .
Attention sinks were also studied for streaming language models ( Xiao et al., 2024 ) ; S2 adopts sink blocks within its video context design ( Zhang et al., 2026d , Zhang et al., 2026c ) .
APEX retains this context design for its shared model.
Quant VideoGen ( Xi et al., 2026 ) reduces autoregressive history-storage costs through two-bit KV-cache quantization, using semantic-aware smoothing and progressive residual quantization.
ViewRope ( Xiang et al., 2026 ) instead injects camera-ray geometry into rotary position embeddings and selects relevant historical frames to improve spatial consistency in camera-controlled video world models.
These methods address history storage and retrieval, complementing APEX’s focus on the training cost of distribution estimation. For streaming video understanding, ChronoReplay ( Hu et al., 2026a ) organizes heterogeneous spatial supervision into chronological episodes, while Supervising the Visible State ( Hu et al., 2026b ) separates supervision of temporal state from adaptive selection of visual evidence.
These studies provide complementary context for the role of temporal supervision and historical evidence in streaming video understanding. A.5 Audio-Driven and Interactive Video Audio-driven character generation includes portrait animation with Hallo3 ( Cui et al., 2025 ) , body animation with OmniAvatar ( Gan et al., 2025 ) , and cinematic video with Wan-S2V ( Gao et al., 2025b ) .
StableAvatar studies extended audio-driven generation ( Tu et al., 2025 ) .
Live Avatar ( Huang et al., 2025 ) , SoulX-FlashTalk ( Shen et al., 2025 ) , and AptAvatar ( Zhang et al., 2026a ) address real-time or long-form avatar generation.
AvatarForcing specifically studies one-step streaming talking avatars ( Cui et al., 2026 ) .
Joint audio and video generation is studied by OmniForcing ( Su et al., 2026b ) , Hallo-Live ( Li et al., 2026a ) , and Omni-LiveAvatar ( Zhu et al., 2026b ) .
TurboT2VA ( Yang et al., 2026 ) extends consistency distillation to joint video-audio generation with modality-specific normalization and a progressive curriculum that introduces distribution matching after consistency training.
This connects few-step distillation to audiovisual synchronization, although its joint-generation setting differs from the causal replay setting studied here.

TurboDiffusion accelerates video diffusion ( Zhang et al., 2025h ) , and TurboServe addresses efficient streaming service ( Jiang et al., 2026 ) .
Pyramidal Flow Matching studies efficient video generation across scales ( Jin et al., 2025 ) ; the present experiments account separately for the low-resolution backbone and one-step high-resolution spatial Refiner. Efficient attention methods can be organized into hardware-efficient, sparse, compact, and linear approaches ( Zhang et al., 2025b ) .
Sparse VideoGen ( Xi et al., 2025 ) exploits spatial and temporal attention-head patterns, while Sparse VideoGen2 ( Yang et al., 2025b ) uses semantic-aware token permutation to identify and group important attention blocks.
SVG-EAR ( Zhou et al., 2026 ) complements sparse computation with parameter-free centroid compensation and error-aware routing for skipped blocks. S2’s attention implementation draws on several families of efficient operators.
SageAttention and SageAttention2 use low-bit attention ( Zhang et al., 2025e , Zhang et al., 2025a ) ; SageAttention2++ improves its implementation ( Zhang et al., 2025g ) , and SageAttention3 explores FP4 inference and low-bit training ( Zhang et al., 2025d ) .
SageBwd further studies trainable low-bit attention ( Zhang et al., 2026b ) .
SpargeAttention studies sparse attention without additional training ( Zhang et al., 2025f ) , while SpargeAttention2 uses trainable masking and distillation ( Zhang et al., 2026e ) .
SLA and SLA2 combine sparse and linear attention. SLA2 incorporates learnable routing together with quantization-aware training ( Zhang et al., 2025c , Zhang et al., 2026f ) . At the linear-layer level, 6Bit-Diffusion ( Su et al., 2026a ) dynamically allocates NVFP4 and INT8 precision according to quantization sensitivity and reuses temporally stable block residuals.
Speculative Decoding for Autoregressive Video Generation ( Hu and Zhang, 2026 ) uses a smaller model to draft video blocks and a quality router to decide which blocks require regeneration by the larger target model.
Both affect inference computation, a separate consideration from the number of networks maintained during distillation. S2 also draws on block-level low-bit computation ( Zhang et al., 2025i ) and Ulysses-style parallelism ( Jacobs et al., 2023 ) .

Appendix C Training Data We reuse the complete S2-Avatar training data and preparation pipeline, including filtering and annotation ( Zhang et al., 2026c ) .
The data include livestream and talking-head videos, film and television, solo dance, and 2D/3D animation, covering facial expressions, body motion, and animated characters. Full-clip and event-aligned chunk captions describe actions and outcomes chronologically, using object detection and speech recognition to ground the annotations.

D.1 StreamAV-Bench We evaluate all 320 StreamAV-Bench cases ( Liu et al., 2026f ) : 160 Progressive and 160 Interactive scenarios, using 180-second outputs.
The Interactive track introduces five runtime condition updates at 30-second intervals, at 30, 60, 90, 120, and 150 seconds.
We measure APEX and S2-Avatar. Other models’ VIF scores are from StreamAV-Bench ( Liu et al., 2026f ) , with other scores from Vidu S2 ( Zhang et al., 2026c ) . Visual Aesthetics (VA) uses the LAION Aesthetic Predictor ( LAION-AI, 2022 ) .
Visual Quality (VQ), Video Instruction Fulfillment (VIF), Audio Quality (AQ), and Audio Instruction Fulfillment (AIF) use the benchmark’s Gemini judge.
Subject Consistency (SC) and Background Consistency (BC) measure full-sequence stability.
Audio-Visual Alignment (AVAlign) uses ImageBind similarity ( Girdhar et al., 2023 ) to assess cross-modal semantic agreement.
Audio-Visual Synchronization (AVSync) uses Synchformer ( Iashin et al., 2024 ) to measure temporal offset error in seconds.
Production Quality (PQ) assesses the output audio with AudioBox Aesthetics ( Tjandra et al., 2025 ) .
All benchmark metrics except AVSync are higher-is-better. VA, PQ, AVAlign, and AVSync are evaluated over six non-overlapping 30-second windows per video, whereas SC and BC use the full 180-second sequence.
Track summaries average case-level scores with equal weight. Overall summaries pool all 320 cases. Since the tracks have equal size, this equals the average of the two unrounded track means.

D.2 Paired Human Comparisons Each comparison uses 70 paired 180-second I2V cases.
The five preference dimensions are consistency, instruction following, audiovisual synchronization, video quality, and motion quality.
Good/Same/Bad (GSB) counts wins, ties, and losses for the evaluated model relative to the reference, with G + S + B = 70 G+S+B=70 for each dimension.
Net preference is 100 ​ ( G − B ) / 70 100(G-B)/70 , retaining tied cases in the denominator. One net win therefore changes the reported preference by approximately 1.43 percentage points.
Mean Opinion Scores (MOS) assess instruction following and synchronization, with higher values indicating better quality.
A sequence is counted as usable when both scores exceed 2.
Synchronization MOS and the benchmark’s AVSync error therefore have opposite directions.
In the main study, consistency and video quality each have G/S/B counts of 13/47/10 (three net wins), whereas motion quality has 2/60/8 (six net losses), matching Figure 4 .
