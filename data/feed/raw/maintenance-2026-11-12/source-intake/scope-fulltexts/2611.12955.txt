Existing methods do not deliver all 3 at once, and the obstacle is structural, rather than logistical.
Meaningful temporal patterns cannot be defined without knowing segment boundaries, yet meaningful boundaries cannot be placed without knowing which patterns to expect.
Existing approaches break this circularity by using fixed windows or expert-annotated vocabularies, thereby sacrificing at least one of the 3 properties.
We introduce ConceptTime , which closes the loop through a single self-supervised signal.
A frozen probabilistic forecaster predicts a distribution over the next observation at every timestep of the sequence.
We call the mismatch between its prediction and the realized signal the predictive surprise .
This signal both locates segment boundaries and characterizes each resulting segment through the forecaster.
Segments are thus grounded by how they behaved relative to expectation, not by what they look like .
These summaries cluster into a vocabulary of dynamical regimes that we call concepts .
A lightweight head classifies from the concept sequence, and the distance to the nearest concept yields reliability by design.
Across 17 public benchmarks, ConceptTime is competitive with state-of-the-art black-box accuracy, beats every interpretable baseline, and retains near-98% accuracy on Epilepsy with 1% of labels.
It further remains competitive with softmax, prototype-distance, and SHAP/TimeX/TimeX++ on OOD detection and deletion faithfulness, without human annotations, language model calls, or domain experts.

Concepts grounded in deviation from prediction, therefore, distinguish dynamical regimes that shape-based vocabularies cannot. Time-series classifiers deployed in healthcare [ 24 , 32 ] , wearable sensing [ 22 ] , environmental monitoring [ 41 ] , and industrial telemetry [ 47 , 61 ] face 3 simultaneous demands:
(1) predictions must be accurate enough to compete with end-to-end deep models,
(2) they must be interpretable so that users can inspect which temporal patterns drove a decision,
and (3) they must support calibrated abstention, so that the system defers when its evidence is weak.
However, existing methods cannot satisfy all 3 requirements simultaneously (Figure 1 ). Black-box classifiers target higher downstream accuracy; post hoc explainable methods describe a model’s output without constraining how it was produced, while interpretable-by-design methods come closest, but they fail to address the cost of concept annotation, which is rare for sequential data and expensive to obtain at scale.
The deeper obstacle is structural and unique to sequential domains.
Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs) for time series face a chicken-and-egg problem:
meaningful temporal concepts cannot be defined without knowing where one segment ends and another begins,
yet meaningful segment boundaries cannot be placed without knowing what concepts to look for.
Every prior method sidesteps this circularity by decoupling the 2 stages.
They first segment with fixed windows or threshold-based change detection, then learn concepts from the resulting chunks.
Each gives up one of the 3 properties as a result.
(a) Fixed-window CBMs [ 62 , 57 , 16 ] achieve interpretability but require predefined vocabularies and lose calibrated abstention.
(b) Adaptive-patching methods [ 1 , 46 ] segment well but feed patches into standard encoder-classifier pipelines, exposing no concepts at all.
(c) Vector-quantized models [ 55 , 60 , 5 ] produce discrete tokens, but the tokens are latent codes without inherent semantic structure. The same self-supervised signal that locates segment boundaries can also describe what each resulting segment looks like.
A frozen probabilistic forecaster predicts a distribution over the next observation at each time step.
The mismatch between this prediction and the realized signal, which we call predictive surprise , is the natural place to draw a boundary, since high mismatch indicates a regime change.
The same forecaster also produces predictive statistics that describe how each segment behaved relative to expectation.
A single frozen model can therefore close the segmentation-concept loop, and no prior method exploits this dual use. We introduce ConceptTime , which operationalizes this opportunity.
An Autoregressive Gaussian Density Model (ARGDM) is trained once on raw multivariate signals via next-step density estimation.
At each time step, it predicts a Gaussian distribution over the next observation conditioned on the history.
The negative log-likelihood of the realized signal under that prediction is our predictive surprise .
This single quantity locates segment boundaries via length-constrained dynamic programming, placing exactly K K patches at the moments when the signal most strongly violates prior expectations.
The same model characterizes each resulting patch by its predictive statistics, yielding contextual fingerprints that describe how a segment behaves relative to expectations rather than what it looks like.
Patches that violate prior expectations in similar ways receive similar fingerprints, even when their raw waveforms differ visibly.
The path from raw signal to concept activation is fully deterministic.
Signatures are statistics of the frozen model; prototypes are cluster centroids in signature space; and patch-to-concept assignment is a nearest-prototype lookup.
A lightweight classifier is the only label-supervised component.
The same prototype distances yield a built-in fidelity score that asks whether the input is even describable in the concept space rather than whether the classifier is confident . Across 17 benchmarks spanning UEA, human-activity, biomedical, and fault-diagnosis tasks, ConceptTime remains competitive with black-box accuracy, beats every interpretable baseline, and retains 98% accuracy on Epilepsy at 1% labels. Its fidelity score is also competitive with softmax and prototype methods on OOD detection and SHAP [ 39 ] , TimeX [ 48 ] , and TimeX++ [ 34 ] on deletion faithfulness, all without human annotations, language-model calls, or domain experts. Further, we demonstrate ConceptTime ’s cross-domain transferability of the learned concept under a limited target label budget. Our main contributions can be summarized as follows: 1. To our knowledge, we introduce the first concept-bottleneck framework that resolves the segmentation-concept circularity through a single self-supervised primitive, eliminating the need for fixed windows, expert annotations, and language-model supervision. 2. We provide a fully non-parametric concept-grounding pipeline whose path from raw signal to concept activation is deterministic and inspectable, with all task-specific learning confined to a lightweight classifier on the concept tensor. 3. We identify prototype distance as a built-in fidelity signal and propose a prototype-distance abstention mechanism for time-series concept bottlenecks. 2 Related Work Concept bottleneck models. Concept bottleneck models (CBMs) route predictions through intermediate concepts that users can inspect and intervene on [ 28 ] .
The original formulation requires concept annotations. Later work reduces this burden through label-free concept discovery, including approaches that draw on vision-language models [ 45 , 66 , 53 , 50 ] . Other work studies robustness, counterfactual explanations, and concept locality [ 42 , 51 , 11 , 25 ] .
Time-series CBMs use predefined statistical summaries [ 62 ] , CKA-aligned concepts for forecasting [ 57 ] , or Signal Temporal Logic predicates [ 16 ] .
These approaches leave open how to discover both a temporal concept and its boundaries when its duration varies and its behavior is difficult to name in advance. Adaptive patching and discrete tokenization. The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) places boundaries at peaks in next-token entropy [ 46 ] . EntroPE adapts this idea to time series by thresholding categorical entropy [ 1 ] . Related work learns boundaries through reinforcement learning [ 64 ] or uses content-aware compression [ 4 ] .
A complementary line of work represents time series with discrete tokens, using learned codebooks in TOTEM and VQShape [ 55 , 60 ] or value quantization in Chronos [ 5 ] .
These methods address where to divide a sequence or how to encode it. A further question is whether the predictive signal that selects a boundary can also describe the behavior of the resulting segment. Prototype, shape-based, and post-hoc methods. Prototype and shapelet classifiers explain predictions through representative subsequences and local shape patterns [ 67 , 43 , 21 , 15 , 59 , 33 ] .
Shape alone can miss a distinction that matters for time series. A flat segment may be expected after a quiet interval and surprising after a spike, even when the 2 segments look identical.
Post-hoc explainers identify influential regions through saliency or perturbation [ 48 , 34 , 8 , 23 ] . Their explanations describe an existing classifier’s behavior, while a concept bottleneck requires the classifier to base its prediction on the concepts it exposes. Selective prediction and concept-level uncertainty. Common signals for selective prediction and shift detection include softmax confidence [ 18 ] , ensemble disagreement [ 29 ] , and distance-based OOD scores [ 30 , 54 , 36 , 37 ] .
In vision, concept-based models support abstention through uncertainty heads, calibration networks, or stochastic concept layers [ 26 , 35 , 58 , 27 , 52 ] .
For time series, a complementary question is whether the learned vocabulary itself can indicate how well it covers a new input. Positioning of ConceptTime . ConceptTime uses one frozen Gaussian density model for both segmentation and concept discovery. Predictive surprise guides patch boundaries, and each patch is summarized by how its observations compare with the model’s predictions.
Clustering these summaries yields concept prototypes without concept annotations or linguistic priors. Patches receive soft assignments based on prototype distance, which also supplies a fidelity score for abstention without an additional confidence model.
The UCI-HAR ablation illustrates why the choice of features matters. With the same adaptive patches and vocabulary size, predictive moments achieve 87.7% accuracy versus 42.7% for raw-signal morphology, a 45.0-point difference (Section 4.4 ). 3 ConceptTime Figure 2: Overview of ConceptTime . Each segment is summarized by a signature vector that combines predictive moments , residual morphology , and the surprise trajectory . Then, signatures are clustered into M M concept prototypes; textual labels are post hoc, not training signals. Stage 2: new inputs are segmented by ℳ θ \mathcal{M}_{\theta} , mapped to concepts by nearest-prototype assignment, and classified through the K × M K{\times}M concept tensor. Each segment’s distance to its nearest prototype yields the fidelity score used for abstention. ConceptTime factors classification into stages with disjoint training signals: a self-supervised density model produces every representation, clustering induces a non-parametric concept vocabulary, and a lightweight transformer head consumes labels. Each intermediate step is human-inspectable, and prototype distances provide a built-in fidelity score for selective prediction at no additional cost. The classifier learns a mapping from concept activations to labels rather than from raw signals, making it intrinsically more sample-efficient (Table 1 ). We further investigate more controlled, matched labeled and unlabeled training samples for the baselines in the few-label experiments, as discussed in Appendix C.4 . Predictive geometry, not adaptive patching alone, drives the gain. Three ablations isolate the source of accuracy.
(1) Replacing dynamic patches with equal-length windows ( ConceptTime -Static) loses 5.64 points on HAR and 2.92 on Epilepsy.
(2) Replacing DP segmentation with discrete categorical entropy (EntroPE) loses 4.5-8.5 points across benchmarks.
(3) Replacing predictive-geometry signatures with classical morphological descriptors on identical patches and clusters (catch22) reduces HAR by 30 points and UEA by 28 points.
The discriminative signal is the deviation from the prediction, not the raw waveform shape; adaptive patching is necessary but not sufficient. Cross-domain transfer. Patches are removed in ranked order; lower AUC indicates more faithful attribution. Fidelity and classifier confidence. Max-softmax measures how strongly the classifier favors its predicted class given the patch-to-concept assignments. These assignments depend only on relative distances: adding the same constant to all prototype distances for a patch leaves its assignment unchanged (Eq. 4 ). Fidelity uses the mean distance from each patch signature to its nearest prototype, so it can distinguish inputs with identical assignments but different distances to the learned concepts. Further examples appear in Appendix F . 5 Conclusion We introduced ConceptTime , a concept-bottleneck framework that resolves the segmentation-concept circularity for time series through a single self-supervised primitive, predictive surprise .
The same frozen model sets adaptive segment boundaries, grounds a non-parametric concept vocabulary, and provides prototype distances that also serve as a built-in fidelity score.
Across diverse classification and cross-domain transfer benchmarks, ConceptTime maintains competitive accuracy with black-box methods, dominates all interpretable baselines, including in few-shot settings. The by-design fidelity score remains competitive with softmax, prototype-distance, and post-hoc explainers on OOD detection and deletion faithfulness.
Interpretability, label efficiency, and reliable abstention emerge jointly when concept grounding is anchored in predictive surprise rather than human annotation. Limitations and Future Work. We fix the vocabulary size in advance, and the meaning of each learned prototype depends on human interpretation after training. Hierarchical vocabularies could capture patterns at multiple levels of detail, while online updates could help the vocabulary adapt as signal patterns change. Low-fidelity segments could also guide active relabeling by identifying examples for human review. Beyond classification, we plan to explore whether these concepts can help discover operating regimes, locate anomalies, and support decisions that users can inspect. Classical CBMs require dense concept annotations, motivating a line of work on label-free variants that source concepts from large pretrained models.
Label-free CBM [ 45 ] and Language-in-a-Bottle [ 66 ] use CLIP and LLMs to mine concept names; VLG-CBM [ 53 ] grounds concepts via open-vocabulary detectors; recent work eliminates the CLIP dependency through unsupervised classifier-distribution alignment [ 50 ] .
Other extensions improve concept robustness [ 42 , 51 ] , support counterfactual reasoning [ 11 ] , and add concept locality [ 25 ] . These approaches motivate learning a vocabulary directly from time-series recordings. In ConceptTime , a frozen density model supplies the features used to fit prototypes without concept annotations. Direct assignment to those prototypes avoids a separately trained concept predictor, one possible source of concept leakage [ 42 ] . Time-series concepts. Wu et al. [62] use predefined statistical summaries as concepts for clinical signals; van Sprang et al. [57] apply CKA-aligned concepts to forecasting transformers; Ferfoglia et al. [16] use Signal Temporal Logic formulae as interpretable predicates. These methods begin with a specified vocabulary. In time series, the duration and identity of a pattern can both be unknown. ConceptTime uses the same forecaster to locate segment boundaries and characterize the resulting segments. A.2 Segmentation and representation learning Adaptive patching. Patch-based transformers such as PatchTST have demonstrated the value of segmenting time series into patches.
The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) [ 46 ] extends this idea by placing boundaries at points of high next-token entropy in language modeling.
EntroPE [ 1 ] adapts the idea to time series, training a discrete autoregressive model and segmenting via a greedy dual-threshold rule on categorical entropy from quantized tokens. ReinPatch [ 64 ] learns patch boundaries through reinforcement learning, and TimeSqueeze [ 4 ] uses content-aware compression for forecasting. In ConceptTime , a continuous Gaussian forecaster provides predictive means, variances, and realized surprise. By default, a length-constrained dynamic program selects a fixed number of patches using surprise. The same forecaster supplies the statistics used to describe each patch. Discrete tokenization. TOTEM [ 55 ] learns a VQ-VAE codebook for time-series patches; VQShape [ 60 ] learns a shape-level codebook with subsequence reconstruction; Chronos [ 5 ] tokenizes scaled values into a 4096-symbol vocabulary for autoregressive forecasting. ConceptTime clusters signatures whose coordinates summarize expected behavior, residual morphology, and surprise within a patch. These features and nearby training patches give users two ways to inspect a prototype. Predictive representations. Our use of a probabilistic backbone for representation learning connects to predictive state representations, regime discovery, and information-bottleneck approaches for sequential data [ 40 , 7 , 56 ] .
Time-series foundation models such as MOMENT [ 20 ] and Chronos [ 5 ] demonstrate that self-supervised pretraining on raw signals yields useful general-purpose representations. In ConceptTime , predictive statistics connect self-supervised pretraining to the downstream classifier by defining both segments and the vocabulary used to encode them. A.3 Prototypes, explanations, and selective prediction Prototype and shapelet classifiers. A long line of work uses prototypes or shapelets as interpretable units for time-series classification [ 67 , 43 , 17 , 65 ] .
ProSeNet [ 43 ] learns a small library of prototype sequences with case-based reasoning.
Shapelet methods [ 67 , 21 ] discover discriminative subsequences and classify by distance.
ECATS [ 15 ] and related work [ 59 , 60 ] use symbolic predicates and abstracted shapes. UniShape [ 33 ] uses supervised pretraining with prototypes of multi-scale windowed shapes for transferable classification. ConceptTime retains the local, example-based interpretation of prototype methods and adds the preceding temporal context through the forecaster. Two patches with similar waveforms can therefore receive different concept assignments. Post-hoc explanations. A complementary line of work explains pretrained black-box classifiers post hoc through saliency, attribution, or perturbation [ 48 , 34 , 8 , 23 ] .
TimeX [ 48 ] and TimeX++ [ 34 ] apply information bottleneck principles to extract sub-instance explanations; ShapeX [ 23 ] aligns Shapley attribution with shapelet segments. These methods identify evidence used by a pretrained classifier. In ConceptTime , the classifier receives the sequence of concept assignments as its input. Selective prediction. Selective prediction has been studied through softmax confidence [ 18 ] , ensemble disagreement [ 29 ] , and distance-based OOD scoring [ 30 , 54 ] ; recent work explores prototype-based OOD detection with mixtures [ 36 ] , and distance to in-distribution centroids.
In vision, concept-based selective prediction has emerged through Conceptual Safeguards [ 26 ] and Sidecar-CBM [ 35 ] , which propagate concept-level uncertainty into the abstention decision, and through stochastic concept representations [ 58 , 27 ] and selective concept models [ 52 ] . Time-series research also studies generalization to out-of-distribution inputs [ 37 ] . Prototype distance in ConceptTime measures how closely segment signatures match the learned vocabulary. This supplies a score for abstention without fitting a separate confidence model. Comparison of approaches. Table 4 summarizes the methods discussed above. ConceptTime brings together adaptive segmentation, concept discovery without concept annotations, and a prototype-distance score for selective prediction. Appendix B Datasets and Training Setup B.1 Benchmarks and data splits Table 5 summarizes the main benchmark groups used in our experiments. • UCI HAR. The UCI Human Activity Recognition dataset contains smartphone inertial recordings from 30 subjects performing 6 daily activities: walking, walking upstairs, walking downstairs, sitting, standing, and lying down.
The original study used a waist-mounted Samsung Galaxy S2 to record three-axis acceleration and angular velocity.
Each sample is represented as a 9-channel window of length T = 128 T=128 , and the task is 6-way activity classification. • UCI Epilepsy. The Epileptic Seizure Recognition dataset consists of single-channel EEG recordings.
The original recordings span 23.6 seconds each.
The original dataset contains 5 classes, but only one class corresponds to epileptic seizure activity.
Following common practice, we merge the remaining 4 non-seizure classes into a single class and formulate the task as binary seizure detection.
Each classification example contains T = 178 T=178 time steps from an original recording. • Sleep-EDF. Sleep-EDF contains whole-night polysomnography recordings from the PhysioBank database.
The task is sleep-stage classification into 5 classes: Wake, N1, N2, N3, and REM.
Following prior work, we use the single EEG channel Fpz-Cz sampled at 100Hz.
Each input window has length T = 3000 T=3000 , corresponding to a 30-second EEG epoch. • UEA multivariate archive subset. We evaluate on a subset of 10 datasets from the UEA multivariate time-series classification archive.
These datasets cover diverse domains, including alcohol industry measurements, face detection, handwriting, heartbeat monitoring, speech, transportation, EEG self-regulation, spoken digit recognition, and gesture recognition.
They vary substantially in dimensionality, sequence length, and number of classes, making them useful for testing whether the learned concept representation generalizes beyond a single sensing modality. • Fault Diagnosis. The Fault Diagnosis dataset is a real-world bearing fault classification benchmark collected under 4 working conditions, denoted FD-A, FD-B, FD-C, and FD-D.
We treat each working condition as a separate domain because signal characteristics differ across operating regimes.
All domains share the same label space (3 classes): healthy, inner fault, and outer fault.
We use this dataset for cross-domain transfer experiments, where a model is trained on one operating condition and adapted or evaluated on another. E.3 Patch-level deletion faithfulness E.3.1 Deletion protocol and attribution baselines All rankings are evaluated on the same frozen ConceptTime classifier. We progressively replace the highest-ranked patch assignments and measure how quickly accuracy falls. Fidelity ranks patches by their prototype-distance score. GradientSHAP uses classifier gradients, with attributions aggregated over concepts within each patch. For TimeX and TimeX++, we train timestep-mask generators to explain the frozen classifier’s predictions and average each mask over the timesteps in each adaptive patch. Random and reversed fidelity rankings provide controls. The simplex-preserving comparison on Epilepsy evaluates fidelity, GradientSHAP, and random ordering. E.3.2 Deletion with valid concept assignments A zero vector has no probability mass, whereas each observed patch assignment sums to one. We therefore repeat the deletion experiment with 2 replacements that lie on the probability simplex: a uniform assignment and the mean training assignment at the same patch position (Algorithm 3 ). On Epilepsy, across 3 seeds, both fidelity and GradientSHAP give lower deletion AUC than random ordering under both replacements. With uniform replacement, fidelity gives the lowest AUC (0.7514), followed by GradientSHAP (0.7634) and random ordering (0.7879). Broader Impacts In healthcare and industrial monitoring, ConceptTime can help users inspect a prediction by tracing it to specific signal segments and comparing those segments with learned prototypes. A low fidelity score can flag recordings that the learned vocabulary describes poorly and help prioritize them for human review. A familiar signal pattern can still be misclassified, and a learned prototype may reflect a sensor artifact as readily as a physiological event or machine fault. Treating high fidelity as evidence of correctness could therefore create false reassurance. Before deployment, users should validate predictions and abstention thresholds on data from the intended population, sensors, and operating conditions, and retain human oversight for consequential decisions.
