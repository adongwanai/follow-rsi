On questions that remain unsolved by the base certainty-aware strategy even after 100 samples, frequency-aware remasking recovers solutions for 3.75% to 18.75% of examples. 1 Introduction Diffusion language models (DLMs) have emerged as a promising alternative to autoregressive language models for text generation. Rather than generating tokens strictly from left to right, DLMs produce sequences through iterative denoising, where masked tokens are gradually unmasked into a complete response. This direction builds on discrete and masked diffusion formulations for language ( Austin et al., 2021 ; Hoogeboom et al., 2021 ; Li et al., 2022 ; Sahoo et al., 2024 ) , and recent large-scale DLMs such as LLaDA and Dream perform competitively with similarly sized autoregressive models ( Nie et al., 2025 ; Ye et al., 2025 ) . A key design choice in DLM inference is the token update or remasking strategy. Existing methods typically use the model’s instantaneous predictive distribution to decide which tokens should be fixed and which should remain available for further processing ( Nie et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2026b ) . For example, LLaDA uses confidence-driven remasking, where high-confidence tokens are progressively unmasked while low-confidence tokens remain masked longer ( Nie et al., 2025 ) . DREAM-style implementations commonly use entropy-based remasking, where high-entropy positions are treated as uncertain and kept active for additional denoising ( Ben-Hamu et al., 2025 ; Gong et al., 2026 ) . Recent fast inference frameworks further accelerate DLM decoding through confidence-thresholding or confidence-aware parallel decoding, committing tokens once their confidence exceeds a predefined threshold ( Wu et al., 2026b ; Ma et al., 2025 ; Mohamed et al., 2025 ) . These approaches improve inference efficiency, but they share a common principle: local certainty under the current predictive distribution is used as a proxy for whether a token has been correctly resolved. The choice of decoding strategy has a substantial effect on DLM performance. For Dream-7B-Instruct under a fixed 256-step budget, switching the decoding heuristic from low-confidence to entropy changes MATH-500 accuracy from 21.80 % 21.80\% to 32.20 % 32.20\% ( Li et al., 2026a ) , and reported LLaDA-8B-Instruct baselines on MATH-500 range from roughly 26 % 26\% to 42 % 42\% depending on generation length, denoising steps, and decoding strategy ( Nie et al., 2025 ; Bai et al., 2026 ; Feng et al., 2026 ; Zhao et al., 2025 ) . DLM performance should therefore not be characterized by a single fixed decoding run. More importantly, these observations point to a structural property of DLM decoding: different strategies can reach similar aggregate accuracy while solving different subsets of questions. This complementary view motivates an adaptive view of DLM decoding. Rather than applying a fixed sampler uniformly to all inputs, the decoder should identify which questions a fast strategy solves and which need a different decoding behavior. We therefore study DLM test-time scaling as an adaptive allocation problem over both number of responses and decoding strategy . The remaining question is what that second decoding behavior should be. Within this framing, we identify false confidence as a limitation of certainty-aware decoding: tokens can appear locally confident while still belonging to an incorrect reasoning trajectory. On hard reasoning problems, these confident commitments quietly steer every sample toward the model’s preferred reasoning path, so repeated samples from the same certainty-aware decoder converge to the same wrong answer. Local confidence and entropy alone cannot diagnose this failure, because they are snapshots of a single denoising step. To address this limitation, we propose frequency-aware remasking, which augments certainty-aware remasking with a trajectory-level signal based on how often token predictions change across denoising steps and reverses the usual commitment order: frequently changing tokens are committed first, so that different samples make different choices at the positions where the model is still undecided and go on to explore different solution paths. We incorporate this signal into a two-stage adaptive method: a fast base sampler first resolves easy questions, and frequency-aware remasking is then applied only to the remaining unresolved questions. We evaluate our approach on mathematical reasoning and code generation benchmarks, examining both standard test-time scaling behavior and the recovery of challenging questions left unresolved by certainty-based decoding. The results show that adaptive allocation and frequency-aware remasking improve the efficiency–accuracy tradeoff by combining the speed of certainty-based decoding with the robustness of frequency-aware remasking. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We formulate DLM test-time scaling as an adaptive allocation problem over the number of responses and decoding strategies , and propose a two-stage algorithm that switches decoding strategy for the questions the fast base sampler fails to solve. • We identify false confidence as a motivation for trajectory-level decoding signals, and introduce frequency-aware remasking to complement instantaneous confidence- and entropy-based token update rules. • We show that frequency-aware remasking recovers solutions for up to 18.75 % 18.75\% of challenging reasoning questions, while also yielding up to 7.33 % 7.33\% absolute accuracy gains on MATH-500 and 11.39 % 11.39\% coverage gains on HumanEval. 2 Related Work 2.1 Test-Time Scaling for Language Models Test-time compute scaling has proven effective for autoregressive models via best-of- N N sampling, beam search, chain-of-thought, tree search, and adaptive allocation ( Brown et al., 2024 ; Snell et al., 2025 ; Wei et al., 2023 ; Yao et al., 2023 ; Levi, 2025 ; Liu et al., 2025 ; Yu et al., 2026a ) . Adapting these ideas to diffusion language models is less straightforward, as the natural scaling axis—denoising steps—is often already saturated. Wang et al. (2025a) establish a formal connection between remasking and inference-time scaling, showing that increasing sampling steps monotonically improves quality. Subsequent work scales along complementary axes: Prism ( Bai et al., 2026 ) combines hierarchical search with self-verification; Bilal et al. (2026) searches over denoising trajectories; RFG ( Chen et al., 2026 ) provides reward-free guidance via log-likelihood ratios. These search-based methods scale compute along the width axis. For AR models, adaptive allocation—deciding where to spend compute rather than how many samples to draw—is a complementary and important scaling dimension ( Wang et al., 2025b ; Zuo and Zhu, 2026 ; Damani et al., 2025 ; Fu et al., 2025 ; Zuo et al., 2026 ) . In the DLM setting, this dimension remains underexplored. Liang et al. (2026) identify rigid unmasking schedules as a key limitation, and propose adaptive NFE allocation through a redesigned training objective. Cholakov et al. (2026) address the same problem at the scheduling level, learning via RL which token positions to denoise at each step. Our work targets this allocation problem from a complementary angle: rather than changing the training objective or learning a token-level denoising schedule, we adaptively allocate questions across decoding strategies at inference time. 2.2 Remasking Strategy and Efficient Sampling in Diffusion Language Models A central design choice in DLM inference is the token update rule: which tokens should be fixed, and which should remain available for refinement. Standard masked diffusion decoders often treat unmasking as effectively irreversible: once a token is accepted, later steps focus on the remaining masked positions. This improves efficiency but can also lock in incorrect tokens. Recent work therefore studies remasking and refinement mechanisms that allow generated tokens to be reconsidered. Wang et al. (2025a) formulate remasking as inference-time scaling for masked diffusion models. Tian et al. (2025) propose a two-stage fill-then-refine
procedure, while Huang et al. (2026) train the model to self-reflectively remask incorrect tokens using predicted confidence scores. Zhai et al. (2026) target context-brittle tokens and argue that confidence alone is a poor proxy for correctness. Another line of work improves DLM sampling efficiency by reducing denoising steps, accelerating token updates, or designing better schedules. Fast-dLLM and dInfer accelerate decoding through confidence-based token selection and thresholding ( Wu et al., 2026b ; Wu et al., 2026a ; Ma et al., 2025 ) . Recent information-gain-based sampling methods further suggest that token selection should account for the downstream effect of an update on future denoising uncertainty; we discuss this and other contemporary work in Section A.1 . Our work shares some of the motivation of these remasking and efficient sampling methods, but differs in both signal and allocation target. Rather than applying remasking uniformly or learning a new schedule, we use a trajectory-level frequency signal and apply frequency-aware remasking only to challenging questions. 3 Problem Setting We frame test-time scaling as an allocation problem over both compute and token update strategies. To design such an adaptive algorithm, we first formalize test-time scaling for DLMs in Section 3.1 . We then examine the principle shared by certainty-aware token update strategies and identify a failure mode that motivates allocating additional computation to hard questions in Section 3.2 . 3.1 Test-Time Scaling for Diffusion Language Models Let 𝒱 \mathcal{V} denote the token vocabulary and let [M] be a special mask token. A diffusion language model generates a response y ∈ 𝒴 y\in\mathcal{Y} for a question x ∈ 𝒳 x\in\mathcal{X} through iterative denoising. Starting from a partially or fully masked sequence, the model repeatedly predicts token distributions for masked positions conditioned on the current sequence state. Different token update rules induce different tradeoffs between inference cost and generation quality. Confidence-thresholding strategies ( Wu et al., 2026b ; Ma et al., 2025 ) commit tokens once their confidence exceeds a predefined threshold. Intuitively, at each denoising step, the decoder fixes tokens whose confidence is sufficiently high and leaves the remaining tokens available for later processing. In contrast, uncertainty-based remasking strategies, such as low-confidence or high-entropy remasking for LLaDA and Dream , keep uncertain positions active for further processing. These strategies can improve robustness on some hard questions, but they are often significantly slower than confidence-thresholding. This creates a test-time scaling problem for diffusion decoding. Since the best decoding behavior is not known in advance, the problem is not to choose a single globally best strategy, but to adaptively decide when to spend additional compute and when to switch decoding behavior . 3.2 Certainty-Aware Token Updates Within this test-time scaling framework, we identify a limitation of widely used certainty-aware token update strategies. A common design principle across diffusion language models and related inference frameworks is to use the model’s current predictive distribution to decide which positions should be fixed and which should remain masked for further processing. However, local certainty need not imply global correctness: on hard reasoning problems, a token may appear confident because it is locally compatible with an incorrect partial trajectory. We refer to this failure mode as false confidence and analyze it in Section 4.1 . 4 Methods We now present our two-stage adaptive sampling method and the frequency-aware remasking rule used in its second stage. Such failures are difficult to address by simply drawing more samples from the same certainty-aware decoder. 4.2 Frequency-Aware Remasking We attribute the failure above to the commitment order: a certainty-aware sampler commits the positions it is most sure about first, and these early commitments steer every sample down the same path. Frequency-aware remasking reverses this order. It tracks how often each position’s proposal changes across denoising steps and commits the frequently changing positions first, so that different samples make different choices at the positions where the model is still undecided and branch into different reasoning paths, while the positions the model has already settled on are filled in afterwards. The sampler first follows a certainty-aware warm-up rule during a per-block warm-up phase to collect this statistic, then applies Support-Aware Frequency Selection (SAFE) to choose the high-frequency positions to commit. For each generated position i i , we record token proposals while that position remains masked. proposal, and let x ^ i , j \hat{x}_{i,j} denote the j j -th observed proposal. We initialize x ^ i , 0 = [ M ] \hat{x}_{i,0}=[M] and define the change frequency as f i ( t ) = ∑ j = 1 N i ( t ) [ x ^ i , j ≠ x ^ i , j − 1 ] max ⁡ ( N i ( t ) , 1 ) . f_{i}^{(t)}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N_{i}^{(t)}}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\hat{x}_{i,j}\neq\hat{x}_{i,j-1}\right]}{\max\!\left(N_{i}^{(t)},1\right)}. This statistic includes the initial comparison against the mask token. A high frequency indicates that a position’s proposal changes often, while a low frequency indicates that it remains stable. Frequency statistics are maintained independently for each generated response and accumulated across its denoising trajectory. They are not reset at block boundaries. After warm-up, the sampler must decide which masked positions to commit using the accumulated frequencies f i ( t ) f_{i}^{(t)} . A natural choice is to commit every position whose frequency exceeds a fixed threshold. We therefore introduce SAFE in Section 4.3 , which chooses the high-frequency group adaptively from the observed frequency levels. 4.3 Support-Aware Frequency Selection (SAFE) First, SAFE looks for a clear separation in the support of the frequency values: the high-frequency group is defined by the largest gap between adjacent raw frequency levels, subject to a size constraint. Second, it commits this group only when it is consistent with the group found at the preceding step in the same block. Let 𝒮 = { x 1 , … , x n } \mathcal{S}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\} denote the set of evaluation questions. For each question x ∈ 𝒮 x\in\mathcal{S} , we maintain a response set g ⁡ ( x ) g(x) and a selected response y ˇ ​ ( x ) \check{y}(x) . A correctness oracle O : 𝒳 × 𝒴 → { 0 , 1 } O:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\{0,1\} determines whether a generated response correctly solves the question. The first stage performs adaptive allocation ( Manvi et al., 2024 ; Zuo and Zhu, 2026 ) using a base DLM sampler π base \pi_{\mathrm{base}} , instantiated as confidence-thresholding in our experiments. At each round, the algorithm samples one new response for every active question. If the oracle finds a correct response for a question, that question is removed from the active set. This stage exploits the efficiency of the base sampler and avoids spending additional compute on questions that are already solved. The second stage applies the frequency-aware sampler π freq \pi_{\mathrm{freq}} only to the remaining active questions. These questions were not solved in the base stage, so the base sampler’s commitment order is unlikely to reach a different reasoning path. The frequency-aware sampler π freq \pi_{\mathrm{freq}} denotes a DLM sampler whose warm-up rule is an uncertainty-aware remasking rule (low-confidence for LLaDA , entropy-based for Dream ) and which then applies SAFE ( Section 4.3 ) in the remaining denoising steps of each block. Thus, frequency-aware remasking is reserved for unresolved questions, rather than being applied uniformly to all inputs. By default, the algorithm performs one inference per question per round, for a total of K K rounds. 5 Experiments We describe the experimental setup in Section 5.1 . We evaluate our approach on two representative diffusion language models: LLaDA-8B-Instruct and Dream-7B-Instruct . For brevity, we refer to them as LLaDA and Dream , respectively. These models allow us to study how different token update and remasking strategies affect iterative decoding under test-time scaling. We report two metrics: coverage@k and accuracy. Coverage@k measures whether at least one of the generated responses for a question is correct after k k rounds, as determined by a ground-truth checker. We report coverage rather than pass@ k k ( Chen et al., 2021 ) because our adaptive algorithm may allocate different numbers of inference calls to different questions, which violates the fixed- n n sampling assumption used by the unbiased pass@ k k estimator. For mathematical reasoning, we report oracle-measured accuracy: the fraction of questions for which the correctness oracle accepts a response within the allocated budget. We use Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite to judge each response independently. In Algorithm 1 , the first accepted response becomes the selected answer and sampling stops for that question; questions with no accepted response count as unsolved. Thus, when the same oracle is used, this accuracy equals oracle-measured coverage rather than the accuracy of a separate candidate-ranking procedure. axes—how many responses to sample and which decoding strategy to apply—departing from the standard view of scaling only sample count under a fixed decoder. Our two-stage algorithm instantiates this view: a fast base stage resolves most questions cheaply, and frequency-aware remasking is applied only to those that remain unresolved. This requires a second-stage decoder that fails differently from the base decoder on the questions the base decoder misses. We identify one source of such failures, false confidence , and address it with frequency-aware remasking, a trajectory-level signal based on how often token predictions change across denoising steps. Committing the frequently changing positions first makes different samples branch at the positions where the model is still undecided instead of collapsing onto its preferred reasoning path, which is why the second stage recovers questions that further samples from the base decoder do not. Adapting both where compute is spent and how decoding is performed improves performance on difficult instances while
preserving efficiency. The framework is training-free but introduces hyperparameters such as the warm-up ratio ρ \rho . In addition, our signal tracks only changes in token identity, so it is most effective when the difficulty stems specifically from false confidence.
The two-stage formulation is agnostic to the second-stage decoder, leaving richer signals and more adaptive commitment rules to future work. References Austin et al. (2021) Jacob Austin, Daniel D. Appendix A Extended Related Work A.1 Additional Contemporary Work on DLM Sampling and Remasking We also discuss several recent works that examine the limitations of local-certainty-based decoding in diffusion language models. Fang et al. (2026) identify a quality–exploration dilemma: low-confidence remasking improves single-sample quality by prioritizing confident tokens, but can suppress exploration and limit multi-sample gains. Yang et al. (2026) argue that greedy local-certainty heuristics ignore how current token choices affect future masked positions, and propose an information-gain sampler that balances immediate uncertainty with downstream uncertainty reduction. Olausson et al. (2026) study diversity in DLM sampling and introduce tempered variants of confidence-based remasking heuristics to improve pass@ k k behavior while retaining efficient sampling. Yu et al. (2026b) instead improve pass@ k k and answer diversity of DLMs at training time through risk-sensitive GRPO. These works are complementary to ours. They primarily design alternative token-level sampling rules to improve exploration, diversity, or future uncertainty reduction. In contrast, our work studies adaptive allocation across questions and decoding strategies: a fast base sampler is first used to resolve easy questions, and frequency-aware remasking is applied only to unresolved questions. At the token level, our method also uses a different signal—the observed frequency of token prediction changes across denoising steps—rather than only instantaneous confidence, entropy, or estimated information gain. Test-time improvement without additional supervision has also been studied beyond sampling, through self-training at test time ( Zhu et al., 2026 ) and active evaluation under a labeling budget ( Liu et al., 2026 ) . Appendix B Additional false confidence analysis To support our claim of false confidence, we conduct a diagnostic experiment that tests whether locally confident and stable tokens are always reliable candidates for commitment. Our hypothesis is that, on hard reasoning problems, a token can receive high confidence under the current predictive distribution while still belonging to an incorrect reasoning trajectory. For each active token position i i , let conf i \mathrm{conf}_{i} denote the model confidence and let f i f_{i} denote the normalized change frequency of the predicted token across denoising steps. We consider the following confidence-frequency transfer score: s i trans = ( 1 − β ) ​ conf i − β ​ f i , s_{i}^{\mathrm{trans}}=(1-\beta)\,\mathrm{conf}_{i}-\beta f_{i}, where β ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] \beta\in[0,1] controls the tradeoff between confidence and frequency. This score prioritizes tokens that are both high-confidence and low-frequency, i.e., tokens whose
predictions are locally confident and stable across the denoising trajectory. If local confidence and trajectory stability were reliable indicators of correctness, then using this score for token commitment should preserve or improve decoding quality. We evaluate this setting using LLaDA on a hard subset of MATH-500 containing 150 questions, with confidence-thresholding as the base strategy and β = 0.7 \beta=0.7 . This result suggests that stable high-confidence tokens are not always safe to commit: they may instead reflect locally coherent but globally incorrect reasoning paths. This supports our false-confidence claim and motivates our design choice of committing frequently changing tokens first during the frequency-aware phase, so that samples diverge at the undecided positions rather than collapsing onto the model’s preferred reasoning path. Scope. This analysis also clarifies the scope of our method. Frequency-aware remasking is designed to address cases where the model has a viable path to the correct solution but becomes trapped by misleading local certainty during decoding. In such cases, committing the undecided positions first lets different samples branch away from the model’s preferred reasoning path. Thus, our method should be understood as a mechanism for recovering from false-confidence failures, rather than a solution for questions that are intrinsically unsolvable for the underlying model. The warm-up length is computed from the prescribed per-block denoising-step budget using the rounding and clipping rule in Section 4.2 . Frequency statistics are collected online and maintained independently for each generated response. For every masked generated position, we accumulate the number of observed proposals and the number of changes relative to the preceding proposal. The previous proposal is initialized to the mask token, so the initial mask-to-proposal comparison is included. The cumulative change frequency is the ratio of the accumulated change count to the accumulated observation count. Frequency statistics are initialized at the start of each response and retained across block boundaries. Masked positions in future blocks also accumulate observations, although they are not yet eligible for commitment. Once a position is committed, its statistics stop updating. At the beginning of each block, the sampler follows the warm-up rule while continuing to update the frequency statistics. After warm-up, SAFE is evaluated at every nonterminal step. It selects a high-frequency candidate from the largest admissible gap between the observed raw frequency levels and commits the candidate only when it is consistent with the preceding candidate in the same block. Otherwise, it follows the warm-up rule. Candidate history is reset at each block boundary, while frequency statistics remain cumulative across the response. If the warm-up rule selects no position, one position is committed according to its ranking to ensure progress. On the final scheduled step, all remaining positions in the block are committed.
