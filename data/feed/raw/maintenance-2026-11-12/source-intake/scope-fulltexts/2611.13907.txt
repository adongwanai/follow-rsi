The code, model, and datasets are available at EVAThink . 1 Introduction Vision-language models (VLMs) have made substantial progress in video understanding, including video captioning, temporal grounding, and video question answering [ 30 , 40 , 52 , 23 , 37 , 38 ] .
In practice, these models often operate under a limited visual budget, accessing a sampled sequence of frames rather than the complete video.
Their answers therefore depend on the question-relevant information preserved in those frames.
We refer to this information as frame evidence , which includes visual cues within individual frames and temporal relationships across frames.
With a fixed budget of uniformly sampled frames, some sampled sequences provide sufficient evidence for a reliable direct answer, while others offer only sparse coverage of question-relevant content, particularly when that content occupies a small fraction of the video.
We term the extent to which frame evidence supports reliable direct answering by the model evidence sufficiency , an important consideration when allocating reasoning computation. Figure 1 : Motivation for evidence sufficiency-guided training for adaptive thinking.
For the same question and frame budget, sufficient frame evidence supports direct answering, while thinking can help make better use of limited frame evidence. Explicit reasoning can help models interpret frame evidence by connecting observations across frames.
However, when the evidence already supports a reliable direct answer, additional reasoning may yield little benefit while increasing generation cost and introducing unsupported inferences.
When useful cues are present but require further analysis, reasoning may instead improve the answer.
This motivates using evidence sufficiency to guide adaptive-thinking training, encouraging direct answering when frame evidence is sufficient and reasoning when a direct answer is less reliable but further analysis may help. Recent work on adaptive reasoning for video understanding selects response modes using initial-answer confidence [ 25 ] or objectives that balance answer quality, including correctness and grounding, against generation cost [ 31 , 19 ] .
These approaches provide useful signals for mode selection given a visual input, but supervision across different evidence conditions remains less explored. Such supervision could use observed differences in direct-answer accuracy and reasoning gains to guide adaptive-thinking training. Learning such a response-mode policy also poses challenges in training stability.
Prior studies report that adaptive reasoning training can prematurely favor one mode, limiting exploration of the other and potentially leading to mode collapse, in which the model consistently answers directly or always reasons [ 7 , 32 , 25 ] .
Existing methods can also require substantial training data, e.g., 83K samples for VideoAuto-R1 [ 25 ] and 140K for Video-FLAIR [ 19 ] .
Some further rely on a supervised fine-tuning (SFT) cold start, adding the burden of preparing and annotating response demonstrations [ 19 , 31 ] . To address these challenges, we introduce EVAThink , which learns adaptive thinking from differences in frame evidence sufficiency.
During data curation, our Recoverable Evidence Gap Mining constructs evidence-contrastive pairs of dense and sparse video views that share the same question and answer under a fixed frame budget.
We retain pairs with higher direct-answer accuracy on the dense view, lower direct-answer accuracy on the sparse view, and a sufficient accuracy gain from explicit reasoning on the sparse view.
These pairs capture a recoverable evidence gap, linking differences in frame evidence sufficiency to the benefit of reasoning.
Building on these pairs, we develop Evidence-Guided Two-Stage Reinforcement Learning , which requires no SFT cold start.
Using stage-specific rewards, the first stage guides response-mode training with evidence-based mode preferences from high-confidence pairs while rewarding answer correctness; the second removes view-dependent mode preferences and adds a small bonus for correct direct answers to improve efficiency.
First-token advantage regularization mitigates mode collapse. With only 6.7K curated samples, EVAThink consistently improves over the base model across six video understanding benchmarks and outperforms a range of strong baselines overall.
Its adaptive thinking policy outperforms direct-only and think-only mode by 2.3 and 3.6 percentage points, respectively, while reducing average output length by approximately 62% compared with think-only inference.
Our main contributions are as follows: • We propose Recoverable Evidence Gap Mining to construct evidence-contrastive pairs comprising evidence-dense and evidence-sparse views of the same question-answer pair. These pairs link a decline in direct-answer accuracy on the sparse view to a gain from explicit reasoning, providing targeted supervision for evidence-guided adaptive thinking. • We develop Evidence-Guided Two-Stage Reinforcement Learning with stage-specific rewards that first guide training with evidence-based mode preferences and then refine response efficiency. The framework incorporates first-token advantage regularization to mitigate mode collapse. • Using only 6.7K samples for training, EVAThink consistently improves over the base model across video understanding benchmarks and outperforms strong baselines overall under controlled visual budgets. Analysis shows that adaptive thinking achieves higher accuracy than both direct-only and think-only modes while substantially reducing output length relative to think-only mode. 2 Related Work 2.1 Video Understanding Vision-language models (VLMs) have advanced video understanding through instruction tuning and temporal modeling [ 30 , 40 , 52 , 50 , 2 , 1 , 48 ] .
Building on these capabilities, recent studies explore explicit reasoning and reinforcement learning (RL) for video tasks.
Video-R1 [ 8 ] introduces temporal-aware rewards, while VideoRFT [ 34 ] combines reasoning supervision with RL.
Temporal-RLT [ 20 ] and Video-RTS [ 35 ] investigate data-efficient RL, while LongVILA-R1 [ 3 ] extends RL to long videos.
VITAL [ 46 ] , LOVE-R1 [ 11 ] , and VideoChat-R1.5 [ 43 ] incorporate tool use or iterative perception into reasoning.
Complementary approaches enhance video understanding through dedicated frame-selection modules [ 14 , 13 ] , explicit evidence-grounding mechanisms [ 16 ] , or agentic pipelines to guide further observation and retrieval [ 36 , 42 ] .
These designs incur additional inference-time overhead for selection, grounding, or retrieval, while leaving native adaptive thinking less explored.
EVAThink uses RL with paired evidence conditions to train the VLM itself to choose when to reason, without external tools or additional retrieval at inference. 2.2 Adaptive Thinking Adaptive thinking adjusts reasoning computation to balance answer quality and efficiency.
For language models, existing approaches include response-mode selection [ 7 , 47 ] , multi-stage reinforcement learning [ 32 ] , accuracy-aware length rewards [ 49 ] , and confidence-guided reflection [ 33 ] .
For video understanding, VideoAuto-R1 [ 25 ] uses initial-answer confidence to determine whether further reasoning is needed.
AdaThinkV [ 31 ] estimates the utility of explicit reasoning by balancing accuracy gains against generation cost, while Video-FLAIR [ 19 ] compares reasoning modes based on correctness, grounding, and cost.
While these methods adapt reasoning to individual inputs, explicit supervision across paired evidence conditions for the same question remains less explored.
EVAThink constructs paired video views that share the same question and answer but differ in the availability of question-relevant evidence under a fixed frame budget.
It uses differences in direct-answer accuracy and reasoning gains across these views to guide adaptive-thinking training. Figure 2 : Overview of EVAThink. Recoverable Evidence Gap Mining constructs the evidence-contrastive pairs through video cropping or concatenation, retaining pairs with a direct-answer accuracy gap and a reasoning gain on the sparse view. Evidence-Guided Two-Stage Reinforcement Learning first trains response-mode selection using preferences derived from evidence-contrastive pairs, then refines response efficiency on the remaining pairs, using stage-specific rewards and first-token advantage regularization. 3 EVAThink We propose EVAThink, which enhances video understanding through evidence sufficiency-guided training for adaptive thinking. As illustrated in Fig. 2 , EVAThink comprises data curation and policy training, where Recoverable Evidence Gap Mining constructs evidence-contrastive pairs for Evidence-Guided Two-Stage Reinforcement Learning. We first construct a candidate pool of 118.2K multiple-choice video-question samples from eight existing datasets, covering diverse video understanding tasks. As shown in Fig. 3 , we organize the pool into three groups based on their primary focus: general video understanding (55.3K, 46.8%), including LLaVA-Video-178K [ 50 ] , Molmo2-VideoCapQA [ 6 ] , and VideoMarathon [ 24 ] ; reasoning-oriented video understanding (49.1K, 41.5%), including LongVILA [ 4 ] , SR-91K [ 27 ] , and Holmes-train [ 5 ] ; and perception-oriented video understanding (13.8K, 11.7%), including NeXT-QA [ 41 ] and PerceptionTest [ 28 ] . This pool provides the candidate samples for subsequent Recoverable Evidence Gap Mining.
See Appendix A for more details. 3.1 Recoverable Evidence Gap Mining Here, we construct evidence-contrastive pairs comprising an evidence-dense view and an evidence-sparse view that share the same question and answer. The two views differ in the fraction of video duration occupied by the evidence segment relevant to the question: this fraction is larger in the dense view and smaller in the sparse view. Under a fixed budget of uniformly sampled frames, the dense view is therefore expected to provide greater coverage of question-relevant evidence.
We retain pairs for which direct-answer accuracy decreases from the dense to the sparse view, while explicit reasoning improves accuracy on the sparse view.
These pairs provide supervision for adaptive-thinking training across different evidence conditions. Starting from the candidate data pool, we construct such pairs in three steps (see Appendix B for more details). 1) Candidate pair construction. Throughout data curation, the target model receives 32 frames uniformly sampled from each video.
For each evaluated view and response mode, we estimate answer accuracy using eight rollouts.
We first estimate direct-answer accuracy, A dir A_{\mathrm{dir}} , using eight rollouts per sample. We discard samples answered correctly in all eight rollouts, as they are likely too easy to provide useful signals for subsequent reinforcement learning (RL).
For samples with A dir < 0.5 A_{\mathrm{dir}}<0.5 , we use the original video as the sparse view and construct the dense view by cropping around an evidence segment annotated by a stronger model given the video, question, and answer.
We extend the crop to include 2–3 sampled frames before and after the annotated segment, using their timestamps in the original video’s uniformly sampled 32-frame sequence.
For the remaining samples, with 0.5 ≤ A dir < 1 0.5\leq A_{\mathrm{dir}}<1 , we use the original video as the dense view and construct the sparse view by concatenating another video from the same data source, placing it randomly before or after the original video.
For questions involving temporal intervals, we restrict cropping and concatenation to the portion after the annotated segment to preserve the original timestamps. 2) Evidence-gap filtering. For each candidate pair, we evaluate the newly constructed view using eight direct-answer rollouts and retain pairs satisfying Δ evi = A dir dense − A dir sparse > 0.125 , \Delta_{\mathrm{evi}}=A_{\mathrm{dir}}^{\mathrm{dense}}-A_{\mathrm{dir}}^{\mathrm{sparse}}>0.125, (1) where A dir dense A_{\mathrm{dir}}^{\mathrm{dense}} and A dir sparse A_{\mathrm{dir}}^{\mathrm{sparse}} denote the direct-answer accuracies on the dense and sparse views, respectively.
We use this accuracy gap as an empirical proxy for the difference in how reliably the two views support direct answering. 3) Recoverability filtering. For each pair retained after evidence-gap filtering, we prompt the model to use explicit reasoning on the sparse view and retain pairs satisfying Δ think = A think sparse − A dir sparse > 0.125 , \Delta_{\mathrm{think}}=A_{\mathrm{think}}^{\mathrm{sparse}}-A_{\mathrm{dir}}^{\mathrm{sparse}}>0.125, (2) where A think sparse A_{\mathrm{think}}^{\mathrm{sparse}} denotes the reasoning accuracy on the sparse view.
These pairs exhibit a recoverable evidence gap: direct answering is less accurate on the sparse view than on the dense view, but explicit reasoning improves accuracy on the sparse view using the same sampled frames.
Here, recoverability refers to improved answer accuracy, not the recovery of unobserved visual information.
The resulting evidence-contrastive pairs are used for subsequent training. 3.2 Evidence-Guided Two-Stage Reinforcement Learning To explicitly anchor the two response modes, we specify distinct response formats for two response modes during training.
Specifically, we append a training prompt to each question that instructs the model to answer directly when the visual evidence clearly supports an answer, and to use explicit reasoning when further analysis is needed.
The prompt requires each response to follow one of two formats, with distinct openings identifying the selected mode: Direct answer: <answer>[Answer]</answer> Think: Alright,[Reasoning] <answer>[Answer]</answer> This prompt elicits both response modes during initial rollouts, enabling RL training without an SFT cold start, avoiding the need to curate supervised response demonstrations.
The distinct openings also support first-token advantage regularization, as described below.
The full training prompt is provided in Table 11 in the Appendix B.4 . Building on the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) [ 29 , 12 ] , we develop a two-stage RL framework that first guides training with evidence-based mode preferences and then refines response efficiency.
Our training incorporates two key designs:
(i) stage-specific rewards that guide mode selection in Stage 1 and refine response-efficiency in Stage 2;
(ii) a first-token advantage regularization mechanism to mitigate entropy collapse during training and discourage premature concentration on a single response mode;
To maintain balanced exposure to paired evidence conditions, we additionally use paired sampling, including the dense and sparse views of each selected pair in the same training batch.
Besides, following DAPO [ 45 ] , we omit the KL-divergence penalty and adopt token-level mean loss aggregation throughout both training stages. In both training stages, we uniformly sample 32 frames from each video, consistent with data curation.

After the Recoverable Evidence Gap Mining, we obtain 1.76K high-confidence contrastive pairs (i.e., 3.5K samples) and 4.17K remaining contrastive pairs (i.e., 8.3K samples).
EVAThink is initialized from Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct and trained on 32 Ascend 910B NPUs with a batch size of 32.
Stage 1 uses the high-confidence pairs for 110 steps, corresponding to approximately one epoch. Stage 2 continues training on the remaining pairs for 100 steps, using 3.2K samples. In total, the two stages use 6.7K training samples (See Appendix C for more details). Baselines. We compare EVAThink with the Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct [ 2 ] and Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct [ 1 ] , as well as representative video reasoning methods: Temporal-RLT [ 20 ] , Video-RTS [ 35 ] , VITAL [ 46 ] , LongVILA-R1 [ 3 ] , LOVR-R1 [ 11 ] , VideoChat-R1.5 [ 43 ] , Video-R1 [ 8 ] , VideoRFT [ 34 ] , VideoAuto-R1 [ 25 ] , AdaThinkV [ 31 ] , and Video-FLAIR [ 19 ] .
We distinguish results reported under the respective original evaluation protocols from those obtained under a controlled visual budget.
For the latter, we evaluate available baseline checkpoints using uniformly sampled 32 or 64 frames, with a fixed per-frame pixel budget of 256 × 28 × 28 256\times 28\times 28 . VideoAuto-R1 supports adaptive thinking and serves as a strong baseline evaluated under these same visual budgets.
For adaptive-thinking methods AdaThinkV and Video-FLAIR, we report published results under their original evaluation protocols because their checkpoints are unavailable.
Notably, AdaThinkV allows up to 256 frames, compared with our fixed budgets of 32 and 64.
In addition, we compare different response modes and ablate individual components to evaluate adaptive thinking and component contributions.
During evaluation, we remove EVAThink’s training-specific prompt. Benchmarks. We evaluate video understanding on six benchmarks: Video-MME [ 10 ] , MVBench [ 21 ] , LongVideoBench [ 39 ] , MMVU (multiple-choice split) [ 51 ] , Video-MMMU [ 15 ] , and VSI-Bench [ 44 ] . These benchmarks cover general and long-video understanding, as well as video perception and reasoning. We report accuracy on each benchmark and the unweighted average across all six, computing the average only when all results are available.
To assess zero-shot transfer to image understanding, we further evaluate on MMBench (MMBench-EN and MMBench-CN) [ 26 ] , MME (MME-P and MME-C) [ 9 ] , AI2D [ 18 ] , and POPE (accuracy) [ 22 ] without additional image-task training. Under both fixed-budget settings, EVAThink achieves the highest average accuracy among the evaluated baselines with results on all six benchmarks. With 32 frames, EVAThink reaches 63.5%, outperforming Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct and VideoAuto-R1-Qwen3 by 0.6 and 1.7 percentage points, respectively. With 64 frames, its average accuracy increases to 65.6%, extending these margins to 1.4 and 2.6 percentage points. These improvements are achieved using only 6.7K training samples, compared with 83K for VideoAuto-R1, demonstrating effective training with a small trainset.
Beyond the average gains, EVAThink consistently improves upon base model Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct on every benchmark under both frame budgets, demonstrating benefits across video understanding tasks. Increasing the frame budget from 32 to 64 also reveals a consistent shift in response-mode selection.
Increasing the frame budget from 32 to 64 improves EVAThink’s accuracy on all six benchmarks while reducing its thinking ratio on all five benchmarks where explicit reasoning is used.
This pattern is consistent with the motivation for evidence sufficiency-guided training: a larger frame budget may provide more question-relevant evidence and make direct answering more effective.
Meanwhile, the thinking ratio remains highest on Video-MMMU, consistent with its demands for concept understanding and knowledge application.
Conversely, the lowest thinking ratio on VSI-Bench suggests a preference for direct spatial judgments, aligning with prior findings [ 44 , 17 , 19 ] that explicit verbal reasoning can hinder visual-spatial performance. 4.3 Extended Results Effect of adaptive thinking. We use a stronger model to annotate evidence segments (the prompt is shown in the Table 9 ). Given 32 uniformly sampled frames in chronological order, together with the question, candidate options, and ground-truth answer, the annotator identifies the minimal continuous frame interval containing the supporting evidence. When the required clues occur at separate moments, the interval covers all of them. Annotations use inclusive frame indices and include a localization status and confidence level.
Ambiguous or absent evidence is explicitly marked as partial or not_localized , respectively, and the corresponding samples are discarded. Annotation accuracy. The annotations serve as candidate evidence regions for mining, so precise temporal boundaries are not required. Sample acceptance is subsequently determined by two filtering stages based on rollout accuracy, which verify whether each candidate exhibits the behavior required by our mining criteria. These checks reduce sensitivity to localization errors: an annotation alone is insufficient for a sample to be retained. Thus, the retained samples satisfy our operational criteria for recoverable evidence gaps, without requiring frame-exact annotations. Annotation cost. Although annotation uses a stronger model, it is applied only to a small subset of the original data pool. After removing samples for which all preceding rollouts are correct, only the remaining samples identified as sparse-view cases require evidence annotation. Empirically, these account for less than 22.7% of the original pool. Each annotation call receives only 32 sampled frames and requests a compact localization output, keeping the overall annotation budget controlled. B.4 Training Prompt Table 11 presents the training prompt, which instructs the model to select between direct answering and evidence-grounded reasoning based on the available visual evidence. Direct responses begin with <answer> , while reasoning responses begin with Alright and briefly connect observed clues before answering. Both modes place the final answer within <answer></answer> tags. The distinct openings allow the selected response mode to be identified directly from the output.
