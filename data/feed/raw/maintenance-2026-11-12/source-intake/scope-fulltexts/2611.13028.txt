Sim-to-real transfer is a common approach to developing real-world agent capabilities: models are trained in simulation and then transferred to physical environments [ 20 , 15 ] .
Among simulated environments, AAA open-world games offer a rich testbed: navigation, vehicle control, and interactions with moving characters require spatial understanding, temporal reasoning, and action execution [ 1 , 18 ] .
Vision-language models are increasingly deployed as agents that translate visual observations and natural-language goals into actions [ 22 , 18 ] .
Evaluating these agents requires measuring whether their actions achieve task goals over an extended interaction. Existing work follows three broad directions: verifiable tasks in sandbox and purpose-built games [ 5 , 13 , 8 ] , agents that act in commercial 3D worlds [ 18 , 1 , 19 ] , and benchmarks that assess gameplay through visual trajectories or offline questions [ 25 , 27 , 21 ] .
These directions advance task verification, realistic interaction, and broad coverage, respectively.
Bringing them together requires an environment that preserves objectives from real AAA missions, verifies outcomes automatically, and generates new executable tasks for training.
Table 1 compares these features across representative benchmarks. We introduce GTAGym, an environment and framework that decomposes main-story missions from GTA V [ 16 ] into reusable tasks defined by their goals, interaction stages, completion constraints, and dynamic behaviors.
Its evaluation benchmark, GTAVBench, contains 103 online tasks across nine families of navigation, vehicle control, and dynamic interaction, plus 47 image-based shooting and sniping tasks using GTA V imagery.
GTAVBench supplies textual task instructions; its GTAVBench-AGI branch presents them only in the first screenshot, as in-game objectives, and uses a harness with automatic context compression.
A web server exposes a shared screenshot-and-action interface for parallel evaluation, with built-in evaluators checking online outcomes from engine states.
The same framework samples task parameters within user-defined ranges to generate batches of RL environments with automatic reward and termination signals.

Our contributions are: • We provide GTAGym, an environment for parallel evaluation of tasks derived from real AAA gameplay. Built-in evaluators determine task outcomes directly from engine states, removing the need for manual outcome judgments and bringing the programmatic task verification available in Minecraft platforms such as MineDojo [ 5 ] to tasks derived from GTA V missions. • We construct GTAVBench from GTA V main-story missions and introduce its GTAVBench-AGI branch. The former supplies textual task instructions, while the latter presents goals in the initial game image and uses a harness with automatic context compression. The results reveal persistent weaknesses in completing these mission-derived tasks, particularly in aerial control, evasion and interception. • We equip GTAGym with a scalable, template-based generator of reinforcement-learning task environments. Each template fixes the scene and task rules while exposing sampling parameters such as movement mode, route length, turn count, and actor speed. Users restrict their ranges or candidate sets to generate 3D and image-based shooting environments in batches, with automatic reward and termination signals to support RL training of exploration, control, and aiming skills. 2 Related Work 2.1 Foundation-Model Agents for Video Games Foundation models combine visual perception, instruction following, and action selection to operate in commercial games.
Cradle uses screenshots and keyboard–mouse control with planning, memory, and reflection modules, demonstrating main-story progression in Red Dead Redemption 2 [ 18 ] .
SIMA learns instruction-following behavior across simulated 3D worlds, using ground-truth checks in research environments and OCR or human judgments in commercial games [ 1 ] ; SIMA 2 extends this direction with higher-level reasoning and self-improvement [ 2 ] .
Lumine trains an end-to-end vision-language policy for keyboard–mouse control in open worlds and uses human evaluation for its task benchmark [ 19 ] .
OctoGTA instruments GTA V through scripting plugins, exposing game entities and programmatic action functions for visually guided task execution [ 23 ] .
GTAGym instead reconstructs mission-derived tasks with engine-state evaluators and a shared screenshot-and-action interface, supporting parallel evaluation and RL environment generation. 2.2 Multimodal Game Benchmarks Games have long served as testbeds for learning and evaluating interactive agents.
Procgen studies RL generalization through procedurally generated levels [ 3 ] , while MineDojo provides configurable Minecraft tasks and rewards for open-ended learning [ 5 ] .
More recent benchmarks evaluate foundation-model agents across diverse games: lmgame-Bench studies perception, memory, and reasoning [ 7 ] , Orak examines agent modules and fine-tuning across commercial games [ 14 ] , and VideoGameBench measures visual gameplay progress and the effects of inference latency [ 25 ] .
Specialized benchmarks such as LongPuzzleBench further test long-horizon decision-making in visual puzzles [ 26 ] . Recent work also develops evaluation protocols for richer game environments.
GameWorld provides state-verifiable browser tasks and parallel execution [ 13 ] , while OmniGameArena introduces custom UE5 games for solo, competitive, and cooperative evaluation [ 8 ] .
For commercial AAA games, GameVerse scores progress through VLM-based milestone matching and studies improvement from video reflection [ 27 ] ; GameHorizon-Bench evaluates AAA gameplay offline, with its verifiable online track implemented in Minecraft [ 21 ] .
Our work brings objectives extracted from GTA V main-story missions into an environment with built-in outcome checks, parallel web execution, and parameterized RL instance generation.

GTAGym provides the 3D evaluation environment, spanning urban, indoor, water, and aviation scenarios, with unified task initialization, interaction, and outcome verification.
Native engine states enable automatic verification of online task outcomes. Figure 2 illustrates the task categories and representative objectives across the two tracks; Table 3 in Appendix A.1 summarizes their mission requirements.
The online 3D track contains 103 tasks across nine dimensions.
These categories organize the mission-derived goals, interaction stages, completion constraints, and dynamic behaviors that the environment implements.
The image-based environments add 47 shooting and sniping tasks using source gameplay imagery, covering visual target localization and aiming.
Together, the two tracks contain 150 tasks.
This organization supports a capability-level assessment of agents, with results reported separately for the two tracks.
Across both environment types, we group the eleven categories into navigation, interaction, and gun use. Figure 2 : Overview of the GTAVBench task suite. Numbers in parentheses indicate the number of tasks in each category. 3.2 Task Construction Task extraction and specification. We manually decompose 75 GTA V main-story missions into subtasks based on collected walkthrough videos, each corresponding to a distinct stage in a mission.
For each subtask, we specify the goal, required interaction order, completion constraints, and entity behaviors used to reconstruct it in our environment.
These annotations and source game images support the construction of 150 benchmark tasks across eleven categories. Online scenario reconstruction. We build the 3D environment using Babylon.js and implement task controllers that expose task-specific states.
Task specifications configure initial positions, destinations, vehicles, interaction objects, and moving actors.
For navigation tasks, we manually trace paths on the source minimap and seek routes with comparable turn counts in the available city geometry.
We add climbing surfaces and landing areas where required by the task.
Dynamic-interaction tasks use predefined NPC behaviors and mechanics such as collision-induced slowdown.
The resulting scenarios preserve the selected goals, interaction dependencies, and movement constraints while adapting their spatial layout to the evaluation environment. Image-based task construction. Shooting and sniping use a parallel construction process based on source game imagery.
For shooting, we select gameplay frames and associate targets with annotated hit regions.
For sniping, we capture images directly in GTA V to construct distinct unscoped and scoped views with magnification-dependent rendering. Template instantiation and verification. We instantiate each subtask description as a scenario template with an initial state, an instruction, a task budget, and executable success and failure predicates.
The goal specifies the destination, target, or interaction outcome; stage transitions specify the required order of interactions.
Completion predicates check movement mode, height or depth, target distance, and interaction events according to the task.
For following and pursuit, a target controller executes a predefined route that the agent must track through visual observations.
For evasion, pursuer controllers update detection and capture states as the agent moves freely.
Section 3.4 describes how template parameters are sampled to generate RL environments in batches. 3.3 Evaluation Shared interaction interface. GTAVBench and GTAVBench-AGI use the same task instances, action interface, and scoring rules, differing in instruction presentation and context management.
Both branches follow a closed-loop screenshot–action protocol with keyboard–mouse control.
By default, online 3D simulation pauses during inference and advances during action execution.
The agent specifies a hold duration d ∈ ( 0 , d max ] d\in(0,d_{\max}] for each key, with d max = 1 d_{\max}=1 second.
Keys execute concurrently, and the turn duration is the longest scheduled hold.
Section 5.4 examines alternative duration limits and evaluation without inference-time pausing.
The image-based track uses the same action schema with its aiming interface.
Complete trajectories are recorded for analysis.
The action space is specified in Appendix A.3 . GTAVBench. Following screenshot-based computer-use evaluation [ 22 ] , agents use a fixed system prompt, a textual task instruction, and screenshot–reply history to generate JSON actions, which an adapter validates and executes.
Online history is capped at ten pairs; exceeding the cap drops the oldest five while preserving the instruction.
Image-based tasks retain the full history.
See Appendix D for prompt and history details. GTAVBench-AGI. We introduce GTAVBench-AGI as a separate harness-based evaluation branch of GTAVBench.
The task instruction appears only within the first screenshot , presenting the objective through the game view as in real gameplay.
The agent must read the goal from the image and act through the same keyboard–mouse interface.
As interaction history grows, the Codex harness automatically compresses the context supplied to the model [ 10 ] . Scoring. Episodes terminate upon task success, task failure, or exhaustion of an applicable turn or game-time budget.
We report category-level accuracy and overall accuracy micro-averaged over 150 tasks.
Task-specific outcome conditions are detailed in Appendix A.2 ; model configurations are specified in Section 4 . 3.4 RL Environment Generation Templates and sampling ranges. Procedural environment generation provides a way to vary training instances and evaluate RL generalization [ 3 ] . To support controlled variation, GTAGym organizes environment generation around task templates.
Each template T T fixes the scene, available assets, interaction logic, evaluator, and episode budget.
Users control variation within that template through numeric intervals or finite candidate sets 𝒞 \mathcal{C} : θ ∼ P T ( ⋅ ∣ 𝒞 ) , ℰ = G ( T , θ ; ξ ) , \theta\sim P_{T}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{C}),\qquad\mathcal{E}=G(T,\theta;\xi), (1) where θ \theta contains sampled parameter values and ξ \xi is the generation seed.
Parameter definitions, sampling domains, and template-specific settings are provided in Appendix B . Batch generation for RL. The rule-based generator in GTAGym samples configurations subject to the user’s bounds and the template’s feasibility constraints.
For example, a driving template can restrict route length to [ 200,500 ] [200,500] meters and turn count to { 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 } \{2,3,4,5\} .
These constraints jointly determine eligible routes; the generator rejects incompatible configurations and reports when no feasible instance can be found.
It constructs the full route and initial state from the accepted values, then saves the resolved configuration and seed for reuse.
Instances share the screenshot–action interface and use task outcomes as sparse rewards, with success and failure ending an episode and budget exhaustion recorded as truncation.
The evaluator uses engine-state predicates for 3D tasks and target-hit checks for image-based tasks.
Repeated sampling generates batches of RL environments within the requested parameter ranges. 4 Experiments 4.1 Models and Baselines We evaluate six models on GTAVBench under the conventional GUI protocol: GPT-6-Astra [ 12 ] , GPT-6.1-Sol [ 11 ] , DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017 [ 4 ] , GLM-5.3-Flash [ 24 ] , Kimi-K3 [ 9 ] , and Qwen3.8-27B.

We contrast road-navigation behavior across models, then examine dynamic-control failures that persist even in frontier models. Table 11 in the appendix provides paired observations and executed actions for all four mechanisms. Mistimed steering. In road navigation, non-GPT models sometimes recognize an upcoming turn on the minimap but initiate steering too late or hold it too long for the current speed, overshooting the road. GPT models more effectively translate these cues into early braking and smaller heading corrections. Late descent correction. In aerial navigation, reaching the runway does not ensure a safe landing. In the illustrated GPT trajectory, reducing throttle fails to arrest a rapid descent; pitch correction follows too late to prevent a crash. Coordinating attitude, speed, and descent remains difficult even when the destination is correctly identified. Failure to sustain blocking. In interception, slowing the target through collision is only an intermediate step. The agent continues accelerating after impact and loses contact instead of braking to hold a blocking position, allowing the target to resume its route. Wall entrapment. In evasion, the agent continues applying forward throttle and steering against a wall after hitting a building, producing no displacement before eventually reversing. Such delays interrupt escape progress. Together, these cases expose difficulty anticipating motion and switching control strategies as the vehicle, target, or obstacle relationship changes. 5.2 Error Recovery Across Models To study the recovery ability of different models, we manually identify and annotate the first task-relevant error in each trajectory.
We group first errors into four capability categories: Perception, Planning, Calibration, and Regulation.
Across 900 trajectories from six models, our analysis includes 565 with an identified and categorized first error.
Figure 6 shows that Calibration and Regulation dominate, together accounting for 63.2% of assigned first errors.
This concentration suggests a bottleneck in closed-loop control: calibrating action direction, magnitude, and duration, and adjusting maneuvers as motion and target states change. We then examine whether the agent subsequently recovers.
Figure 6 shows that GPT models recover more reliably: Astra and Sol restore task progress within ten subsequent decisions in 65.7% and 49.3% of categorized first-error cases, compared with 21.6%–25.6% for the other models.
In road-navigation trajectories, GPT models correct off-road deviations and rejoin the intended route, resuming progress after local control errors. Figure 6 : Recovery within ten decisions, as a percentage of categorized first-error cases for each model and capability. 5.3 Harness Trade-offs We study the instruction-presentation and context-management differences between GTAVBench and GTAVBench-AGI.
Compared with the JSON runner’s ten-image window, the Codex harness preserves longer histories and uses textual summaries to maintain continuity.
This provides memory of attempted strategies, explored rooms, and interaction feedback, helping agents revise their plans and avoid repeated ineffective actions.
In motorcycle interception, GPT-6-Astra recalls a blocked approach and changes its maneuver before re-engaging; in indoor navigation, DeepSeek-Flash retains explored rooms and unsuccessful door interactions to guide further search.
However, longer histories and compression can also preserve mistaken interpretations and weaken goal adherence.
Kimi-K3 initially identifies a rooftop objective correctly but, after compression, substitutes a driving objective and later drops the original goal from its summary.
GLM-5.3-Flash similarly carries reversed minimap-marker identities into subsequent decisions.
These cases illustrate trajectory drift and goal loss around context handoffs. 5.4 Effects of Evaluation Settings We examine how the timing settings of the online 3D evaluation protocol affect task completion.
Both ablations use GPT-6.1-Sol with the Codex harness on the 103 online 3D tasks in GTAVBench-AGI.
Figure 7 compares the four distinct settings across the two ablations. Maximum action duration. We compare three values of d max d_{\max} : 0.5 s, 1 s (default), and 2 s.
The agent chooses each key’s duration within ( 0 , d max ] (0,d_{\max}] ; simulation pauses during inference in all three conditions.
Relative to the 1 s baseline (62.1%), reducing the cap to 0.5 s raises overall accuracy to 66.3%, while increasing it to 2 s lowers accuracy to 60.5%.
Figure 7 shows the largest gains in following and interception, where shorter actions provide more frequent visual feedback.
The tradeoff is more interaction and longer cumulative context: reducing the cap from 1 s to 0.5 s increases mean rounds from 62.6 to 121.2, input tokens from 4.32M to 11.20M, and output tokens from 4.27k to 7.66k. The mean maximum duration selected within an action increases with the allowed cap: 0.494, 0.916, and 1.468 s.
At the 2 s cap, 66.6% of actions exceed 1 s, although shorter inputs remain available. This suggests that model does not reliably choose appropriate action durations under our interface. Figure 7 : Accuracy (%) of GPT-6.1-Sol with Codex across nine 3D task categories under four action-duration and inference-time pausing settings. Evaluation without pausing. With d max = 1 d_{\max}=1 second, we let the simulation continue during model inference, releasing each key after its specified duration.
Without pausing, overall accuracy drops by 22.3 percentage points to 39.8%.
Following, interception, and police evasion fall to zero success, while on-foot and indoor navigation retain 100% accuracy and vertical navigation remains at 93.3% (Figure 7 ).
This contrast points to inference latency as a bottleneck for dynamic interaction: after a short action ends, the environment continues evolving while the agent waits for its next decision.
Targets can move beyond following range or reach their destinations, and police can capture the player before another corrective action is available.
Tasks that tolerate waiting remain more tractable.
Thus, real-time performance depends on whether the inference-and-action cycle is fast enough for the task’s response window. 6 Conclusion We presented GTAGym, a web-server-based environment and framework for evaluation and RL on tasks derived from GTA V main-story missions.
GTAVBench provides text-instructed evaluation, while its GTAVBench-AGI branch presents task goals only in the first screenshot and uses a harness with automatic context compression.
Both support parallel task execution and automatic outcome verification through a unified interface.
Experiments show that current agents remain limited in aerial control, evasion and interception.
The environment generator in GTAGym complements GTAVBench by sampling numeric and categorical parameters within user-defined bounds under fixed templates, creating 3D and image-based shooting tasks with reward and termination signals for RL training.
LoRA RL on generated on-foot navigation tasks improves Qwen3.8-27B’s task completion, with gains extending to unseen task categories and native GTA V missions.
Together, these components provide a basis for evaluating and improving agents on tasks derived from real AAA gameplay. Acknowledgements We thank the developers and contributors of GTA_SZ for making their game environment openly available.

A.2 Task-Specific Outcome Conditions The evaluator determines completion from engine states and task events for online tasks, and from annotated hit regions for image-based tasks.
Success is binary: incomplete route progress, unfinished interactions, and partially cleared target sets receive no partial credit.
Both evaluation branches use the same predicates; an agent’s declaration of completion cannot substitute for satisfying them. Road and On-Foot. The agent must enter the destination region using the required movement mode: driving for road tasks and walking for on-foot tasks.
Default horizontal tolerances are 24 m and 3 m, respectively, with smaller task-specific regions for precise destinations.
Arrival tests include the segment traversed between consecutive physics updates, so a fast vehicle crossing the region is not missed between frames.
Walking arrivals require ground support and cannot be triggered during a jump or traversal animation.
The displayed route and intermediate turns guide navigation without imposing mandatory checkpoints; physically feasible shortcuts are allowed.
Vehicle-acquisition variants additionally require the agent to find and enter a vehicle. Their ground-truth reference sequence includes the initial walking and boarding stages.
Driving into deep water initiates irreversible sinking and prevents arrival success. Vertical and Indoor. These templates add height and interaction requirements to arrival.
For example, the pickup-to-rooftop task requires a grounded walking agent within 1 m of the goal and 0.15 m of the roof height; the ladder-to-rooftop task uses 1.1 m horizontally and 0.35 m vertically.
Passing beneath an elevated goal does not complete it.
Interior exits can use an oriented passage region instead of a small circular goal, and the agent must reach the correct floor.
Required interactions are checked through explicit mission state: the bank task requires pressing E within 1.5 m of the cash interaction point, opening the exit shutter, and leaving the building.
The cover task requires pressing Q between the cover and the rear wall, within 0.85 m of its interaction point, and completing the crouched pose.
Other interiors can require an outward-and-return sequence before the final exit becomes valid. Following. The target follows its configured route while the evaluator checks the agent–target distance throughout the episode.
The maximum horizontal separation is 20 m on foot and 200 m in road vehicles.
Exceeding the applicable threshold causes immediate failure, whether the agent falls behind or moves too far ahead.
Success occurs only after the target reaches its destination and the agent remains in the required movement state.
Thus the agent must sustain following throughout the NPC’s traversal.
Returning within range after a violation does not reverse a terminal failure. Interception. For ordinary vehicle targets, the agent must establish frontal collision contact and continue blocking the target.
Success requires the target’s speed to remain at or below 3 m/s for 1.5 s while the agent remains ahead, with a collision gap below 2.5 m and its own speed at or below 4 m/s.
Braking from a collision is integrated over time; a naturally slow corner does not satisfy the interception condition.
Motorcycle targets instead require three distinct knockdowns, each caused by an impact with at least 2 m/s closing speed.
Further hits count only after recovery and separation; the third knockdown completes after the visible fall is complete and the motorcycle slows to at most 1 m/s.
A companion-recovery variant uses a separate predicate: the agent must align alongside the moving truck and complete the companion transfer.
For these tasks, the target reaching its endpoint before completion causes failure, including ties within a physics update. Evasion. Evasion has no destination or prescribed route.
Evasion has a fixed maximum duration of 180 simulated seconds.
The agent succeeds by remaining outside every pursuer’s scanning sector for sixty consecutive simulated seconds within this limit.
Each sector has a 12 m radius and a total angle of 90 ∘ 90^{\circ} .
Any exposure resets the undetected timer; three continuous seconds inside the union of scanning sectors causes capture.
Leaving all sectors resets the capture timer.
Pursuers and their visible minimap sectors use the same sensing geometry. Aquatic. Completion depends on the water-task subtype.
Swimming goals use a 3 m horizontal tolerance and a 0.8 m depth tolerance; the submerged-entry task, for example, requires a depth of 6 ± 0.8 6\pm 0.8 m.
Boat-arrival tasks normally require successful boarding and arrival within 7 m of the destination.
Swim-to-boat tasks instead end near the stern platform, while boarding tasks require the boarding transition itself.
Shore-arrival tasks require grounded walking on land, above the water surface, within 2.5 m of the endpoint.
The beaching variant additionally checks that the boat is grounded and its full footprint is on land.
Shore-to-platform transport-choice variants accept surface arrival by swimming or boat.
The submarine variant requires crossing the pipe mouth and reaching its interior goal section with valid depth, alignment, and hull clearance. Aerial. The agent must board the aircraft, take off, satisfy the configured aerial objective, and land at the designated recovery site.
Flight waypoints are ordered three-dimensional gates, with radii of 65 m for planes and 16 m for helicopters.
Approach objectives require coming within 60 m of the moving aircraft; hover objectives require three continuous seconds within 12 m of the target at a speed no greater than 3 m/s.
Landing requires ground contact at the correct runway or helipad after a recorded takeoff and landing, speed at most 1.5 m/s, throttle below 5%, and two continuous seconds stopped.
Helicopters must also be within 12 m of the designated landing point.
Crashing, exiting the task aircraft, or leaving the allowed flight region causes failure.
An aircraft-approach task also fails if the target leaves its task airspace before the approach condition is met. Shooting and Sniping. The evaluator transforms the reticle’s impact point back into the annotated image coordinate system, accounting for camera movement and scope magnification.
Ordinary shooting tests the annotated target box and any required target-part mask; sniping tests the corresponding target polygons.
In both cases, exclusion regions are removed from the accepted hit region, and head-specific tasks use the annotated head region.
Single-target shooting and sniping allow one shot: a hit succeeds and a miss fails.
In multi-target sniping, a task with K K targets supplies exactly K K bullets and requires K K distinct valid hits.
An unscoped shot, a miss, or a repeated hit on an already cleared target immediately fails a multi-target task.
Single-target sniping uses seeded dispersion for unscoped fire where supported, while scoped fire uses a point impact. Interaction budgets and termination precedence. Online episodes allow up to 300 interaction rounds, and image-based tasks allow up to 20.
Exhausting the round budget without success yields zero credit.
Following requires the agent to maintain the required proximity until the NPC reaches its endpoint.
Task-specific failures, including capture, aircraft destruction, and the target reaching its endpoint in Interception, take precedence over completion. A.3 Agent Action Space The agent controls the environment through a shared keyboard–mouse interface.

Let P ⁡ ( τ ) P(\tau) denote the clipped fractional distance reduction in Eq. 2 .
Normalization makes equal fractional reductions comparable across route lengths.
Moving farther from the destination yields zero progress, and revisiting locations provides no accumulated bonus: only the final distance is used.
The factor ( 1 − S ) (1-S) applies the distance bonus only to unsuccessful rollouts.
Thus a successful rollout receives either 1 1 or 0.9 0.9 , while an unsuccessful rollout receives at most 0.3 0.3 . Traversable-distance measurement. Distances are approximated on a one-meter, eight-neighbor walking grid using A* search with the environment’s collision and surface-height checks.
The search is independent of the displayed purple route and permits physically feasible shortcuts.
An unreachable or unsupported-height final position receives P = 0 P=0 .
Distance computation failures are treated as invalid reward evidence, not as zero task progress.
These measurements are host-side training signals and are not included in the agent’s observations. Round penalty and terminal scoring. The reward is assigned once at the end of the rollout, with no intermediate distance rewards.
