Email: donggeun.kim@nota.ai Abstract Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) enables multimodal large language models
(MLLMs) to scale capacity efficiently, but expert parameters dominate
memory even though only a few experts are activated per token: they
account for over 90% of model size in modern MoE-MLLMs (e.g., 54 GB
out of ∼ {\sim} 58 GB in BF16 for InternVL3.5-30B-A3B and
Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B).
Expert pruning has emerged as an effective compression strategy, but
existing methods aggregate importance uniformly across
tokens, ignoring that the same expert may serve different roles for
vision versus language processing.
To address this, we propose MAEP ( M odality- A ware E xpert P runing), a training-free framework
that decomposes expert importance by modality in a single forward pass.
MAEP introduces (1) Delta-H Output ( Δ ​ H \Delta H ), a
redistribution-aware metric measuring the MoE output change when an
expert is removed, and (2) low-attention vision tokens as a
vision-side pruning signal that is dataset-stable : its
expert-importance ranking remains stable across different datasets.
Text-side importance serves as a preservation signal while vision-side
importance serves as a pruning signal, and experts are ranked globally
across layers.
Experiments on three MoE-based MLLMs across multimodal and text
benchmarks show that MAEP outperforms modality-agnostic baselines
on the majority of multimodal benchmarks while
remaining competitive on text-only tasks, reducing the BF16 expert
weight footprint by ∼ 27 {\sim}27 GB ( ∼ \sim 47% of total memory) so
that the full weight footprint of a ∼ 58 {\sim}58 GB model fits within a single 40 GB GPU.
Code is available at https://github.com/nota-github/MAEP . 1 Introduction Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have increasingly adopted
the Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture as a key strategy for scaling
model capacity while maintaining computational
efficiency [ 11 , 57 , 23 ] .
By activating only a subset of experts per token through sparse routing,
MoE models achieve a favorable trade-off between total parameter count
and inference cost.
This paradigm has naturally extended to multimodal LLMs
(MLLMs) [ 49 , 1 , 51 , 16 , 25 , 3 , 42 ] ,
where MoE layers handle the joint processing of vision and
language inputs. However, while sparse MoE reduces computation by activating only a few
experts per token, all expert parameters must remain in memory regardless
of activation—making the MoE architecture inherently memory-intensive.
In models such as InternVL3.5-30B-A3B and Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B,
expert parameters account for 93–94% of the total model size
(54 GB out of ∼ {\sim} 58 GB in BF16; see Appendix C ),
despite only 8 of 128 experts being activated per token.
This gap between memory footprint and effective utilization motivates expert compression —reducing the number or size of experts
through techniques such as pruning, merging, or quantization.
For instance, pruning 50% of experts can reduce the BF16 routed-expert weight footprint by ∼ {\sim} 27 GB (47% of
total weight memory), bringing the BF16 weight footprint of a 58 GB model below the nominal
capacity of a single 40 GB GPU, thereby enabling deployment in
memory-constrained settings. Existing expert compression approaches, including merging [ 31 , 5 , 30 ] ,
pruning [ 27 , 2 ] , and decomposition/quantization [ 20 , 34 ] ,
have proven effective for text-only MoE LLMs.
However, these methods aggregate importance statistics uniformly
across all input tokens without distinguishing token modality,
making their direct application to MoE-based MLLMs inadequate.
Indeed, recent analysis [ 22 ] reveals a consistent
distributional gap between text and vision tokens in expert layers,
with each modality interacting with experts differently,
highlighting the need for a modality-aware expert compression strategy. To this end, we propose MAEP ( M odality- A ware E xpert P runing) ,
a training-free framework with two key components.
Given only a small calibration set, MAEP computes all importance
scores in a single forward pass without any gradient computation or
weight updates: First, we propose Delta-H Output ( Δ ​ H \Delta H ,
where H H denotes the MoE layer’s hidden-state output)
as the expert importance metric, defined as the squared L2 change
in this output when an expert is removed with routing redistribution.
Unlike metrics based on routing frequency or an expert’s isolated
contribution, Δ ​ H \Delta H captures the residual output change after redistributing weight to the remaining experts;
smaller values indicate higher functional redundancy among co-activated experts.
Because Δ ​ H \Delta H averages over a token subset, it can be computed
separately for text and vision tokens, yielding per-modality importance
scores. Second, we find that not all vision tokens are equally reliable for
importance estimation.
Evaluating Δ ​ H \Delta H over the full set of vision tokens yields
dataset-dependent rankings because vision content is tied to
image-specific objects, making the resulting importance estimates
domain-sensitive.
Instead, low-attention vision tokens —those receiving
minimal attention from text tokens—provide a substantially more
dataset-stable signal. We evaluate MAEP across multiple MoE-based MLLMs, pruning ratios, and benchmarks.
Our results suggest that modality-aware importance scoring outperforms modality-agnostic baselines,
supporting the value of modality decomposition for expert compression in vision-language models. 2 Related Work 2.1 Expert Compression for MoE Models Prior work on compressing MoE-based LLMs has explored several directions.
Among methods that reduce the expert pool, two prominent strategies
are merging , which combines similar experts [ 31 , 5 , 30 , 45 , 44 , 43 ] , and pruning , which removes
redundant experts based on estimated importance.
Pruning methods differ mainly in how they identify redundant
experts—e.g.,
router-weighted activation norms [ 27 ] ,
differentiable non-uniform layer-wise expert
pruning [ 2 ] , second-order atomic-expert
removal [ 29 ] , or calibration-based search over preserved expert subsets [ 39 ] .
At the weight level, MoE-Pruner [ 55 ] extends
activation-aware weight pruning to LLMs by scaling
weight-activation scores with router weights.
Complementary strategies include replacing pruned experts with
lightweight replacement modules [ 60 ] ,
neuron-level recombination [ 64 ] , and
quantization [ 20 ] or low-rank
decomposition [ 34 , 17 , 58 ] .
However, most prior pruning and merging methods have been developed
primarily for LLMs, leaving expert-compression strategies
for MoE-MLLMs relatively underexplored. Moreover, many importance-based criteria—particularly those derived
from activation frequency or routing
statistics [ 39 , 55 ] —aggregate
signals uniformly over tokens without distinguishing modality, which
can obscure modality-specific expert roles in vision-language models.
Vision tokens are themselves substantially
redundant [ 6 , 62 ] , which raises the
concern that frequency-aggregated importance may overweight
non-essential tokens; indeed, calibrating weight pruning on all text
tokens but only a subset of visual tokens has been shown
to improve pruning quality [ 33 ] .
This motivates modality-aware importance
measures that decompose expert contributions by token type rather
than aggregating uniformly across all tokens. 2.2 Efficient MLLMs As MLLMs grow larger, their memory and compute demands increasingly
constrain real-world deployment.
Prior work has improved inference efficiency by reducing the number of
visual tokens forwarded to the language
model [ 6 , 62 , 40 ] ,
lowering weight precision through
quantization [ 32 , 59 ] , and compressing
the KV cache to reduce runtime
memory [ 48 , 19 ] . As MoE architectures—which scale model capacity while keeping
per-token computation fixed—have also become common in
MLLMs [ 36 , 49 , 1 ] ,
improving efficiency for MoE-based architectures has been explored.
FastMMoE [ 53 ] combines expert activation reduction
for visual tokens with routing-aware visual token pruning, while RoE [ 50 ] and
SMAR [ 54 ] optimize expert routing strategies.
MoDES [ 22 ] is closest in motivation to our work:
it accounts for modality-dependent expert utility and
applies modality-specific thresholds to skip experts during inference.
Existing MoE-MLLM efficiency methods mainly reduce inference-time
computation through expert skipping or visual-token
pruning; the expert parameters themselves, however, persist in GPU
memory. MAEP targets this complementary but underexplored problem:
permanent routed-expert pruning for MoE-based MLLMs, using
modality-decomposed importance scores. 3 Preliminaries 3.1 Mixture-of-Experts Layers Within each transformer block, the Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture

This shared-pool design raises a fundamental question: is expert importance uniform across modalities, or does
it differ between text and vision tokens? We investigate this empirically in Section 4 . 4 Empirical Motivation 4.1 Why We Need a Modality-Aware Proxy Metric Following OBD [ 28 ] ’s principle, which measures a component’s
importance by the loss change upon removal, a direct way to score an expert is to remove
it and measure the induced change in the model’s next-token
distribution.
This change can be captured by the LM-head KL divergence D KL ( p orig ∥ p pruned ) D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\text{orig}}\|p_{\text{pruned}}) between the
unpruned and pruned output distributions.
Directly measuring this KL for every expert would capture the effect on output but is prohibitively expensive: with N N experts
per layer and L L MoE layers, it requires N × L N\times L full forward
passes per calibration sample (e.g., 128 × 48 = 6,144 128\times 48=6{,}144 passes
for InternVL3.5-30B-A3B).
We therefore use Delta-H Output ( Δ ​ H \Delta H ), the MoE-output
perturbation induced by expert removal, as a lightweight proxy.
We formalize this proxy in Section 5.1 . Furthermore, a proxy aggregating uniformly over tokens is insufficient.
Prior work has observed modality-dependent expert behavior in MoE-based
MLLMs [ 54 , 22 , 56 ] ; we find that this extends to expert importance : when ranked by Δ ​ H \Delta H on text tokens versus vision tokens, the resulting expert
importance rankings show limited agreement (Spearman ρ \rho between − 0.11 -0.11 and 0.05 0.05 at specific layers; Appendix F ). The expert removal also induces an asymmetric output-side perturbation:
the LM-head output distribution shifts up to 29× more on vision-token
positions than on text-token positions, indicating modality-asymmetric
representational sensitivity to expert removal.
These observations imply that an effective metric must be computationally efficient and modality-decomposable . 4.2 Vision Token Heterogeneity and Signal Stability Prior work on vision token pruning [ 6 , 62 , 40 ] has shown that not all vision
tokens contribute equally.
This suggests that modality decomposition alone is not sufficient on

show a pronounced weakness on ChartQA.
We trace this to calibration domain mismatch : ShareGPT4V
contains primarily natural-image conversations with little chart or
document content, so experts specialized for chart reasoning receive
limited activation during calibration and are consequently under-scored
by Δ ​ H \Delta H .
This implies that MAEP’s importance scores are responsive to
deployment-domain signal in the calibration set—a property
practitioners can exploit to target a specific deployment
distribution. To demonstrate this, we construct a mixed calibration set of 700
ShareGPT4V [ 7 ] and 300
ChartNet [ 26 ] reasoning samples, where ChartNet
pairs chart images with extended reasoning text that matches
ShareGPT4V’s text-token density.
Under this mixed calibration, MAEP on Qwen3-VL at 50% pruning
(Table 2 (b)) recovers 13.9 points on ChartQA , while also improving MMStar by 4.1 points;
MME and TextVQA are nearly identical.
The absence of a substantial trade-off supports two claims:
(i) the ChartQA gap is attributable to calibration domain mismatch; and
(ii) MAEP can be targeted at a deployment domain by
augmenting calibration with in-domain data. This is consistent with the REAP’s [ 27 ] strategy of combining
general pre-training corpora with domain-specific data for
calibration: a diverse mixture prevents catastrophic drops on
specialized distributions and, as we observe, can further improve
in-domain performance. We leave to future work calibration-mixing
strategies and extension to reasoning-heavy generative
benchmarks (e.g., coding, mathematical problem solving). Calibration size. Beyond composition, we test how far the calibration budget can be
reduced by subsampling ShareGPT4V from the default 1,000 down to 100
samples (Table 2 (b), bottom block).
These results suggest MAEP is robust to calibration set
size: even at 100 samples, MAEP retains most of the default-1K
performance, with some fluctuation on individual benchmarks (e.g.,

joint removal of experts that back each other up or input-conditional
sparsity. Moreover, the adaptive α \alpha rule is empirically validated rather than derived from a
theoretical optimum. Finally, MAEP also targets the training-free
setting; fine-tuning (e.g., LoRA [ 18 ] ) is orthogonal and
could recover residual gaps. Pairwise redundancy and one-shot scoring. Δ ​ H \Delta H is not a fully independent score: its redistribution
counterfactual (Section 5.1 ) measures the
residual output change after the remaining co-activated experts absorb
the removed expert’s routing mass, so first-order redundancy among
co-activated experts is captured implicitly. What it does not model is
the joint removal of experts that back each other up. To quantify how
much this matters at our operating ratio, we compared the one-shot
50% prune mask on InternVL3.5 against a two-stage variant that prunes
25%, re-scores Δ ​ H \Delta H on the pruned model, and then prunes to 50%
in total. The two masks differ substantially (Jaccard 0.77 0.77 ; 800 of
the 3,472 experts in their union differ), but the two-stage variant
is strictly worse (one-shot → \rightarrow two-stage:
MME 2160.3 → 1956.7 2160.3\rightarrow 1956.7 , MMStar 63.3 → 53.9 63.3\rightarrow 53.9 , ChartQA 76.7 → 54.3 76.7\rightarrow 54.3 , TextVQA 75.6 → 64.9 75.6\rightarrow 64.9 ). We attribute
this to re-scoring on an already-redistributed model: Δ ​ H \Delta H re-measured after partial pruning no longer reflects the original
model’s redundancy structure, whereas the one-shot score models that
redistribution analytically without altering the model it measures.
This experiment therefore does not isolate interaction effects, and
we leave them, as well as input-adaptive or iterative pruning at more
aggressive ratios, open. Calibration-domain sensitivity. As with other calibration-based post-training compression methods,
MAEP’s prune decisions depend on the calibration distribution:
domains that the calibration set under-represents can lead to
under-scored experts (Section 6.3.2 ). This
sensitivity is confined to the text-side preservation signal, whereas
the low-attention vision signal is stable across calibration datasets
(Appendix G.3 ); it is therefore controllable rather
than intrinsic, and we do not claim domain invariance. We recommend
(i) a diverse calibration mixture as the default; (ii) augmenting it
with in-domain data when the deployment domain is known
(Table 2 (b)); and (iii) when the domain is unknown,
broadening the mixture with chart-, document-, and OCR-style data as an
inexpensive safeguard against under-coverage. Promising future directions include layer-wise α \alpha adaptation,
calibration-domain mixing strategies for deployment specialization,
and stacking with orthogonal compression families
