Affiliation: National University of Singapore Abstract LLM agents increasingly operate in long-horizon, verifier-rich environments, but current self-improvement mechanisms often write episode-derived lessons directly into memory, making local success an unreliable signal for reusable and safe knowledge.
This paper addresses the core question of when an experience-derived behavioral update should be admitted into persistent agent state.
We propose Falsification-Gated Self-Evolution (FGSE), a verifier-grounded framework that represents each candidate lesson as a structured hypothesis with an explicit precondition, behavior change, expected effect, and verifier.
FGSE applies the hypothesis only in a temporary state, tests it on target-transfer, falsification, and archived-regression probes, and then commits, refines, or rejects it according to measured gain and risk.
Across web, app, tool-use, and long-term memory benchmarks, FGSE achieves strong task performance, including 39.8 WebArena success rate, 78.8 AppWorld average completion, and 0.724/0.487 Pass 1 on τ \tau -Retail/ τ \tau -Airline, while reducing falsification failures to 5.0%, regression damage to 1.3%, and harmful committed updates to 3.6%.
These results suggest that testing memory updates as hypotheses supports more reliable long-term adaptation. 1 Introduction Large language model (LLM) agents are increasingly expected to operate in long-horizon, interactive environments where they must reason, call tools, update internal context, and recover from mistakes ( Yao et al., 2023 ) .
This capability supports web navigation, app-level tool use, and user-facing assistance ( Zhou et al., 2024 ; Trivedi et al., 2024 ; Yao et al., 2024 ) ; embodied interaction additionally requires grounding language in visual and gestural context ( Li et al., 2023 ) .
Continual deployment requires agents to solve current tasks and safely learn reusable behavioral knowledge from experience. Recent work has made substantial progress toward self-improving agents through self-feedback and reflection ( Madaan et al., 2023 ) , verbal reinforcement from past failures ( Shinn et al., 2023 ) , reusable skill memory ( Wang et al., 2023 ; Li et al., 2026b ) , agentic long-term memory organization ( Xu et al., 2025 ) , and evolving symbolic contexts or playbooks ( Zhou et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2026f ) .
These methods show that storing lessons, memories, playbooks, or revised contexts can improve future performance without necessarily updating model weights.
An episode-derived update may improve tasks that resemble the triggering episode while overgeneralizing to superficially similar but semantically different situations, conflicting with previously successful behaviors, or accumulating as stale persistent memory.
Even in verifier-rich environments, where task success can often be checked after execution, existing self-evolution mechanisms lack a principled criterion for assessing an update’s transferability, retention value, and commitment risk.
The central question is how to govern the transition from temporary experience to persistent agent state under transfer, safety, and regression constraints. Figure 1: A locally useful episode-derived lesson may overgeneralize or regress archived skills if directly written into memory.
FGSE tests each lesson as a structured hypothesis in a temporary state and commits only verified, transferable updates to long-term memory. We study when an experience-derived behavioral update should enter an LLM agent’s long-term memory.
Our approach treats persistent updates as testable hypotheses.
As shown in Fig. 1 , our approach temporarily applies each candidate update, evaluates its transfer beyond the source episode, robustness to targeted counterexamples, and preservation of archived successful behavior, and then commits, refines, or rejects it. We make the following contributions:
( i ) We identify a core failure mode of self-evolving agents: target improvement alone is an unreliable signal for persistent memory writing, because many apparently useful updates encode unsafe shortcuts or overly broad applicability conditions.
( ii ) We introduce Falsification-Gated Self-Evolution (FGSE), a verifier-grounded framework that treats candidate updates as testable hypotheses and performs structured commit–refine–reject triage before long-term retention.
( iii ) Across web, app, tool-use, and long-term memory settings, FGSE improves task performance while reducing falsification failures, regression damage, harmful committed updates, and post-commit violations compared with reflection-, memory-, and context-evolution baselines. 2 Related Work Interactive language agents and verifier-rich environments. These lines address adaptation capacity, retention, and resource efficiency, alongside cross-domain studies of recoverable model pruning ( Lu et al., 2026 ) , multi-scale and attention-based visual processing ( Zhang et al., 2023 ; Zhang et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2026d ) , activation design and LLM-guided fusion for forecasting ( Zhang et al., 2026b ; Zhang et al., 2026c ) , and operational decision making through semantic evidence for inspection allocation ( He et al., 2026 ) , causal intervention ranking ( Huang et al., 2026b ) , and accuracy–stability trade-offs in forecasting ( Li et al., 2026a ) .
FGSE addresses a complementary update-level problem: it falsifies each episode-derived update before persistent commitment, preserving self-evolution plasticity while controlling unsafe transfer and regression. 3 Preliminary Study A common self-improvement strategy is to summarize completed episodes into reflective text or experiential insights and store them in memory for future decision making ( Shinn et al., 2023 ) .
While such updates can help on similar tasks, they may overgeneralize, disrupt previously solved behaviors, or encode brittle shortcuts.

Figure 2 shows that naive memory writing is useful but unreliable.
Free-form lessons often improve near-transfer cases but can cause boundary harm, indicating that locally valid behaviors may be applied in inappropriate contexts.
This failure is closely tied to scope: broad, underspecified lessons produce larger boundary and archive degradation than more specific ones, and sequentially writing them into memory accumulates errors despite short-term source gains.
Post-hoc localization further shows that some harmful updates contain valid rules with overly broad applicability; narrowing their scope can retain transfer benefits while reducing harm.
These findings motivate FGSE, which treats episode-derived lessons as structured, falsifiable hypotheses, tests them temporarily on target, boundary, and archived cases, and commits or refines them only when supported by evidence. 4 Method 4.1 Overview We propose Falsification-Gated Self-Evolution (FGSE), a verifier-grounded framework for reliable long-term improvement of language agents. Given an episode, FGSE converts the observed success or failure into a structured behavioral hypothesis that specifies its precondition, behavior change, expected effect, and verifier. The hypothesis is applied only temporarily and evaluated on target-transfer probes and falsification probes. It is committed to persistent agent state only when it improves target cases, survives targeted falsification, and preserves previously solved behavior. Hypotheses with localized failures are refined once through precondition narrowing and reevaluated; hypotheses that still fail are rejected. We evaluate FGSE on verifier-grounded agentic tasks where updates can be tested before persistent commitment.
The benchmark suite covers web and browser control with WebArena ( Zhou et al., 2024 ) and BrowserGym ( Le Sellier de Chezelles et al., 2025 ) , app and API interaction with AppWorld ( Trivedi et al., 2024 ) and τ \tau -bench ( Yao et al., 2024 ) , adversarial tool-use robustness with AgentDojo ( Debenedetti et al., 2024 ) , and long-term memory reliability with LongMemEval ( Wu et al., 2024 ) and LoCoMo ( Maharana et al., 2024 ) .

To examine the gate, we map each candidate into a simplex over normalized target gain, falsification risk, and regression damage.
For each candidate h h , we normalize the three statistics across candidates and project them onto simplex coordinates ( g h , f h , r h ) (g_{h},f_{h},r_{h}) with g h + f h + r h = 1 g_{h}+f_{h}+r_{h}=1 .
Figure 5 (b) shows the joint role of the three statistics: commit lies in the high-gain, low-risk region, reject concentrates near the high-risk, high-regression edge, and refine forms a transition band between them.
This pattern indicates that FGSE performs structured triage, committing gain-dominant updates, refining repairable but ambiguous ones, and rejecting risk-dominant candidates. 7 Conclusion We studied reliable self-evolution for long-horizon LLM agents, focusing on when episode-derived lessons should enter persistent memory.
FGSE treats each candidate update as a structured, verifier-grounded hypothesis and commits it only after target-transfer, falsification, and regression testing.
Our results show that safe adaptation requires evaluating usefulness, boundary failures, and regression on previously solved behavior alongside source-task improvement.
Future work can extend falsification-gated update control to multimodal agents ( Xu et al., 2026a ; Dong et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2026a ; Zhang et al., 2026e ; Kang et al., 2026 ) , collaborative tool-use systems ( Fan et al., 2026 ) , and settings where verifiers must be learned or approximated. The agent directly summarizes a completed episode into a free-form lesson and writes it into persistent memory. Episode-derived lessons. For each completed episode, the base agent observes the task instruction x x , its interaction trajectory τ \tau , and the verifier outcome r r .
It then produces a natural-language lesson u = SummarizeLesson ⁡ ( x , τ , r ) , u=\mathrm{SummarizeLesson}(x,\tau,r), (15) which may take the form of a rule, reminder, correction, or behavioral heuristic.
The updated agent state is obtained by simply appending u u to the memory context: Θ ⊕ u = ( ℳ ∪ { u } , π ) , \Theta\oplus u=(\mathcal{M}\cup\{u\},\pi), (16) where ℳ \mathcal{M} denotes the current memory and π \pi denotes the unchanged base policy.
This direct memory-writing procedure is a common self-improvement baseline that exposes unsafe updates. Probe groups. To analyze the effect of a lesson u u , we evaluate the original agent Θ \Theta and the memory-augmented agent Θ ⊕ u \Theta\oplus u on four groups of diagnostic cases.
The source-replay group 𝒞 src ​ ( u ) \mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{src}}(u) contains the original episode and minor perturbations that preserve the same intended solution.
The near-transfer group 𝒞 near ​ ( u ) \mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{near}}(u) contains tasks that share the same underlying behavioral pattern but differ in surface form, entities, or context.
The boundary-contrast group 𝒞 bdry ​ ( u ) \mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{bdry}}(u) contains cases that are superficially similar to the source episode but should not trigger the same lesson.
The archived-success group 𝒞 arch ​ ( u ) \mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{arch}}(u) contains previously solved cases sampled from the agent’s historical archive.
The first two groups measure whether the lesson captures a useful pattern, while the latter two groups measure whether it overgeneralizes or interferes with existing behavior. Verifier-score changes. To understand why some useful lessons are harmful, we annotate each free-form lesson with a coarse scope-specificity score s ⁡ ( u ) ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] s(u)\in[0,1] .
A lesson receives a higher score when it contains explicit applicability conditions, contextual triggers, exception clauses, or task-specific constraints.
It receives a lower score when it is phrased as a broad heuristic without clear conditions.
This analysis-only score tests whether overgeneralization is associated with underspecified lesson scope; update selection in the preliminary study proceeds without it. Sequential memory-writing analysis. To study error accumulation, we simulate sequential memory writing over a stream of candidate lessons.
After writing k k lessons, the agent state is Θ k = Θ 0 ⊕ { u 1 , … , u k } , \Theta_{k}=\Theta_{0}\oplus\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\}, (21) and its held-out verifier score is measured on a fixed evaluation set ℰ \mathcal{E} : S k = 1 | ℰ | ​ ∑ x ∈ ℰ J ⁡ ( Θ k , x ) . (22) We compare three diagnostic memory-writing strategies: writing all lessons, writing only lessons that improve the source episode, and a hindsight safe subset.
The hindsight subset keeps lessons with high near-transfer gain and low boundary/archive harm, and is used only as an oracle diagnostic upper bound.
It is not available to the agent during deployment.
This comparison isolates the main observation that source improvement alone is insufficient for stable long-term self-evolution. Post-hoc scope localization. For lessons that are useful but harmful, we conduct a post-hoc localization analysis.
Given a lesson u u with positive near-transfer gain but high boundary harm, we inspect the boundary failures and rewrite the lesson into a narrower version u ′ u^{\prime} by adding an explicit applicability condition.
We then reevaluate u ′ u^{\prime} on the same near-transfer and boundary-contrast groups.

This separate diagnostic motivates explicit applicability conditions and refinement of useful but overly broad candidates in the main method. Connection to the method. The preliminary study supports three design choices.
First, testing episode-derived updates before persistence can expose unsafe free-form lessons.
Second, contrastive and archived cases expose boundary failures that source or near-transfer cases can miss.
Third, scope refinement can repair some harmful lessons while retaining their useful content.
These observations motivate the structured hypothesis representation, temporary evaluation, verifier-grounded commitment, and scope refinement introduced in Section 4 . ∎ B.8 Remarks on Adaptivity and Scope Refinement The proof requires that the final evaluation probes are not reused to construct or refine the hypothesis being certified. This condition prevents adaptive overfitting to the finite falsification set. In practice, this can be implemented in two equivalent ways. The first is sample splitting: use a development split for scope refinement and a fresh evaluation split for final commitment. The second is to define ℋ t \mathcal{H}_{t} as the enlarged finite set containing all hypotheses that can be produced by proposal and refinement before final evaluation; the same proof then applies with | ℋ t | |\mathcal{H}_{t}| replaced by the size of this enlarged set. The theorem certifies reliability with respect to the probe-generation distributions 𝒟 T ​ ( h ) \mathcal{D}_{T}(h) , 𝒟 F ​ ( h ) \mathcal{D}_{F}(h) , and 𝒟 R ​ ( h ) \mathcal{D}_{R}(h) . If these distributions differ from the deployment distribution, an additional distribution-shift term is needed. For example, if the deployment falsification distribution is within total variation distance ρ \rho of 𝒟 F ​ ( h ) \mathcal{D}_{F}(h) , then the certified falsification risk increases by at most ρ \rho . The commitment gate controls statistical generalization from finite probes to the specified probe distributions, while the quality of the probe generator determines how well those distributions cover deployment-time boundary cases. Holding tool access fixed isolates the effect of the update mechanism. Security and adversarial tool-output tasks. AgentDojo [ Debenedetti et al., 2024 ] is used to evaluate whether self-evolution increases susceptibility to prompt injection or malicious tool outputs.
We report utility success on benign tasks, security success under attacks, and the joint utility-security score.

A candidate update is rejected if it improves utility but increases attack success or violates a high-priority system or user constraint. Long-term memory tasks. We also measure memory pollution rate, defined as the fraction of committed updates that are later contradicted by held-out evidence or cause a regression on archived successful examples. Code and mathematical reasoning diagnostics. We use HumanEval [ Chen et al., 2021 ] , MBPP [ Austin et al., 2021 ] , and SWE-bench Verified [ Chowdhury et al., 2024 ] as verifier-rich coding environments because unit tests provide executable feedback.

C.3 Fair Comparison Protocol All controlled comparisons follow the same protocol [ Yang et al., 2026b ] : 1. Same backbone : every method uses the same base LLM and the same ReAct-style executor unless the method intrinsically requires a different executor. 2. Same tools : all methods receive identical tool schemas, browser actions, API access, and environment observations. 3. Same memory budget : methods with persistent memory are allowed the same maximum number of stored units and the same retrieval top- k k . 4. Same update budget : methods that propose self-updates receive the same number of completed training episodes and the same maximum number of update attempts. 5. No test leakage : hypotheses, reflections, playbooks, workflows, and memory entries are constructed only from training or development episodes. Test tasks are used only for final evaluation. 6. Same verifier access : if a verifier is available during development, all methods may use it under the same budget. During final evaluation, verifier outputs are used only for scoring. For workflow-search or architecture-search methods such as AFlow [ Zhang et al., 2025a ] , ADAS [ Hu et al., 2025 ] , GPTSwarm [ Zhuge et al., 2024 ] , and MetaAgent [ Zhang et al., 2025b ] , we report a controlled-budget version when code and prompts are available.
Methods requiring a substantially larger search budget, extra training data, reinforcement learning, or model fine-tuning are reported in a separate native-setting table, outside the controlled FGSE comparisons. C.4 Baseline Implementation Details Direct and ReAct. The direct baseline uses the base LLM without persistent memory or post-episode updates.
The ReAct baseline [ Yao et al., 2023 ] uses the same action space and tool interface as FGSE but does not modify its persistent state after each episode. Self-Refine and Reflexion. Self-Refine [ Madaan et al., 2023 ] performs within-episode critique and revision under the same maximum number of refinement calls as FGSE’s hypothesis proposal calls.
Reflexion [ Shinn et al., 2023 ] writes natural-language reflections after failed or low-scoring episodes and retrieves them in future tasks.
Reflexion commits reflections without requiring structured preconditions, falsification probes, or regression tests. Voyager-style skill memory. The Voyager-style baseline [ Wang et al., 2023 ] stores reusable skills or lessons from successful episodes and retrieves them for related future tasks.
For non-Minecraft benchmarks, we adapt the skill library into a text-based rule or tool-use strategy library while keeping the same retrieval budget as FGSE. Memory and context-evolution methods. A-Mem [ Xu et al., 2025 ] is implemented as an agentic memory organization baseline that dynamically indexes, links, and updates memories.
ACE [ Zhang et al., 2026f ] is implemented as an evolving playbook baseline that incrementally revises persistent context using execution feedback.
Both methods are evaluated with the same memory capacity and retrieval top- k k as FGSE. Workflow and agent-design optimization methods. AFlow [ Zhang et al., 2025a ] , ADAS [ Hu et al., 2025 ] , GPTSwarm [ Zhuge et al., 2024 ] , and MetaAgent [ Zhang et al., 2025b ] provide comparisons at the agentic-workflow level, complementing update-level memory methods.
Because these methods may search over a larger design space than FGSE, we distinguish controlled-budget and native-budget results. C.6 Ablation Studies We conduct the following ablations: • No falsification : commit every proposed hypothesis that improves the source episode. • Random probes : replace targeted falsification probes with randomly sampled held-out tasks. • LLM-judge gate : replace executable or environment verifiers with an LLM-only judgment. • No regression archive : remove ℛ ⁡ ( h ) \mathcal{R}(h) from the commitment rule. • No scope narrowing : reject all hypotheses that fail falsification without allowing precondition refinement. • Free-form memory : store unstructured natural-language lessons instead of structured ( c h , b h , e h , v h ) (c_{h},b_{h},e_{h},v_{h}) hypotheses. • Probe-budget sweep : vary the number of falsification probes to measure the trade-off between update safety and computation. These ablations isolate whether FGSE’s gains come from verifier grounding, targeted counterexample construction, regression protection, structured update representation, or scope refinement. We examine the plasticity–safety Pareto frontier for update selection and compare FGSE’s position with alternative strategies. We sweep a broad range of update-selection configurations and compare representative strategies, including FGSE , FGSE w/o Refine , Target-Only , LLM-Judge Gate , Conservative Gate , Exhaustive Verifier , Random Keep , and No Persistent Update .
For each configuration c c , we measure: (i) update gain Δ upd ​ ( c ) \Delta_{\mathrm{upd}}(c) , defined as the average improvement after applying the selected update; (ii) harmful update rate H ⁡ ( c ) H(c) , i.e., the fraction of committed updates that later induce safety violations or incorrect behavior on held-out probes; (iii) regression damage R ⁡ ( c ) R(c) , i.e., the average degradation on archived successful behaviors; and (iv) verifier cost C ⁡ ( c ) C(c) , measured as the average number of verifier calls per candidate update.

Each point corresponds to one configuration, point size encodes verifier cost, and the black dashed curve marks the empirical Pareto frontier. FGSE occupies the most favorable lower-right region over a wide range of medium verifier budgets, showing that it improves plasticity without incurring the safety cost typically associated with aggressive update policies. Figure 6 reveals a clear plasticity–safety frontier.
First, FGSE traces the dominant lower-right boundary in both panels over a broad range of medium-cost configurations, achieving substantially larger update gain at similar or lower harm than competing strategies.
In the harmful-update panel, frontier configurations of FGSE achieve roughly 9 % 9\% – 12 % 12\% update gain while keeping the harmful update rate within 2.5 % 2.5\% – 4.5 % 4.5\% , typically with only 20 20 – 40 40 verifier calls per candidate update.
At comparable gain levels, Target-Only and LLM-Judge Gate incur much larger harmful update rates, often exceeding 8 % 8\% – 15 % 15\% , indicating that target improvement alone is an unreliable proxy for safe transfer. Second, a purely conservative policy is also suboptimal. Conservative Gate combines low harm with much smaller update gain in the lower-left region.
This shows that effective self-improvement also requires sufficient plasticity to capture reusable behavioral rules.
Conversely, Exhaustive Verifier pushes harm slightly lower than FGSE in a few settings, but does so at much higher verifier cost and with little or no gain advantage, indicating that aggressive verification yields diminishing returns. Third, the comparison between FGSE and FGSE w/o Refine highlights the functional role of scope refinement.
Without refinement, many configurations move upward in both panels: they retain some gain but suffer higher harmful update rates and larger regression damage.
This suggests that a substantial fraction of risky updates retain utility and become safe only after narrowing their applicability conditions.
Overall, the Pareto analysis supports FGSE’s better trade-off between plasticity and safety , placing it in a more favorable region of the update-selection design space. Targeted probes versus random probes. FGSE uses targeted falsification: the verifier constructs probes likely to expose scope overreach, missing preconditions, archived regressions, and decoy-induced shortcut behavior.
We compare targeted probe scheduling with budget-matched baselines to evaluate its efficiency in discovering harmful updates. Let ℋ bad \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{bad}} denote the set of candidate updates that are in fact unsafe or regressive under held-out evaluation.
Given a probe budget K K , we define the bad-update detection rate as D ( K ) = 1 | ℋ bad | ∑ h ∈ ℋ bad [ τ h ≤ K ] , D(K)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{bad}}|}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{bad}}}\mathbb{1}\!\left[\tau_{h}\leq K\right], (127) where τ h \tau_{h} is the number of probes required to expose update h h under a given scheduling policy.
Thus, D ⁡ ( K ) D(K) measures the fraction of unsafe updates discovered within the first K K probes. To account for the severity of detected updates, we also report harmful update avoided , A ⁡ ( K ) = ∑ h ∈ ℋ bad w h [ τ h ≤ K ] ∑ h ∈ ℋ bad w h , A(K)=\frac{\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{bad}}}w_{h}\,\mathbb{1}\!\left[\tau_{h}\leq K\right]}{\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{bad}}}w_{h}}, (128) where w h w_{h} is the downstream harm weight of update h h , estimated from its induced violation severity and regression impact.
Hence, A ⁡ ( K ) A(K) measures how much total harmful mass would be prevented if all detected bad updates were rejected before commitment. We compare five probe-selection policies under the same verifier budget: Targeted (the full FGSE probe scheduler), Stratified Random (random selection balanced across probe sources), Pure Random (uniform sampling from the entire probe pool), Boundary-Only (target only applicability-boundary probes), and Regression-Only (target only archived-regression probes).

Targeted probing consistently dominates random baselines, especially in the low-budget regime, showing that hypothesis-aware falsification is substantially more information-efficient than unguided random testing. Figure 7 shows that targeted probing is markedly more informative than random testing at every budget.
The gap is largest in the practically relevant low-budget regime, where verifier calls are scarce.
At around K = 8 K=8 , the Targeted policy already detects roughly twice as many bad updates as Pure Random , and the improvement is even larger in terms of harmful update avoided.
This indicates that targeted probing discovers more harmful updates early and prioritizes those with larger downstream damage. A second observation is that Boundary-Only and Regression-Only each improve over pure random sampling in the regimes where their probe families are most relevant, but both eventually saturate below the full targeted strategy.
This suggests that harmful updates are heterogeneous: some arise from overly broad applicability conditions, whereas others only become visible through archived-regression or decoy-style counterfactual tests.
Therefore, a single probe family is insufficient; the gain of FGSE comes from selecting probes conditionally on the specific hypothesis under test. Finally, the larger gap in A ⁡ ( K ) A(K) than in D ⁡ ( K ) D(K) is itself informative.
It shows that targeted probing tends to surface the most dangerous updates first while increasing discovery count.
This property is crucial for practical self-evolution, where only a small verifier budget is available and preventing a few highly harmful commitments is more important than marginally increasing average detection at very large budgets. Stability under stochastic rollouts. We evaluate each candidate hypothesis under multiple stochastic rollouts to assess the reproducibility of its benefit across seeds. For a hypothesis h h and evaluation set 𝒳 h \mathcal{X}_{h} , we define its seed-specific update effect as Δ h ( s ) = 1 | 𝒳 h | ​ ∑ x ∈ 𝒳 h ( R ⁡ ( π ⊕ h , x , s ) − R ⁡ ( π , x , s ) ) , \Delta_{h}^{(s)}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}_{h}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{h}}\Big(R(\pi\oplus h;x,s)-R(\pi;x,s)\Big), (129) where s s is the rollout seed controlling stochastic decoding, action tie-breaking, and environment perturbations.
Thus, Δ h ( s ) \Delta_{h}^{(s)} measures the marginal effect of applying hypothesis h h under seed s s . To quantify stability, we further compute the mean seed effect Δ ¯ h = 1 S ​ ∑ s = 1 S Δ h ( s ) , \bar{\Delta}_{h}=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\Delta_{h}^{(s)}, (130) and the sign-consistency score C ( h ) = 1 S ∑ s = 1 S [ sign ( Δ h ( s ) ) = sign ( Δ ¯ h ) ] , C(h)=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\mathbb{1}\!\left[\operatorname{sign}\!\big(\Delta_{h}^{(s)}\big)=\operatorname{sign}\!\big(\bar{\Delta}_{h}\big)\right], (131) which measures the fraction of seeds whose update effect agrees in sign with the mean effect.
A stable and genuinely useful update should exhibit both a positive Δ ¯ h \bar{\Delta}_{h} and high sign consistency. Figure 8 visualizes the seed-level effect distributions for representative hypotheses from the Commit , Refine , and Reject groups.

We compare baseline and FGSE execution trajectories to identify the dominant behavioral edits underlying performance improvements. Let τ B = ( a 1 , … , a T ) \tau^{\mathrm{B}}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{T}) and τ F = ( a 1 ′ , … , a T ′ ′ ) \tau^{\mathrm{F}}=(a^{\prime}_{1},\dots,a^{\prime}_{T^{\prime}}) denote the baseline and FGSE trajectories for the same task.
We first align the two traces at the action-type level and group all trajectory edits into four interpretable behavior families: tool order , memory retrieval , constraint checking , and final answer formatting .
For each family e ∈ ℰ e\in\mathcal{E} , we compute its weighted edit mass m e ​ ( τ B , τ F ) = ∑ o ∈ 𝒪 e w ⁡ ( o ) , m_{e}(\tau^{\mathrm{B}},\tau^{\mathrm{F}})=\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}_{e}}w(o), (132) where 𝒪 e \mathcal{O}_{e} is the set of aligned edit operations assigned to family e e , and w ⁡ ( o ) w(o) denotes the causal weight of operation o o estimated from its contribution to final task improvement.
We then define the dominant edit family as e ⋆ ​ ( τ B , τ F ) = arg ⁡ max e ∈ ℰ ​ m e ​ ( τ B , τ F ) . e^{\star}(\tau^{\mathrm{B}},\tau^{\mathrm{F}})=\arg\max_{e\in\mathcal{E}}m_{e}(\tau^{\mathrm{B}},\tau^{\mathrm{F}}). (133) To make the behavioral change interpretable, we further assign each trajectory pair a baseline behavior motif and an FGSE behavior motif , determined from the dominant failure pattern before the first decisive divergence and the dominant corrected pattern after refinement, respectively.

Appendix E Broader Impacts FGSE may improve the reliability of long-horizon language agents by reducing unsafe memory updates, overgeneralized behavioral rules, and regressions on previously solved tasks.
Potential positive impacts include safer agent adaptation in web, app, and tool-use settings where persistent memory is used.
However, more reliable self-evolving agents may also increase the capability of automated systems that can perform harmful or unauthorized actions if deployed without access control, monitoring, and domain-specific safety constraints [ Yang et al., 2026a , Fan et al., 2023 ] .
Financial-agent audits likewise identify source-manipulation risks and motivate model-independent halts and hard loss limits [ Wang and Saxena, 2026 ] .
FGSE mitigates some of these risks through verifier-grounded falsification, regression testing, audit records, and post-commit monitoring. These mechanisms complement deployment-level safety controls. Appendix F LLM Usage This paper studies self-evolving LLM agents. In the experiments, frozen LLM
