Existing robotics benchmarks are built around visuomotor policy learning, leaving it open whether coding agents can reliably control diverse robots under physical-world constraints.
We therefore introduce the Embodied First Exam , a unified, comprehensive, and agent-native evaluation framework where coding agents can choose their own control approach: direct control, code to control, or train a policy to control. We hope it serves as a first exam for coding agents before they enter the physical world, with 1,156 tasks in 35 suites on 47 robots, including 9 new suites that go beyond pick-and-place to spatial puzzles, dexterous manipulation, multi-robot interaction, dynamic whole-body control, and sim-to-sim engineering.
Each task can be run in up to three evaluation settings. The Standard Setting mimics the real world: the agent receives only sensor observations and, by default, cannot reset the environment. Its Realtime variant keeps the environment running while the agent reasons. The Privileged Setting gives the agent access to the full simulator state and unlimited resets, allowing it to search for an oracle solution for training purposes. Success rates in this setting serve as an approximate upper bound. On the 456 core tasks, the strongest of eight agents, GPT-6 Astra in Codex, achieves a success rate of 34% in the Standard Setting, leaving much room for improvement. To see where agents fail, we label the stage at which failed attempts go wrong and compare failures across agents, settings, and stage categories. 1 Introduction Robots acquire new skills either by manual programming or by learning from data, as in recent vision-language-action models [ 11 , 69 , 10 , 122 , 110 , 38 ] and world action models [ 182 , 159 , 133 ] . Coding agents powered by large language models suggest a third way. They already complete challenging software engineering tasks in the digital world [ 64 , 102 , 104 , 73 ] . What makes these agents promising for robotic engineering is their capabilities to turn instructions into executable code and revise their plans through automated experimentation [ 84 , 141 , 151 ] . Recent early success in agentic robots suggests such a potential paradigm shift, where agents directly control robots [ 47 , 191 , 199 , 130 ] , write robot programs [ 62 , 32 , 101 , 167 , 145 , 48 ] , or train policies [ 165 , 83 ] . This motivates a systematic assessment of the capability boundary of coding agents: What robotic tasks can general-purpose coding agents solve, under what physical-world constraints, and where do they fail? Existing benchmarks do not yet fully address this question. The capability boundary cannot be established from a focused set of robotics tasks [ 59 , 184 , 87 , 76 , 106 , 16 ] , nor from automated programming tasks without imposing physical-world constraints [ 64 , 102 , 198 , 168 , 14 , 116 ] .
This requires defining which robotic capabilities to evaluate, measuring how physical-world constraints affect success, and supporting different models and harnesses through task interfaces. To answer this question, we introduce the Embodied First Exam (EFE) , a unified evaluation framework that connects coding agents to simulated robots ( ), following three principles. First, include diverse tasks that go beyond pick-and-place. Spatial puzzles test reasoning about spatial constraints, while sim-to-sim engineering tests how agents revise controllers from execution feedback under unfamiliar dynamics. Dynamic tasks test responses to a changing world. Multi-robot interaction and whole-body control test coordination.
EFE therefore contains 1,156 tasks in 35 suites on 47 robots. It combines 26 suites adapted from existing robotics benchmarks with 9 new suites covering these capabilities and safety. Second, measure how success depends on physical-world constraints. Its tasks should follow the constraints of a real robot as closely as possible. Success with full simulator state and resets does not establish what an agent can achieve through partial observations, with no resets, or while the dynamic world keeps running. We therefore define up to three evaluation settings per task. Standard Setting follows real-robot conditions: the agent observes the environment only through the robot’s sensors and cannot reset it by default. The simulation waits while the agent reasons, and its Realtime variant keeps the environment running while the agent makes decisions. Privileged Setting provides an approximate upper bound and can be used to generate training data, where the agent has access to the full simulator state and unlimited resets, allowing it to search for an oracle solution by trial and error. We call each <task–setting> pair a test . Tests of the same task share the initial scene and success rule, allowing us to compare success as state access, reset limits, and timing change. Third, provide unified infrastructure for different models, harnesses, and control approaches. With a single command, EFE runs different combinations of models and harnesses across diverse robotics tasks.
Given a natural-language task instruction, the agent chooses its control approach ( Figure 1 ). In Direct control , it directly outputs actions after receiving observations [ 47 , 191 , 199 ] . In Code to control , it outputs a planner, controller, or other program that generates the robot’s actions [ 84 ] . In Train a policy , it trains a policy in simulation and uses it to control the robot [ 95 , 83 ] . The framework enforces the access permitted by each evaluation setting, and a verifier outside the agent’s control grades every attempt [ 203 , 196 ] . Figure 1 : Examples of the control approaches, each with an excerpt of the agent’s commands or code. Dark tabs mark the approaches used ( model is direct control), and the framed line is a policy. We evaluate eight coding agents on a core set of 456 hard tasks. The strongest agent, GPT-6 Astra [ 115 ] in Codex, solves 77% of the Privileged tests but only 30% of the Standard tests of the same 391 tasks. Apart from the three strongest agents, GPT-6 Astra, Claude Opus 5.5 and Gemini 4 Argon, the best result comes from Kimi 3.1 Pro, which solves 9% of the Standard tests of the same 391 tasks (34 tests, Figure 10 ) and 7% of the Realtime tests ( Figure 7 ). The Realtime Setting is harder still: 63% of its tests remain unsolved by any agent, compared with 50% of the Standard tests of the same 54 tasks. To identify where failures occur within a task, we divide each task into stages. Each stage corresponds to a change in the world required to complete the task. For example, the task of putting a tomato into a closed fridge has three stages: the fridge is open, the tomato is inside, and the fridge is closed again. For each failed attempt of the eight agents on 427 tasks, a separate coding agent labels the stage at which the failure occurred. Our main findings are as follows. • Privileged versus Standard ( Section 3.2 ). Resets help GPT-6 Astra more than the exact object state does, and with both, its success rate closes most of the gap to the Privileged Setting. Stages that need precision become much harder in the Standard Setting. At these stages, resets help, but the exact state alone does not. • Where agents fail ( Section 3.3 ). Failures on a task tend to happen at the same stage, usually the first stage of the task. This stage also tends to be the same in the Privileged and Standard settings. Stages that change a material or move the robot’s body have the highest failure rates. • Differences between agents ( Section 3.4 ). Weaker agents are much more likely than stronger agents to fail before making the required contact. In the Standard Setting, the three strongest agents solve partly different sets of tests. Switching harnesses can change a model’s success rate by up to 12 percentage points. • How agents control the robot ( Section 3.5 ). In the Standard and Realtime settings of a soccer task, agents trained a policy when given a GPU and a training library, and only trained policies won. Agents mostly control the robot directly in the Standard Setting and write programs to control it in the Privileged and Realtime settings. 2 The Embodied First Exam: A Unified Evaluation Framework for Coding Agents in Robotics In this section, we describe how we evaluate coding agents on robotics tasks ( Section 2.1 ), the resource constraints of each task ( Section 2.2 ), and the tasks of the exam ( Section 2.3 ). In Standard and Realtime , the simulator and verifier are separated from the agent and grade the live episode. 2.1 Framework and Evaluation Protocol Existing robotics benchmarks focus on visuomotor policy learning with a relatively fixed input-output interface, whereas we evaluate coding agents that can choose their own control approach. Our design principle is therefore to give the agent broad freedom over how it acts and to constrain only the result: we fix where it starts, what resources it gets, and how success is checked. To achieve this, we package every robotics task in the format of Harbor [ 44 ] , the evaluation framework of Terminal-Bench [ 102 ] , which runs the task in containers, as shown in Figure 2(a) . The agent has its own workspace, where it can install packages, write and run programs, and train models. Each task starts from a fixed initial state; a verifier outside the agent’s container reports whether the agent’s answer meets the task’s success rule. On top of this framework, we define a unified evaluation protocol with up to three settings per task ( Figure 2(b) ). We design Privileged as an approximate upper bound on performance, with full access to the simulator for trial and error, while Standard and Realtime make the evaluation as close to a real robot as possible. The bound holds over a set of tasks, not for every single task, because an agent can solve a task in Standard that it fails in Privileged ( Appendix D ). For a given task, all settings share the same initial scene, success rule, and time limit, so their results are paired. They differ in what the agent observes, whether it can reset, whether the world waits, and where the simulator and the verifier run, never in the control approach the agent may choose. We ablate the first two differences in Section 3.2 . Privileged. In Privileged , the agent runs in the same container as the simulator, so it can read the full simulation state and reset without limit. The agent must submit a low-level robot trajectory (or a controller program for some tasks). The verifier replays the trajectory (or runs the controller) in a separate container to determine task success. Standard. In Standard , we run the simulator as a service in a separate container. Through predefined APIs, the agent has limited access to the simulator: it can query proprioceptive state (e.g., joint positions, velocities, and torques), and sensor data (e.g., calibrated RGB-D images), and send action chunks to control the robot. The simulation is paused between API requests, so the agent faces no real-time pressure. Resets are disallowed unless the task provides an explicit reset budget. The verifier grades task progress online as the agent controls the robot, and in most suites the interface reports a success to the agent and ends the episode. Realtime. Realtime is the same as Standard , except that the simulation does not pause between agent actions but keeps running in real time; for computationally expensive tasks, it runs at a fixed fraction of real time (e.g., 0.5 × \times ). Due to compute constraints, we currently evaluate Realtime only on dynamic tasks. The large number and the tick give the median over tests (for GPU memory, over the tests that reserve a GPU), the line spans the range, and the dark box spans the middle half of the tests. As shown in Figure 3 , we set resource constraints for each task: a time limit and a token budget for the agent, a reset budget and, in some tasks, a step limit for the robot (RoboDojo’s own limit in Figure 3 , or a cap on the episode length that the instruction states), and caps on CPU, memory, GPU memory, and disk. The token budget is high enough that no attempt reaches it. The time limit is the same in every setting of a task, and when it runs out we stop the agent and grade the result it has at that point. Resets are unlimited in Privileged . In Standard and Realtime , there are no resets unless the task grants a budget, which nine suites do, with 3 to 50 resets. We set the compute caps per task from its measured needs and monitor usage throughout the run ( Appendix C ). We observe that coding agents may choose different control approaches under different resource constraints and in different settings ( Figures 1 and 3.5 ). For example, if we provide a GPU, agents can train a policy to control the robot ( Figure 18 ). 2.3 Task Suites The exam contains 1,156 tasks in 35 suites. A suite is a set of related tasks on one simulator. Each task states a goal, usually in natural language, such as untying a knot or turning the page of a book with a dexterous hand, and the agent must control the robot to reach that goal. For the three strongest agents, 56% of the failure pairs of a test share a stage on average, against 31% by chance. • This stage also tends to be the same in the Privileged and Standard settings. The concentration of failure stages measures this ( Figure 13 ). For each test, it is the share of pairs of failed attempts, by different agents, that failed at the same stage. We use the attempts with a located failure stage and average this share over tests. The baseline draws each failure stage at random, with the probability of each relative position in the task taken from the failures on the other tasks of the setting ( Section H.5 ). Over the eight agents, the concentration is 61% in the Standard Setting, against 37% for the baseline. The five weaker agents often fail before they reach any stage of a task ( Section 3.4 ), so much of this agreement could come from failures at the first stage. We therefore check the three strongest agents separately. Over the 217 Standard tests with at least two located failures among them, the concentration is 56%, against 31% for the baseline built from their failures on the other Standard tasks. The most common failure stage of a test is usually the first stage of its task: of the 264 Standard tests whose failures have a single most common stage, it is the first stage in 65% ( Section E.2 ). Coding agents and other language-model agents are evaluated mostly on digital tasks, such as resolving issues in real repositories [ 64 , 104 ] , completing tasks in a terminal [ 102 ] , machine learning engineering and research [ 14 , 143 ] , and operating websites and desktop applications [ 198 , 168 ] . The interface between the model and the computer affects how often an agent succeeds [ 174 ] , and other work measures how often agents exploit the tests that grade them [ 196 ] . The Embodied First Exam extends these evaluations to robotics tasks. In its Standard and Realtime settings, the agent must interpret sensor observations and often cannot undo an action. In every setting, a program that the agent cannot change grades each attempt. Language models for robots. Language models have been used to sequence skills into plans [ 1 , 54 ] , to write robot programs that call perception and control primitives [ 84 ] , to specify value maps or keypoint constraints that a planner turns into motions [ 53 , 52 ] , and to write reward functions [ 95 ] . Most of these systems pair a model with an interface designed for it. The Embodied First Exam evaluates unchanged general-purpose coding agents on the whole loop of reading an interface, choosing a control approach, carrying it out and revising it. In embodied benchmarks for language models [ 139 , 78 , 176 ] , the model chooses from an action space that the benchmark defines. The exam gives the agent the robot’s control interface, so the agent also decides how its actions are produced. Agentic robots and their harnesses. Recent work runs general-purpose agents on robots [ 125 ] with the three control approaches of Figure 1 . In direct control, GPT-6 Astra serves as the policy on manipulation benchmarks [ 191 , 199 , 61 ] . In code to control, coding agents write programs for simulated and real robots [ 150 , 62 , 50 , 56 ] , often with skill libraries or other added tools [ 93 , 48 ] . In train a policy, agents train policies in the real world or in simulation [ 165 , 83 ] . Most of these studies fix the control approach in advance. The exam supports all three approaches and leaves the choice to the agent. Harnesses for these agents differ in the action interface they provide [ 18 , 34 , 23 ] , in whether the agent can call learned policies as tools [ 80 , 33 ] , and in the support they add for tracking state and checking outcomes [ 157 , 30 ] . The exam runs each harness unmodified with the models of its own provider, and through an adapter with other providers’ models ( Appendix G ), so different harnesses can be compared on the same tasks. Benchmarks for robots. Most robotics benchmarks focus on visuomotor policy learning and evaluate learned policies, such as vision-language-action models [ 11 , 69 , 10 , 38 ] , on manipulation, household and humanoid tasks [ 87 , 17 , 76 , 106 , 132 ] . A learned policy receives only a fixed set of observations. A coding agent can run any program on its computer, so the exam adds rules about what the agent may access ( Section 2.1 ) and adapts 26 existing benchmarks ( Section A.2 ). Benchmarks for agentic robots have also appeared. CaP-X [ 32 ] varies the level of abstraction of the primitives that coding agents compose, RobotWorld [ 178 ] spans manipulation, locomotion, driving and aerial control, and EmbodiedSWE [ 134 ] and BiGym 2.0 [ 194 ] evaluate coding agents on dexterous and humanoid tasks. The Embodied First Exam covers 47 robots of nine types. It also separates what an agent can do with full access to the simulator from what it can do through the robot’s sensors alone. 5 Conclusion We introduced the Embodied First Exam, a unified evaluation framework that connects any coding agent to simulated robots and lets the agent execute actions in three ways. The exam contains 1,156 tasks on 47 robots. On the 391 hard core tasks that have both settings, the strongest agent reaches a success rate of 77% in the Privileged Setting but only 30% in the Standard Setting. In controlled runs with this agent, adding unlimited resets closes more of this gap than adding the exact object state, and at stages that need precision, resets help but the exact state alone does not. Failures on a task tend to happen at the same stage, weaker agents are much more likely than stronger agents to fail before making the required contact, and in the Standard and Realtime settings of a soccer task, agents trained a policy when given a GPU and a training library, and only trained policies won. The strongest agent already solves most tasks when it has full access to the simulator, but in the Standard and Realtime settings, which are designed to mirror how a real robot is deployed and tested, it still has much room for improvement. Future work. Running each core test several times will separate failures that an agent repeats from failures of a single run, and it will give confidence intervals for the comparisons between agents and between settings. We will also add more Realtime tasks, since the Realtime Setting has the largest share of tests that no agent solves. Finally, because the Standard Setting gives the agent only the robot’s sensor readings and control interface, the same interface can be used on physical hardware once there is a way to grade the outcome. In a first demonstration, two agents played a buzz wire game with a physical robot arm ( Appendix I ), and we plan to evaluate agents on real robots systematically through this interface. Limitations. Each agent makes a single attempt per test, the stage-level and control-approach labels come from agents, and all results are in simulation. Appendix J discusses these and further limitations. Acknowledgements We acknowledge and disclose the use of AI tools in coding and paper writing. We package every task in the Harbor format with a fixed directory structure. The file instruction.md gives the agent its prompt: the goal, the submission requirements, the limits, and every convention the agent cannot infer, including coordinate frames, units, the action layout, how to use the robot client, and the reset budget. Any goal specified as an image, video, or other file is placed inside the agent’s container and named in instruction.md . We store the task configuration in task.toml , which specifies the time limit, the CPU, memory, and disk caps of the agent’s container, the verifier settings, and task metadata (the simulator, the robot, and whether a reference solution exists). The environment/ directory defines the runtime via a Dockerfile for the agent’s workspace container and a docker-compose.yaml that adds the remaining containers (the simulator service in Standard and Realtime , and a resource monitor) along with their resource caps. Within it, environment/instance.json defines the frozen scene (the objects, the robot, and their initial state), which the simulator checks against a stored hash at every build and reset so every episode starts from the same state. The tests/ directory contains the verifier: its own Dockerfile and docker-compose.yaml , the entry script test.sh , and the grading code with the success rule, all running in an isolated container that the agent never sees. When a task has a reference solution, we include solution/solve.sh , which validates the task and is never shown to the agent, and we provide README.md to describe the task for human readers. Finally, we pin every base image to a fixed tag or digest so every run of a task uses the exact same software. Each attempt runs in a container with 2 to 16 CPU cores and 4 to 39 GiB of memory, with a GPU where the simulator or the task needs one, with a wall-clock limit of 30 minutes to four hours, depending on the suite, and with a token budget that no attempt reaches. The harness can reach only the API of its model and the package registries, except in Embodied Olympics - Fun, which runs with open network access. The harness’s web search is off in every task. A proxy between the harness and the model provider forwards only the requests to the model’s API. Reference solutions and integrity checks. Every task must fail when the robot does nothing and pass with its reference solution wherever one exists (686 tasks have a full reference solution, and 78 have a partial one). Tasks without a reference solution rely on the original success rule and a human review. Reference solutions never enter the agent’s container, and the solutions of the original benchmarks are removed from the container images. Before a suite is added, an agent is instructed to pass its tasks without solving them. If the agent succeeds on any task, the suite is not added until its verifier is fixed. Leaderboard rules. Each agent makes one attempt per test. An attempt that ends with an infrastructure or API error is rerun, and the latest finished attempt counts. A test counts as solved when the verifier reports success for its attempt. We report each setting over all of its core tests, including those of Embodied Olympics - Fun, whose tasks run with open network access, and of the suites with their own runtime, Embodied Olympics - Match and RoboEngineer, and Figure 9 gives the results by suite. The harness comparison uses the 456 core tests of the Standard Setting. When we pool the three strongest agents, as in Sections 3.1 and D , each test counts once per agent, and rates are reported per attempt. Approach labels. For Section 3.5 , an agent read the logs of a random sample of 1,489 attempts and assigned each attempt the approach that produced its graded answer. Code to control covers three kinds of program. An open-loop program computes the whole motion in advance, as waypoints, key frames, inverse-kinematics solutions or a recorded sequence, and runs it without feedback. A closed-loop program reads the state or the cameras at every control step or action chunk and adjusts its commands. A parameter search finds a passing motion, or its parameters, by trying many episodes and resets or by running an optimizer such as the cross-entropy method (CEM), CMA-ES, or a grid or random search. In Direct control , the agent sends one action chunk at a time and decides on the next one after looking at images or readings. In Train a policy , the agent first trains a control policy, such as a neural network or a reinforcement-learning policy, which then controls the robot. Attempts that switched approach midway or used two approaches equally are labeled mixed, and attempts that only explored or never ran anything are labeled as having no control plan. Figure 1 shows an example of each approach. Appendix C Interface, Grading and Physics This section expands Section 2.1 with the protocol rules and each suite’s values and examples. C.1 Interface Privileged Setting: a simulator library in the agent’s container. The agent’s container holds the simulator of the suite and a small Python library that the exam provides, which we call the tooling. The tooling rebuilds the frozen scene, resets it, advances it by applying actions, reads the state of every object and records each applied action as one row of an array. The agent may also call the planning primitives of the original benchmark, such as the grasp and place primitives of RoboTwin 2.0 with its CuRobo planner, or write its own planner, optimizer or learned controller. Whatever the agent calls, the tooling records the low-level action row applied at every control step, and the answer is this array of actions, saved as a trajectory file. For RoboTwin 2.0, for example, a row has 34 columns (16 joint position targets, 16 joint velocity targets and two gripper commands) and one row is applied per 4 ms physics step. In the Privileged tests of Embodied Olympics - Match and EmbodiedSWE the answer is instead a program (one that plays the match, or one that controls the robot through the task), and the verifier runs it in a fresh simulator. Standard Setting: the simulator as a service. In the Standard and Realtime settings, the simulator runs as the simulator service in a separate container that the agent cannot access. The agent reaches it over HTTP through a Python client library that the exam provides. One call returns the specification of the robot: the action types with their layouts, units and limits; every joint with its position, velocity and effort limits and drive gains; the grippers and tool frames; the cameras with their intrinsics and mounting; the layout of the joint readings; the conventions of the world frame; and the budgets. A second call returns observations: either the current camera images, without advancing the simulation, or the images after the simulation has run for a requested number of seconds with the last command held. A third call executes an action chunk of T T rows, each of dimension d d , and returns the video of the execution. Where the service offers it, a fourth computes forward and inverse kinematics on the model of the robot without advancing physics. A reset call exists only in tasks that grant resets. Every response contains the camera images (RGB, and depth on request), one row of joint readings per image (joint positions and velocities, gripper opening, tool pose and an estimate of the drive effort), the camera calibration, the simulated time and the time the agent has left. Object poses, segmentation, scene files, the seed and the success rule are never sent. Contact forces on objects are not sent either, except as safety telemetry that a suite documents field by field, as in RoboARC, and that never gives an object’s pose. Any other request, such as a query for object states or for success, returns an error. The images come back as binary data that the agent’s code can store and process, so they do not have to pass through the model’s context. From a chunk to motion. Joint position rows are d ​ t dt seconds apart, where d ​ t dt is a multiple of the control period and at most 0.5 s. The service rejects a value of d ​ t dt that breaks this rule and does not round it. The service streams the rows to the drives of the joints in the way a position controller on a real robot follows a trajectory, either interpolating between rows or holding each row until the next. It clips any value outside the joint limits and reports each clipped entry in the response. End-effector rows go through the inverse kinematics or the motion planner of the suite. Where the original benchmark controls the robot through a higher-level controller, the agent sends the commands of that controller, as in RoboCasa and in the whole-body controller of BiGym 2.0. Each suite keeps its own control rate and physics step. Control rates range from 5 Hz (the mobile robot in MolmoSpaces) to 500 Hz (AutoBio, where one row is one 2 ms physics step), and one control step runs up to 100 physics steps (VLABench). The success rule is checked after every action chunk and every observation request that advances physics, and in most suites the first success ends the episode. In the Standard tests of RoboDojo, a success does not end the episode, so that the end of the episode does not tell the agent that it has succeeded, which would give it a success check to try against until it passes. The episode ends at the task’s step limit, when the agent ends it, at its time limit, or when a check of the task fails, and it passes if the success rule held at any point, which matches RoboDojo’s own success within its step limit. Realtime Setting: a simulation that keeps running. The Realtime Setting uses the same calls. From the first request that advances physics, the simulation advances one control step every S S control periods of wall-clock time, whatever the agent does, where S ≥ 1 S\geq 1 is a slowdown factor that each task states. A submitted chunk is answered at once and replaces the queued actions starting at a control step that the agent specifies. When the queue is empty, the robot holds its last target. The agent can therefore run a program in its own container that observes the robot and sends new chunks in a loop. By default, S S is 1 when the simulator runs at least three times faster than real time, and larger otherwise, so that the service can run each step on time. The service records the steps it could not run on time, and the time the robot stood still because of them. Suites with their own runtime. RoboEngineer and Embodied Olympics - Match use their own runtime instead of the simulator service, in their Standard tests and in the Realtime tests of Embodied Olympics - Match. In RoboEngineer, the agent tunes a controller in a simulator that the agent can access and deploys the controller to a second simulator, which the agent cannot access and whose dynamics differ from those of the first in ways not disclosed to the agent. The verifier then scores the controller that the agent selects on episodes that the agent has not seen. Step limits and time budgets. The protocol adds no step limit to the Standard and Realtime settings. RoboDojo keeps its own step limit ( Figure 3 ), and many other suites cap the episode length, which the instruction states. A Privileged trajectory may have at most a stated number of rows. The main limit is the agent’s wall-clock time, from 30 minutes to four hours depending on the suite, and it is the same in every setting of a task. Each response reports the time left, counted from the first request of the agent. One call may return only a stated number of images and may run for at most 15 minutes by default, so that one call cannot exhaust the memory of the service or occupy it indefinitely. Limits. The limits of a test are the agent’s wall-clock time and token budget, the resets that the task grants, the slowdown S S in the Realtime Setting, the per-call limits above, and the CPU, memory, process, disk and GPU memory caps of each container, which a resource monitor records and enforces where the container runtime does not. Privileged tests allow unlimited resets, and Standard and Realtime tests allow no resets unless the task grants a budget, which its instruction states ( Section 2.2 ). These values are part of the configuration of a result, and results are compared only between runs with the same configuration. C.2 Grading Success rules. Each task is graded by one success rule, a condition on the simulator state. Adapted suites keep the success rule of the original benchmark and tighten it in some tasks. Examples are the success function of a RoboTwin 2.0 task, the BDDL goal of a BEHAVIOR-1K activity evaluated on OmniGibson object states, and the solved flag of MyoSuite. For the suites that we built, we wrote our own rules on the simulator state. The success rule of Embodied Olympics - Fun checks the suite’s scoring checkpoints, legal contacts and fouls after every 1 ms physics step, RoboDynamicExam checks ordered phases (a catch, a hold, a crossing, a release) after every 1 ms physics step, and DexHandExam computes its checkpoints from contact pairs and contact forces. The instruction states each condition of the success rule with the same thresholds that the rule uses. Settle and hold windows. Some rules are checked over a time window. A hold window requires the goal to hold for a stated time, so that an object in flight or a grasp that is about to slip does not count. In BEHAVIOR-1K the goal must hold for 1 s with the object released and at rest. MyoSuite requires its solved flag for 1 s (5 s for the Baoding balls), BiGym 2.0 requires its goal for 1 s and no fall during the episode, and ManiGuard requires its goal for 10 consecutive control steps and no safety violation during the episode. A settle window keeps the simulation running with the last command held while the rule is checked, as in RoboLab, which holds the last command for 2 s. Rules without a window are checked on a single state. A window is part of the rule of the task and applies in every setting. It is checked on the live simulator in the Standard and Realtime settings and after the last row of the replay in the Privileged Setting. Privileged Setting: a fresh replay. The verifier rebuilds the frozen scene in a fresh process, checks that its initial state matches the stored hash, replays the submitted actions open loop and applies the success rule to the replay. By default it replays once. In BEHAVIOR-1K, AM-Bench, RoboDojo and RoboLab, whose simulators do not replay bit for bit, it replays up to three times, each in a fresh process, and the attempt passes if any replay succeeds. Where the answer is a program, as in Embodied Olympics - Match and EmbodiedSWE, the verifier runs the program in a fresh simulator instead. Because only the actions are replayed, a change that the agent made directly to the state of its own simulator, such as moving an object directly, has no effect on the result. A file that is not a valid trajectory fails. Standard and Realtime settings: the live episode. The simulator service checks the success rule on the episode that it ran, and the verifier reports the result from the service’s record of the episode. The service checks the hash of the start state when the episode begins and after every reset. When the agent’s time is up, the program that runs the evaluation stops every process in the agent’s container before it signals the service. From then on the service refuses every request that would advance the simulator, runs any settle window, checks the success rule on the live simulator and writes the result with the recorded trajectory. The verifier reports that result, and nothing is replayed to decide it. The result depends only on the state of the simulator, never on what the agent reports. An episode in which the agent’s actions cause a simulator error fails. A failure of the infrastructure, such as a scene that does not rebuild to its stored start state or a service that crashed, gives no result, and the whole attempt can be rerun. With resets, only the last episode is graded. We also record whether the first episode, before any reset, succeeded, and report this as first-episode success. Isolation. The verifier runs in its own container, built from the task’s grading files, without network, and receives only the files that the task declares for grading: the trajectory file (or the submitted program) in the Privileged Setting and the record of the simulator service, which checks the success rule, in the Standard and Realtime settings. It first removes from the agent’s output directory every entry that is not a regular file, such as a link. No writable volume is shared between the agent’s container and the containers of the simulator service and the verifier, and the simulator service opens no port on the host. The agent can never read the reference solution or the files of the verifier, and in the Standard and Realtime settings it cannot read the code of the success rule either. A program submitted as the answer runs under a user account without administrator rights and without network, cannot read the files of the simulator or the verifier, and every process it starts ends before the result is written. The result is written before the video of the attempt is rendered, and the video never changes it. C.3 Physics Simulators. Each suite keeps the simulator of its original benchmark, and new suites use the simulator that suits their physics. MuJoCo [ 149 ] runs most suites, directly or through robosuite, dm_control and MyoSuite, in versions 3.2 to 3.14 and mostly on the CPU. NVIDIA Isaac Sim and Isaac Lab with PhysX run RoboLab, RoboDojo, YAMLab, AM-Bench, LabUtopia and EmbodiedSWE, and OmniGibson on Isaac Sim runs BEHAVIOR-1K, ManiGuard and iSpatialGym. SAPIEN 3 with PhysX runs RoboTwin 2.0, and through ManiSkill 3 it also runs RoboPaint and RoboDynamicExam. Genesis runs RoboWits. RoboEngineer runs MuJoCo, and its drone tasks add learned dynamics models, a learned ground-effect model of a quadrotor and a wind-force model fitted to published flight logs. What is simulated. All suites simulate rigid bodies with frictional contact, joint limits and actuator limits. Several also simulate deformable and particle matter: cloth in the blanket tasks of RoboARC (a deterministic GPU solver) and the folding tasks of RoboDojo (GPU PhysX), ropes and cords as soft bodies in RoboARC, liquids as GPU PhysX particles in RoboDojo, and the object states of OmniGibson in BEHAVIOR-1K, where objects can be sliced, cooked or filled with particles. MyoSuite moves its limbs with simulated muscles instead of motors, and RoboPaint models brushes and pens on paper. Physics steps range from 1 ms (Embodied Olympics - Fun and RoboDynamicExam) to 1/60 s (LabUtopia), with 2 ms in DexHandExam, DexJoCo and AutoBio and 1/120 s in iSpatialGym, ManiGuard, AM-Bench and YAMLab. Determinism. Privileged grading replays the submitted actions, so when we build a task we check whether fresh replays of a trajectory end in the same final state on the hardware used for evaluation. Where they do not, the verifier replays up to three times ( Section C.2 ). The frozen scene is also rebuilt and checked against its stored start-state hash before every episode. Where a task does not replay reliably at all, as with the GPU cloth and liquids of RoboDojo, the task is offered only in the Standard Setting, where the live episode is graded, and its video is a recording of the live episode rather than a replay. Realism rules. The robot moves only through its own actuators and the controllers stated in the task. In the Standard and Realtime settings the agent has no way to set the state of the simulator, and in the Privileged Setting only actions are replayed, so moving an object directly has no effect on the grade in any setting. The suites we built for the exam add stricter rules. In Embodied Olympics - Fun, the simulator never attaches a held object rigidly to the hand, never pins a body or moves it with a motion-capture constraint, and never sets a velocity directly after the start. Balls come from ball machines and goalkeepers are force-limited mechanisms. Each robot uses its own hands and actuators, modeled on measured actuator data from the manufacturer (torque-speed curves, rotor inertia and friction). Where we increased a parameter above the manufacturer’s value, the increase is at most a factor of 1.5 to 2 and is stated in the task; the one stated exception is the grip on the high bar. DexHandExam has no rigid attachments or scripted object motion, so objects move only through contact and gravity, and in the furniture assembly tasks of RoboARC, the robot holds parts only by finger friction. Where a suite keeps a grasp aid, its documentation states it. In the knot, ring and rope tasks of RoboARC, a rigid attachment holds a grasped object and slips when the pull exceeds 60 to 65 N for 20 ms. Adapted suites keep the grasp model of the original benchmark, such as the assisted grasping of OmniGibson and the hand of MetaWorld+, which a motion-capture constraint moves. Appendix E Further Analysis This section gives the full analyses behind the key takeaways of Sections 3.2 , 3.3 , 3.4 and 3.5 , in the same order. The definitions and statistics that these analyses use are in Appendices G and H . E.1 Causes of the Drop from the Privileged to the Standard Setting To measure how much two features that the Standard Setting lacks contribute to the drop, we ran GPT-6 Astra in Codex on the default tests of the core tasks with one or both of them added to the Standard interface ( Figure 11(a) , Appendix G ). The first feature is a query that returns the state of every object, including its pose, size and velocity. The second is unlimited resets together with calls that save the simulator state and restore it later. On the 391 core tasks that have both settings, GPT-6 Astra solves 28% with the unmodified interface. The state query raises this rate to 45%, and unlimited resets raise it to 53%. With both features, the agent solves 68%, against 77% on their Privileged tests. With unlimited resets, GPT-6 Astra uses saved states to retry a hard stage without repeating the whole task. Of the 206 successes of the reset run on these tasks, 14% came in the first episode, and 66% came in an episode that started from a saved state. The stage labels show at which kinds of stages the Standard Setting raises the failure rate ( Figure 11(b) ). The failure rate of a stage type is the number of its stages at which attempts fail, divided by the number of its stages that attempts reach. We compare it between the two settings within each suite and average over the suites ( Table 12 ). Section H.5 defines the precise and coarse groups of stage types and gives the statistical tests. Among the stage types compared in at least two suites, the failure rate rises by 24 to 46 percentage points for precise types and by 1 to 20 points for coarse types. The failure rate of cleaning, which is in neither group, also rises by 51 points. Pooled within suites, the failure rate rises from 22% to 56% at precise stages and from 22% to 38% at coarse stages. Figure 12 shows an example, and Section H.6 gives it in full. The difference between the two groups is smaller, but still significant, when the same agent reaches the same stage of a task in both settings. There, the rise is 25 points at precise stages and 15 points at coarse stages. At follow-path stages, most of them waypoints of DexToolBench, the rate rises by 11 points in this comparison. Without these stages, the rise at precise stages is 33 points. Combining the controlled runs with the stage labels shows which feature helps at which kind of stage ( Figure 11(c) , Table 14 ). We take the Standard tests that GPT-6 Astra failed in the baseline run of the controlled runs and group them by the type of the failure stage. Of these failures, 91 are at precise stages and 97 are at coarse stages. Some of these failures would turn into successes if the test were simply run again. We therefore compare each run with the run without the success signal, a further controlled run in which the interface no longer reports when an episode succeeds. This run repeats the same tests and changes only the success signal, so we use it as a rough approximation of a second attempt. Its success rate is within 4 points of the baseline’s ( Table 5 ), but this run does not separate the variation between attempts from the effect of hiding the success signal. At precise stages, the state query solves 21% of the failures, against 19% for the run without the success signal. Unlimited resets solve 36%, and 31 of these 33 successes came after a reset or a restored state. The agent did call the state query in the runs that offer it. At precise stages, adding the state query to the resets raises the share solved from 36% to 47%. At coarse stages, the state query alone solves 34% of the failures, against 14% for the run without the success signal, and the two features together solve 67%, against 60% in the Privileged Setting. With both features, the agent solves 47% of the precise failures, compared with 73% in the Privileged Setting, and most of this remaining gap lies at follow-path, thread and assemble stages. Neither added feature lets the agent advance the physics one step at a time or call planning primitives, as the Privileged Setting does, and these abilities may account for part of the remaining gap. The comparisons in this subsection have limits. The controlled runs use one agent. The labels cannot separate errors of perception from errors of control ( Section H.5 ). E.2 Where Agents Fail Failures on a task tend to happen at the same stage ( Figure 13 ). We measure this by the concentration of failure stages, which is the probability that two of the failed attempts on a test, chosen at random, failed at the same stage. Each agent makes one attempt per test, so the two attempts always come from different agents, and the measure pools the agents that failed the test. On the Standard tests that at least two agents failed at located stages, on tasks with at least two stages, the concentration averages 61%, and we compare it with two baselines. In the first baseline, each agent fails at a stage chosen at random, with the probability of each stage position set by how often failures occur at that position on the other tasks of the same setting, so early stages are more likely. This baseline gives 37%. In the second baseline, an agent fails at each stage with the failure rate of the stage’s type on the other tasks, and this baseline gives 48% ( Table 16 ). For the three strongest agents alone, the concentration is 56%, against 31% for the first baseline built from their failures on the other tasks ( Section H.5 ). Failures therefore concentrate more than the stage types predict. The most common failure stage is usually the first stage of the task. Of the 264 Standard tests whose failures have a single most common stage, this stage is the first stage in 65%, and it is the first stage in 56% of those whose tasks have at least two types of stages. In a RoboDynamicExam task, for example, seven of the eight Standard attempts caught a thrown ball in a cup and then failed at the recatch, in three different ways ( Figure 14 , with all eight attempts in Section H.6 ). Failure rates differ between stage categories ( Figure 13 ). Pooled over the Privileged and Standard settings, attempts fail at 66% of the material stages (such as wiping or pouring) that they reach, at 45% of the body stages (such as walking or keeping balance) and at 20% of the mechanism stages. Within a category, the rate also depends on how the robot must act. Opening a door or a lid fails in 38% of cases, whereas closing one fails in 19% when it does not already hold at the start ( Section H.5 ), and pressing fails in 3% ( Table 18 ). Relation stages, which place or fit one object relative to another ( Table 7 ), fail more often when two arms act together or the legs carry the robot’s weight than when one hand acts ( Figure 27 in Section H.5 ). However, which stage types fail most differs from suite to suite. When we rank each pair of stage types by their failure rates on all suites but one, the ranking holds in the held-out suite for 55% of the 313 pairs, close to what a random ranking would give. Whether an attempt fails at a stage that it reaches is also predicted better by the identity of the agent than by the stage type ( Section H.5 ). Each category occurs mostly in particular suites and with particular robots, so these rates do not show that the category itself causes the failures. The most common failure stage of a task is also usually the same in both settings. Of the tasks whose failures have a single most common stage in both settings, 81% have the same such stage in the two settings, compared with 49% if each setting’s failure stages were sampled independently. Excluding the tasks whose most common failure stage is the first stage in both settings, the share is still 57%, compared with 9% for random failure stages ( Section H.5 ). Of the attempts that get past the stage at which the other agents on the same test most often failed, 64% pass the test in the Privileged Setting and 33% in the Standard Setting. Part of this link between getting past the stage and passing follows from the order of the stages, because an attempt that gets past the common stage is already closer to the end of the task. E.4 Control Approaches A separate coding agent read the logs of 1,489 attempts, sampled at random, and labeled each attempt with the approach that produced its graded answer ( Appendix B ). Many Privileged tests give the agent a GPU to run the simulator, and their instructions name no training library. Apart from these, only eight tests give the agent a GPU: the GPU versions of the soccer match below and of a table tennis match, in each setting, and two RoboEngineer tests that ask the agent to train a walking policy. The labels split Code to control into three kinds. Open-loop programs compute the whole motion in advance. Closed-loop programs read the robot’s state or cameras as they run. Parameter searches try many episodes to find a passing motion. In the Privileged Setting, agents use the simulator to compute or search for a motion before they submit it. Most of their programs in this setting are closed-loop, and 114 of the 549 programs search over many episodes. The same agent can use different control approaches for the same task in different settings. On a RoboTwin 2.0 task, GPT-6 Astra scripted a hand-over of the bottles with the simulator’s motion primitives in the Privileged Setting. In the Standard Setting, it sent end-effector commands one at a time and checked the camera images after each command ( Appendix F ). In the Standard Setting, Direct control places the model inside the control loop, so the robot waits for the model’s reasoning before each chunk. In the Realtime Setting, the environment keeps running, and most attempts run the control loop in a program. In a badminton match of Embodied Olympics - Match, GPT-6 Astra let a program track the shuttlecock and swing the racket. Appendix G Controlled Runs This section defines the controlled runs of Sections 3.2 and 3.4 ( Figures 11(a) and 15 ). Baseline. Every controlled run of Figure 11(a) uses GPT-6 Astra in Codex with the same reasoning effort, time limit and task versions as the leaderboard run of Figure 7 , and the baseline is that leaderboard run. Each run covers the default tests of the 456 core tasks, and Table 5 and Figure 11(a) compare the runs on the 391 core tasks that have both a Privileged and a Standard test.
Each run changes one or two features of the Standard interface and nothing else. Apart from these features, every task behaves exactly as in the leaderboard run. Object-state query. We add two read-only calls to the Standard interface: one lists the objects in the scene, and one returns their state. The state consists of position, orientation, size and bounding box, linear and angular velocity, and the joint positions of the robot and of articulated objects such as doors and drawers. In Embodied Olympics - Match, the state of the opponent and of the ball counts as object state. The calls do not advance the simulation or use up simulated time, and they never return success flags, scores, thresholds or goal regions. The only progress value they return is the paint coverage of the canvas in RoboPaint. We remove the fields that would reveal what the agent is meant to find out itself: goal markers and zones, objects that appear only later in a BEHAVIOR-1K task, hint notes in RoboCasa365, and hazard labels in Embodied Safety Exam. Unlimited resets with saved states. The reset budget of every task is raised to 1,000, and the interface gains calls that save the current simulator state under a handle and restore it later. The simulator service still grades the last episode as it runs. Restoring a saved state does not erase a rule violation that has already been recorded. In Embodied Safety Exam, for example, recorded violations remain after a restore. Both. This run combines the two features above. No success signal. The interface no longer reports that an episode has succeeded. The episode does not end at success, and no success flag, progress or score is returned. The agent ends the episode itself with a finish call, or the episode ends when the time runs out or the task’s own failure rule is triggered. The simulator service checks the success rule on the state at the end of the episode, so a success that was reached and then undone counts as a failure. Harness comparison. For Figure 15 , we ran GPT-6 Astra in Claude Code, Claude Opus 5.5 in Codex, and Gemini 4 Argon in Codex and in Claude Code, with the same tasks, time limits and reasoning effort as the leaderboard runs. Codex and Claude Code support only their own provider’s API format, so an adapter passes their requests to the other provider and translates the answers back. The adapter also adapts the system prompt and model metadata, so these runs only approximate a native setup. We score every run on the 456 core tests of the Standard Setting, the tests of the Standard leaderboard. H.1 Labeling Procedure Task graph. For each of 427 core tasks, the labeling agent, GPT-6.1 Sol in Codex, wrote one graph of stages, such as the graph of Figure 6(a) in Section 3 , from the task instruction, the verifier’s code and a successful attempt when one exists. A stage is a change in the world that a person would list when describing how to do the task, such as “open the fridge” or “put the first mango in the fridge”. Conditions on how well a stage must be done, such as thresholds, alignment or “without touching X”, belong to that stage’s condition and are not stages of their own. A condition that is already true at the start and must still be true at the end, such as a closed fridge, becomes a stage of its own that is met from the start, so that the progress map shows when an attempt breaks it. Each stage has a stage type, its prerequisite stages, a condition that can be checked on the replay video, and the contact it needs. The contact is a list of pairs of the robot part that touches and how it touches (who × \times how), one list for each way of doing the stage. The edges of the graph give the order in which the stages must be met for the task to pass; a stage that holds from the start can hold before its prerequisites. The stage types fall into nine categories ( Table 7 ). For example, material stages change the state of a material, such as pouring or cleaning, and body stages move the robot’s own body, such as walking or keeping balance. The 427 graphs have 2,411 stages, 5.6 per task on average. Progress map. For each attempt, the labels give the time intervals during which each stage is met, on the timeline of the graded episode’s replay video ( Figure 6(b) ). The condition of a stage can stop holding after it has been met, so the progress map can go down. Progress is read from the replay video, or from the logs where there is no video; attempts without a submitted trajectory usually have no readable progress. An attempt reaches a stage if it meets the stage’s condition at some moment or if the stage is its failure stage. Failure stage, phenomena and root causes. Each failed attempt is assigned at most one failure stage, so that failures can be counted by stage and compared across attempts and agents. A failure stage is located when the labels give one.
If an irreversible event occurred, such as a fall or a violation of a task rule that makes the attempt fail, the failure stage is the stage during which the event occurred, unless an earlier stage had already failed permanently. Otherwise, it is the stage with the earliest uncorrected mistake among the stages whose condition was never met. Mistakes that the agent later corrected do not count. The labels also record where the attempt was when it failed: during the stage, between stages, while exploring, before the first stage or at the final success check. Up to two failure phenomena describe what visibly went wrong at the failure stage, and up to two root causes describe why it happened, with a video time or log entry supporting each cause. A time limit, a used-up budget or the agent stopping is never a phenomenon or a cause: the label names what was going wrong when the attempt ended. A root cause is given only when the evidence supports it, and it may stay at the category level “Control” when the subtype cannot be determined, as in 47% of the analyzed failures. Writer and reviewer. GPT-6.1 Sol in Codex, at high reasoning effort, wrote every graph and the label files of most attempts from the inputs above, and Claude Sonnet 5.5 in Claude Code wrote those of the remaining attempts from the same inputs with the same prompts. Claude Sonnet 5.5 in Claude Code reviewed each file, with up to two rounds of fixes and a final confirmation round. The prompts were frozen before the final labeling. Section 3.5 uses a second set of labels, which give the control approach of a sample of attempts ( Appendix B ). Agents wrote both sets of labels. Human oversight. We defined the taxonomy ( Section H.2 ) and settled it in team meetings before the final labeling, and the agents assign its values to each task and attempt. Earlier versions of the labels were reviewed by hand and in team meetings. Each error we found was written into the prompts as a worked example: the task or attempt, the wrong label, the right label and the rule. Most of the worked examples in the final prompts come from this review, and the rest come from rechecks by agents. The labels used here were then written again with the final prompts. The failure rate of a stage is the number of attempts that failed at the stage divided by the number of attempts that reached it. Attempts that never reached the stage are not counted, so the rate measures whether an attempt that gets to the stage fails there. For the rate of a stage type, each stage of that type counts separately, so an attempt that reaches three put-in stages adds three stages. A stage counts as failed when it is the attempt’s failure stage, and as met when its condition held at some moment, even if it already held at the start or stopped holding later. The two settings differ in two conditions that matter for Section 3.2 . In the Privileged Setting the agent reads the exact pose of every object and can reset the simulator without limit, so it can compute a precise motion from the full state, test it and try again within the time limit before it submits an answer. In the Standard Setting it must estimate poses from camera images and depth, the episode is graded as it runs, and the task limits the number of resets, so a precise stage is attempted on estimates and an error can remain in the graded episode. The comparisons of Section 3.2 concern the stages of a task. They do not concern how the agent controls the robot, which changes with the setting ( Section 3.5 ). We split the stage types into two groups by their definitions in the taxonomy ( Table 7 ). A stage type is precise if it needs a tight fit, a mated or threaded relation, a target pose or path, or contact with a moving object. The 13 precise types are insert, assemble, screw/unscrew, thread/hang, connect, adjust pose, follow path, stabilize, regrasp, throw, strike, catch and dribble. The 12 coarse types are place, put in, take out, gather, pick up, push, pull, open, close, press, switch on/off and navigate. All other types, such as the material types and the body types other than navigate, are in neither group. Section 3.2 , Section E.1 and Table 14 use these groups. The concentration of a test measures how much the agents that failed it agree on the stage at which they failed. We measure it with the Simpson index: for a test that n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 agents failed at located stages, with n k n_{k} of them at stage k k , it is D = ∑ k n k ​ ( n k − 1 ) / ( n ⁡ ( n − 1 ) ) D=\sum_{k}n_{k}(n_{k}-1)/\bigl(n(n-1)\bigr) , the share of pairs of these agents that failed at the same stage. We average D D over the tests of a setting whose tasks have at least two counted stages, and resample tests 2,000 times for the 95% interval. If each agent’s failure stage is sampled independently with probabilities q k q_{k} , the expected value of D D is ∑ k q k 2 \sum_{k}q_{k}^{2} . The random-stage value, the gray bars of Figure 13 , assumes that each agent fails at a random stage but keeps one property of the observed failures: where in a task they usually fall. First, we order the K K stages of each task graph topologically, breaking ties by their order in the task file, so that the i i -th stage covers the interval [ ( i − 1 ) / K , i / K ] [(i-1)/K,i/K] . Second, over all failures in the other tasks of the same setting that have at least two stages, we build a ten-bin histogram of the relative position ( i − 1 / 2 ) / K (i-1/2)/K of the failure stage. Third, each stage receives the mass of this histogram over its interval, and these masses are the probabilities q k q_{k} . A simpler uniform baseline, q k = 1 / K q_{k}=1/K for a task with K K stages, gives 28% in the Privileged Setting, 28% in the Standard Setting and 30% in the Realtime Setting. This baseline ignores that early stages fail more often, so the main text does not use it. The rates of Figures 13 and 27 show an association between kinds of stages and failure. They cannot be read as causal effects, for three reasons. First, each kind of stage occurs mostly in particular suites and with particular robots: the part that makes contact depends on the robot, so legs and torso belong to legged robots, and most material stages come from RoboPaint and Embodied Safety Exam. Second, stages differ in how much the robot must do in them. A relation stage usually covers grasping, carrying and releasing its object, because the task graphs list picking up as a stage of its own in only 11 of the 229 tasks with relation stages. A rate per stage therefore compares units of different size. Third, the taxonomy records what a stage changes but not how much contact it needs. Explaining the rates by contact therefore requires a judgment of what each kind of stage asks of the robot. Opening a door or a lid fails at 38% of the stages that attempts reach. Closing one fails at 19% when it does not already hold at the start, and at 7% if the doors and fridges that are closed from the start count too. Relation stages fail at 36% of the stages that attempts reach with one hand or gripper, at 52% with two arms and at 65% with the legs or torso. Rates with tool contact can be lower or higher than with direct contact: stages on moving objects fail less often when the robot acts through a cup or a racket, while pose stages fail more often when it moves an object through a tool. The same mode of contact can be easy or hard: stages done by pressing or wiping fail at 8% of the stages that attempts reach when they press on a mechanism, such as a button, and at 69% when they wipe or press on a material. The pose, relation and dynamic categories lie between these extremes, and their order differs between the two settings, so Figure 13 , which counts both settings together, does not rank these three reliably. The labels used here were written and reviewed by agents ( Section H.1 ), and GPT-6.1 Sol, which wrote most of them, is from the same family as GPT-6 Astra. Appendix I Real-Robot Demonstration Because the Standard Setting gives the agent only the robot’s sensor readings and its control interface ( Section 2.1 ), an interface of the same kind can connect a coding agent to a physical robot. As a first test, we connected two coding agents to a physical robot arm, and each agent played a buzz wire game for eight trials in a row ( Figure 33 ). This demonstration is not part of the exam, and its results are not included in any success rate in this paper. (a) (b) Figure 33 : Buzz wire game on a physical robot arm. (a) Four frames in time order of one trial of GPT-6 Astra and one of Claude Opus 5.5, with a close-up of the light and the buzzer and the command being executed. In the last frame of Claude Opus 5.5’s trial, the loop has touched the wire and the light is on. (b) The plot gives the progress score of each agent in each of its eight trials. Task. In a buzz wire game, a player guides a metal loop along a bent wire from one end to the other without touching the wire. The loop and the wire belong to one electric circuit, so a contact closes the circuit and turns on a light and a buzzer. In our setup, both ends of the bent wire are fixed to a wooden board, and the robot holds the handle of the loop in its gripper ( Figure 33(a) ). A contact counts as a failure, as in the buzz wire tasks of RoboARC ( Section A.1 ), in which a simulated Franka Panda arm guides a metal loop along a wire. Commands and video. The agent controls the robot with discrete commands. Each command moves the gripper up, down, left or right or rotates it in either direction. In each panel of Figure 33(a) , the pad at the lower left highlights the command being executed, and the inset shows a close-up of the light and the buzzer, so a contact is visible in the video. Agents and trials. The two agents use GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5, the two models with the highest Standard success rates in Figure 7 . Each agent ran one sequence of eight trials on the same wire. Progress score. Each trial receives a progress score, the percentage of the wire that the loop covers before the trial ends. A score of 100% means that the loop passed along the whole wire without a contact. Results. Both agents score higher in the last four trials than in the first four, and GPT-6 Astra scores higher than Claude Opus 5.5 in every trial ( Figure 33(b) ). Averaged over the first four trials, the progress is 65.0% for GPT-6 Astra and 35.0% for Claude Opus 5.5. Averaged over the last four trials, the progress is 93.8% and 62.5%. GPT-6 Astra scores at least 80% in five of its eight trials, and Claude Opus 5.5 does so in two. GPT-6 Astra starts at 60% and passes along the whole wire in trials 7 and 8. Its progress falls once, from 80% in trial 3 to 60% in trial 4. Claude Opus 5.5 scores 0% in trial 1 and between 40% and 80% in trials 2 to 8, and it does not pass along the whole wire in any trial. Its progress varies more from trial to trial. It rises to 80% in trial 6, falls to 40% in trial 7 and returns to 80% in trial 8. In the trial shown in Figure 33(a) , Claude Opus 5.5 touches the wire, and the light in its close-up turns on. The two agents rank in the same order as in the Standard Setting in simulation, where GPT-6 Astra solves 33.6% of the Standard tests and Claude Opus 5.5 solves 25.0% ( Figure 7 ). Scope. The demonstration covers one task, one robot and two agents, with one sequence of eight trials per agent. It shows that a coding agent can control a physical robot through an interface of this kind, and that the progress of both agents rose over the eight trials. With one sequence per agent, the demonstration cannot separate this rise from variation between runs. It also does not measure how well success rates in simulation predict results on hardware ( Appendix J ). A systematic evaluation on physical robots, with a grading program and several runs per agent, remains future work. Appendix J Limitations Each result holds for one model in one harness. Each agent also makes a single attempt per test, so the results cannot separate an agent’s ability from the variation between runs. The core set excludes tasks that we judged easy and tasks that GPT-6 Luna solved in a screening run, so its success rates are lower than those of the full exam by an amount that differs between suites. Because GPT-6 Luna belongs to the same family as GPT-6 Astra, the screening may also bias the core set against tasks that this family solves. Many per-suite rates are based on few tests. In a suite with five or six tests, a single test changes the rate by 17 to 20 percentage points, and a single Realtime test changes an agent’s success rate over all Realtime tests by about two points. The stage-level and control-approach labels come from agents. The model that wrote the stage-level labels, GPT-6.1 Sol, belongs to the same family as GPT-6 Astra. This shared family could bias the label-based comparisons between agents. Of the 1,156 tasks, 392 have no reference solution, so we have not shown that each of them can be solved in every setting. Time limits differ between suites, the tasks of Embodied Olympics - Fun run with open network access, and results on tasks adapted from public benchmarks may partly reflect memorization of published solutions ( Appendix B ). Finally, all results outside Appendix I are in simulation. The demonstration in Appendix I covers one task on a physical robot, so how well the simulation results predict results on hardware remains open. Appendix K Extended Related Work This appendix extends the review in Section 4 . We first review benchmarks for coding agents and benchmarks for robot learning, and then work that uses language models for robots. We then review recent work that runs general-purpose agents on robots, together with the harnesses and benchmarks built for these agents. Benchmarks for coding agents. Language models and the agents built on them are evaluated mostly on digital tasks. These tasks include writing programs from a problem description [ 15 , 58 ] , resolving issues in real repositories [ 64 , 175 , 104 ] , completing long software tasks and tasks in a terminal [ 73 , 102 ] , engineering algorithms for hard optimization problems [ 57 ] , machine learning engineering and research [ 14 , 164 , 143 ] , writing efficient GPU kernels [ 116 ] , cybersecurity [ 189 ] , and operating websites, desktop applications and other tools [ 198 , 168 , 90 , 103 , 179 ] . Most of these agents interleave reasoning with tool calls [ 180 ] and revise their work from feedback [ 137 ] . SWE-agent [ 174 ] shows that the interface between the model and the computer affects how often an agent succeeds, and OpenHands [ 160 ] provides an open platform for building and evaluating such agents. Other work measures how often agents exploit the tests that grade them [ 196 ] and proposes practices for building rigorous agentic benchmarks [ 203 ] . The Embodied First Exam keeps each coding agent’s harness unchanged and extends these evaluations to robotics tasks. In its Standard and Realtime settings, the agent must interpret sensor observations and often cannot undo an action, and in the Realtime Setting the environment keeps running while the agent reasons. In every setting, a program that the agent cannot change grades each attempt. Benchmarks for robot learning. Most robotics benchmarks focus on visuomotor policy learning and evaluate learned policies, from imitation-learning policies [ 22 , 195 ] to generalist policies and vision-language-action models [ 12 , 11 , 112 , 69 , 67 , 10 , 120 , 122 , 121 , 110 , 38 ] trained on large robot datasets [ 113 , 66 ] . Recent world action models build policies on pretrained video generation models such as Cosmos [ 109 ] and predict future observations together with actions [ 68 , 182 , 94 ] , extending earlier work that extracts actions from generated videos [ 25 ] or learns a joint representation of video and actions [ 81 ] . These benchmarks are built on physics simulators, control suites and robot learning frameworks [ 149 , 147 , 98 , 105 , 111 , 202 , 41 , 146 , 144 , 123 , 39 ] . They cover tabletop and bimanual manipulation [ 59 , 184 , 100 , 87 , 63 , 124 , 172 , 99 , 190 , 17 , 16 , 193 , 8 , 110 ] , household activities [ 138 , 142 , 76 , 106 , 107 , 71 ] , humanoid and legged locomotion and whole-body control [ 132 , 3 , 194 , 187 ] , dexterous and tool manipulation [ 20 , 65 , 155 ] , musculoskeletal control [ 13 ] , physical reasoning and creative problem solving [ 55 , 85 ] , aerial manipulation [ 162 ] , laboratory automation [ 74 , 79 ] and safety [ 119 ] . Policies trained on real data are also evaluated in simulated counterparts of real setups [ 82 ] , on real robots at scale [ 201 , 9 ] and in learned world models [ 126 , 42 , 36 ] , which can also generate training data for policies [ 177 , 60 ] . Most of these benchmarks do not need to restrict what the evaluated system can access, because a learned policy receives only a fixed set of observations. A coding agent can run any program on its computer and could read the simulator’s files, so the exam adds rules about what the agent may access ( Section 2.1 ). The exam adapts 24 of these benchmarks and two others, EmbodiedSWE [ 134 ] and ABC Sim [ 4 ] , and keeps their scenes and success rules, so that coding agents can attempt their tasks under the exam’s rules ( Section A.2 ). Its Standard Setting gives the agent observations of the kind that a learned policy receives, so coding agents and learned policies can be compared with the same observations in future work. Language models for robot planning and control. Language models have been used to plan tasks for robots, write robot programs and design training signals. SayCan [ 1 ] , Inner Monologue [ 54 ] , ProgPrompt [ 141 ] , Text2Motion [ 86 ] and SayPlan [ 127 ] sequence learned or scripted skills into plans, PaLM-E [ 24 ] and Gemini Robotics-ER [ 38 , 37 ] train multimodal models for embodied reasoning, and embodied chain-of-thought reasoning [ 188 ] trains a vision-language-action model to reason about plans, sub-tasks, motions and object locations before it acts. AutoRT [ 2 ] uses foundation models to propose and assign tasks to a fleet of robots, and SOAR [ 200 ] uses them to propose tasks and practice them autonomously. Code as Policies [ 84 ] , ChatGPT for Robotics [ 151 ] , Instruct2Act [ 51 ] and RoboTool [ 171 ] turn instructions into programs that call perception and control primitives, and Code-as-Monitor [ 197 ] applies the same idea to failure detection, with a model that writes code to check spatio-temporal constraints during execution. VoxPoser [ 53 ] , ReKep [ 52 ] and MOKA [ 29 ] have a model specify value maps, keypoint constraints or keypoints selected from candidates marked on the image, which a planner or an optimizer then turns into motions. Language to Rewards [ 185 ] , Eureka [ 95 ] , Text2Reward [ 169 ] , DrEureka [ 96 ] and RDA [ 75 ] have a model write and refine reward functions, which a controller or a reinforcement learning algorithm then optimizes. RoboGen [ 161 ] and GenSim [ 156 ] generate simulated tasks and training data, and Voyager [ 154 ] builds a library of code skills in an open-ended game. Most of these systems pair a model with an interface designed for it, such as a set of primitives, a planner or a training pipeline. Primitives and planners contribute the kind of classical engineering that Goldberg [40] argues robotics still needs, because robot data are far scarcer than the internet data used to train vision-language models. The Embodied First Exam builds on this line of work and evaluates unchanged general-purpose coding agents on the whole loop of reading an interface, choosing a control approach, carrying it out and revising it. Embodied benchmarks for language models. ALFWorld [ 139 ] pairs text-based household tasks with their embodied versions and has become a common testbed for language-model agents [ 180 , 137 ] . Embodied Agent Interface [ 78 ] , EmbodiedBench [ 176 ] and VisualAgentBench [ 91 ] evaluate language models as decision makers in simulated households and other embodied environments, and MineDojo [ 28 ] provides open-ended Minecraft tasks for embodied agents. Embodied Agent Interface divides embodied decision making into goal interpretation, subgoal decomposition, action sequencing and transition modeling, and EmbodiedBench evaluates both high-level tasks and low-level navigation and manipulation. In these benchmarks the model chooses from an action space that the benchmark defines, which ranges from high-level skills such as picking up an object to discretized end-effector poses. The exam gives the agent the robot’s control interface, so the agent also decides how its actions are produced. Agentic robots. A growing body of recent work runs general-purpose agents on robots, many with GPT-6 Astra [ 115 ] or Claude Fable [ 6 ] as the model. Recent surveys organize this work by model, data, environment and harness [ 125 ] and compare agents that use robots with agents that use computers [ 19 ] . This work differs in two respects: the control approach that the agent uses, and the harness that connects the model to the robot. This paragraph groups the work by the three control approaches of Figure 1 , and the next paragraph reviews harnesses. Direct control. In this approach, the agent observes the scene and outputs each action itself. Several studies use GPT-6 Astra as the policy in this way. They evaluate it on RoboDojo, RoboTwin 2.0 and other benchmarks [ 191 , 199 , 130 ] , on memory-dependent manipulation tasks [ 61 ] , on contact-rich tasks with tactile feedback [ 163 ] , and with demonstrations and its own interaction history in context [ 88 , 21 ] . Code to control. In this approach, the agent writes programs that produce the robot’s actions. Several studies give a general-purpose coding agent the robot’s programming interface and add few tools for the task. FAEA [ 150 ] runs the unmodified Claude Agent SDK on simulated manipulation benchmarks with access to the simulator state, and Agent as Policy [ 62 ] has a general-purpose agent interpret camera images, write programs and send motion commands on real robots. Claude Code (Opus 5) and Codex (GPT-5.6 Sol and GPT-6 Astra) write task and motion planning programs that generalize to unseen problem instances [ 101 ] . With Claude Fable, a coding agent writes a controller that solves Push-T without demonstrations [ 167 ] and writes force-feedback programs for contact-rich tasks [ 145 ] . In Anthropic’s Project Fetch, human teams programmed a quadruped robot with and without Claude [ 31 ] , and Claude Opus 4.7, working without human help, was later about 20 times faster than the fastest human team at all the tasks that the teams had completed [ 56 ] . Other systems add procedures that help the agent write better programs. They search over multi-file policy repositories that compose perception, planning and control primitives [ 27 ] , build libraries of programs from execution feedback [ 93 , 72 ] or of verified skills from a few demonstrations [ 108 ] , reuse body descriptions, past experience and skills derived from the agent’s own routines [ 45 ] , write a shared code library that makes every decision without a model at test time [ 49 ] , infer a program from a single human demonstration [ 92 ] , let the agent check and retry primitives within Python cells [ 140 ] , let the agent develop its tools in simulation before it controls a real robot [ 48 ] , or have one agent revise, on a real robot, the tools that another agent calls [ 35 ] . RAX [ 50 ] has a coding agent explore online without hand-designed primitives, given at most a few goal images or demonstrations, and turn the successful action sequences into programs for single-arm, bimanual, dexterous, mobile and humanoid systems, both in simulation and on real robots. Train a policy. In this approach, the agent trains a policy and then uses it to control the robot. ENPIRE [ 165 ] gives coding agents a real-world loop of resetting the scene, running a policy, verifying the outcome and refining the policy. With this loop, frontier coding agents train policies that reach a 99% success rate on dexterous tasks such as organizing a pin box. HARBOR [ 83 ] has a team of agents set up simulated environments, design rewards and tune reinforcement learning. The reward design methods above automate one step of this approach. Most of these studies fix the control approach in advance, and Agent as Policy and ENPIRE let the agent choose between two of the approaches. The exam supports all three approaches and leaves the choice to the agent, so it also measures which approach agents choose ( Section 3.5 ). Harnesses for agentic robots. The harness is the program between the model and the robot. Harnesses for robots differ in the action interface they provide, in whether the agent can call learned policies, and in the support they add for tracking state and checking outcomes. Action interfaces. The success rates of coding agents depend on the level of abstraction of the interface [ 32 ] . Show-Harness [ 18 ] gives vision-language models a small set of semantic action units, which robot-specific interpreters convert into robot actions, and VIA [ 47 ] gives off-the-shelf agents a visual interface with tools to probe pixels and set end-effector targets. HexaShell [ 34 ] shares 14 general tools for motion, perception and state across all tasks and robots, because code refined for one benchmark tends to overfit it. OpenRUA [ 23 ] gives an unmodified coding agent only terminal access to the robot’s ROS 2 interface, its documentation and basic tools. The uni-robot-agent harness [ 186 ] supports further training of open-weight models. Learned policies as tools. Hi Robot [ 136 ] trains a high-level vision-language model that sends language commands to a vision-language-action policy. RoboClaw [ 80 ] and Harness VLA [ 192 ] let the agent call vision-language-action policies as tools, Recursive Harness Distillation [ 70 ] distills a strong agent’s interventions in a vision-language-action policy into a playbook for a lighter agent, and Gemini Robotics-ER 1.5 orchestrates the Gemini Robotics 1.5 vision-language-action model [ 37 ] . The Physical Agency orchestrator [ 33 ] plans subgoals, calls vision-language-action policies and parametrized skills, checks the outcomes and recovers from failures. Yao et al. [181] evaluate GPT-6 Astra on EBench both as the policy and as an agent that can also call a vision-language-action or world action model policy, and compare both with the policies alone. State tracking and verification. Other harnesses add a persistent representation of the scene and a check of each action’s outcome [ 157 ] , calibrated 3D measurements and a preview of each motion [ 153 ] , sub-agents for perception, verification and memory [ 30 ] , or a vision-language model that monitors a policy and corrects it after a confirmed failure [ 77 ] . The Embodied First Exam runs each harness unmodified with the models of its own provider, and through an adapter with other providers’ models ( Appendix G ), so different harnesses can be compared on the same tasks. Benchmarks for agentic robots. Several benchmarks evaluate agents that control robots. CaP-X [ 32 ] evaluates coding agents that compose perception and control primitives at several levels of abstraction and finds that their success rates depend on the abstractions that designers provide. RobotWorld [ 178 ] evaluates agents on 84 tasks in manipulation, mobile manipulation, locomotion, driving and aerial control with executable success checks. EmbodiedSWE [ 134 ] evaluates coding agents on long-horizon dexterous tasks and turns successful agent solutions into demonstrations for training vision-language-action policies. RoboQuest [ 89 ] evaluates whether agents explore to find the information a task requires, BiGym 2.0 [ 194 ] compares learned policies with programs that coding agents develop on humanoid household tasks, and Xu et al. [170] evaluate whether coding agents avoid an obstacle they are told not to touch and propose obstacle-aware harnesses for route planning and contact. Earlier safety studies jailbreak robots that language models control into harmful actions [ 128 ] , test whether models recognize undesirable situations in images and text [ 131 ] , and test whether agents that plan with high-level actions reject hazardous household tasks [ 183 ] . In the exam’s Embodied Safety Exam, the agent controls the robot itself, and the verifier decides success from what physically happens in a physics simulation. Of the agentic-robot studies above, many report results on RoboDojo, several on RoboTwin 2.0 and one on KinDER [ 101 ] . The exam adapts all three suites. The Embodied First Exam covers 47 robots of nine types, which include humanoids, quadrupeds, dexterous hands, aerial robots and a wheeled car.
