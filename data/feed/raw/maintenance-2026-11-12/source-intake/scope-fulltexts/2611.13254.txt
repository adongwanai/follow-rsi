When an LLM agent fails part-way through a long task, the usual analysis records a symptom: a failure-mode label, or the agent and step judged responsible. It does not say where the error entered, which state carried it to the symptom, whether anything in the trajectory contradicted it, or which control could have caught it. We define an episode record that ties a candidate fault to its origin, propagation, detection, response and consequence, located by operational phase and state domain, and that names the control families whose observation and reach cover it. We turned the record into a written annotation protocol and applied it to 95 failed runs of six multi-agent systems from the public Who&When and MAD corpora, reading each trajectory in full. Faults enter early (median at 0.18 of the run), mostly as wrong decisions or fabrications over task state, and in 37 of 88 Who&When runs before the step the corpus’ human labels mark as decisive. In 72% of failed runs the trajectory already contained evidence against the error before the failure; in 88% of those no agent acted on it. Something flagged a deviation in 31% of runs, but no response removed the deviated state. Independent judges reading the unused evidence, task contracts fixed at admission, and re-checking beliefs against the environment reach most episodes; restoring the agent’s own state would have sufficed in 35 of 95 runs. A text scan of the raw corpus also shows that 45% of the hand-crafted Who&When failures ended with a provider content-filter crash after which the harness emitted a final answer no agent produced, a failure class that neither symptom taxonomies nor responsible-agent labels represent. Protocol, records and scripts are included. Introduction Large language model (LLM) agents carry out tasks by selecting tools, observing the results, and revising their actions over many steps ( Yao et al., 2023b ; Yang et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2025b ; Packer et al., 2023 ) . For such systems, dependability is a property of the whole trajectory : the sequence of decisions and state changes from the first step to the last. Long trajectories change how errors behave. A retrieved statement can introduce a false assumption. Memory can keep that assumption alive, another agent can reuse it, and an action can turn it into a change in an external system that no checkpoint can undo. In a simulated vending business, every evaluated model had runs that derailed through misread delivery schedules, forgotten orders, or repeated loops ( Backlund and Petersson, 2025 ) . In 1,794 annotated coding-agent trajectories, the first error typically occurred early, while its symptoms appeared only much later ( Zhao et al., 2026 ) . In current checkpoint-and-rollback frameworks, a restored agent can re-issue actions that were already committed, including payments ( Wu et al., 2026 ; Zheng et al., 2026b ) . One released trajectory shows how these effects combine ( Xu et al., 2025a ) . An agent in a simulated software company is asked to obtain a repository location from a colleague, Alex Turner, through the company chat, then clone the repository and start a server. Logged into the chat as an administrator, it opens the user administration page and renames the account it is logged in with to “Alex Turner”. It then sends its question to a different colleague, addressed to Alex, and waits. For the remaining 81 turns it reports that it is still waiting for a reply, until the run ends 22 minutes later with nothing cloned and the renamed account never restored. No single step is a familiar model error. The damage comes from how the steps connect: an action outside the task’s scope, a message to the wrong recipient, a belief that the message reached its target, and no point at which the run stops to check. This trajectory serves as a running example in Section 2 . Classical dependable computing gives us the vocabulary and tools for such problems. It distinguishes faults, errors, and service failures, and it offers mature theories of checkpointing, compensation, and redundancy ( Avižienis et al., 2004 ) . LLM agents, however, give three of its classical problems new forms. (1) Requests are generated. Conventional systems make retries safe with idempotence keys and deduplication, but an agent’s model re-generates the request, so a retry may differ in content and form, and a key derived from the original request may not match it. (2) State is partly semantic. Integrity checks confirm that a stored item is intact; in an agent, an intact item can still be wrong, because its correctness depends on where it came from and what it means. (3) Effects reach services the agent does not control. As in distributed transactions, restoring local state leaves committed external effects in place, and agent frameworks often do not record which effects were already released. When a run like this is analyzed, the analysis usually stops at the symptom. The failure gets a mode label such as “task derailment” or “incorrect verification” ( Cemri et al., 2025 ) , or an annotator names the agent and step that were responsible ( Zhang et al., 2025b ) . Both are useful, and both leave out what one would need to fix the system: where the error actually entered, which part of the agent’s state carried it to the point where it became visible, whether anything in the trajectory contradicted it along the way, and which kind of control could have caught it. As far as we know, nobody has measured these things on released trajectories at any scale. That is what this paper does. We define an episode record that ties a candidate fault to its origin, its propagation, its detection and response, and its consequence. Each event is located by operational phase (P0–P7) and state domain (R1–R6), and the record names the control families whose observation and reach would cover the episode (Section 2 ). We wrote the record down as an annotation protocol and applied it to failed trajectories from two public corpora, Who&When ( Zhang et al., 2025b ) and the MAST/MAD traces ( Cemri et al., 2025 ) , reading each trajectory in full (Section 3 ). Three questions: • RQ1. In which phase and state domain do faults enter, how far before the visible symptom, and how does the origin relate to the “decisive step” that the corpus authors labelled? • RQ2. When a run fails, was there evidence in the trajectory that contradicted the error? Did any agent use it? Did detection, when it happened, lead to a response that worked? • RQ3. Which control families would have observed and reached each episode, and how often would restoring the agent’s own state have been enough? We completed 95 failed runs from six multi-agent systems (Section 4 ). The short version of what we found: • Faults enter early and are mostly decisions over task state. Origins cluster in deliberation and execution (P1–P2) over task and knowledge state (R2), at a median of 0.18 of the run; the median gap to the symptom is 0.67 of the run. In 37 of 88 Who&When traces our origin is earlier than the step the corpus marks as decisive. • The evidence is usually there and usually ignored. 72% of failed runs contain, before the failure, an observation or message that contradicts the erroneous belief; in 88% of those nobody acted on it. Something flagged a deviation in 31% of runs. Not one response removed the deviated state. • The controls that would reach these episodes are not the ones people mostly build. Independent judges that read the unused evidence, task contracts, provenance on stored facts, and re-checking beliefs against the environment cover most episodes. A local checkpoint alone would have helped in 35 runs; in 56 the fault sat in a plan or a policy that a restore would simply replay. • A harness-level failure hides behind the attribution labels. In 26 of the 58 hand-crafted Who&When runs (45%) a provider content filter killed the orchestrator and the harness emitted a final answer that no agent had produced. The corpus attributes 21 of these 25 runs to an agent other than the orchestrator. The protocol, all annotation records and the scripts that produce every number in the paper are included as ancillary files. Appendix A gives the background the framework draws on; readers who want the dependability vocabulary and the control families in detail should start there. 2. A Framework for Trajectory Dependability Figure 1 connects execution phases, affected state domains, and the dynamics through which errors persist or spread; this section defines each element. 2.1. Service properties and the unit of execution An agent is a model plus a harness that builds context, calls tools, tracks state, and schedules checks and recovery. Dependability is delivering service that can justifiably be trusted under stated conditions ( Avižienis et al., 2004 ; Laprie, 1985 ) . Its attributes (availability, reliability, safety, integrity, maintainability) need separate measures because they can conflict. Stopping, for example, can preserve safety but block completion. This study covers the attributes a single run can violate: reliability (task completion), safety (policy-compliant completion or safe termination), and integrity (the task’s invariants hold, and injected content gains no authority over actions); availability and maintainability enter only through oversight and audit (Sections B.6 and C.6 ). We use dependability for the whole and reliability for the single attribute, and read the broader use of reliability in agent papers as dependability. Let a task specification be 𝒮 = ( G , ℐ , 𝒜 , B ) \mathcal{S}=(G,\mathcal{I},\mathcal{A},B) , with outcome predicate G G , required invariants ℐ \mathcal{I} , authorized actions 𝒜 \mathcal{A} , and resource budget B B . A trajectory τ \tau is the alternating sequence of states, actions, and observations x 0 , a 0 , o 1 , x 1 , … , a T − 1 , o T , x T x_{0},a_{0},o_{1},x_{1},\ldots,a_{T-1},o_{T},x_{T} , where x t x_{t} is the relevant, partly observable agent and environment state, a t a_{t} an action, and o t + 1 o_{t+1} its observed result; the dynamics may be nonstationary and non-Markov. In the running example from Section 1 , τ \tau is the released log of 201 events, G G requires a cloned repository and a running server, and 𝒜 \mathcal{A} does not include writes to the user directory. Task completion means G ⁡ ( x T ) G(x_{T}) holds. Policy-compliant completion also requires a t ∈ 𝒜 a_{t}\in\mathcal{A} for all t t , with each invariant in ℐ \mathcal{I} holding where applicable. Safe termination instead permits a specified abstention or escalation. A fault is a candidate cause of an error, an error a deviation in state, and a failure a violation of the service specification. The distinction matters because a malformed call can be a recovered task error, but an unauthorized disclosure is a safety failure whatever the final answer. 2.2. Measuring the horizon The realized horizon of a run has several components: loop iterations, tool calls, tokens, wall-clock time, external mutations, participating agents, and sessions. The running example is long in turns (98 agent actions) and time (22 minutes) but contains only two external mutations, a renamed account and a message sent to the wrong person, and these are what matter. No single step count defines a long task, so we call a task long-horizon when its outcome materially depends on retaining state, maintaining dependencies, or recovering across repeated interactions. Benchmarks stress different components: Vending-Bench runs exceed 20 million tokens with simple decisions ( Backlund and Petersson, 2025 ) , whereas RE-Bench fixes wall-clock budgets of up to eight hours ( Wijk et al., 2025 ) . The required horizon also differs from the realized horizon, because repeated failures can turn a twenty-operation task into eighty, so observed length often reflects error. Human-equivalent task duration instead measures how demanding a task is, and its 50% completion horizon has doubled roughly every seven months since 2019 ( Kwa et al., 2025 ) . 2.3. Episodes, causal links, and uncertainty An episode is an analytical record: a chain or branching subgraph of the trajectory that links a candidate fault to the states, responses, and consequences it affects: (1) E = ⟨ O , ℐ E , ℒ , D , C , M , σ , U ⟩ . E=\langle O,\mathcal{I}_{E},\mathcal{L},D,C,M,\sigma,U\rangle. The record captures the origin O O , affected invariants ℐ E \mathcal{I}_{E} , dependency links ℒ \mathcal{L} , detections D D , responses C C , manifestations M M , evaluation scale σ \sigma , and uncertainty U U . Each event retains its phase, domain, order, version, and source; H1–H7 label the links. Each dependency is marked as observed, intervention-supported, or hypothesized. In the running example, the origin O O is the deliberation step (P1) that chose to rename an account rather than search the directory; the affected invariant is the authorization scope 𝒜 \mathcal{A} . The action changes an identity record in a shared system (R4, R6). The link ℒ \mathcal{L} runs from the message sent to the wrong colleague (R5) to the belief (R2) that Alex Turner has been asked, which is contaminated (H2). Two observations displayed the evidence, but D D and C C are empty, and the manifestation M M is 81 turns of waiting (H7). Restoring the agent’s own state would not undo the renamed account, which is why the recovery boundary is part of the record. Section 3 turns this record into an annotation protocol and applies it at scale. 3. Study Design 3.1. Corpora We wanted failed runs of real multi-agent systems with the complete trajectory released, not a summary of it. Two public corpora fit. Who&When ( Zhang et al., 2025b ) contains 184 failed runs on GAIA and AssistantBench questions. 58 are hand-crafted runs of Magentic-One (an orchestrator with a web surfer, a file surfer, a coder and a terminal agent); 126 are algorithm-generated runs of AG2 CaptainAgent teams, where a manager assembles a group of role-named experts. Every run has a human label that names the responsible agent and the decisive mistake step . We did not use these labels during annotation; they serve as an outside reference afterwards (Section 3.4 ). MAD ( Cemri et al., 2025 ) , human-labelled split. 19 traces from AG2, MetaGPT, ChatDev, HyperAgent, AppWorld, Magentic-One and OpenManus, each labelled by three annotators with failure modes from a development version of the MAST taxonomy. We keep the majority labels for reference. The traces are raw logs in several formats and some are very long. Each trajectory was normalized into one text file. Who&When messages become numbered steps ( ### step i i [role/name] ); MAD logs are kept as they are, with line numbers. We dropped seven traces longer than 200,000 characters (one from Who&When, six from MAD) because nobody, human or model, reads those in one pass. 3.2. Annotation protocol The protocol is a written document of about four pages; it is included with the submission. It turns the episode record of Equation ( 1 ) into 38 fields with fixed vocabularies. For each failed run the annotator records one primary episode : the earliest candidate fault whose consequences reach the final failure. A second, causally independent fault can be recorded as a secondary episode. The fields that matter most for the results: • Origin. Step, phase (P0–P7), domain (R1–R6), and kind. The kind follows a short decision tree, because the pilot showed that annotators otherwise disagree on it: specification if the brief itself was defective at admission and the agent could not have known; fabrication if an unobserved fact, result or placeholder argument was asserted; integration if an observation that was in the agent’s input was misread or dropped; decision if the choice was wrong although the information was adequate; and interface , environment , coordination for faults in tools, external systems and handoffs. • Propagation. The hops at which the deviated state was reused, the domains crossed, and which of the dynamics H1–H7 (Table 7 ) apply. • Symptom. The step at which the failing output is emitted as final, or at which the run ends, and the distance from the origin. • Evidence. Whether some observation or message before the symptom contradicted the erroneous belief, and what happened to it: used , acknowledged but not acted on, in an agent’s input but never mentioned , or never seen by any agent. • Detection and response. Who, if anyone, flagged a deviation (the agent itself, another agent, a tool or verifier), what response followed (retry, replan, rollback, compensation, escalation, termination), and whether the response actually removed the deviated state. • Controls. Every control family from Table 10 whose inspection basis would have seen the deviation and whose boundary reaches the affected state, with the most direct one marked as primary. A separate field, restore reach , asks whether restoring the agent’s own state to just before the origin would have been enough. • Harness fault. Whether a model-provider error ended the loop, and whether the harness itself emitted a final answer that no agent step produced. We added this field after the pilot, for reasons that Section 4.6 makes clear. Every step-valued field has to point at a concrete step or line of the normalized trace, so a reader can check it. Pilot. We first applied version 0.1 of the protocol to 20 traces chosen to span the length distribution. The annotator reports came back with 14 points where the vocabulary did not fit what was in the trace: runs whose final answer was correct but whose process failed, evidence that was present before the fault was even made, provider crashes that coincided with a fabricated answer, and so on. Version 0.2 resolves these, mostly by adding a value or a rule. The 20 pilot records were migrated to 0.2. 3.3. Who annotated, and how many The annotation was done by an LLM (Claude, Anthropic), one independent instance per trace, instructed with the protocol, the field schema and one finished example record. Each instance read the whole normalized trajectory before writing anything. Records were checked mechanically against the schema and the controlled vocabularies; none failed. The trajectories were treated as data throughout, never as instructions to the annotator. We did not get through the whole corpus. Within the budget of this study we completed 96 records: 29 hand-crafted and 59 algorithm-generated Who&When runs and 8 MAD traces. The sample is the 20 pilot traces plus the first annotation batches; batches were formed by balancing character counts, so there is no selection on content. For Who&When the sample matches the corpus in run length (median 33 vs. 32.5 steps for hand-crafted runs, 9 vs. 10 for algorithm-generated ones). The MAD part is small and skewed toward short traces by the length cap, and we treat it accordingly. One MAD run turned out to have a correct final answer and a purely procedural failure; it is kept in the records but left out of the outcome statistics, which leaves 95 failed runs. The annotator rated its own confidence high for 38 records, medium for 55 and low for 2. 3.4. Checks that do not depend on the annotator Two. First, for all 88 annotated Who&When runs we compare our origin step with the human-labelled decisive step. The two should not be expected to coincide exactly, since they answer different questions: the decisive step is the step whose correction would have avoided the failure, our origin is the earliest step at which the state deviated in a way that led to it. An origin at or before the decisive step is consistent with both readings; an origin after it is a real disagreement, and we look at those cases. Second, the harness-fault result in Section 4.6 comes from a deterministic text scan over all 184 raw Who&When trajectories and does not involve any annotation. 4. RQ2: Evidence, detection, and response In 68 runs (72%) the trajectory contains, before the symptom, an observation or message that contradicts the erroneous belief or action; in 32 of them the evidence was already present when the fault entered. Agents acted on it in only 7 runs. In 41 the evidence was in an agent’s input and was never mentioned; in 19 an agent explicitly acknowledged it and continued unchanged, as when a verifier noted that distances were “simulated” and still reported the gyms as verified (Figure 2 b). So in 88% of the runs that had evidence, the run failed with the evidence needed to stop it sitting in the trajectory. Some agent, tool, or verifier flagged a deviation in 29 runs (31%): the erring agent or its orchestrator itself in 17 (for example a progress ledger noting a loop), a tool or verifier in 9, and another agent in 3. In a further 14 runs an agent only voiced uncertainty (“please verify”) and nobody acted on it. A response followed in 34 runs, usually a replan or retry. Not one of these responses removed the deviated state; 4 removed part of it. The typical case is a replan that changes the next action and keeps the belief that caused the loop, so the loop comes back in a different form. 4.3. A harness-level failure hidden in attribution labels This one we did not go looking for. While annotating the Magentic-One runs we kept seeing the same ending: an HTTP 400 from the model provider’s content filter on the orchestrator’s progress-ledger call, after which the experiment script printed a FINAL ANSWER that no agent had produced. A deterministic scan of all raw Who&When trajectories finds this crash in 26 of 58 hand-crafted runs (45%), 25 of which end with such a fallback answer, and in 0 of 126 algorithm-generated runs. For the 25 fallback runs the corpus attributes the failure to the orchestrator in 4 cases and to another agent (mostly the web surfer) in 21. In our records the event is a P6 termination fault in the interface domain (R3), the kind of fault that neither an agent-level symptom taxonomy nor a responsible-agent label represents. Attribution methods evaluated on this split are therefore partly scored on runs whose final answer came from the harness, not from any agent. 5. Discussion What the record adds to a label. The clearest illustration is MAST mode 3.3, incorrect verification . We assigned it to 44 runs, and those runs have five different kinds of origin: 16 wrong decisions, 16 fabrications, 6 corrupted handoffs, 4 misread observations, 2 defective briefs. The verifier rubber-stamped something in all of them, but what it should have checked differs completely: the provenance of a claimed fact, the environment state behind a belief, or whether the task it was verifying was the task the user asked. A label says “the verifier failed”; the record says what it would have had to look at. The comparison with the Who&When decisive step says the same thing from the other side. The step a human picks as decisive is often downstream of where the wrong belief was first written into a plan or a fact sheet. Evidence is there; nobody reads it. If one number from this study should be remembered, it is that 72% of failed runs contain, before the failure, something in the trajectory that contradicts the error, and that in 88% of those cases no agent did anything with it. This is consistent with reports that agents rarely catch their own mistakes ( Rahman et al., 2026 ) and that a verifier sharing the worker’s information adds little ( Huang et al., 2024 ; Zanolini, 2026 ) . What our data add is a target: a control earns its place if it reads the evidence the worker left on the table. Independent judges and environment re-verification do this by construction, and they are the two families that reach most of our episodes. Detection is not the problem either. Magentic-One’s orchestrator has loop detection, and it fired in a good share of the runs. The replans that followed changed the next action and kept the belief that had caused the loop, so the loop came back in a different form. Recovery that does not revise the contaminated state reproduces the failure. This is also why a local checkpoint would have helped in a minority of runs only: in most, replaying from the checkpoint would replay the plan. Harness faults belong in the denominator. The content-filter crashes were a surprise. Almost half of one benchmark split ends with the harness, not an agent, producing the answer that gets scored. Attribution benchmarks should mark such runs, and evaluations of agents should report harness-terminated runs separately from agent errors; otherwise a method that learns to blame the web surfer for a provider’s HTTP 400 will look good. 5.1. Threats to validity Annotator. The records were produced by an LLM following a written protocol. We have no human inter-annotator agreement to report in this version. What we have instead: every step field is anchored to a line in the trace and can be checked; records passed mechanical schema validation; our origins agree with the human decisive steps within one step in 59% of cases and disagree in the direction of “later” in only 11 of 88; and the harness-fault result is independent of annotation. A human check of a subset is the obvious next step, and we would not be surprised if it moved some of the kind labels, which were the hardest part of the protocol. Sample. 96 annotated runs out of 203 normalized traces, selected by annotation batch rather than at random. The Who&When part matches the corpus in run length. The MAD part is small and short-biased, and we only show it for completeness. Corpora. Both corpora are question answering and programming in sandboxes. Not one run in our sample changes a shared or third-party system, so external impact (H5) and recovery of committed effects, which the framework was partly built for, cannot be studied here. That needs corpora like τ \tau -bench or TheAgentCompany trajectories, or fault injection. Counterfactual controls. “Would have reached” is a judgment about the trace, constrained by the control contracts of Table 10 . It is not a measured intervention. Whether a control would actually have prevented a given failure requires re-running the agents with the control in place, which we have not done. 6. Conclusion We took an episode-level record of faults, wrote it down as an annotation protocol, and applied it to 95 released failures of multi-agent LLM systems. Faults enter early, mostly as decisions or fabrications over task state, and often before the step that human labels call decisive. In most failed runs the trajectory already contained the evidence that would have stopped the episode, and nobody used it. Detection happened in a third of the runs and never led to a response that removed the deviated state. Nearly half of one benchmark split was terminated by the harness rather than by any agent. The controls that reach these episodes are the ones that read evidence the worker ignored, fix the task contract at admission, and re-check beliefs against the environment; local checkpointing reaches few of them. The protocol, the records and the scripts are included so the analysis can be repeated, extended to new corpora, and checked against human annotation. Acknowledgements. Closest to an intervention, Sinha et al. supply knowledge and plans, then show that injecting erroneous earlier outputs into history reduces later accuracy ( Sinha et al., 2026 ) . In repeated Toolathlon runs, controlling for model and task, an off-canonical call is associated with a 22.7-point increase in next-call deviation ( Lee, 2026 ) . SWE-agent’s GPT-4 edits eventually succeed 90.5% of the time, but only 57.2% after a failed edit ( Yang et al., 2024 ) . At larger scale, every Vending-Bench model had runs that derailed, and breakdowns did not coincide with context-window filling ( Backlund and Petersson, 2025 ) . Across 1,794 coding-agent trajectories, annotated failures were predominantly epistemic, with early onset and late-surfacing symptoms ( Zhao et al., 2026 ) . Across 2,518 TRAVERSE trajectories, agents often failed to recover from a first mistake and rarely caught their own errors ( Rahman et al., 2026 ) . The injected-history design and a natural experiment holding model and task fixed ( Lee, 2026 ) support causal claims. The rest are observational, and because errors are not randomly assigned, task difficulty may partly explain their associations; in pooled failure-prediction data, almost every pair behind the reported AUROC compares runs of different tasks, and within-task discrimination early in a run is near chance ( EsfandyariDoulabi et al., 2026 ) . B.2. Persistent state and contaminated evidence This subsection turns to the state an agent carries between steps: context, memory, and stored evidence. Agents can fail to use evidence, not only lose it through truncation, summarization, or cache compression. For example, relevant information placed in the middle of a long prompt can go unused ( Liu et al., 2024a ) , while retrieval and external memory ( Lewis et al., 2020 ; Packer et al., 2023 ; Shinn et al., 2023 ) add further problems of selection, freshness, and retraction. Planning benchmarks show what losing constraints costs. In TravelPlanner, GPT-4 reached a 0.6% final success rate, failing to stay on task, choose tools, and track multiple constraints ( Xie et al., 2024a ) . MemoryAgentBench separately evaluates retrieval, test-time learning, long-range understanding, and selective forgetting, and no evaluated memory design masters all four ( Hu et al., 2026b ) . Stored evidence should therefore keep its source, version, and uncertainty through summarization, because otherwise a summary that turns “not checked” into “verified” silently changes the item’s meaning (Section C.2 ). B.3. Actions, trust boundaries, and external impact This subsection concerns actions, the point where an agent’s decision becomes an effect in a system it does not control. A valid tool call can be the wrong action, so tool-learning benchmarks separate tool-selection errors from invocation errors ( Qin et al., 2024 ) . Emulation and injection studies show harmful actions can also be syntactically ordinary ( Ruan et al., 2024 ; Debenedetti et al., 2024 ) . Indirect prompt injection crosses a central boundary between data that informs a decision and authority that permits an effect, along three paths: instructions embedded in retrieved content ( Greshake et al., 2023 ) , poisoned memory that persists across sessions, as in AgentPoison ( Chen et al., 2024b ) , and tool ecosystems, where Model Context Protocol (MCP) servers present 16 lifecycle threat scenarios ( Hou et al., 2026 ) and MCP tools can coerce leading models into malicious code execution and credential theft ( Halloran et al., 2026 ) . Measured attack success depends on the threat model. InjecAgent reports 24% for its ReAct-prompted GPT-4 setting over 1,054 cases ( Zhan et al., 2024 ) , whereas AgentDojo pairs 97 legitimate tasks with 629 security cases ( Debenedetti et al., 2024 ) . Recovery after an action takes effect depends on deployment: a test database can be reset, but a sent message stays sent. Silence is not evidence of failure, because a lost response may follow a successful commit. Retry safety therefore requires execution evidence, deduplication, or reconciliation ( Wu et al., 2026 ; Zheng et al., 2026b ) . Two 2026 benchmarks measure the cost of getting this wrong: in LIMBO, frontier models duplicate an in-flight or redelivered write in 56–74% of episodes, and an idempotency key on every write lowers the duplicate rate from 28% to 4% ( Li, 2026 ) ; in UndoBench, naive retry after a lost acknowledgment duplicated external effects in 53% of trials ( Sah et al., 2026 ) . B.4. Verification with incomplete or dependent evidence This subsection considers what a check can establish when it shares evidence with the work it checks. Verification can target action validity, state fidelity, progress, or completion; a correct final state can coexist with harmful intermediate actions. The central difficulty is that verifiers often share the worker’s information. Intrinsic self-correction can fail to improve or even degrade reasoning ( Huang et al., 2024 ) , whereas Self-Refine and chain-of-verification gain when feedback or independent questions are available ( Madaan et al., 2023 ; Dhuliawala et al., 2024 ) . Diagnosing agent traces is itself hard: on the 148 annotated traces of TRAIL (Trace Reasoning and Agentic Issue Localization), the best long-context model scored 11% ( Deshpande et al., 2025 ) . Agents also misreport their own checking: coding agents skipped files they were asked to review in 68% of runs and, in 80% of those, claimed a complete review or left the gap undisclosed ( Smyth et al., 2026 ) ; under injected tool faults, agents flagged an explicit error in 91% of trials but a plausible wrong value in only 59% ( Kraishan, 2026 ) . Given shared evidence, verification claims should state what was checked and where, separately from confidence and correctness. B.5. Coordination and common-cause failure This subsection looks at errors that arise between agents rather than within one. Multi-agent frameworks distribute work through roles, messages, and shared artifacts ( Hong et al., 2024 ; Qian et al., 2024 ; Wu et al., 2024a ; Li et al., 2023 ; Chen et al., 2024a ) , which improves decomposition but introduces stale reads, conflicting writes, and unclear ownership. An enforced access rule prevents one agent from overwriting another’s work, although it protects only the operations it covers. Fault injection into inter-agent messages shows how far errors travel: across 29,000 executions, 21% of failures produced no immediate signal, and a flat topology kept 53% task success under message faults where sequential and hierarchical topologies kept about 3%, because an alternative path preserved the evidence ( Zhou et al., 2026 ) . Adversarial content in shared artifacts reached 60–80% of agents over up to eight handoffs ( Pulipaka et al., 2026 ) , and CaMeL’s per-agent guarantee does not compose when one agent calls another as a tool ( Peters-Gill et al., 2026 ) . Classical results also limit coordination: FLP rules out guaranteed deterministic consensus in an asynchronous system with even one crash fault ( Fischer et al., 1985 ) , so practical protocols assume partial synchrony ( Castro and Liskov, 1999 ) . B.6. Human oversight and limited attention The last challenge is the human in the loop. Oversight adds judgment and authority, but its capacity depends on reviewer workload and available evidence. Pan et al.’s study of 86 deployed systems across 26 domains (MAP) found that 68% of systems run at most ten steps before intervention and 74% rely primarily on human evaluation ( Pan et al., 2026 ) . READY makes oversight cost measurable: two systems 0.3 points apart in autonomous accuracy required 39.2% versus 29.6% human review to reach the same 76% reliability target ( Chatrath et al., 2026 ) . Programming-assistant studies show the same need to measure review effort ( Vaithilingam et al., 2022 ) , and in a controlled study of 36 participants overseeing a coding agent, people were confident while missing silent defects, because they ran generic checks rather than generating diagnostic evidence ( Dissanayake et al., 2026 ) . Planning and memory support consistent decisions (Sections C.1 – C.2 ), while enforcement, recovery, and verification limit incorrect effects (Sections C.3 – C.4 ), and coordination and oversight govern shared work (Sections C.5 – C.6 ). Section C.7 compares all families and their composition. C.1. Planning, decomposition, and learned execution Deliberation and search. These methods address how agents reason and plan before acting. Chain-of-thought and self-consistency help where intermediate reasoning or sampled answers are informative ( Wei et al., 2022 ; Wang et al., 2023 ) , and ReAct couples deliberation to observations ( Yao et al., 2023b ) . ReWOO separates planning from execution and reduces model calls ( Xu et al., 2023 ) . Tree of Thoughts and LATS search with evaluation and backtracking ( Yao et al., 2023a ; Zhou et al., 2024a ) , while SayCan ties plans to feasible actions ( Ahn et al., 2022 ) . LLM-Modulo delegates plan validity to external verifiers ( Kambhampati et al., 2024 ) . RAP plans with an LLM world model ( Hao et al., 2023 ) . On long tasks, plans go stale, replanning follows faulty observations, search depends on its evaluator, and planning on its model. Revision, skills, and harness structure. These methods add revision, retained experience or control structure. Self-Refine revises answers, Reflexion retains episodic feedback, and Voyager accumulates executable skills ( Madaan et al., 2023 ; Shinn et al., 2023 ; Wang et al., 2024a ) . A skill with a testable postcondition is a stronger contract than an unverified lesson, because it can be checked. Revision needs discriminating feedback, or iteration can reinforce a wrong diagnosis ( Huang et al., 2024 ) . CRITIC grounds revision in tool feedback, and process supervision evaluates intermediate steps ( Gou et al., 2024 ; Lightman et al., 2024 ) . Decomposition with local error detection and voting sustains a million-step task ( Meyerson et al., 2025 ) , while fixed workflows such as Agentless show that simpler control can be preferable ( Xia et al., 2025 ) . LongHorizon-Harness stores task state outside the model’s context, updates it with environment-verified facts, and reports gains on Terminal-Bench and OSWorld ( Ma et al., 2026 ) . This trades a contamination risk in task state (H2, R2) for an environment check (P4, R4) performed before each planning decision. Training and admission. These methods train agents for long tasks. WebRL trains web agents with a curriculum built from failed attempts and an outcome reward model ( Qi et al., 2025 ) . SWE-RL uses a rule-based similarity reward on software-evolution data ( Wei et al., 2025 ) , and AREX combines constraint-wise verification with long-horizon training ( Lu et al., 2026 ) . Such rewards can improve completion yet leave unrewarded properties, such as avoiding harmful intermediate actions, to runtime checks. A task contract fixed at admission (P0) records outcome, non-goals, evidence, limits and authority, so acceptance criteria stay unchanged as the plan evolves. C.2. Memory and compute-state fidelity This subsection covers controls over what an agent remembers, from the KV cache to external memory. Memory operations differ in what they preserve, so each kind needs its own evaluation (Figure 4 ). Exact operations. PagedAttention changes where key–value (KV) cache entries are stored but preserves logical access, and FlashAttention computes exact attention up to floating-point reordering ( Kwon et al., 2023 ; Dao et al., 2022 ) . Divergence beyond floating-point nondeterminism therefore indicates an implementation or numerical fault, not lossy retention. Lossy retention. H 2 O, Scissorhands, and SnapKV select which positions to retain ( Zhang et al., 2023 ; Liu et al., 2023b ; Li et al., 2024 ) , whereas KIVI quantizes stored values ( Liu et al., 2024c ) . Their selection signals do not measure a task constraint’s importance, so both approaches should be evaluated by their effect on later decisions under matched budgets. Agent-context compression. ACON refines natural-language compression guidelines from analyses of agent failures. This reduces peak tokens by 26–54% while improving success on AppWorld, OfficeBench, and multi-objective QA ( Kang et al., 2026 ) . The result supports judging compression by its effect on later decisions. C.3. Interface enforcement and recovery boundaries This subsection covers controls at the interface between the agent and its environment, before and after an action takes effect. Valid format, permission, and reversibility are independent guarantees. Enforcement constrains an action before it occurs, whereas isolation, rollback, and compensation address its effects afterward. Pre-execution schemas, policies, and guardrails. Constrained decoding enforces grammars ( Willard and Louf, 2023 ; Beurer-Kellner et al., 2023 ) , but a valid parse does not prove authorization. AgentSpec evaluates user rules at runtime triggers ( Wang et al., 2026a ) . CaMeL separates trusted control flow from untrusted data and checks capabilities ( Debenedetti et al., 2026 ) . Progent checks tool calls against symbolic privilege rules, narrowing automatically but requiring approval to expand ( Shi et al., 2025 ) . Llama Guard constrains conversational inputs and outputs ( Inan et al., 2023 ) , whereas GuardAgent and ShieldAgent check proposed actions with generated code or policy-derived rule circuits ( Xiang et al., 2025 ; Chen et al., 2025 ) . Instruction Hierarchy trains models to resist injected instructions ( Wallace et al., 2024 ) . Deterministic decisions are only as reliable as their rules, learned decisions need their error measured on the deployment distribution, and a check goes stale unless it is bound to the resource version it examined. Post-execution isolation, restoration, and compensation. MicroVMs limit reachable resources ( Agache et al., 2020 ) , emulated tools avoid real services ( Ruan et al., 2024 ) , and checkpoint/restart and message logging restore computational state ( Elnozahy et al., 2002 ) . The question is what a snapshot contains and which effects escaped it. Retry, replan, rollback, compensation, and escalation differ in preconditions and residual risks. Retry is safe only with evidence of non-execution, idempotence, or deduplication. Compensation issues an offsetting action, as in sagas ( Garcia-Molina and Salem, 1987 ) , and can itself fail. Self-healing designs ( Ghosh et al., 2007 ) also need versions, because a rewound agent can conflict with peers. Agent-specific recovery runtimes. Several 2026 works, mostly preprints, show that checkpoint-and-restore can replay or fork externally visible actions unless it records which effects were released. After a restore, an agent may reconstruct a slightly different request that the server treats as new (Figure 5 ; Table 9 compares the systems). ACRFence names these semantic rollback attacks , including action replay and authority resurrection, and enforces replay-or-fork semantics over recorded irreversible effects ( Zheng et al., 2026b ) . Wu et al. identify five checkpoint-and-rollback failure modes across five frameworks, demonstrating malware-verification bypass, unauthorized mail forwarding, and double payment ( Wu et al., 2026 ) . Among constructive systems, Cordon stages outward effects in an outbox and validates the composed flow before release ( Chen et al., 2026b ) . Zheng et al. decide exactly, with mechanized proofs, whether a checkpoint, fork, restore, or merge is safe ( Zheng et al., 2026a ) . AgentRewind checkpoints agent context with a controlled environment ( Zhuang et al., 2026 ) , and REVISE recomputes only work invalidated by a revision ( Qi et al., 2026 ) . Planarian snapshots local state and records compensating actions for remote changes, which rollback replays ( Guo et al., 2026 ) , and a commit-time authority service rejects stale proposals and dispatches effects through an idempotent outbox ( Salas, 2026 ) . Recovery can also harm: the same intervention rescues some trajectories and disrupts others, so a router that decides when to intervene raises success ( Xiao et al., 2026 ) . All still depend on provider semantics for released effects. Verification: tests, monitors, and model judges This subsection covers the checks that decide whether work is acceptable: tests, monitors, and model judges. Runtime verification checks properties against execution events ( Leucker and Schallhart, 2009 ) , so its verdicts cover only those properties and events. Software tasks offer tests ( Jimenez et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2024 ) , but suites differ in coverage, and stronger tests change which solutions pass ( Liu et al., 2023a ) . WebArena, AppWorld, and τ \tau -bench check environment state ( Zhou et al., 2024b ; Trivedi et al., 2024 ; Yao et al., 2025 ) with privileges deployments may lack, so a benchmark oracle is not a runtime verifier. Model judges assess semantic requirements, and chain-of-verification, debate, and multi-judge systems organize this checking differently ( Dhuliawala et al., 2024 ; Du et al., 2024 ; Chan et al., 2024 ) . MT-Bench and FairEval document judge biases, including sensitivity to answer order ( Zheng et al., 2023 ; Wang et al., 2024b ) . Agent-as-a-Judge inspects intermediate artifacts. On 55 development tasks it outperformed LLM-as-a-Judge and matched the reliability of its human-evaluation baseline ( Zhuge et al., 2025 ) . Deferral thresholds likewise need calibration on the agent’s outputs ( Guo et al., 2017 ) . A judge adds most value when it reads evidence the worker did not use. Coordination protocols and redundancy This subsection covers how shared work among agents is organized and when redundancy helps. Belief–desire–intention (BDI) models, blackboards, and protocols organize shared work ( Wooldridge, 2009 ; Rao and Georgeff, 1995 ) , whereas LLM frameworks use role-based pipelines, multi-agent conversations, and state machines ( Hong et al., 2024 ; Qian et al., 2024 ; Wu et al., 2024a ; Wu et al., 2024b ) . For example, HuggingGPT and LLMCompiler dispatch specialized models or parallel calls ( Shen et al., 2023 ; Kim et al., 2024 ) . Role names do not supply dependability, which requires enforced ownership, versioned handoffs, restricted writes, and bounded termination. Self-consistency, debate, and mixtures of agents aid candidate selection ( Wang et al., 2023 ; Du et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2025a ) , but, like N-version programming and recovery blocks ( Avižienis, 1985 ; Randell, 1975 ; Lyu, 1995 ) , they depend on diversity, because replicas of one model tend to share errors. Agreement protocols ( Schneider, 1990 ; Castro and Liskov, 1999 ) make replicas agree but not correct, a limit that majority voting among agents shares. C.6. Observability, human review, and audit This subsection covers the records and human processes that support review during and after a run. Records serve three purposes: tracing, review, and audit. Distributed tracing links events through causal context ( Sigelman et al., 2010 ) , but agents also need semantic links, such as links from sources to claims and from approvals to effects. Who&When, AgentRx, LongRCA, and TRAIL localize responsible agents, steps, and error types ( Zhang et al., 2025b ; Barke et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026 ; Deshpande et al., 2025 ) . Dependency-guided attribution outperforms chronology-based judges on Who&When ( Deng et al., 2026a ) , and BRIDGE compiles handoff traces into a provenance graph to repair broken handoffs at run time ( Su et al., 2026 ) . The Agent Error Dataset pairs 50,000 failures with replay-checked diagnoses ( Zhu et al., 2026 ) , while an audit of attribution benchmarks finds surface cues that let simple predictors match GPT-4.1 ( Xu and Gao, 2026 ) . Review packets should therefore expose proposed effects, checks, uncertainty, and required decisions. READY selects the minimum-cost oversight policy meeting a reliability target and qualifies it statistically ( Chatrath et al., 2026 ) , LEASH escalates from a weak to a strong computer-use agent when step-level uncertainty rises ( Wang et al., 2026c ) , while cognitive-forcing experiments show that interface design changes overreliance ( Buçinca et al., 2021 ) . Model cards and datasheets are precedents for agent records ( Mitchell et al., 2019 ; Gebru et al., 2021 ) , and such records need maintenance as systems change ( Sculley et al., 2015 ) . C.7. Hazards expressible as predicates over actions or information flow suit deterministic enforcement, which holds by construction within its mediation; semantic hazards suit learned guards whose false-acceptance rate is measurable on the deployment distribution. Where effects can be deferred, staging removes the need to undo them. Three evaluation lanes show task cohorts, failed-trace diagnosis, and adversarial stress, each linked to its measures and inference limits. D.1. Task benchmarks and their observability SWE-bench and SWE-Bench Pro test repository-scale correctness via versioned tasks and executable tests ( Jimenez et al., 2024 ; Deng et al., 2026b ) , so results depend on test adequacy, environment setup, and release version. WebArena, OSWorld, and AppWorld expose web, desktop, and API actions with functional or state-based evaluators ( Zhou et al., 2024b ; Xie et al., 2024b ; Trivedi et al., 2024 ) , whereas Mind2Web’s offline task measures reproduction of demonstrated actions ( Deng et al., 2023 ) . Further benchmarks add visual grounding, enterprise software, mobile, conversational navigation, and lengthy open-web tasks ( Koh et al., 2024 ; Drouin et al., 2024 ; Rawles et al., 2025 ; Lù et al., 2024 ; Yoran et al., 2024 ) . Others cover heterogeneous environments, progress, API use, and tool-supported answers ( Liu et al., 2024b ; Ma et al., 2024 ; Qin et al., 2024 ; Mialon et al., 2024 ) , with BrowserGym unifying evaluation ( De Chezelles et al., 2025 ) . τ \tau -bench scores database state under policies with simulated users ( Yao et al., 2025 ) . In τ 2 \tau^{2} -bench, agent and user both act on shared state, so evaluation must check its consistency ( Barres et al., 2026 ) . Newer benchmarks target consequential, long-running work: a simulated software company ( Xu et al., 2025a ) , Kaggle competitions ( Chan et al., 2025 ) , priced freelance tasks ( Miserendino et al., 2025 ) , hard command-line tasks ( Merrill et al., 2026 ) , and 20-million-token business simulations ( Backlund and Petersson, 2025 ) . RE-Bench, comparing agents with eight-hour expert attempts ( Wijk et al., 2025 ) , shows that budget length decides who looks stronger. Agents scored four times higher than humans at two hours; humans scored twice as high at 32 total hours. Because these benchmarks stress different components of the horizon (Section 2.2 ) and measure horizon differently, their scores share no single horizon scale. D.2. Adversarial, long-horizon, and diagnostic evidence Security benchmarks each define an attacker surface and threat model. AgentDojo pairs legitimate tasks with injections, while InjecAgent injects malicious tool content ( Debenedetti et al., 2024 ; Zhan et al., 2024 ) . Agent Security Bench broadens attack and defense settings, whereas AgentHarm tests explicitly harmful requests ( Zhang et al., 2025a ; Andriushchenko et al., 2025 ) . Indirect hijacking and deliberate misuse are distinct threats, so report their rates separately. Long-horizon stress tests reach hundreds of tool calls or thousands of trajectories ( Luo et al., 2026 ; Wang et al., 2026d ) . Recovery now has benchmarks of its own: CUA-Recovery places agents in 2,250 verified erroneous desktop states, where error awareness reaches at most 44% ( Hu et al., 2026a ) ; ReTrace judges a recovery by whether downstream actions yield the same outcomes as from the target state ( Bang et al., 2026 ) ; and UndoBench and LIMBO score recovery against ledgers of committed effects ( Sah et al., 2026 ; Li, 2026 ) . Memory benchmarks isolate temporal reasoning, updates, abstention, and selective forgetting ( Maharana et al., 2024 ; Wu et al., 2025 ; Hu et al., 2026b ) . Failure-attribution corpora identify which role or step explains a failed run ( Zhang et al., 2025b ; Deshpande et al., 2025 ; Barke et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026 ) . Step-level diagnosis is far harder. The best Who&When method found the responsible agent 53.5% of the time, but the decisive step only 14.2%. In LongRCA, root causes lie a median 48 steps before run end, and the strongest baseline reached 13.2% exact root-step accuracy. TRAVERSE adds 2,518 human-annotated trajectories, yet the best of six frontier judges found the first mistake in under a third of runs ( Rahman et al., 2026 ) . These corpora assess diagnosis of known failures only, not failure frequency or whether one root step exists, and their labels have their own uncertainty: MAST’s κ = 0.88 \kappa=0.88 was measured by three annotators on 15 of its 150 development traces, and its 1,642-trace dataset is labeled by an LLM judge ( Cemri et al., 2025 ) . Role accuracy, step accuracy, and repair usefulness therefore need separate reporting ( Wang et al., 2026b ; Chen et al., 2026a ) . D.3. A minimal measurement protocol Trajectory-level claims need a minimal record so that outcome changes can be attributed to the right harness change: task, trial, versions, budget, realized horizon, disposition, and harness configuration (model, prompts, tools, memory, verifier, recovery policy, human boundary). Repeated-trial reliability needs care because common metrics answer different questions. Pass@ k k asks whether at least one of k k trials succeeds, whereas pass k \mathrm{pass}^{k} , introduced in τ \tau -bench ( Yao et al., 2025 ) , asks whether all k k succeed. Verifier coverage also needs a denominator, which is the set of applicable requirement–event obligations 𝒪 ⁡ ( τ ) \mathcal{O}(\tau) with checking deadlines: (7) Cov ( τ ) = | { o ∈ 𝒪 ⁡ ( τ ) : the required check ran before its deadline } | | 𝒪 ⁡ ( τ ) | . \operatorname{Cov}(\tau)=\frac{|\{o\in\mathcal{O}(\tau):\text{the required check ran before its deadline}\}|}{|\mathcal{O}(\tau)|}. Cost per success, ∑ i C i / ∑ i Y i \sum_{i}C_{i}/\sum_{i}Y_{i} , must include failed attempts, recovery, and review effort ( Chatrath et al., 2026 ) . D.4. Experimental designs that isolate a mechanism Three designs each change one preset property while keeping the task goal fixed. The first two vary horizon and dependency structure, so the compared tasks must match on everything except duration or dependency distance. The third studies error propagation and containment.
A dependency-delay intervention varies the steps between observing a fact and using it, crossed with the summarization policy; a repeated-operation intervention adds validated operations whose postconditions are known; and a state intervention injects a false premise, stale response, omitted message, or numerical perturbation at a declared phase and domain, which tests containment under that injection. Each design should randomize at task or trial level, retain clean controls, include successful and failed runs, and compare at a fixed budget and at equal task success. Crossing two controls factorially estimates their interaction, which tests whether aligned controls reduce escaping errors. D.5. Statistical reporting Estimate uncertainty by treating tasks as clusters, because steps correlate, and audit a subset of model-judged outcomes for false acceptance and rejection. Under a fixed budget, count every attempted task and distinguish completion, policy failure, escalation, and timeout. For time-to-failure estimates, treat successful completion as a competing terminal event and report cumulative incidence; only stops unrelated to failure risk, such as a budget cutoff, count as censored. Combining failure-enriched corpora with representative runs requires a sampling model and weights, while stratifying by realized length requires addressing errors that lengthen runs. Appendix E Published Evidence and Research Priorities E.1. Findings supported by published results We grade each finding’s evidence: strong by construction if it rests on a demonstrated mechanism or a design guarantee, moderate if consistent but observational or setting-specific, and descriptive if it concerns research attention rather than system behavior. F1–F3 concern where errors enter and spread, F4–F6 which controls interrupt them, and F7 and OP1–OP5 what evaluations establish. • F1. Errors make later errors more likely (moderate). In coding, tool-use, and controlled reasoning tasks, error rates after an error exceed baseline, and failure onset tends to precede symptoms by many steps ( Sinha et al., 2026 ; Lee, 2026 ; Yang et al., 2024 ; Zhao et al., 2026 ; Rahman et al., 2026 ) . A history-dependent hazard (Equation 3 ) is therefore a better starting model than independent steps. Outside injected-history and natural-experiment designs, part of the effect may reflect task difficulty. • F2. Length alone does not explain reliability (moderate). Decomposition with local error control sustained a million-step task. Vending-Bench derailment decouples from context saturation, and the RE-Bench human–agent ranking reverses with the time budget ( Meyerson et al., 2025 ; Backlund and Petersson, 2025 ; Wijk et al., 2025 ) . Claims about “long-horizon failure” should therefore name the changed horizon component and dependency structure. • F3. Losing evidence and failing to use it are different faults (moderate). Long-context studies show evidence present but unused, and planning agents leave gathered constraints unused ( Liu et al., 2024a ; Xie et al., 2024a ) . Memory and compression studies instead show evidence lost before use ( Hu et al., 2026b ) . Compression should thus be judged by its effect on later decisions ( Kang et al., 2026 ) . • F4. For indirect prompt injection, data–authority separation is a central security boundary (strong by construction). Attacks through content, memory, or tool metadata succeed when data gains the power to trigger actions ( Greshake et al., 2023 ; Debenedetti et al., 2024 ; Hou et al., 2026 ) . Architectural separation with deterministic privilege control blocks these attacks by design when the policy is correct and covers the relevant operations ( Debenedetti et al., 2026 ; Shi et al., 2025 ) , a guarantee different in kind from the probabilistic robustness of trained defenses, although it does not compose across agents that call one another ( Peters-Gill et al., 2026 ) . The utility cost is measurable: on AgentDojo, CaMeL completes 77% of tasks against 84% without a defense ( Debenedetti et al., 2026 ) . • F5. Local checkpointing alone does not make retry of external actions safe (strong by construction; defenses are recent preprints). Restored agents re-issue already committed actions, including payments ( Wu et al., 2026 ; Zheng et al., 2026b ) , and recovery benchmarks measure duplicate effects in half or more of naive retries ( Li, 2026 ; Sah et al., 2026 ) . Safe recovery needs a record of released effects and an explicit restoration boundary ( Chen et al., 2026b ; Zheng et al., 2026a ) . • F6. Agreement is weak evidence when evidence is shared (moderate). Self-correction can degrade reasoning, judges show position bias, and on TRAIL the best model localizes only 11% of trace errors ( Huang et al., 2024 ; Zheng et al., 2023 ; Wang et al., 2024b ; Deshpande et al., 2025 ) . Gains appear when tests or inspected artifacts supply new evidence ( Zhuge et al., 2025 ) . A verifier therefore adds value through independent evidence or measured error diversity, and approval rules should count independent sources rather than votes ( Zanolini, 2026 ) . • F7. Benchmark success and deployability differ (moderate). Repeated-trial reliability falls far below single-trial success. On τ \tau -bench retail, pass 8 \mathrm{pass}^{8} was below 25% for a model succeeding on under half of tasks ( Yao et al., 2025 ) . Final-state checks miss collateral changes unless they test for them, as AppWorld does ( Trivedi et al., 2024 ) , so runs scored as solved can still delete data ( Rahman et al., 2026 ) . Equal accuracy can demand very different amounts of human review ( Chatrath et al., 2026 ) . E.2. Illustrative case analysis: what an episode record adds We test the framework on two published failure traces: the running example from TheAgentCompany ( Xu et al., 2025a ) and a multi-agent trace that MAST publishes with human annotations (Appendix N.8 of ( Cemri et al., 2025 ) ). We classify each failure with the symptom label given by its source, with Ding et al.’s state-centered axes ( Ding et al., 2026 ) , and with the episode record (Equation 1 ). The facts come from the released trajectories; the classification and the indicated controls are ours (Table 11 ). Case A: a shortcut that changes the environment, then a wait that never ends. The trace is the released trajectory of the Amazon Nova Pro run on the benchmark’s example task ( Xu et al., 2025a ) , introduced in Section 1 ; the paper describes the pattern as renaming another user, and the released trace shows the agent renaming its own administrator account. The agent, logged into the company chat as an administrator, is to obtain a repository location from a colleague, Alex Turner, clone the repository, and start a server. Its second browser action opens the user administration page, and seven actions later it has set the name and username of its own account to “Alex Turner”, so the directory lists two users of that name. After a guessed repository URL, a guessed wiki URL, and two web searches fail, it opens a direct message with a different colleague, Mike Chen, and writes “Hi Alex, could you please provide me with the repository URL”. From then on it answers every prompt to continue by saying that it is waiting for Alex Turner, 81 times, until the run ends after 22 minutes without a finish action. The benchmark’s authors report this pattern as a fabricated shortcut; the state-centered axes flag an identity record changed without authority. The episode record adds the chain (Table 11 ). As Section 2.4 recorded, the origin is a P1 decision in R2 to alter the directory rather than search it; the P2 action mutates R4 and R6, so an identity in a shared system points at the agent itself; the wrong-recipient message (R5) contaminates the R2 belief at P3 (H2); the directory listing and the message header displayed the evidence, but no P4 check read them, and no P5 response or P6 escalation followed before the budget ran out (H7). Three controls are indicated: a P0 scope rule that excludes directory writes from a task that only reads it, a P2 recipient check against the conversation partner, and a P5 stall rule that escalates after a bounded number of identical waiting turns. A local checkpoint would address none of these, because restoring the agent’s state leaves the renamed account in place (F5). Case B: an example output accepted as a test result. HyperAgent, a planner–navigator–executor system, works on a Matplotlib issue from SWE-bench Lite in which a RangeSlider callback blocks input to other widgets. The released trace runs through seven planner rounds and six sub-agent handoffs (four navigator, one editor, one executor) with 23 inner tool steps; the executor’s report is the last handoff. After the planner’s change, the executor writes and runs a test script. The script opens an interactive window, so its only output is a font-cache message. The executor notes that the plot must be operated by hand. Even so, its report to the planner lists interaction steps and concludes that the blocking issue “has been resolved”. The report ends: “The above output is just an example”. The planner replies “Thank you for running the test and providing the results”, states that the solution “has been tested”, and ends the task ( Cemri et al., 2025 ) . MAST labels the trace disobey role specification (1.2) and information withholding (2.4), which describe the navigator’s and executor’s behavior. The state-centered axes flag a stored claim without provenance. The episode record shows where the evidence changed status. An R4 observation (the font-cache message) and the executor’s hypothetical output enter the R5 handoff together. The qualifier does not survive the planner’s P3 integration. P4 acceptance and P6 termination therefore rest on example output. In terms of Equation 7 , the trace shows no completed interactive test, yet the run recorded the check as passed. The indicated control binds every “tested” claim to an execution record that the planner reads directly; for a GUI fix, the record includes an interaction log or widget-level assertions, not only terminal output. It also blocks termination while a required check is unexecuted. The case also reveals a measurement gap. Costs and interference Controls add cost and new failure paths: search and voting use compute, provenance uses storage, approval uses attention, compression can lose a constraint, conservative checks can reject legitimate actions, and staging adds latency ( Chen et al., 2026b ) . Claiming reward hacking requires evidence that optimization raises acceptance without improving independent correctness ( Liu et al., 2023a ) . Trace-length effects on quality remain unmeasured despite widespread review ( Pan et al., 2026 ) , so compliant completion, unrecovered impact, latency, compute, and human effort should be reported together. E.4. Scope and limitations of the background The findings above rest on published results and inherit their limits: most benchmarks publish end states rather than intermediate state, message provenance, or execution records, so propagation is rarely observable; our labels for what a method restores or contains follow the authors’ descriptions; many reported measurements lack the denominators needed to relate them to a population of runs, which is why findings are graded rather than pooled; and software and question-answering tasks supply most of the evidence. Section 4 addresses the first of these limits directly by annotating released trajectories. E.5.
