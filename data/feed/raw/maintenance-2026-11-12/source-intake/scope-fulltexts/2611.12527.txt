Corresponding author: Sang-Hoon Lee (sanghoonlee@ajou.ac.kr) Affiliation: Department of Artificial Intelligence, Ajou University, Suwon, Korea Ha-Yeong Choi 1 1 footnotemark: 1 Affiliation: Frontier AI Lab, KT Corp., Seoul, Korea Abstract This work proposes WaveGen, a fully end-to-end diffusion-based text-to-waveform framework based on Spectral Forcing, which jointly learns an internal spectral trajectory and the target waveform trajectory within a single model, enabling direct end-to-end text-to-waveform generation. Unlike recent TTS pipelines, WaveGen does not rely on separately trained or externally decoded intermediate representations, such as neural audio codecs, Mel-spectrogram vocoders, or VAE-based latent models. In addition, WaveGen performs internal text-speech alignment within a single model, eliminating the need for external alignment modules such as duration predictors. To preserve a truly end-to-end formulation, our framework further avoids dependence on self-supervised representations, such as BERT-based text embeddings and Wav2Vec 2.0-based semantic representations, as well as semantic distillation methods for training optimization. To achieve this, we carefully design Spectral Forcing, a model architecture with disentangled diffusion heads, and a training objective based on a multi-scale spectral v v -loss. We further validate the scalability of end-to-end TTS models by scaling the model size from 0.1B to 0.6B parameters. Using a single architecture, open-source data, and one-stage training, without external modules such as duration predictor or REPA, WaveGen demonstrates fully end-to-end waveform generation achieving state-of-the-art performance on the Emilia training dataset. In particular, WaveGen achieves a WER of 1.47 and a SIM of 0.75 on the Seed-en benchmark. Source code and checkpoints are available at https://github.com/sh-lee-prml/WaveGen . 1 Introduction Recent progress in language models and generative models has substantially advanced text-to-speech (TTS) synthesis.
Large language model (LLM)-based TTS systems [ 58 , 22 ] have replaced conventional acoustic representations with neural audio codecs, enabling speech generation through discrete token modeling and in-context voice prompting.
In parallel, diffusion and conditional flow matching (CFM)-based TTS models [ 33 , 5 ] have improved the quality and flexibility of Mel-spectrogram generation through masked infilling and zero-shot voice conditioning.
More recently, latent diffusion approaches [ 75 , 46 ] further improve the efficiency of generative modeling by operating in compressed representation spaces rather than directly modeling high-dimensional audio signals. Despite these advances, most recent TTS systems still rely on intermediate representations and external waveform decoders.
Codec-based models [ 7 , 6 , 57 ] depend on the capacity and quality of neural audio codecs [ 72 , 8 , 32 , 23 , 70 , 10 , 61 , 11 ] ; Mel-spectrogram-based models require separately trained neural vocoders; and latent diffusion models require an additional pre-trained latent.
These intermediate modules have been highly effective in practice, but they also introduce additional training stages, architectural dependencies, and upper-bound constraints on the final quality.
In particular, high-fidelity waveform generation often requires GAN training, carefully designed architectures, or representation-specific optimization, making the overall pipeline increasingly complex. Meanwhile, although end-to-end (E2E) TTS models [ 52 , 26 , 12 ] were explored in earlier studies, their limited scalability, training instability, and inferior zero-shot quality led the field to converge toward multi-stage pipelines.
These limitations stem from the high temporal resolution of raw waveforms, which makes text-speech alignment difficult and often requires complex adversarial training for perceptually realistic waveform generation. Only E3-TTS [ 17 ] demonstrated the potential of waveform-level diffusion for TTS. However, it still relies on BERT-based text representations to facilitate text-waveform alignment, and directly applying diffusion modeling to waveform signals imposes a substantial computational burden due to the high temporal resolution of audio. In this work, we propose WaveGen , a fully E2E diffusion-based text-to-waveform framework built upon Spectral Forcing .
WaveGen directly generates high-resolution waveform from text within a single architecture. The key idea is to revisit the prediction and loss spaces of CFM for raw waveform generation. We found x x -prediction with v v -loss is essential for E2E waveform generation.
Instead of forcing the model to learn waveform dynamics alone, Spectral Forcing jointly guides the model to learn an internal spectral trajectory while optimizing the target waveform trajectory.
This design provides a structured inductive bias for high-resolution audio generation while preserving a truly E2E text-to-waveform formulation. To this end, we carefully design WaveGen architecture with disentangled diffusion heads and introduce a multi-scale spectral loss for stable waveform-level flow matching.
We further investigate the scalability of E2E TTS by increasing the model size from 0.1B to 0.6B parameters, showing that direct text-to-waveform generation can benefit from model scaling when the training objective and architecture are properly designed. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose WaveGen , a fully E2E text-to-waveform framework that directly generates raw waveform from text without separately trained intermediate representations. • We introduce Spectral Forcing , which jointly learns an internal spectral trajectory and the target waveform trajectory, enabling stable CFM-based optimization in the high-resolution waveform domain. • We investigate prediction and loss spaces for waveform generation and design a scalable waveform-level diffusion architecture with disentangled diffusion heads. • We demonstrate that WaveGen achieves state-of-the-art zero-shot TTS performance using a single architecture, open-source data, and one-stage training, providing evidence that fully E2E text-to-waveform generation can be a viable alternative to increasingly complex multi-stage TTS pipelines. 2 Related Work 2.1 Conventional TTS Traditional TTS systems consist of an acoustic model and a neural vocoder. Acoustic models generate acoustic features such as Mel-spectrogram, and neural vocoders convert acoustic features into raw waveform signals. Tacotron [ 64 ] paved the way of deep-learning based TTS system by introducing attention-based encoder-decoder structure to align text with speech, and Tacotron 2 [ 55 ] utilized WaveNet [ 44 ] to generate waveform from synthetic Mel-spectrogram. Transformer TTS [ 40 ] verified the effectiveness of encoder-decoder Transformer. However, these models suffer from unstable attention alignment and slow inference speed due to their autoregressive (AR) nature. To address these issues, FastSpeech [ 53 ] introduced parallel synthesis using a duration which is extracted from teacher AR models. Also, GAN-based neural vocoders accelerated TTS research in terms of both quality and inference speed. Specifically, PWG [ 67 ] and MelGAN [ 31 ] introduced GAN-based vocoder, and HiFi-GAN [ 29 ] introduced multi-period discriminator (MPD) which significantly enhances perceptual quality of synthetic speech. BigVGAN [ 35 ] scaled up the neural vocoder via snake periodic activation and an anti-aliasing low-pass filter. iSTFTNet [ 24 ] and Vocos [ 56 ] enhance the sampling efficiency by modeling features in a low-resolution space with iSTFT. UniAudio [ 68 ] and Moshi [ 9 ] proposed efficient RVQ decoding method using multi-scale Transformer. Recently, most models utilize a pre-trained LLM for TTS backbone, and finetuned them to decode semantic token [ 14 , 13 ] or RVQ tokens [ 27 , 22 , 18 ] which are extracted from large-scale high-quality audio codec via multi-token prediction (MTP) AR Transformer. E2-TTS [ 15 ] introduces novel text-speech alignment learning with filler tokens. F5-TTS [ 5 ] and ZipVoice [ 77 ] introduce efficient CFM-based TTS models without any external duration predictor. Also, hybrid models such as CosyVoice [ 14 , 13 ] utilized CFM-based Mel-spectrogram decoder conditioned on semantic tokens. Meanwhile, latent diffusion models (LDM) [ 34 ] have been investigated for efficient TTS modeling. Furthermore, continuous language models such as VoxCPM [ 75 ] and VibeVoice [ 46 ] show their effectiveness of AR diffusion via highly-compressed latent representation. 2.2 End-to-End TTS E2E TTS has long been investigated as a way to simplify conventional cascaded speech synthesis pipelines. Early neural TTS systems such as Tacotron [ 64 ] established a unified sequence-to-sequence framework for text-to-acoustic modeling, but still required a Griffin-Lim algorithm [ 19 ] . ClariNet [ 48 ] explored parallel waveform generation through knowledge distillation from autoregressive WaveNet models. FastSpeech 2s [ 52 ] extended non-autoregressive TTS toward direct waveform generation. EATS [ 12 ] jointly optimized learnable duration modeling and waveform generation with adversarial training. VITS [ 26 ] introduced a strong text-to-waveform framework by combining variational inference, normalizing flows [ 25 ] , and adversarial waveform training [ 29 ] . Despite these advances, prior E2E TTS systems still have important limitations: many rely on adversarial training to obtain perceptually realistic waveforms, which require carefully designed multiple discriminators, auxiliary losses, and significantly long training. More importantly, most of these models were not primarily designed for zero-shot speech synthesis, where the model must generate high-quality speech for unseen speakers from text and speech prompts. As a result, recent zero-shot TTS research has largely shifted toward codec-based language models, Mel-spectrogram diffusion models, or LDM rather than direct waveform-level generation. Recently, E3-TTS [ 17 ] revisited diffusion-based text-to-waveform generation, demonstrating the potential of waveform-level generative modeling for TTS; however, it still relies on pretrained BERT-based text representations to facilitate text-waveform alignment, and directly applying diffusion modeling to high-resolution waveform signals introduces substantial computational cost. In contrast, this paper revisits fully E2E TTS under a stricter formulation: a single model directly generates waveform from text without neural audio codecs, Mel-spectrogram decoders, VAE, neural vocoders, duration predictors, self-supervised representations, or adversarial training as indicated in Table 1 . We analyze raw waveform as generative targets (Section 3.1 ), introduce a novel framework (Section 3.2 ), and efficient architecture (Section 3.3 ). Also, we revisit the prediction space and loss space for raw waveform (Section 3.4 ) and introduce the improved loss (Section 3.5 ). 3.1 CFM-based Waveform Generation Recently, diffusion and CFM-based waveform generation models [ 54 , 65 , 36 , 38 ] have emerged as a promising alternative to GAN-based models, offering a new direction for high-fidelity speech synthesis. PeriodWave [ 37 ] introduced a CFM-based periodic-aware generator for waveform generation. RFWave [ 42 ] presented efficient CFM-based models with STFT/iSTFT. Flow2GAN [ 69 ] improved CFM objective of waveform in spectral space. StreamFlow [ 6 ] proposed streaming flow matching for streaming waveform generation and efficient waveform diffusion Transformers using linear-reshape transformation. However, these works only employ waveform-level diffusion with strongly aligned acoustic conditions such as Mel-spectrogram or pre-trained neural audio codec. In our study, basic CFMs fail to align text-speech in high-resolution waveform space without any external modules. 3.2 Spectral Forcing Due to the difficulty of waveform-level generation, previous works utilize intermediate representations such as Mel-spectrogram. To bridge the gap between text and waveform, we introduce Spectral Forcing, a method to jointly learn an internal spectral trajectory and the target waveform trajectory, enabling stable CFM-based optimization in the high-resolution waveform space.
Specifically, we introduce a low-resolution spectral trajectory as an auxiliary generation target, which provides a structured inductive bias for text-speech alignment. Unlike cascaded approaches, the spectral trajectory is jointly generated within the same model and is used only as internal frequency-rich guidance, rather than as an external intermediate representation. We adopt a simple DiT [ 45 ] -only architecture with AdaLN-SOLA [ 20 ] . The model takes as input the concatenation of the reshaped noisy waveform x t w ​ a ​ v ​ e x^{wave}_{t} and its noisy spectral representation x t s ​ p ​ e ​ c x^{spec}_{t} which is a lower-resolution Mel-spectrogram with 64 bins. For text conditioning, we adopt average pooling of ZipVoice [ 77 , 76 ] that expands the average-pooled text embeddings along the temporal dimension to match the speech length without any external encoder. Following [ 33 , 5 ] , we utilize masked-infilling training for prompted generation by concatenating masked representations together. 3.3 Disentangled DiT Heads We found that concatenating input representations is necessary for better text-speech alignment, but disentangling DiT heads for each representation is required for robust estimation. We use shared DiT blocks as an alignment backbone, and simply add shallow disentangled DiT heads as representation-specific generation. Baseline model uses 14 shared DiT blocks and two disentangled heads. We only compute the loss on the masked regions of the target representation. 3.4 Decoupled Prediction and Loss Spaces for Raw Waveform Generation Recent flow matching-based waveform reconstruction models, such as PeriodWave [ 37 ] and RFWave [ 42 ] , have demonstrated the potential of flow-based models for raw waveform reconstruction by improving periodic modeling and sampling efficiency. Despite these advances, we observe that conventional velocity-based objectives can be insufficient for preserving fine-grained waveform energy, often leading to degraded perceptual quality in generated audio. Motivated by JiT [ 41 ] , which highlights the effectiveness of clean-data prediction for high-dimensional raw signals, and Latent Forcing [ 2 ] and ReGen [ 39 ] , which leverage latent-assisted generation trajectories to better structure raw-space generation, we revisit the design of prediction and loss spaces in raw waveform CFM.
Specifically, our model predicts clean waveform and spectral targets through Waveform DiT and Spectral DiT, while computing the training objective in the velocity space. This decoupled x x -prediction and v v -loss strategy enables effective waveform energy modeling without introducing the severe audible artifacts observed with direct x x -loss optimization. LibriTTS is a high-quality speech dataset with a sampling rate of 24,000 Hz in WAV format. We filtered LibriTTS to include only utterances with durations of up to 12s for efficient training (319,627 samples). For large-scale training, WaveGen-S/B (v0.1) use the English subsets of Emilia (CC-BY-NC-4.0) and Emilia-YODAS (CC-BY-4.0) [ 21 ] provided as MP3-compressed audio with a sampling rate of 24,000 Hz. Following [ 5 ] , we filter out several speakers and use utterances with durations of up to 30s. We also released WaveGen (0.6B) trained with multilingual Emilia datasets. Training We train LibriTTS models with a learning rate of 1 × \times 10 -4 using the AdamW optimizer [ 43 ] , a fixed batch size of 128 for 1.1M steps. We train Emilia models with a learning rate of 1 × \times 10 -4 using AdamW optimizer [ 43 ] , a dynamic batch size with total 480s frame of waveform for 1.1M steps. All models except for the 0.6B models are trained on four GPUs using mixed precision. Due to limited computational resources, we train the models only for four epochs. We train WaveGen (0.6B) on eight GPUs for 11 epochs.
During training, we use a masked-infilling strategy by dropping the speech prompt and text condition (0.3 and 0.2, respectively). Sampling We adopt the Euler method as the ODE solver. For CFG scheduling, we build upon the strategy used in ZipVoice, where the text condition is dropped only at early time steps, and all ablation studies are conducted using this. However, when using a threshold of t < 0.5 t<0.5 , we found that this substantially degrades speaker similarity in our model. Therefore, we restrict text-condition dropout to t < 0.02 t<0.02 . In the following subsections and Appendix F , we further provide detailed analyses of the waveform diffusion optimization techniques used in WaveGen. 4.11 Information bottleneck pre-net for multi-representation generation We further investigate the effect of Spectral Forcing during large-scale training. When using a large-scale dataset, the dependency of Spectral Forcing increases because the model has difficulty aligning text with speech at the initial stage. While Spectral Forcing is weak guidance, the model is prone to learn to reconstruct waveforms from Spectral Forcing, resulting in delayed emergence of text-speech alignment. To reduce this limitation, we revisit the simple techniques, pre-net used in Tacotron [ 64 ] by adding information bottleneck layers with two MLP layers and dropout to reduce dependency on teacher forcing for AR training. Specifically, we utilize 8n Mel bins and smaller bottleneck dimension of 32 similar to [ 3 ] . We apply this bottleneck layer with dropout of 0.2 during training and inference. It improves the robustness of multi-representation generation by learning to generate waveform from multiple conditions. We investigated the effect of dropout ratio on Spectral Forcing in Appendix F.2 . 4.12 Prompt Anchor Following [ 66 ] , we observe a train-inference mismatch in iterative generation under masked-infilling training. During training, the prompt region is fixed to the GT speech and is therefore not explicitly optimized for iterative updates. At inference time, however, repeatedly updating x t x_{t} in the prompt region can introduce undesirable deviations from the GT prompt trajectory. To address this issue, we anchor the prompt region to a GT-based intermediate state during iterative sampling. Specifically, at each sampling step, we construct the prompt-region x t x_{t} by interpolating between the corresponding x 0 x_{0} and x 1 x_{1} , rather than using the model-updated values. This simple prompt anchoring strategy substantially improves speaker similarity by reducing the train-inference mismatch introduced by masked-infilling training. By contrast, modern TTS models require large-scale training datasets to model diverse speaking styles including prosody and timbre in zero-shot scenarios. Although Emilia enables large-scale training, its MP3-compressed audio can limit the amount of recoverable high-frequency detail, which is particularly important for end-to-end waveform generation. In contrast, models trained on LibriTTS preserve and generate richer high-frequency information as illustrated in Figure 2 .

This efficient simplification, which enables speech generation with a single model without relying on external modules, enhances the accessibility of speech technologies and provides a foundation for broader adoption in both academic and industrial settings.
Furthermore, in terms of application technology, this method can have a positive impact on diverse fields such as voice interfaces, education, and media content. In particular, the generation of clear and natural speech can improve the user experience and contribute to the advancement of speech language model. Societal Negative Impact Although the model and method proposed in this work do not directly raise ethical issues, there is a possibility of misuse when combined with voice cloning due to their ability to generate high-quality speech. For example, the model could be used to imitate a specific speaker’s voice or generate realistic fake speech to convey misleading information, which may lead to issues such as voice phishing or impersonation.
To mitigate these potential risks, it is necessary to pursue complementary research on fake audio detection as well as speech safety, including methods for identification and authenticity verification. As part of future work, we plan to further explore watermarking and authentication techniques to enable tracing the origin of generated audio. Limitation We introduce an E2E TTS model that does not rely on any external modules, including SSL models. Nevertheless, SSL-based guidance, such as REPA, can be incorporated to accelerate training. Due to limited computational resources, we restrict large-scale training to four epochs. The observed scaling trend suggests that longer training may further improve performance, which we leave for future work. In addition, we plan to extend our model to 48 kHz waveform generation. 7 Conclusion We proposed WaveGen, a fully E2E diffusion-based text-to-waveform TTS system. Unlike previous TTS systems, WaveGen directly generates raw waveforms from text without separately trained intermediate representations. By introducing Spectral Forcing, disentangled DiT heads, and a multi-scale spectral v v -loss, WaveGen enables stable CFM-based optimization in the high-resolution waveform domain. Appendix D Crowdsourcing Details Figure 3: MOS interfaces We conducted Mean Opinion Score (MOS) evaluations using Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) 16 16 16 https://www.mturk.com/ to qualitatively assess the quality of synthesized speech from the TTS. Specifically, we carried out two types of evaluations: nMOS, which measures the naturalness of the generated speech, and sMOS, which measures the similarity of the generated speaker timbre to that of the prompt speaker.
For each evaluation, 20 native speakers of American English participated. Each participant rated 100 samples per model on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 to 5. To reduce unreliable responses, such as participants randomly selecting scores, and to improve the reliability of the MTurk evaluation, we included Gaussian-noise samples as control samples. Responses from participants who failed to identify these samples as fake were excluded from the final analysis. In addition, only MTurk workers with a prior task approval rate of at least 80% and at least 100 approved HITs were allowed to participate in the evaluation. For a fair comparison, we utilize the same architecture of F5-TTS to demonstrate the effectiveness of waveform-level diffusion training and Spectral Forcing. F.1 Spectral Loss Basically, we introduce multi-scale spectral v-loss for our models. However, we adopt multi-scale Mel-spectrogram x-loss to compare Spectral Forcing based on WavTTS implementation. We observe that multi-scale Mel-spectrogram loss could improve the initial alignment learning and entire objective performances including WER and SIM. However, this loss induces buzzing sound on the generated waveform. To address this perceptual limitation, we released the perceptually-improved models together using multi-scale spectral v-loss which could significantly improve the perceptual quality. While it shows lower performance in objective metrics, we highly recommend adopting our new objectives for perceptual quality. We released the model finetuned with multi-scale spectral v-loss for perceptually improved speech synthesis. F.2 Spectral Dropout We further investigated the use of spectral dropout in spectral forcing to improve robustness to noise in multilingual datasets. Figure 4 presents the objective evaluation metrics for different spectral dropout rates. The results suggest that higher dropout rates can improve initial alignment when training on noisy waveforms, but may also cause the spectral path to collapse during training. Without spectral dropout, the model tends to rely on the noisy Mel-spectrogram for waveform reconstruction. We therefore selected a dropout rate of 0.2 to reduce this reliance while maintaining the stability of the spectral path.
