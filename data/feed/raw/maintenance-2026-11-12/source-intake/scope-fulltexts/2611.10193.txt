matteo.mordacchini@iit.cnr.it 2026 Abstract. Large language models (LLMs) increasingly support Web-based services that must balance response quality and inference cost across heterogeneous requests. Conventional routing selects one model per prompt, while fixed ensembles cannot adapt their composition and cost to individual requests. We introduce PISA, a prompt-adaptive, cost-aware single-model router, and PISA-E, its extension to ensemble routing. At the core of both methods, PISA combines soft cluster memberships with model-specific predictors to estimate individual model capabilities. Building on these estimates, PISA-E jointly selects ensemble composition and cardinality under an explicit cost–quality preference that can be adjusted at inference time without retraining. A tractable ensemble-success surrogate and efficient candidate selection enable this extension without ensemble-specific training. Across four LLM routing benchmarks under in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions, PISA achieves the highest macro-average cost–performance hypervolume among the evaluated single-model routers in both settings. Meanwhile, PISA-E achieves the highest macro-average task performance among the evaluated ensemble approaches when prioritizing response quality, surpassing the strongest competitors by 2.09 and 2.87 percentage points under ID and OOD conditions, respectively. At selected lower-cost operating points, it also exceeds the strongest ensemble competitors’ macro-average task performance while reducing average inference cost by approximately 36% and 25%, respectively. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of prompt-adaptive capability estimation for both single-model routing and selective ensembling. Introduction Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in Web-based services, supporting applications such as search and information discovery, recommendation, and intelligent agents ( Hui et al., 2026 ; Behdin et al., 2025 ) . Their adoption also extends to publicly accessible LLM services, creating new challenges for efficient and scalable inference ( Liu et al., 2026b ) . These services must handle heterogeneous user requests while balancing response quality and inference cost. When multiple LLMs with different capabilities and pricing structures are available, invoking expensive models indiscriminately wastes resources, whereas consistently selecting inexpensive models can compromise response quality. Moreover, different users and requests may have different cost preferences, making it desirable to adapt inference strategies to both prompt characteristics and inference-cost preferences ( Varangot-Reille et al., 2026 ) . LLM routing addresses this challenge by selecting models according to prompt characteristics, expected response quality, and inference cost ( Moslem and Kelleher, 2026 ) . Recent approaches extend routing to budget-adaptive selection ( Zu et al., 2026 ) , reasoning-strategy selection ( Pan et al., 2026 ) , and additional deployment constraints ( Li et al., 2026b ) . However, selecting a single model cannot exploit the complementary capabilities of multiple LLMs. Ensembling offers an alternative by combining multiple model responses ( Chen et al., 2026 ) , but introduces additional inference costs. Existing ensemble approaches explore adaptive model composition, diversity-aware selection, and budget-constrained optimization ( Huang et al., 2024 ; Cohen et al., 2026 ; Tekin et al., 2024 ) .
Nevertheless, existing approaches typically address only parts of the problem: some adapt ensemble composition without explicitly optimizing inference cost, while others rely on classification-specific aggregation or sequential execution. Moreover, ensemble cardinality is generally fixed during training or configuration, limiting the ability to adjust the number of invoked models to individual prompts and changing cost preferences at inference time. This motivates ensemble routing , where both the composition and cardinality of the invoked model subset are determined dynamically at inference time according to prompt characteristics and an explicit cost–quality preference. We introduce PISA , a prompt-adaptive, cost-aware single-model router that estimates the capabilities of individual LLMs, and PISA-E , its extension to prompt-adaptive ensemble routing.
PISA-E reuses the individual capability estimates to construct a tractable surrogate for ensemble success, eliminating the need to train separate quality estimators for different model combinations. By combining these estimates with inference cost, PISA-E selects prompt-specific model subsets, jointly determining which models to invoke and how many. Crucially, the cost–quality preference can be adjusted at inference time without additional hyperparameter optimization or retraining, allowing the same framework to support different operating points. The selected responses are combined through capability-weighted semantic similarity to produce the final ensemble response. We evaluate PISA and PISA-E on four LLM routing benchmarks under in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions. PISA achieves the highest macro-average hypervolume among the evaluated single-model routers in both settings, ranking first in six of the eight benchmark–setting combinations. PISA-E achieves the highest macro-average task performance among the evaluated ensemble approaches in both settings, improving over the strongest competitors by 2.09 percentage points under ID and 2.87 under OOD conditions when prioritizing response quality. At selected lower-cost operating points, it also exceeds the strongest competitors’ macro-average task performance while reducing average inference cost by approximately 36% and 25%, respectively. Finally, comparisons with fixed-cardinality variants demonstrate the benefits of adapting ensemble size to individual prompts. Our main contributions are: • We develop PISA , a prompt-adaptive, cost-aware single-model router that estimates individual model capabilities through soft cluster memberships and model-specific predictors, providing the foundation for ensemble routing. • We propose PISA-E , a framework for prompt-adaptive, cost-aware ensemble routing that uses ensemble-level utility estimates derived from individual model capabilities to jointly determine ensemble composition and cardinality at inference time, according to prompt characteristics and inference-cost preferences. • We evaluate both methods across four benchmarks under ID and OOD conditions, demonstrating the highest macro-average single-model routing HV and ensemble task performance among the evaluated approaches in both settings. We also demonstrate operating points with higher task performance at lower cost than the strongest ensemble competitors and quantify the benefits of prompt-dependent ranking and adaptive ensemble cardinality. 2. Methodology Our approach builds on a shared capability-estimation framework to support both single-model and ensemble routing. PISA estimates the prompt-dependent capabilities of individual LLMs and uses them for cost-aware single-model selection. PISA-E extends this framework to ensemble routing by combining the same capability estimates into an ensemble-success surrogate, allowing model subsets of different compositions and cardinalities to be evaluated under the cost–quality objective introduced in Section 2 . Figure 1 illustrates the resulting inference pipeline. 3.1. PISA: Model Routing via Soft Assignments The first stage estimates how likely each candidate model is to produce a correct response to a given prompt. PISA learns prompt-dependent capability estimates from historical model outcomes and semantic representations of the input queries. Assume a training set of N N prompts, where each prompt q i q_{i} is represented by an embedding x i = f ⁡ ( q i ) ∈ ℝ d x_{i}=f(q_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d} and is associated with a correctness score s i ​ m ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] s_{im}\in[0,1] for each model m ∈ ℳ m\in\mathcal{M} . At inference time, only the embedding x = f ⁡ ( q ) x=f(q) of the incoming prompt is available. To capture similarities among prompts, we partition the fit-set embeddings using X-means, which determines both the number of clusters K K and their centroids. PISA represents each query through soft memberships over the learned centroids, allowing its representation to incorporate information from multiple clusters. After ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -normalizing embeddings and centroids, these memberships are computed from cosine distances as (9) u k ​ ( x ) = exp ⁡ ( − β ​ d k ​ ( x ) ) ∑ j = 1 K exp ⁡ ( − β ​ d j ​ ( x ) ) , d k ​ ( x ) = 1 − x ⊤ ​ μ k , u_{k}(x)=\frac{\exp(-\beta d_{k}(x))}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp(-\beta d_{j}(x))},\qquad d_{k}(x)=1-x^{\top}\mu_{k}, where μ k \mu_{k} denotes the centroid of cluster k k and β > 0 \beta>0 controls the concentration of the assignments. This representation allows capability estimation to exploit information from multiple neighboring regions instead of relying on a single hard assignment. Using the resulting membership vector u ⁡ ( x ) u(x) , PISA learns an independent logistic predictor for each candidate model. Each predictor is trained using the soft memberships of the training prompts and their corresponding binary correctness labels, obtained by thresholding evaluation scores at 0.5 0.5 . For an incoming prompt, the estimated capability of model m m is (10) p ^ m ​ ( x ) = σ ⁡ ( w m ⊤ ​ u ​ ( x ) + b m ) , \hat{p}_{m}(x)=\sigma\!\left(w_{m}^{\top}u(x)+b_{m}\right), where σ ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \sigma(\cdot) is the logistic sigmoid and ( w m , b m ) (w_{m},b_{m}) are the parameters fitted for model m m using L2-regularized logistic regression. The regularization strength λ m \lambda_{m} is selected independently for each model through 5-fold cross-validation minimizing log loss over 10 logarithmically spaced values between 10 − 4 10^{-4} and 10 4 10^{4} . The selected predictor is then refitted on the full training set. To incorporate inference cost into the routing decision, we estimate each model’s cost from its average observed training-set cost. Let c i ​ m c_{im} denote the cost of invoking model m m on training prompt q i q_{i} , and define (11) c m avg = 1 N ​ ∑ i = 1 N c i ​ m , c ¯ m = c m avg max m ′ ∈ ℳ ⁡ c m ′ avg . c_{m}^{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{im},\qquad\bar{c}_{m}=\frac{c_{m}^{\mathrm{avg}}}{\max_{m^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}}c_{m^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{avg}}}. The normalized cost c ¯ m ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] \bar{c}_{m}\in[0,1] provides the cost term in Eq. ( 3 ). For each incoming prompt and cost preference α \alpha , PISA selects the model maximizing the corresponding single-model utility in Eq. ( 2 ). The resulting capability estimates also provide the foundation for the ensemble-routing extension described next. 3.2. PISA-E: Prompt-Adaptive Cost-Aware LLM Ensembling While PISA selects a single model, PISA-E extends the routing decision to model subsets, allowing both ensemble composition and cardinality to vary with the prompt and cost preference. Given the capability estimates { p ^ m ​ ( x ) } m = 1 M \{\hat{p}_{m}(x)\}_{m=1}^{M} , PISA-E considers ensembles E ⊆ ℳ E\subseteq\mathcal{M} with 1 ≤ | E | ≤ k max 1\leq|E|\leq k_{\max} , where k max k_{\max} denotes the maximum ensemble size. Its central challenge is to evaluate the expected utility of candidate subsets before invoking their constituent models. Directly estimating the performance of every possible ensemble would require modeling the behavior of numerous model combinations. Instead, PISA-E constructs an ensemble-level utility estimate from the individual capabilities already provided by PISA. Specifically, we use the probability that a strict majority of the selected models produce correct responses as a tractable surrogate for comparing candidate ensembles. This criterion captures how the predicted capabilities of the constituent models combine, without requiring ensemble-specific predictors or observing their generated responses. For an ensemble E E of size k k , we model the correctness of each constituent model through a Bernoulli variable Z m Z_{m} with success probability p ^ m ​ ( x ) \hat{p}_{m}(x) . Assuming conditional independence of these variables given the prompt, the strict-majority success probability is (12) p ^ E maj ​ ( x ) = Pr ⁡ ( ∑ m ∈ E Z m ≥ ⌊ k 2 ⌋ + 1 ) , Z m ∼ Bernoulli ⁡ ( p ^ m ​ ( x ) ) , \hat{p}_{E}^{\mathrm{maj}}(x)=\Pr\!\left(\sum_{m\in E}Z_{m}\geq\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor+1\right),\quad Z_{m}\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\hat{p}_{m}(x)), with the Z m Z_{m} treated as independent. We compute this probability using the standard Poisson-binomial dynamic program. The strict-majority success probability serves as a surrogate for ensemble selection, since the outcome of the final response resolver also depends on relationships among the generated responses. Accordingly, p ^ E maj ​ ( x ) \hat{p}^{\mathrm{maj}}_{E}(x) provides the quality term in the ensemble-routing objective independently of the response resolution procedure. To balance this surrogate with inference cost, PISA-E estimates the cost of an ensemble by summing the average costs of its constituent models. Let the model costs defined in Eq. ( 11 ) be ordered as c ( 1 ) avg ≥ ⋯ ≥ c ( M ) avg c_{(1)}^{\mathrm{avg}}\geq\cdots\geq c_{(M)}^{\mathrm{avg}} . We normalize the ensemble cost as (13) C ¯ ​ ( E ) = ∑ m ∈ E c m avg ∑ j = 1 k max c ( j ) avg . \bar{C}(E)=\frac{\sum_{m\in E}c_{m}^{\mathrm{avg}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{k_{\max}}c_{(j)}^{\mathrm{avg}}}. The denominator corresponds to the maximum cost of any subset containing at most k max k_{\max} models and remains constant across candidate ensembles. Combining this normalized cost with the strict-majority surrogate yields the ensemble utility in Eq. ( 8 ), allowing subsets of different cardinalities to be compared under the same cost–quality preference. Since evaluating all feasible subsets would require a combinatorial search, PISA-E uses a greedy candidate-construction procedure. For each prompt, the models are first ranked according to their utilities, using the same cost normalization as for all candidate ensembles: (14) J { m } ​ ( x , α ) = α ​ p ^ m ​ ( x ) + ( 1 − α ) ​ ( 1 − C ¯ ​ ( { m } ) ) . J_{\{m\}}(x;\alpha)=\alpha\hat{p}_{m}(x)+(1-\alpha)\bigl(1-\bar{C}(\{m\})\bigr). Here, C ¯ ​ ( { m } ) \bar{C}(\{m\}) is obtained from Eq. ( 13 ) with E = { m } E=\{m\} , so the cost of each model is normalized by the sum of the k max k_{\max} largest average model costs. Candidate ensembles are then constructed as progressively larger prefixes of this ranking, considering odd cardinalities up to k max k_{\max} . Each candidate is evaluated using its strict-majority success probability and normalized inference cost, and the ensemble maximizing the resulting utility is selected. For k max = 1 k_{\max}=1 , both the cost normalization and the selection rule reduce to those of PISA. Restricting the candidates to odd cardinalities follows directly from the strict-majority surrogate. For any even-cardinality ensemble, removing one constituent model produces an odd-cardinality subset whose strict-majority success probability is at least as high, since the required number of correct models decreases by one. As the subset also incurs lower inference cost, the even-cardinality ensemble cannot achieve higher utility under the surrogate objective. Consequently, considering only odd cardinalities does not exclude an optimal solution to the surrogate-based subset optimization problem. The greedy prefix construction further restricts the search space and does not guarantee the globally optimal subset, but provides an efficient procedure for selecting among candidate ensembles. A comparison with exhaustive surrogate optimization is provided in Appendix E . Once an ensemble E E has been selected, PISA-E queries its constituent models in parallel and combines their responses through self-weighted BERTScore ( Zhang* et al., 2020 ) , a capability-aware extension of minimum Bayes risk (MBR) decoding. The resolver incorporates the estimated capabilities of both candidate and reference responses, weighting their contributions according to their predicted reliability. Let y i y_{i} denote the response produced by model i ∈ E i\in E . We first normalize the capability estimates over the selected ensemble: (15) w i ​ ( x , E ) = p ^ i ​ ( x ) ∑ j ∈ E p ^ j ​ ( x ) . w_{i}(x,E)=\frac{\hat{p}_{i}(x)}{\sum_{j\in E}\hat{p}_{j}(x)}. The score assigned to each candidate response is then (16) S i ​ ( x , E ) = w i ​ ( x , E ) ​ ∑ j ∈ E w j ​ ( x , E ) ​ BERTScore ⁡ ( y i , y j ) . S_{i}(x,E)=w_{i}(x,E)\sum_{j\in E}w_{j}(x,E)\operatorname{BERTScore}(y_{i},y_{j}). The final ensemble response is selected as (17) y ∗ = arg ⁡ max i ∈ E ​ S i ​ ( x , E ) . y^{*}=\arg\max_{i\in E}S_{i}(x,E). Alternative response resolution strategies are described in Appendix A . Experiments 4.1. Experimental Setup Benchmarks. We evaluate PISA and PISA-E on four LLM routing benchmarks: LLMRouterBench Track-2 ( Li et al., 2026a ) , RouterBench ( Hu et al., 2024 ) , FusionBench ( Feng et al., 2026 ) , and SPROUT ( Somerstep et al., 2025 ) . These benchmarks cover heterogeneous candidate model pools and task groups, with performance measured using their respective evaluation scores. Evaluation Protocol. We consider both ID and OOD evaluations. For ID experiments, we use the released data partitions when available; otherwise, we split the data into 70% development and 30% test, further dividing the development set into 80% training and 20% validation. For OOD experiments, we train exclusively on ID task groups, select configurations using ID validation data, and evaluate on held-out task groups. ID and OOD experiments use separately fitted routers. All experiments are evaluated over five seeds, with fixed data partitions, and all approaches are evaluated using the model responses provided by the benchmarks. Baselines. We compare PISA against four single-model routing baselines: EmbedLLM ( Zhuang et al., 2025 ) , Avengers-Pro ( Zhang et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2025c ) , K-NN, and a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP). For PISA-E, we consider five LLM ensembling approaches: DFPE ( Cohen et al., 2026 ) , LLM-TOPLA ( Tekin et al., 2024 ) , SelectLLM ( Maurya et al., 2025 ) , PairRanker ( Jiang et al., 2023 ) , and BERTScore-based MBR ( Zhang* et al., 2020 ) . PairRanker employs PairRM, the pairwise reward model introduced by LLM-Blender, to rank candidate responses. We evaluate its pretrained checkpoint (PairRanker-PT) and a fine-tuned variant (PairRanker-FT), trained following the original procedure. We additionally report an oracle single-model selector that uses ground-truth evaluation scores to select the best-performing model for each prompt, providing a reference for the performance attainable through perfect model selection. Metrics. We report task performance and inference cost, computed from benchmark-specific scores and model invocation costs. Task performance is macro-averaged across task groups. To summarize cost–performance trade-offs, we use normalized dominated hypervolume (HV), multiplied by 100. The reference performance corresponds to a random router, while the reference cost is set to the 40th percentile of the full-pool cost distribution for single-model routing and the 70th percentile for ensemble routing. These reference points are chosen to accommodate all the operating points of the compared methods. Since existing ensemble approaches generally provide a single operating point rather than a cost–performance frontier, we compare them directly in terms of task performance and inference cost. Implementation. We employ Qwen3-Embedding-8B ( Zhang et al., 2025a ) for creating the prompt embeddings required by the routers. PISA-E considers ensembles of up to eleven models. Hyperparameters and ensemble configurations are selected independently for each benchmark using validation task performance at α = 1 \alpha=1 . The same fitted capability estimators are reused across different cost–performance operating points. Further implementation details, including the HV definition, are provided in Appendix A . 4.2. Related Work Cost-Aware Model Routing LLM routing selects a model for each prompt by estimating response quality and accounting for inference cost. EmbedLLM ( Zhuang et al., 2025 ) learns latent representations from a model–question correctness matrix. Clustering-based methods instead use similarities among prompts to estimate model performance: UniRoute ( Jitkrittum et al., 2026 ) and Avengers-PRO ( Zhang et al., 2025c ; Zhang et al., 2026 ) rely on hard cluster assignments and cluster-level statistics, while ZeroRouter ( Yan et al., 2026 ) combines clustering with item response theory. PISA differs in using soft cluster memberships and model-specific predictors to estimate prompt-dependent capabilities. Related cost-aware inference strategies include LLM cascades ( Chen et al., 2024 ; Dekoninck et al., 2025 ) and risk-aware routing ( Hao et al., 2026 ) . Cascades may escalate requests sequentially; PISA-E instead selects models for parallel execution before generating their responses. LLM Ensembling and Response Aggregation LLM ensembling combines responses from multiple models to exploit complementary capabilities. Aggregation methods include majority voting ( Wang et al., 2023 ; Ai et al., 2026 ) , learned ranking and fusion, as in LLM-Blender ( Jiang et al., 2023 ) and URG ( Lv et al., 2024 ) , and similarity-based minimum Bayes risk decoding, including variants that account for uncertainty ( Daheim et al., 2025 ) . Multi-agent debate ( Du et al., 2024 ) and Mixture-of-Agents ( Wang et al., 2025 ) also combine model outputs, but involve interaction across successive rounds or stages. PISA-E does not organize such collaboration: after selecting an ensemble, it queries the models in parallel and resolves their responses using capability-weighted semantic similarity. Adaptive and Cost-Aware Ensemble Selection Ensemble selection determines which models to invoke, and whether that choice changes across queries. LLM-TOPLA ( Tekin et al., 2024 ) and DFPE ( Cohen et al., 2026 ) construct ensembles that are subsequently reused across queries, whereas SelectLLM ( Maurya et al., 2025 ) adapts ensemble composition to the input while retaining a fixed cardinality. ThriftLLM ( Huang et al., 2025 ) jointly adapts composition and cardinality under cost constraints, but addresses classification queries rather than open-ended response generation. Other adaptive multi-model approaches make different inference decisions. EMS ( Liu et al., 2026a ) expands the set of invoked models sequentially; LightRouter ( Zhang et al., 2025b ) combines model selection with staged collaboration; and retrieval-based selection and response enhancement are investigated in ( Tang et al., 2026 ) . Query-specific agentic systems instead configure task plans, agents, or collaboration strategies ( Su et al., 2026 ; Xiao et al., 2026 ; Hamid et al., 2026 ) . PISA-E targets pre-inference selection of a parallel ensemble for response generation. It scores candidate subsets using a strict-majority surrogate constructed from prompt-dependent individual capability estimates, jointly selecting composition and cardinality under an explicit cost–quality preference without training ensemble-specific predictors. 6. Conclusion We introduced PISA, a prompt-adaptive, cost-aware single-model router, and PISA-E, its extension to ensemble routing. Both methods use prompt-dependent capability estimates, which PISA-E reuses to jointly select ensemble composition and cardinality without ensemble-specific training. Across four heterogeneous benchmarks, PISA achieves the highest macro-average hypervolume among the evaluated single-model routers under both ID and OOD conditions. PISA-E achieves the highest macro-average task performance among the evaluated ensemble approaches, exceeding the strongest competitors by 2.09 and 2.87 percentage points, respectively, when prioritizing response quality. At selected lower-cost operating points, PISA-E also improves macro-average task performance while reducing average inference cost by approximately 36% under ID and 25% under OOD conditions. Component analyses and ablations support the contributions of capability-aware response resolution, prompt-dependent ranking, and adaptive ensemble cardinality. Together, these results demonstrate that a shared capability-estimation framework supports effective single-model routing and selective ensembling, with cost–quality preferences adjustable at inference time without retraining.
For Web-based services such as search, recommendation, and intelligent agents, this flexibility offers a way to adapt inference expenditure to heterogeneous user requests and changing quality–cost preferences. Appendix E Greedy Selection vs. Exhaustive Surrogate Optimization Figure 4. Cost–performance trade-offs of greedy candidate selection and exhaustive surrogate optimization on Track-2 and RouterBench, with k max = 7 k_{\max}=7 . We evaluate the greedy candidate-selection procedure by comparing it with exhaustive optimization of the ensemble-success surrogate. Both approaches use the same capability estimates, Poisson-binomial surrogate, and self-weighted BERTScore resolver, with k max = 7 k_{\max}=7 . Exhaustive optimization evaluates all feasible model subsets, requiring the assessment of ∑ k = 1 k max ( M k ) \sum_{k=1}^{k_{\max}}\binom{M}{k} candidates for a pool of M M models. In contrast, greedy selection considers only ⌈ k max / 2 ⌉ \lceil k_{\max}/2\rceil odd-cardinality prefixes of the single-model utility ranking, substantially reducing the search space. Figure 4 compares the cost–performance curves on Track-2 and RouterBench. Greedy selection achieves trade-offs comparable to exhaustive optimization, with closely aligned curves on Track-2 and favorable performance across several intermediate-cost regions on RouterBench. These results indicate that greedy ranking prefixes provide a computationally efficient alternative while maintaining competitive cost–performance trade-offs in the evaluated settings.
