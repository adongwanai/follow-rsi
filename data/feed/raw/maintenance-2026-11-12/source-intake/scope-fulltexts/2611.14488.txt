Temporal Shortcuts in Memory-Augmented VLA Policies Chubin Zhang    Bo An † Nanyang Technological University chubinzhang23@gmail.com https://github.com/bennidict23/memory-clock Abstract Memory-augmented vision-language-action (VLA) policies are meant to remember what happened.
Many of them, however, fill memory by time: they sample frames uniformly across the episode and stamp each frame with its time step.
Because time passes even when nothing happens, such a memory may not tell waiting from working.
We show that it does not.
On the counting tasks of RoboMME, we make the robot briefly pause or fidget its wrist.
Such a null event changes nothing the task depends on, yet a released memory VLA counts the interval as work done and stops early.
We call this failure phantom progress .
It cuts success from 47 to 16 of 50 on one task and from 44 to 10 on the other.
When the same frames are withheld from memory, success returns, so the cause lies in memory rather than in the interruption itself.
We trace the failure to a temporal shortcut : because demonstrations proceed at a steady pace, elapsed time predicts progress.
Indeed, rescaling the time stamps alone makes the policy stop early when the clock runs fast and repeat when it runs slow.
Surprisingly, removing the time encoding does not cure the failure, because the frames of a null event still occupy slots of the time-sampled memory.
Across RoboMME’s released designs, vulnerability follows how memory is indexed.
Of the fixes the diagnosis suggests, training with injected pauses does not remove the failure and impairs counting, whereas indexing memory by events recovers half or more of the loss without retraining.

A robot asked to pick up a cube three times and then press a button sees much the same scene before its second pick as before its third, so it has to remember how many picks it has made.
Memory-augmented vision-language-action (VLA) policies address this by conditioning each action on a memory of the episode so far [ 41 , 44 , 48 , 11 ] .
A simple and widely used way to fill such a memory is by time: the policy samples past frames uniformly over the episode and stamps each frame with its time step.
This design covers the whole episode at a fixed cost, and on RoboMME [ 11 ] , a benchmark built to test memory, the strongest released policy uses exactly this design. However, time passes even when nothing happens.
Real robots often wait for a slow planner, for an operator who has paused them, or for a safety monitor to clear.
They also hesitate, or reach for an object and come back empty-handed.
We call such an interval a null event .
It takes time, and the robot may even move, yet nothing the task depends on changes.
A memory that records what happened should therefore pass over a null event as if it had not occurred.
A memory filled by time cannot do so, because the interval receives its share of sampled frames and time stamps like any other stretch of the episode.
This raises a simple question: Can a memory filled by time tell waiting from working? We find that it cannot ( Fig. 1 ).
On the two counting tasks of RoboMME, we make the released policy pause twice for 5 seconds, or roll its wrist back and forth for as long.
Success falls from 47 to 16 of 50 on PickXtimes and from 44 to 10 on SwingXtimes.
Almost every failure is an early stop, in which the policy presses the button before it has made the required number of repetitions.
The robot thus perceives progress that never happened.
We call this failure phantom progress .
One might suspect that the interruption itself disturbs the robot, for instance by leaving its arm in an unusual pose.
We therefore repeat each event with its frames withheld from memory.
The robot pauses or fidgets exactly as before, but its memory never sees the interval, and success returns to within three episodes of the baseline.
The failure therefore lies in what the policy remembers. Why would a policy mistake waiting for working?
We trace the failure to a temporal shortcut .
Demonstrations proceed at a steady pace, so elapsed time alone predicts how many repetitions are done 89% and 80% of the time on the two tasks, and over 92% on regular demonstrations from two real teleoperation datasets.
Since demonstrations contain almost no idle stretches, imitation learning never penalises a policy for reading progress from time.
If the policy indeed reads progress from time, rescaling the time stamps alone should shift its failures in a predictable direction.
It does: with no null event and the frames unchanged, a faster clock makes the policy stop early, and a slower one makes it repeat ( Sec. 5.2 ).
The obvious remedy is then to remove the time encoding.
Surprisingly, a policy trained from scratch without it still fails under pauses (21 and 14 of 50), because the frames of a null event still occupy slots of a memory sampled by time ( Sec. 5.3 ). The shortcut thus lies in how memory is indexed, and the time stamps are only one of its carriers. Consistently, among RoboMME’s released designs, every memory indexed by time collapses under null events, a memory that selects tokens by pixel change is hurt less by pauses than by fidgets, and memories indexed by sub-goals withstand both null events on PickXtimes ( Sec. 5.4 ). The diagnosis also suggests where to fix the failure.
Training with injected pauses changes the data but keeps the index, so at best it can teach the policy to discount the null events that we know how to synthesise.
It does not even achieve this: pause augmentation does not remove the failure, and it impairs counting when no null event occurs.
Changing the index should work instead.
Indexing memory by events collapses every stretch in which the world returns to a state it was already in.
Applied at test time without retraining, it recovers half or more of the lost success under pauses, fidgets and air-grasps alike ( Sec. 6 ). We summarize our contributions as follows: • Phantom progress. Brief null events make memory VLAs that fill memory by time stop early, and the damage passes through memory alone. Comparing visible with hidden null events, together with rescaling the time stamps, gives a protocol for testing whether any memory policy tracks events or elapsed time ( Sec. 4 ). • A shortcut in the index. We trace the failure to a temporal shortcut that lives in how memory is indexed, so that removing the time stamps does not remove it, and we show that vulnerability follows the index across RoboMME’s released designs ( Sec. 5 ). • Fixes suggested by the diagnosis. Pause augmentation does not remove the failure, whereas indexing memory by events recovers much of the loss without retraining ( Sec. 6 ). 2 Related Work Memory in robot policies. Most VLA policies act on the current observation or a short window [ 3 , 36 , 58 , 9 ] .
A growing line adds memory: history-aware transformers [ 18 , 23 , 51 , 22 ] , perceptual memory banks [ 41 , 13 ] , keyframe and event selection [ 44 , 30 , 57 , 53 ] , recurrent and test-time-training memories [ 6 , 46 , 27 ] , and mixed video–language memory [ 48 ] .
RoboMME [ 11 ] standardises their evaluation and releases the MME-VLA family we study, and MIKASA-Robo [ 8 ] and MemoryBench [ 13 ] are related suites.
Such benchmarks ask which memory scores highest.
A smaller set of works asks what memory actually does.
History can be encoded yet barely used [ 24 ] , and long or uncurated histories invite spurious correlations [ 30 , 52 , 47 ] .
These works treat the problem as a shift in the distribution of histories and remedy it by choosing which frames to keep.
We instead identify one specific correlate, elapsed time, show by intervention that the policy reads it, and find that selecting memory content by appearance does not remove it.
Closest to us, AGM [ 16 ] shows that memories logging attempted actions as progress turn a failed grasp into a persistent error, and RoboMME-Interference [ 39 ] shows that unrelated sessions crowd frame-sampled memories.
Concurrent event-driven keyframe memories [ 57 , 53 ] report that selecting frames by events beats uniform sampling.
Our analysis explains why such memories should resist null events, and our event index ( Sec. 6 ) is a minimal intervention that tests this account rather than a new architecture. Shortcuts, time, and position. Shortcut learning [ 17 ] is well documented in imitation learning, as causal confusion [ 12 ] and copycat behaviour of history-conditioned policies [ 50 , 10 ] .
Elapsed time is a special case: it is legitimately part of the state under time limits [ 34 ] , many controllers are driven by a phase variable [ 20 , 35 ] , and progress estimators are tempted by temporal position, which is why GVL shuffles frames [ 29 ] .
In sequence models, positional encodings shape counting and length generalisation [ 37 , 1 , 21 , 40 ] , and video moment-retrieval models can answer from the temporal position of a moment rather than the video content [ 33 , 55 ] .
We show that in memory VLAs the time position becomes a substitute for content, and that removing the explicit encoding does not remove it. Events and redundancy in video memory. Video models compress long inputs by merging redundant frames or tokens [ 5 , 43 , 19 ] , and event segmentation [ 56 , 42 , 15 ] groups frames into meaningful units.
Such methods judge redundancy by appearance.
A null event need not look redundant: a fidgeting wrist changes every frame.
Our event-indexed memory instead uses whether the world returns to a previous state, in the spirit of loop closure in SLAM [ 7 ] . Robustness of robot policies. Robustness benchmarks perturb what the robot sees or is told: objects, textures, lighting, camera, language, and initial state [ 38 , 14 , 59 , 49 ] .
Timing has been studied as inference latency [ 4 , 26 ] .
To our knowledge, no prior work injects test-time null events into the rollouts of memory-augmented policies. 3 Setup Tasks and policies. RoboMME [ 11 ] provides memory-dependent manipulation tasks in simulation.
We focus on its two counting tasks, where memory is needed to decide when the task is done: in PickXtimes the robot picks and places a cube K ∈ { 1 , … , 5 } K\in\{1,\dots,5\} times and then presses a button; in SwingXtimes it carries a cube back and forth between two targets K K times.
We study the released MME-VLA policies [ 11 ] , built on π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} [ 36 ] .
Their memory, FrameSamp, keeps 32 frames sampled uniformly in time from the whole episode and stamps each with a sinusoidal encoding of its absolute time step.
The three variants read this memory through cross-attention in the action expert (“Modul”), through a separate memory expert (“Expert”), or in context (“Context”).
TokenDrop instead keeps tokens selected by pixel change, with the same time encoding.
For comparison across memory designs ( Sec. 5.4 ) we also evaluate the released MemER-style keyframe memory [ 44 ] , a text sub-goal memory, a recurrent test-time-training memory, and π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} without memory.
Unless stated otherwise, results use FrameSamp-Modul, the strongest released policy. Models trained from scratch. To test whether the failure needs the time encoding, we train two models that differ only in it.
Both start from the π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} base checkpoint and follow the released recipe on the RoboMME training data for 30k steps, but Clock keeps the time encoding while NoClock removes it.
Without null events both come close to the released model ( Appendix F ). Null events. At action-chunk boundaries after policy steps 48 and 160 we inject one of three events that leave unchanged what the task depends on ( Fig. 2 a):

Figure 2 applies the three null events of Sec. 3 to the released policy, and every one of them is damaging.
Two 5-second pauses reduce success from 47 to 16 of 50 on PickXtimes and from 44 to 10 on SwingXtimes.
Wrist fidgets, in which the robot keeps moving, do as much damage, and air-grasps that return the arm to where it started still halve success on SwingXtimes.
Nor do the events need to be long, since pauses of 1.5 seconds already cause most of the loss ( Appendix B ). How the policy fails is as telling as how often it fails.
A disturbance that merely confused the policy would cause failures of many kinds, such as dropped cubes or extra repetitions.
Under pauses and fidgets, however, nearly all failures are early stops, in which the policy presses the button before the K K -th repetition ( Appendix L ).
The policy thus overestimates how far it has come. Table 1 shows where this matters.
With K = 1 K{=}1 , the first natural moment to press, right after the first repetition, is already the correct one, and most of these episodes survive.
With K ≥ 2 K{\geq}2 , in contrast, not a single episode succeeds under pauses or fidgets, while hiding the pauses restores nearly all of them. Such early stopping could stem from the interruption itself or from what the policy remembers of it.
The hidden condition separates the two, because it keeps the physical event exactly as it was and changes only what enters memory.
The robot pauses, fidgets or grasps at air as before, but the frames of the event are withheld and the time stamps do not advance over it.
In every cell, success then returns to the level without any event ( Fig. 2 b). What breaks the policy is its memory of an interval in which nothing happened. Beyond counting. Null events also hurt some tasks that open with a demonstration video, through a different consequence of the same time-filled memory.
On RouteStick, for instance, two 15-second pauses reduce success from 33 to 7, against 36 when hidden.
The video shares the 32 time-sampled slots with execution, so the null frames push it out of memory.
Indeed, giving the video only the slots it keeps under the pause reproduces the damage without any pause, and protecting its slots during the pause restores success ( Appendix C ).
Whether it adds progress that never happened or pushes out a cue the task needs, the null event acts through the room it takes in memory. 5 Why: A Memory Filled by Time Counts Null Events Figure 3: In demonstrations, elapsed time alone predicts progress. Accuracy of predicting the number of completed repetitions from the time step alone (5-fold cross-validation over demonstrations), against always predicting the most frequent count (baseline).
Three-cycle comparison: demonstrations with three repetitions, in simulation and in two real teleoperation datasets [ 31 , 2 ] .
Broader data: all stanford_hydra tasks, the annotated primitives of FMB [ 28 ] , and the two-object tasks of LIBERO-10 [ 25 ] ( Appendix D ). Section 4 showed that, after an interval in which nothing happened, the policy overestimates how far it has come.
We argue that it judges progress from what time puts into its memory rather than from what happened.
A null interval enters a time-filled memory through two routes.
First, the time stamps advance over it and thereby record how long it lasted.
Second, its frames occupy slots of the memory, which registers that something happened.
Below we show why a policy learns to read time ( Sec. 5.1 ), that it follows the stamps ( Sec. 5.2 ), that the slots alone suffice once the stamps are gone ( Sec. 5.3 ), and that vulnerability tracks the index across designs ( Sec. 5.4 ). 5.1 In demonstrations, time predicts progress Demonstrations proceed at a steady pace, so the time elapsed in an episode reveals how far it has progressed.
To measure this, we predict at every step of a demonstration how many repetitions have been completed, from elapsed time alone, using the most frequent count at that time in held-out demonstrations ( Appendix D ).
This predictor is right 89% of the time on PickXtimes and 80% on SwingXtimes, roughly twice as often as always guessing the most common count.
Nor is this an artefact of scripted simulation data ( Fig. 3 ).
On demonstrations with three repetitions, the predictor is as accurate on two real teleoperation datasets from Open X-Embodiment [ 32 ] as on PickXtimes.
On broader real data, where the pace is less regular, it is less accurate but still far above the baseline.
Moreover, demonstrations contain almost no idle stretches, so nothing in training ever penalises a policy that reads progress from time.
For imitation learning, which rewards any feature that predicts the next action, elapsed time is therefore a cheap and reliable cue. 5.2 Rescaling time shifts the failure Figure 4: Rescaling the time stamps shifts the failure. No null event is injected and the frames are unchanged. Only the time stamps of the memory frames are multiplied by s s .
Released FrameSamp-Modul on PickXtimes, 50 paired episodes.
A slower clock ( s = 0.5 s{=}0.5 ) causes extra repetitions, a faster one ( s = 1.5 s{=}1.5 ) early presses. Hatched: failures whose type was not logged. If the policy reads progress from time, then altering the time stamps alone, without any null event, should shift its estimate of progress in a predictable direction.
We therefore multiply the time stamps of all memory frames by a factor s s and leave the frames themselves untouched ( Fig. 4 ).
When time appears to run faster ( s = 1.5 s{=}1.5 ), success falls from 47 to 18, and every failure is an early press.
When time appears to run slower ( s = 0.5 s{=}0.5 ), the policy makes extra repetitions instead.
The direction of the failure thus follows the direction of the clock.
The flip is weaker on SwingXtimes, but it recurs in FrameSamp-Expert, which reads the same memory through a different pathway, and it persists when the stamps keep the in-distribution layout of a training episode ( Appendix E ). A null event thus acts like a local acceleration of this clock, since time advances while the content stands still.
If so, the policy should respond to how long a null event lasts.
A simpler reading would have it count each null interval as one extra repetition, whatever its length.
Long pauses separate the two readings ( Fig. 5 ).
With two 15-second pauses, the clock passes its usual press time after one more repetition, so a policy that reads it should press after at most one repetition, whatever K K .
A policy that counts the pauses instead should press after K − 1 K{-}1 .
On Clock , 34 of 36 presses come after at most one repetition, and on held-out episodes, with this criterion fixed in advance, all 29 do. The stamps therefore carry how long a null event lasts, and the policy reads it. Yet the stamps are not the only route.
Withholding the pause frames while advancing the stamps over them reproduces the full damage on PickXtimes, but storing the frames while freezing the stamps also hurts ( Sec. E.1 ).
The frames of a null event must thus carry part of the damage on their own. 5.3 Removing the clock does not remove the failure Figure 5: The policy reads how long a pause lasts. (c) Without any pause, blocks of null slots alone degrade NoClock ( Sec. F.6 ). The dashed line marks the share two 5-second pauses take. If the stamps were the whole story, removing them would remove the failure.
To test this, we trained NoClock from scratch without any time encoding ( Fig. 6 a; Appendix F ).
As intended, it ignores rescaled stamps, and it counts as well as Clock .
Yet pauses still cut its success to 21 and 14 of 50, and hiding them restores it, so a policy that never sees a time stamp still counts a pause as progress. The reason is the second route ( Fig. 6 b).
Without stamps, a memory sampled uniformly in time still fills a share of its 32 slots with frames of an interval in which nothing happened.
Two controls show that the harm comes from these uneventful slots themselves, rather than from what the null frames depict or from the real frames they displace.
Replacing every null frame with the last frame before the pause leaves the damage unchanged, while filling the same slots with copies of real frames, without any pause, leaves success intact.
Conversely, inserting blocks of identical frames without any pause degrades NoClock more and more as the blocks grow ( Fig. 6 c). If the damage comes from indexing memory by time, it should depend on how a memory is indexed rather than on whether a policy has memory at all. Table 2 tests this by applying the same null events to every released RoboMME memory policy, without retraining ( Appendix G ).
The three FrameSamp variants, whose slots are sampled and stamped by time, all collapse.
TokenDrop selects tokens by pixel change, so still frames rarely enter its memory, and pauses cost it much less.
A fidget, however, changes the pixels without changing the world, so it passes this filter and costs TokenDrop far more than a pause.
Memories indexed by sub-goals, a MemER-style keyframe memory [ 44 ] and a text memory, record progress only when a sub-goal is reached, and neither null event affects them.
Each design thus fails where its index lets a null event in ( Fig. 7 ).
Sub-goal memories pay for this elsewhere, as they count less well without null events, and the keyframe memory is misled by air-grasps ( Appendix G ). Two independently developed policies on other backbones fit this picture less neatly.
Both add to memory at every step and both read fidgets as progress, mostly through early presses.
The same pauses, however, barely affect SimpleMemVLA [ 54 ] on PickXtimes, whereas they collapse LTM- π \pi [ 45 ] ( Secs. G.1 and G.2 ). If the index decides which null events reach memory, changing the index rather than retraining the policy should keep them out, which Sec. 6 tests. 6 What the Diagnosis Predicts for Fixes Figure 7: What a null event leaves in memory depends on what the memory counts. A memory indexed by time gives a null event slots in proportion to its duration, whether the robot is still or moving.
Selecting by pixel change keeps little of a pause but retains a fidget, which changes the pixels without changing the world.
The event index of Sec. 6 advances only when the world changes and reduces either to a single retained frame. The diagnosis points to two places where the failure could be fixed, and it predicts different outcomes for each.
The first is the data: training with injected null events should break the link between time and progress, but only for null events that can be faithfully synthesised.
A held frame is a valid pause, whereas a moving null event cannot be made by replaying recorded frames of real-robot data, because the replay would move the objects as well and so show a real repetition.
The second is the index: if memory advanced with events instead of time, null events would leave no trace, whether or not the robot moves. Changing the data. We retrained Clock with pauses injected into its demonstrations and compared it with a retrain without them on the same machine ( Fig. 8 a).
The prediction failed: on PickXtimes, pauses no longer cost the augmented policy anything, but only because its success without any null event has already fallen, and on SwingXtimes it does no better under pauses than the retrain without augmentation.
The augmented policy still solves single repetitions, yet it loses most episodes that require counting further.
We did not predict this cost, and a post hoc reading is that its counting leaned on time and lost that support, as a faster clock no longer hurts it, although crowded memory or slower learning may contribute.

Successes of 50 paired episodes, test split; full numbers, a weaker augmentation and a held-out replication in Appendix I . Changing the index. We implement the event index at test time, on the same Clock checkpoint and without retraining.
A stretch of the episode counts as a null event if the world returns to a state it was already in.
Concretely, the robot’s configuration comes back to that of a kept frame, the scene outside the robot is unchanged, and no contact was made or released in between.
Each such stretch is collapsed to a single frame, as is any prolonged stillness, and the time positions are recomputed over the kept frames, so that memory advances only with events.
The detector reads the robot’s silhouette, the gripper width and the external force on the arm from the simulator, with tolerances fixed across all 16 RoboMME tasks ( Appendix I ).
Since its criterion mirrors how our null events are built, the experiment tests whether taking null frames out of the index suffices, rather than offering a deployable detector. Taking null frames out of the index does suffice ( Fig. 8 b).
Event indexing at least doubles success under all three null events, including the two in which the robot moves, and under air-grasps it lifts SwingXtimes from 13 to 43 of 50.
The gain replicates on held-out episodes and on the released policy ( Appendix I ).
Under pauses on SwingXtimes it still falls short of the level without events, so part of the damage survives the index. Where event indexing can fail. The criterion that makes the index work also sets its blind spot.
On a counting task a true repetition also returns the world to a previous state, since the robot releases the cube and comes back to it.
The index can therefore mistake a real repetition for nothing.
On PickXtimes without null events it costs some success, little on Clock but significantly on the released policy, whereas on the other 15 RoboMME tasks it costs nothing overall ( Sec. I.6 ).
Two kinds of null event escape the criterion in part, namely a grasp that ends elsewhere, which never returns to a previous state, and brief, randomly timed micro-stops, which are neither still nor closed loops.
Of the damage done by micro-stops, the index recovers little ( Appendix I ).
Finally, where time itself is the task, as in TimedTransfer, collapsing a pause is wrong by construction.
An event index is thus the right default for memories that must track what happened, while tasks that depend on duration still need a clock. 7 Discussion and Limitations Benchmarks may measure clock reading. Counting tasks are meant to test memory of events, but when demonstrations proceed at a steady pace, a policy can pass them by reading the clock.
The shortcut is unlikely to be specific to RoboMME.
In an exploratory study on MIKASA-Robo [ 8 ] , a policy appears to infer how many times a light blinked from how long the cue lasted ( Appendix J ).
Null events, clock rescaling and fixed-duration cues are cheap probes that any memory benchmark can add to tell event memory from time keeping. What should a memory index? Our results suggest a principle for memory design.
Memory should advance when the world changes, because that is what progress on a task consists of.
Elapsed time is a convenient proxy, since demonstrations proceed at a steady pace, yet it also advances when nothing happens.
Pixel change filters out stillness, yet a robot can move without changing anything.
Changes in the world itself, such as an object moved or a contact made, track progress and are exactly what null events lack.
Time keeps a role where duration is part of the task, as in TimedTransfer, but there it should enter as an input to the policy, while what memory keeps should still follow events.
Detecting such changes from real sensors is harder than in simulation, and we see it as the main open problem for memories that track what happened. Limitations. Our causal analysis covers one family of memory policies (MME-VLA) and models retrained with its architecture, and two independent policies are probed without dissection.
All experiments are in simulation and every null event is scripted, so we do not measure how often unannounced null events occur in deployment, although the correlation behind the shortcut is as strong in regular real teleoperation demonstrations.
The event detector, too, uses simulator signals.
A second seed of Clock and NoClock met every rule fixed for it ( Appendix A ), but the other models, including each augmentation dose, have one seed.
Since training reruns of Clock differ by up to 12 episodes on PickXtimes without events ( Appendix A ), we compare separately trained models only where the gap is larger.
Finally, without time stamps it remains open which feature of the occupied slots the policy reads. Conclusion. Memory VLAs whose memory is filled by time record that time passed as well as what happened, and they read the former as progress.
Null events expose this shortcut, rescaling the stamps alone reproduces it, and removing the clock does not cure it, because null frames still occupy memory.
Indexing memory by events recovers much of the loss, within limits set by its criterion.
A memory meant to track what happened should therefore advance with events rather than with the clock. During a pause the robot holds its last joint target and the policy is not queried; during a fidget the wrist rolls ± 0.3 \pm 0.3 rad around the held target; an air-grasp is executed by the motion planner above a resting object and returns to the pre-event pose.
After each event the evaluator checks and logs that no object moved by more than 1 cm and no button was pressed.
On tasks whose scene draws fading highlights (gripper trail, button glow), the highlight clock is frozen during the pause so that the images after the pause match those before it; on RouteStick, the evaluator’s gripper trace is not extended during the pause.
In the hidden condition, the event is executed identically but its frames are not written to memory and the time stamps do not advance over it. Where a fidget nudges a held cube, the damage is the same as where it moves nothing; the one event that interferes with the task, the air-grasp on PickXtimes, is excluded from the main text. Dose. The damage sets in between 0.5 and 1.5 s per pause and saturates by 5 s: two 15-second pauses do no more harm than two 5-second ones, and hiding either restores success.

With the pause at step 48, K = 1 K=1 episodes are untouched (16 of 16 on PickXtimes), whereas only 2 of 34 episodes with K ≥ 2 K\geq 2 succeed.
With the pause at step 300, PickXtimes episodes with K ≤ 2 K\leq 2 , which are nearly finished by then, are untouched (25 of 26), whereas those with K ≥ 3 K\geq 3 fail (1 of 24).
This rules out an immediate reflex to stop after any pause and matches a stop decision taken when the remembered elapsed time reaches its usual value. Natural stops. Many short stops at random times, as a safety layer or a slow planner might produce, are nearly as harmful as two long pauses, and again only through memory: hidden, they leave success at the no-event level.
They are also a boundary of the event index ( Appendix I ). The PickXtimes air-grasp. Appendix D Time predicts progress in demonstrations In scripted simulation and in real teleoperation alike, the time step alone predicts how many repetitions a demonstration has completed.
This section defines the predictor, lists the data and bounds what the evidence shows. Predictor. At each step t t from the first repetition through the stop decision, the label is the number of repetitions completed by t t .
The predictor sees only t t and outputs the most frequent training-fold label in the same 10-step bin (the overall most frequent label for unseen bins); neither K K nor the task is given.
Accuracy uses 5-fold cross-validation over demonstrations; the baseline predicts the most frequent label. On RoboMME, repetition boundaries come from the recorded sub-goal text; on real data, a gripper cycle (flips under 0.25 s removed; a closure of at least 0.5 s followed by a release; the window ends at the last release). On RoboMME the two definitions agree (gripper-cycle count equals K K in 100 of 100 PickXtimes demonstrations; accuracy 0.894 against 0.890). Successes of 50 paired episodes. I.1 Definition The detector processes the policy’s execution frames online and maintains a kept sequence; only kept frames enter memory, and their time positions are their indices in the kept sequence, so the memory clock advances only with kept frames.
A frame is a barrier if the scene outside the robot changed with respect to the last kept frame, or if a grip or load switch ( Sec. I.2 ) occurred; a barrier is always kept and no stretch is collapsed across it.
Between barriers, two rules apply: • Loop closure. If the robot configuration returns to within tolerance of a kept frame p p after having left it by more than the exit distance, and the scene outside the robot equals that at p p , the kept frames after p p are collapsed to one frame (the kept frame farthest from p p ) and one clock tick. • Stillness. If the configuration stays within the stillness tolerance of the frame at which a stop began and the scene is unchanged, the first 5 frames of the stop are kept (grace) and later ones are dropped. I.2 Signals and how they are obtained The decision uses four signals, none of which is the simulator’s object state. • Robot joint configuration (7 arm joints, 2 finger joints): proprioception, available on any arm. • Robot silhouette in the front camera, used only to ignore the robot’s own pixels when testing whether the scene changed. In all experiments it is the simulator’s segmentation of the robot links. On a robot it can be rendered from the joint readings and the camera calibration; we did not implement this rendering. • Gripper width. The gripper is holding while the width has settled strictly between closed-on-nothing and open; a switch of this state (a grasp or a release) is an event. An air-grasp closes to empty and never holds. • Net external force on the arm : in all experiments the sum of the simulator’s contact impulses between the robot and anything else , with the robot links merged so that grip forces cancel. This is the quantity a wrist force/torque sensor measures; we did not model sensor noise. A switch between loaded and unloaded (lift-off, set-down, press, push) is an event. The simulator-contact variant replaces the last two signals by the simulator’s list of contact pairs. Both variants use the simulator segmentation and noise-free renders; the 5-pixel scene tolerance would have to be revisited for a real camera. False collapse: the episode also fails with the event index and no null event (PickXtimes pause: 6 of 12 lost episodes are among the 8 episodes lost on clean episodes with the event index, chance expectation 2.2, hypergeometric p = .003 p{=}.003 ), or the policy repeats a step.
One-way boundary: the null excursion never returns, so it cannot be collapsed.
Missed null frames: returns that stop 1.3–3 units short (all-or-nothing closure), transition frames at the start of each event, and the last incomplete cycle of a fidget. Randomised micro-stops. Brief stops at random action-chunk boundaries (probability 0.3 per boundary, 5–40 steps each, until a 300-step budget; on average 7.2 stops and ≈ \approx 157 steps per episode; same schedule in every condition of an episode) harm through memory nearly as much as long pauses: hiding their frames restores success ( Tab. 38 ). The event index recovers little and not significantly.
Each stop begins with a deceleration and settling phase that is neither still nor a closed loop, and the event index keeps the first 5 frames of every stop by design (the grace); summed over many short stops, it keeps 35–50% of the stop frames (66–71 per episode) and g0 36–39% (55–60), whereas the hidden condition keeps none. This form of null event, the one a deployed robot is most likely to produce, is not solved here. Summary of the boundaries. The event index removes the damage of null events that return the world to a previous state; it partly fails on null events that do not return (one-way air-grasp, randomised micro-stops); it can fail where a real repetition also returns the world to a previous state (PickXtimes, and in demonstrations VideoRepick); and it is wrong by construction where elapsed time is the task (TimedTransfer, Appendix J ).
These limits follow from a state-return criterion, though we observed them before explaining them, and the miss on micro-stops owes as much to the detector’s mechanics. Appendix J An exploratory study on MIKASA-Robo Summary. On MIKASA-Robo [ 8 ] we trained the same architecture (64 memory slots) from the π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} base checkpoint on BlinkCount , in which a light blinks K K times and the robot must then press a button K K times and submit.
The policy solves only 24 of 50 episodes, below the competence level we set in advance, so this evidence is exploratory.
In the demonstrations the cue lasts longer the more blinks it contains, with non-overlapping ranges, so the time at which the cue ends identifies K K .
This is consistent with reading the cue’s duration: with the cue fixed to 50 steps for every K K , success falls from 24 to 6, and almost all remaining successes are for the one count whose usual cue lasts about that long ( K = 5 K{=}5 : 5 of 9; other K K : 1 of 41); seeing the light does not help (9 with the light hidden as well).
Faster time stamps make it over-press (38 of 42 failures), and a visible pause after the cue reduces success to 8, against 23 when hidden, here through the stored frames rather than the stamps. Model and data. We trained FrameSamp-Modul (clock on, 64 memory slots) from the π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} base checkpoint with the recipe of Clock (30k steps, batch 64) on 1250 MIKASA-Robo demonstrations (about 183k frames) of BlinkCount (Medium and Hard) and TimedTransfer (Easy, Medium and Hard), and evaluate BlinkCount-Medium ( K ≤ 5 K\leq 5 ) and TimedTransfer-Medium.
