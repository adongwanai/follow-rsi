Right : TOME-Math comprises 380,292 QAs from 2,118 textbooks spanning 29 subjects. 1 Introduction Mathematical reasoning in large language models has advanced rapidly, driven in part by improvements in training data and in part by advances in reasoning-time computation [ 28 , 38 , 21 , 13 , 37 , 4 , 27 , 42 , 10 , 29 , 15 , 1 , 2 ] . On the data side, recent efforts have expanded mathematical supervision to millions of QA pairs ( i.e. , Question-Answer pairs) and in doing so have revealed that properties such as question diversity, solution format, and teacher-model quality significantly shape downstream performance [ 39 , 40 , 31 , 18 ] . These findings suggest a broader principle: The value of mathematical supervision is not determined by the number of training examples alone, but also by how those examples are constructed and organized . Despite this, the dominant paradigm for mathematical supervision remains the isolated problem–solution pair. Whether the original knowledge comes from competition archives, grade-school word problems, web exercises, or synthetic augmentation, the resulting training data typically takes the uniform shape of problem → solution \text{problem}\to\text{solution} [ 7 , 14 , 39 ] . This representation is convenient to scale, but it does not explicitly leverage the organizational structure that many source materials already possess. Mathematical textbooks are a notable case in point.
A textbook is not merely a collection of solved problems, but a carefully structured body of knowledge. Definitions introduce core concepts, lemmas and theorems develop them, proofs establish their validity, and exercises reinforce understanding. These elements are organized within a chapter hierarchy that reflects prerequisite relations, conceptual dependencies, and pedagogical progression.
Prior work has explored textbooks as a source of mathematical pretraining data, problem–solution pairs, and proof supervision [ 33 , 30 , 35 , 3 , 16 , 19 , 41 ] . However, textbooks are often treated primarily as sources of mathematical content, leaving their native structure underexplored. In particular, it remains unclear how to scale natural-language proof supervision while preserving chapter order, proposition dependencies, source locations, and exercises in a unified framework. To address this gap, we introduce TOME-Math ( T extbook- O riented M athematical E vidence), a large-scale proof-supervision resource constructed from 2,118 mathematics textbooks. Central to TOME-Math is the notion of structured mathematical supervision, which preserves structure at three levels. First, hierarchical structure retains the native organization of textbooks, including chapters, propositions, and their ordering. Second, dependency structure connects theorem-level proof questions with the intermediate proof obligations derived from gaps in their proofs. Third, curriculum structure organizes supervision according to textbook order and proof granularity, enabling controlled training over related examples rather than independent QA pairs. Following these principles, TOME-Math contains 380,292 proof-oriented QA pairs spanning 29 mathematical subjects. Figure 1 provides an overview of the construction and organization of the resulting corpus. We further validate the effectiveness of TOME-Math through supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on a 30B base model [ 37 ] . Its retained structure enables controlled studies of how the organization of supervision affects mathematical reasoning. Rather than treating scale as the only variable, TOME-Math allows us to examine the roles of retrieval structure, curriculum order, and source coverage under controlled settings. Empirically, TOME-Math SFT improves the six-benchmark average from 29.17% to 45.83%, while further experiments show consistent benefits from structure-guided retrieval, textbook-aligned curricula, and broader source coverage. Building on these directions, TOME-Math combines coverage of 2,118 textbooks with self-contained local proof obligations, chapter order, and proposition references, supporting controlled studies of source coverage, proof-target format, and training order. 3 TOME-Math Figure 2: Overview of TOME-Math construction, from structured textbook parsing and chapter recovery to Proof QA extraction, Gap QA generation, and quality-controlled corpus assembly (Section 3.1 ). 3.1 Data Construction Pipeline overview. We construct TOME-Math through a structure-preserving pipeline that converts raw textbook PDFs into two complementary forms of proof supervision: Proof QA derived from explicit proposition–proof units and Gap QA derived from missing proof obligations in Proof QAs. The source collection is selected against university course syllabi and reviewed by domain experts, yielding 2,118 input PDFs after canonicalization and deduplication (as shown in Section 3.2 ). The pipeline generated the full TOME-Math dataset of 380,292 QA pairs, comprising 174,431 Gap QA pairs and 205,861 Proof QA pairs. Document parsing and structured chapter extraction. DeepSeek-OCR [ 34 ] converts each PDF into multimodal Markdown together with page images. A page-wise gpt-oss-120b [ 23 ] pass separates the table of contents and main body from covers, prefaces, and other front matter, then reconstructs a hierarchical table of contents. Conditioned on this hierarchy, a state machine reads the body in order, judges whether each page belongs to the current chapter, the next chapter, or contains an internal boundary, and retries ambiguous assignments before updating the chapter state. This produces an ordered set of chapter-level documents while preserving the textbook hierarchy. A second page-wise pass separates expository core concepts from textbook exercises within each chapter. The exercises are retained as an independent reservoir, but are excluded from the Proof QA generation path. Proof QA Extraction and Gap QA construction. For each core concept chapter, we use gemini-3.1-flash-lite-preview [ 12 ] to extract theorem–proof, lemma–proof, and definition–proof units and retrieve the earlier propositions needed to interpret each proof. These source-grounded units form the Proof QA stream. We then use gemini-3-flash-preview with thinking enabled [ 11 ] to inspect each proof for gaps ( i.e. , implicit or missing reasoning steps). When a substantive gap is detected, gemini-3.1-flash-lite-preview [ 12 ] rewrites the missing obligation as a self-contained proposition and completes its proof, producing the Gap QA stream. This structured construction preserves explicit textbook proofs while adding targeted supervision for intermediate reasoning that the source leaves unstated. Validation and quality control. Quality control is applied at both the structural and semantic levels. The structural checks validate the table-of-contents hierarchy, ambiguous page boundaries, question continuity across pages, question–answer alignment, and standalone completeness. For proposition extraction, a source-conditioned gpt-oss-120b judge audits candidates along five axes: completeness, accuracy, L a T e X formatting, self-containment, and atomicity. Accuracy is treated as critical: invented proof steps, altered mathematical relations, or other content not supported by the source are failure cases rather than stylistic defects. Starting from the first parsed page, the structure model decides whether a page contains table-of-contents entries and whether the textbook body has begun. For contents pages it extracts pairs of the form {title, level}. Pages that are neither contents nor body are treated as cover or front matter and are discarded. Once the body is detected, a verification pass corrects invalid heading depths and removes entries that belong to prefaces or notation lists. If the model detects body text before extracting any valid contents entries, the whole module is retried, up to five attempts. M1 emits toc.json , main_text.md , and the merged images/ directory. M2: hierarchy-conditioned segmentation. The recovered contents are traversed as a tree. Internal entries direct the traversal to their children, while leaf entries trigger a search for the corresponding body span. For each page and the current/next chapter titles, the model returns one of three states: All-Current (the page belongs to the current chapter), All-Next (it belongs to the next chapter), or Boundary (the transition occurs within the page). Boundary pages are checked a second time; a failed check is fed back to the assignment step. A successful transition advances the state machine and writes the completed chapter to content/ . A.3 M3–M6: Exercise extraction and answer alignment M3: exercise separation. The structure model first tests whether the table of contents contains dedicated exercise chapters. When it does, those chapters are detached directly. When it does not, each content chapter is scanned for a trailing block of exercises, which is removed from the exposition. The same module constructs the multilevel exercise-title index and detects a separate answer chapter, if present. Its outputs are content_pure/ , exercise/ , exercise_index.jsonl , and optionally answer.md . M4: exercise serialization. The parser reads each exercise section page by page and emits one JSON record per problem. A record contains question , answer when an answer is printed immediately after the question, and number . The final problem fragment on a page is carried into the next page before serialization so that a page break does not split a question into two records. Problem numbers are preserved for the matching stage. M5: answer partitioning. The pipeline determines whether numbering is absolute —one number identifies a unique problem in the whole book—or relative , with numbers restarting across chapters. For absolute numbering, the answer chapter is serialized using the same page-wise procedure as M4. For relative numbering, the multilevel heading paths saved by M3 are used to partition the answer chapter before serialization. M6: matching and filtering. Absolute-number records are joined directly on problem number. Relative-number records are matched within their recovered chapter, using the section content when necessary. Books without printed answers follow a third, unmatched branch. All branches then pass through a completeness check that removes questions whose statements depend on an unprovided theorem or on the conclusion of another exercise. A.4 Four-stage proposition pipeline Each pure-content chapter is first split at Markdown headings while preserving its preamble, and the resulting sections are processed concurrently. The four semantic stages are as follows. 1. Proposition extraction. For every section, the model emits
structured proposition records with type , number , label , title , content_tex , and proof_tex fields. 2. Reference resolution. A book-level proposition library is
built from the extracted records. For each proposition with a proof, the
model identifies both explicit \ref citations and semantic
references to earlier results, storing the matches in resolved_references . 3. Proof-gap audit. Each proof is checked for unverified lemma
assumptions, omitted coordinate or operator transformations, and unsupported
jumps marked by phrases such as “obviously” or “clearly.” A candidate
gap stores gap_statement , missing_logic , sub_proposition , and a low/medium/high difficulty label. 4. Self-contained refinement. Each candidate sub-proposition is
rewritten as an independent mathematical statement by declaring symbols,
injecting required definitions, and preserving the source language. The chapter-level structured output contains the extracted propositions, resolved references, and candidate gaps. A separate Proof QA JSONL stream distinguishes original proposition records from candidate sub-propositions with an is_potential_gap flag. This schema describes the pipeline output; dataset-level acceptance, deduplication, and split policies are subsequent steps. Before grading, the verifier removes marked thinking segments and retains the final answer when explicit final-channel delimiters are present. It also truncates text at an end-of-message marker. Existing numbered step tags are sorted by their index; otherwise, the answer is split at blank lines. The resulting segments are wrapped in zero-based step tags. Thus, an untagged answer is graded at paragraph-level granularity rather than using an additional model to infer mathematical steps. The judge receives the problem, the reference solution, and the segmented model response. Rubric. The rubric prioritizes mathematical validity, problem-specific constraints, and sufficient justification. A reference solution is an anchor, not the only acceptable proof: alternative methods, reordered steps, and valid shortcuts are allowed. For numerical or algebraic questions, the final answer must be mathematically correct relative to the reference; a matching conclusion alone is insufficient if its derivation is invalid. Unsupported claims and unjustified leaps are treated conservatively. Invoking an external theorem is insufficient without the hypotheses and explanation needed to check its application. Proof-embedded instructions attempting to relax these standards are ignored. The requested output contains assessment , errors , and first_error_step XML fields; the last field is − 1 -1 if no error is found. Eight-review aggregation. The study in Section 5.1 uses the
300-problem TOME-LongForm evaluation set described in
Appendix D . It contains 50 textbook-native
problems from each of abstract algebra, differential geometry, discrete
mathematics, mathematical analysis, probability, and topology. The source
books are excluded from the TOME-Math training corpus. Every problem is
evaluated under all five context conditions, making the comparison paired at
the problem level. Retrieval inventories. Candidate context is drawn only from TOME-Math records associated with the
exercise’s source book. The proposition inventory contains extracted
definitions, lemmas, theorems, propositions, corollaries, and their associated
proof records. The expanded inventory adds the self-contained local proof
obligations and answers from the Gap QA stream. Source type, chapter position,
and recovered dependency metadata are retained for retrieval. The five conditions are constructed as follows. • Closed : provide only the exercise. • Random Same-Book : sample entries uniformly from the same
source book, without using chapter or dependency information. • Local Chapter : restrict candidates to the exercise chapter
and its immediately preceding chapters, adding locality while ignoring
recovered cross-chapter dependencies. • TOME (Props Only) : retrieve from the proposition inventory
using chapter position and the recovered proposition dependencies. • TOME (Props + Gap) : use the same structure-guided retrieval
procedure over the union of the proposition and Gap QA inventories. Context and leakage controls. All four context-augmented conditions use the same context-token budget and
the same prompt template; only the candidate pool and retrieval constraint
change. Retrieved entries are placed before the exercise and identified by
their source type. The reference solution is never shown to the solver.
Candidates that reproduce the target exercise or overlap with its reference
answer under the frozen leakage filter are removed before retrieval. The same
filtered inventory is used to construct the random and local baselines, so the
comparison does not give the TOME conditions access to otherwise unavailable
source material. Generation and scoring. The solver is the pretrained Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base in every condition. Each
problem receives 16 sampled responses under the decoding configuration in
Table 7 ; adding context does not change the model weights,
prompt instruction, or sampling parameters. Responses are graded with the
same reference-guided eight-review verifier used for TOME-LongForm .
We compute pass@1 as the mean binary correctness across the 16 responses for
each problem, average within each of the six equally sized subjects, and report
their unweighted mean in the Overall column of
Table 3 . Result interpretation. The aggregate score increases from 13.25% without context to 14.00% with
random same-book context and 14.71% with local context. Proposition-based
TOME retrieval reaches 15.54%, and adding the Gap QA inventory raises the
score to 17.38%. The final condition is best in every subject, although the
intermediate conditions are not monotonically ordered within every subject.
Accordingly, the result supports the complete structure-guided retrieval
design and the complementary value of Gap QA. Appendix G Curriculum-Scaling Protocol Subject-wise design. The curriculum study in Section 5.2 covers discrete
mathematics, mathematical analysis, abstract algebra, probability,
differential geometry, and topology. We train a separate model for each
subject so that curriculum order is not confounded with the composition of a
multidisciplinary corpus. Every condition is evaluated on the same
textbook-native exercises from held-out books in the corresponding subject. Normalized chapter stages. Textbooks contain different numbers of chapters, so raw chapter indices are
not directly comparable across books. For an example x x from book b b , let i ⁡ ( x ) i(x) be its zero-based chapter index and N b N_{b} the number of chapters in the
book. We define its normalized position as p ⁡ ( x ) = i ⁡ ( x ) / N b p(x)=i(x)/N_{b} and assign it to
progress stage S ⌊ 10 ​ p ​ ( x ) ⌋ S_{\lfloor 10p(x)\rfloor} , yielding ten stages S 0 , … , S 9 S_{0},\ldots,S_{9} . Examples from different books are interleaved within each
stage, preserving their approximate within-book progression without imposing
an arbitrary order over books. Gap QA makes a locally omitted proof
obligation explicit; all remaining examples are treated as Proof QA. Exposure schedules. The four schedules differ only in presentation order: • Shuffle globally shuffles all examples. • Forward–Gap traverses stages from S 0 S_{0} to S 9 S_{9} and presents Gap before Non-gap examples within each stage. • Reverse–Gap traverses stages from S 9 S_{9} to S 0 S_{0} while retaining the same Gap-first policy. • Forward–Mix follows the forward stage order but mixes Gap and Non-gap examples within each stage. Thus, Forward–Gap versus Reverse–Gap is the controlled
comparison for chapter direction, and Forward–Gap versus Forward–Mix is the controlled comparison for stage-wise Gap-first
ordering. The comparison with Shuffle changes both factors and is
interpreted as the combined effect of the structured schedule. Scales and matched controls. We use per-subject target-token budgets of 0.75M, 1.8M, and 2.4M. Within every
