Abstract Weight-space programming, introduced in recent MIT work by Takehi et al., enables a frontier LLM agent to read a network’s weights and write a repair. Their GPT-6 Astra agent corrected the reasoning loops of Qwen3.5-0.8B without gradients, utilizing 158 agent turns, 89.7 million tokens, and 50 forward-pass requests. Our method, Tree-Ising , casts this repair as a sparse discrete optimization problem over the model’s native BF16 weights. We leverage forward statistics to define an objective that weakens a loop direction, strengthens a concluding direction, and preserves the layer’s output on productive tokens. Within a state-tree representation, where lossless BF16 storage acts as a special case, the admissible edits consist of sign flips and exponent shifts that preserve all shared group exponents and mantissas. We decompose this objective exactly into independent QUBO (Ising) problems, one per output row, which we solve on a GPU using simulated annealing or the quantum-inspired simulated bifurcation method. Evaluated on the two layers targeted by the agent, Tree-Ising achieves 54.6% on MATH-500, outperforming the agent’s edit (51.7%) under the same evaluation protocol, while the base model scores only 7.9%. Furthermore, the simulated bifurcation solver yields a statistically indistinguishable repair at 55.1%. Crucially, both solvers outperform the agent edit across all five math benchmarks, with statistically significant improvements on MATH-500 and GSM8K after correcting for multiple comparisons. Moreover, our approach modifies 13% to 17% fewer weights and reduces BF16 bit flips by a factor of 2.2 to 2.3. It requires only four forward-pass requests and zero LLM tokens, with the annealing recipe costing approximately $0.90 in GPU time. Extending this to Qwen3.5-2B, where layers and dose are selected using only the base model, the same recipe increases greedy GSM8K accuracy from 21.6% to 69.4%. Finally, as a proof of concept, we execute an eight-variable repair subproblem using QAOA on an IBM Heron processor. 1 Introduction Weight-space programming has recently emerged as a compelling paradigm in model editing. Takehi et al. [55] at MIT investigated whether a language model can program the weights of another network: reading its parameters, diagnosing a failure, and writing a fix directly into the weights, with an LLM agent acting as the programmer. Their most prominent demonstration targets a common failure mode in small reasoning models. In thinking mode, Qwen3.5-0.8B frequently falls into a short cycle of repeated text, continuing until the token budget is exhausted; this occurs on 93% of MATH-500 problems under a 32,768-token cap, limiting its accuracy to merely 7.9%. A GPT-6 Astra agent, provided with the checkpoint, practice problems, and a budget of 50 forward-pass requests, traced these loops to the MLPs of layers 15 and 19 and rewrote 64 columns in each of their output projections. Consequently, MATH-500 accuracy rose to 48.1% without a single gradient step.
While this result proves that weights can be programmed, it also highlights the substantial price of doing so with an agent: 158 turns, 89.7 million tokens from a frontier model, 50 forward-pass requests, and 15.6 hours of compute for a single edit ( Table 4 ). Crucially, every step of this process relies on judgment calls made in natural language, offering no guarantee that the resulting edit is the optimal one available.
In this work, we cast the repair as a sparse discrete optimization problem over the stored weights. The evidence gathered by the agent—forward passes on looping answers versus successful ones—can be formalized as a mathematical objective, where an edit is defined as a discrete choice among changes to stored bits. We build this formulation on state trees [ 2 ] , a general representation of neural networks in which every weight is a path of discrete decisions through a tree, and weights sharing the same decision share the recording node. Lossless BF16 storage is a special case of this representation, where each group of 64 weights shares its exponent as a node; this is the specific case we utilize. Our formulation, which we call Tree-Ising , comprises five key steps ( Figure 2 ): 1. Represent the weights as state trees. In the lossless BF16 state tree, multiplying a weight by a power of two or flipping its sign alters only its 4-bit state and sign bit. Moves that preserve each group’s maximum leave the shared exponent untouched. Preselecting one such move per candidate weight reduces the edit to a series of binary decisions. 2. Select the layers. A single forward scan of the base model evaluates how strongly each MLP writes along the loop direction, automatically identifying the layers to edit without requiring an agent. 3. Define the objective. Forward statistics yield a loop direction and a concluding direction. The objective is to minimize the layer’s projection along their combination on loop tokens, preserve the output on productive tokens, and restrict the total number of modified weights. 4. Formulate as an Ising model. For fixed statistics and preselected moves, the objective decomposes exactly into independent subproblems, one per output row. Each row subproblem is formulated as a dense QUBO, equivalently an Ising model. 5. Solve the optimization. A GPU solves all row problems for a given layer simultaneously using simulated annealing or simulated bifurcation, a quantum-inspired Ising solver. Furthermore, replacing the annealer with simulated bifurcation [ 18 , 17 ] yields an equally effective repair, achieving 55.1% on MATH-500 with 4% fewer modified weights, demonstrating that the results do not hinge on a single solver. Crucially, this approach requires only 4 forward-pass requests instead of 50, reducing forward-pass compute by a factor of 34 and eliminating the need for LLM tokens entirely. The entire recipe executes in 31 minutes at a cost of approximately $0.90. Moreover, the resulting edit is substantially smaller: it modifies 13% fewer weights, reduces BF16 bit flips by a factor of 2.2, cuts state-tree bit changes by a factor of 4, and, by construction of its move set, leaves all shared exponents and mantissas untouched.
It is important to clarify the scope of this comparison. The agent incurred a significant cost to discover the failure, identify the direction, and select the layers. In contrast, our recipe was designed after analyzing its public logs; on Qwen3.5-0.8B, it reuses the agent’s two selected layers and calibrates its dose based on the agent’s edit. Thus, the cost figures compare the exhaustive process of finding a fix against the application of a fixed, optimized recipe. Our primary aim is to achieve a superior repair through fewer weight modifications. For context, a dense closed-form solution of the same objective, without sparsity or discrete moves, reaches similar accuracy on our practice screen but modifies 96% of the weights in both matrices—62 times as many as Tree-Ising ( Section 4.5 ).
Extending this recipe to Qwen3.5-2B demonstrates that the agent is not strictly necessary. With layers selected by the forward scan and the dose calibrated from the base model alone, the method raises greedy GSM8K accuracy from 21.6% to 69.4%. Contributions. The contributions of this work are summarized as follows: • We propose a sparse discrete formulation for model repair on state trees, introducing tree-local moves, a forward-only objective, and an exact decomposition into independent row Ising problems for fixed statistics and preselected moves ( Section 3 ). • We demonstrate that on Qwen3.5-0.8B, Tree-Ising produces an edit that is more accurate than the agent-based repair of Takehi et al. [55] , while modifying fewer weights and bits, at a small fraction of the forward passes, time, and computational cost ( Section 4 ). • We show that this recipe transfers effectively to Qwen3.5-2B without any reference edit, achieving a 47.8-point gain in GSM8K accuracy under greedy decoding ( Section 5 ). Finally, as a supplementary proof of concept, we successfully execute an eight-variable repair subproblem using QAOA on an IBM Heron quantum processor ( Section 6 ). 2 Background Reasoning loops. We define an answer as cut when the generation process reaches the maximum token budget before the model produces its final conclusion. In the models evaluated in this work, nearly all cut answers exhibit looping behavior: within the final 2,000 tokens, the majority of 8-grams have already appeared earlier in the same sequence. While repetition in neural text generation has been extensively studied through decoding heuristics and modified training objectives [ 22 , 58 , 59 ] , and recent reports on reasoning models analyze how extended thinking traces interact with decoding configurations [ 12 , 48 ] , we frame looping as a property of the network’s weights. This perspective treats the failure mode as an artifact that a direct weight edit can resolve. Weight-space programming. Takehi et al. [55] enabled a frontier LLM agent to read the parameters of Qwen3.5-0.8B and execute forward passes on practice problems.
Within a budget of 50 requests, the agent identified a specific direction in the residual stream that remains active during loops, traced this behavior to the MLP output projections ( down_proj ) of layers 15 and 19,
and applied a structured modification to 64 columns in each matrix. The comprehensive logs released by the authors [ 54 ] detail every interaction, token count, and forward pass, allowing us to precisely quantify the computational cost of the procedure ( Section 4.3 ). We denote the resulting checkpoint as the agent edit . Every weight is defined as a path of discrete decisions from the root of a tree to a leaf, and weights that share the same decision share the node that records it. This representation seamlessly unifies low-bit compression and exact storage. Lossless BF16 storage is a specific instance of this framework, and it is the one we employ. A BF16 number consists of one sign bit, an 8-bit exponent e e , and a 7-bit mantissa [ 31 ] . The lossless BF16 state tree groups 64 consecutive weights within a row and stores their maximum exponent E E once as a shared node. Each individual weight then stores a 4-bit state D = E − e ∈ { 0 , … , 14 } D=E-e\in\{0,\dots,14\} and a leaf containing its sign and mantissa; state 15 designates a rare escape whose exponent is maintained in a side list. This instantiation reproduces every weight exactly, cleanly separating what a group shares (its exponent) from what each weight owns (its offset, sign, and mantissa), mirroring the structure of block floating-point and microscaling formats in their lossy counterparts [ 13 , 51 ] . Because every stored quantity is a discrete decision, an edit is fundamentally a change of decisions. Tree-local moves. We restrict each weight to be multiplied by a factor from the set ℱ = { − 1 , 2 , 1 2 , 4 , − 2 , − 4 , − 1 2 , 1 4 } \mathcal{F}=\bigl\{-1,\;2,\;\tfrac{1}{2},\;4,\;-2,\;-4,\;-\tfrac{1}{2},\;\tfrac{1}{4}\bigr\} (1) or to remain unchanged. For a standard weight with state D D , a factor with k = log 2 ⁡ | f | k=\log_{2}|f| shifts the state to D − k D-k and flips the sign if f < 0 f<0 . A move is deemed admissible only if it preserves the group’s shared exponent and generates no escape. Growth ( k > 0 k>0 ) requires D ≥ k D\geq k , ensuring no weight exceeds the group maximum; shrinkage ( k < 0 k<0 ) requires D ≥ 1 D\geq 1 and D − k ≤ 14 D-k\leq 14 , guaranteeing that the group maximum is never reduced and no weight falls outside the 4-bit range. Sign flips are universally admissible. Every admissible move is exact in BF16, preserves the mantissa, and alters at most the sign bit and the 4-bit state of a single weight. For instance, the weight 0.125 0.125 in a group with E = 127 E=127 has D = 3 D=3 ; doubling it yields 0.25 0.25 with D = 2 D=2 , resulting in a change to a single stored bit. Binary formulation and the role of the state tree. The binary nature of the formulation arises from preselecting one move per candidate weight ( Section 3.4 ): the sole remaining decision is whether to apply it, rendering the edit a vector of binary variables where every subsequent quantity is a polynomial of degree at most two, conforming to the QUBO format. Multiplying by a power of two is also exact in standard BF16, meaning the tree itself does not create the binary structure. Rather, its contribution lies in providing a format-native move set and its corresponding accounting. Because admissible moves never alter the group maximum, the shared node is never rewritten, and the number of stored bits modified by an edit can be directly read from the tree. The guarantee that the edit writes no shared exponent and no mantissa therefore holds by construction, rather than as an empirical observation. 3.2 Step 2: selecting the layers Forward statistics. Our default solver processes all rows of a given layer as a single GPU batch via simulated annealing [ 33 ] . The final solve executes 16 replicas per row, starting from the zero edit, for 300 sweeps of single-spin Metropolis updates in random order. The temperature decreases geometrically from 2 to 10 − 4 10^{-4} times the row’s median | h a | |h_{a}| , and we retain the lowest-energy replica. Simulated bifurcation. As an alternative solver, we employ discrete simulated bifurcation (dSB) [ 18 , 17 ] , a quantum-inspired method that integrates the classical dynamics of coupled bifurcating oscillators and reads spins from their signs. Each replica initializes near the zero edit; the pump ramps up over the first quarter of 1,200 steps of size 0.5, and the best sampled state of every trajectory, refined by single-flip descent in float64, is retained. Rows are processed in batches of 32, maintaining the same replica counts as in annealing. All other components, including the candidates, the preselected moves, and the calibration protocol, remain unchanged, ensuring the two solvers differ solely in how each row problem is searched. The separately calibrated parameter γ = 0.0844 \gamma=0.0844 adheres to a single-weight threshold rule, and we set λ = λ 0 \lambda=\lambda_{0} ( Appendix B ). With eight binary variables yielding 2 8 = 256 2^{8}=256 possible assignments, exhaustive enumeration establishes the exact optimum at negligible computational cost. We optimized the QAOA angles classically using exact statevector simulation. In a single hardware execution, the depth-two QAOA circuit sampled the optimum in 110 out of 1,024 shots, compared to merely four shots for a uniform control. We note that uniform sampling serves as a relatively weak baseline for assessing optimization performance; matched random-angle circuits and classical solvers applied to identical instances would provide a more rigorous comparison. Full circuit configurations, sampling results, and resource accounting are detailed in Appendix B . Crucially, this experiment establishes that a small, separately calibrated instance can be successfully encoded, executed, and sampled on quantum hardware. However, it does not constitute evidence of a quantum advantage. As demonstrated in Section 4 , simulated annealing, simulated bifurcation, and the closed-form continuous edit all yield statistically indistinguishable accuracy, indicating that the current row-level problems are not computationally challenging for classical solvers, nor do marginal improvements in row energy translate to superior model performance. The primary value of these problems for quantum optimization lies in their role as a source of structured, real-world Ising instances equipped with a meaningful downstream metric: the accuracy of the repaired model. Significantly harder formulations, such as joint move selection or explicit bit budgets ( Appendix C ), would be required before a quantum solver could offer a tangible advantage. Consequently, the full-model accuracy results reported in Sections 4 and 5 are derived entirely from the classical GPU solver. A subsequent investigation into hybrid refinement would condition each block on the GPU solution, apply the established repair calibration, and evaluate the resulting checkpoint on standard benchmarks. 7 Related work Weight-space programming and model editing. Takehi et al. [55] investigated whether a general-purpose LLM can read and write network weights; among other tasks, their agent successfully repairs the reasoning loops of Qwen3.5-0.8B. We study the identical repair task but replace the LLM agent with an optimizer built from the same empirical evidence. Classical model editing typically modifies factual associations through targeted updates of MLP weights [ 41 , 42 ] , locates knowledge within individual neurons [ 11 ] , learns editors that predict weight updates [ 43 ] , or combines fine-tuning differences [ 27 ] . Crucially, these methods produce continuous updates. In contrast, Tree-Ising selects sparse, discrete changes that the stored weight format can represent exactly, and Section 4.5 compares our approach with a ROME-like rank-one solution of our own objective. Furthermore, while bit-flip attacks demonstrate that a few flipped bits can substantially alter a network’s behavior [ 50 ] , we leverage this same sensitivity to repair rather than to harm. Activation steering and directional interventions. Steering methods typically add directions to the residual stream at inference time [ 38 , 56 , 62 ] , and directional ablation can be folded directly into the weights [ 5 ] . Our directions are contrasts of the same kind, measured at the onset of loops and at the conclusion of successful thinking traces. However, our intervention takes the form of a sparse weight edit optimized against a collateral penalty, requiring no modifications at inference time. Mechanistic studies of transformer circuits strongly motivate reading such directions directly from the residual stream [ 14 ] . Closest to our setting, ThinkEdit [ 53 ] identifies a linear direction governing reasoning length, isolates a small set of attention heads driving overly short reasoning, and removes that direction from their output projections, altering approximately 0.2% of the parameters. We address the opposite failure mode—reasoning that never terminates—and edit MLP output projections through sparse discrete moves rather than a continuous projection. Repetition and looping. Degenerate repetition has been extensively addressed through decoding heuristics [ 22 ] and modified training objectives [ 58 , 59 ] , and recent reports on reasoning models analyze how extended thinking traces interact with decoding configurations [ 12 , 48 ] . In contrast, Tree-Ising alters neither the decoder nor the training process; it simply edits the weights once, entirely offline. Weight formats. Block floating-point and microscaling formats share exponents within groups of weights [ 13 , 51 ] , and lossless BF16 compression exploits the low entropy of these exponents [ 60 ] . State trees [ 2 ] provide a general representation of neural networks as paths of shared discrete decisions, with lossless BF16 storage and low-bit compression serving as specific instances; that work also heals a compressed model using gradient and curvature estimates. In our formulation, the state tree fundamentally defines the admissible moves of a forward-only objective. QUBO formulations of network weights. AdaRound derives a binary rounding problem from a second-order approximation of the task loss [ 46 ] , and Muñiz Subiñas et al. [45] formulate layer-output reconstruction for quantization as an exact QUBO that separates into independent output rows, solving it via simulated annealing. Layer-output reconstruction also underpins many pruning and quantization methods [ 19 , 16 ] . Our objective augments this reconstruction term with a behavioral term along a measured direction and a sparsity cost, operating over moves that preserve the stored format intact. Formulated as Ising models, these row problems connect seamlessly to the broad toolbox of Ising formulations [ 40 ] , simulated annealing [ 33 ] , quantum annealing [ 30 , 28 ] , QAOA [ 15 ] , and digital or analogue Ising machines [ 18 , 44 ] . Quantum hardware for neural networks and LLMs. Quantum annealers have been utilized to train binary neural networks [ 52 ] and to select pruning and quantization configurations for convolutional networks [ 57 ] . For LLMs, recent work integrates variational quantum circuits into a pretrained model, either as parameter-efficient fine-tuning modules executed on a superconducting processor [ 34 ] or as unitary adapters inside the projection layers of Llama 3.1 8B running on IBM hardware [ 1 ] . In those studies, the quantum circuit constitutes a novel addition to the model architecture. In Tree-Ising , however, the model form remains entirely unchanged: the quantum device, much like the GPU, simply solves for which of the model’s existing BF16 weights should be modified, as demonstrated here on a single eight-variable subproblem. 8 Limitations Scope. Our study addresses a single failure mode: reasoning loops, specifically within two models of a single family that share a hybrid linear/full-attention architecture, evaluated exclusively on mathematical benchmarks. We have not assessed instruction-following capabilities, although Takehi et al. [55] report that the agent edit raised IFEval from 21.0% to 62.3%, nor have we tested general language modeling or non-mathematical benchmarks. Consequently, side effects remain unmeasured beyond the collateral term, which protects the layer’s output only on tokens derived from the practice problems. Evaluating larger models, alternative model families, and other behavioral failure modes remains a direction for future work. Baselines and Evaluation. The base model’s 7.9% accuracy on MATH-500 is the figure reported by Takehi et al. [55] ; we did not independently rerun the base model in our evaluation harness. Furthermore, the continuous edits detailed in Section 4.5 were evaluated exclusively on the 128-problem practice screen. Early exploratory variants on Qwen3.5-0.8B were screened on 100 MATH-500 problems, meaning the full MATH-500 results include problems exposed during development; however, the performance gap remains consistent on the 400 strictly held-out problems ( Section 4.2 ). Each benchmark evaluation utilized a single generation seed. The p p -values reported in the tables are nominal; notably, only the gains on MATH-500@4 and GSM8K over the agent edit survive a strict Bonferroni correction across the twelve agent comparisons. Design Choices. Our repair recipe was formulated retrospectively after analyzing the agent’s public logs. On Qwen3.5-0.8B, the edit utilizes the agent’s selected layers and a dose 1.6 times that of the agent’s, calibrated on the practice screen; notably, no strictly dose-matched edit was executed. While the Qwen3.5-2B experiment eliminates both of these dependencies, the rule excluding the final layer was established after inspecting the 0.8B scan. Furthermore, the layers selected in both models are full-attention blocks, suggesting that this agreement may stem from their shared architectural design rather than independent validation. For Qwen3.5-2B, greedy decoding and the GSM8K benchmark were selected based on base-model results from a 200-problem GSM8K test subset where the base model struggles most, whereas all edit-specific settings were determined solely on the practice problems. Surrogate Objective and Solvers. Our objective functions as a first-order surrogate derived from the statistics of the unedited model: it inherently ignores how an edit in one layer alters the inputs to subsequent layers, and the dose serves merely as a linear proxy for the desired behavioral shift. What remains mathematically exact is the decomposition of this surrogate into independent row problems once candidate columns and a single move per weight are fixed; however, these two choices, along with the row solvers themselves, are heuristic. The practice screen resolves performance differences of approximately four percentage points, leaving the optimal dose and move set somewhat uncertain. Each solver was executed only once with a single seed, and their run times were not measured under strictly matched conditions; therefore, we make no definitive claims regarding their relative computational speed. Accounting. Our state-tree bit counts assume a straightforward encoder and deliberately exclude modifications within the payloads of existing escapes; furthermore, direct patching of a compressed checkpoint has not yet been implemented. The reported time and cost metrics cover a single execution of a fixed recipe at list prices and explicitly exclude method development costs on both sides. Stage timings are derived from single runs executed with two versions of the solver script and should not be interpreted as a rigorous scaling measurement. Quantum Hardware. The quantum hardware evaluation is confined to a single eight-variable subproblem, encompassing just 8 of the 7.3 million weights across the two edited matrices, with the remainder of its row fixed to zero. This evaluation utilized a dose calibration declared separately from the published edit, angles optimized classically, a single hardware job, and one calibration period. Crucially, the resulting solution was not written back into a model. The control baseline sampled uniformly; a random-angle control on the identical compiled circuits has not been executed. Consequently, this result demonstrates a correct mapping and objective-consistent sampling on quantum hardware, not a computational advantage over classical solvers. Furthermore, Equation 14 bounds the theoretical benefit of any faster solver within the confines of our current recipe. 9 Conclusion While a frontier LLM can repair a looping model by directly editing its weights, we have demonstrated that this same repair task can be formulated as a sparse discrete optimization problem and solved in a matter of minutes. Specifically, Tree-Ising restricts admissible edits to sign flips and exponent shifts within the state tree of each weight group, evaluates them using forward statistics, and efficiently solves one small Ising problem per output row. On Qwen3.5-0.8B, our method produces an edit that is more accurate than the agent’s baseline while modifying fewer weights and far fewer bits, all for a computational cost of approximately $0.90 per run. Crucially, both simulated annealing and the quantum-inspired simulated bifurcation solver yield repairs of equivalent quality, demonstrating that the performance gains stem fundamentally from the mathematical formulation itself rather than the tuning of a specific solver. In contrast, a dense closed-form solution of the identical objective achieves comparable accuracy only by altering nearly every weight across both matrices. Furthermore, when applied to Qwen3.5-2B—with target layers and dosage calibrated entirely from the base model—this same recipe elevates GSM8K accuracy under greedy decoding from 21.6% to 69.4%. Finally, an eight-variable QAOA experiment executed on IBM quantum hardware serves as a preliminary proof of concept for running a repair-derived Ising instance on a quantum processor. Extending this validation to full-layer repairs and rigorously measuring the resulting downstream model accuracy remains a compelling direction for future work. Code Availability.
