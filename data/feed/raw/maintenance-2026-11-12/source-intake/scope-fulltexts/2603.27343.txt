Large language models (LLMs) face a related demand when tracking updates within a single prompt without writing out intermediate states, yet existing tests differ in their constraints on written steps.
We introduce a working-memory-inspired probe of cumulative state tracking with program-computed answers, 3 to 100 updates, and final-state, intermediate-state, or multi-target queries.
It compares protocols on identical items.
Across 18 LLMs, accuracy is 84.9% on single-step arithmetic but 31.7% on core items requiring one number after three to seven updates.
From 50 updates on, no model exceeds 10% accuracy.
Core accuracy reaches 88.0% under a larger budget and instructions to write steps and place the answer on the last line, mainly due to the instruction changes rather than the budget.
Core accuracy also correlates positively with public Berkeley Function Calling Leaderboard scores.
These findings show that strong single-step arithmetic does not ensure reliable cumulative tracking and that output protocols substantially change measured accuracy.
We release the generator, items, scoring code, and responses for reproducible comparisons across depths, query types, and protocols. 1 Introduction Keeping a running score in one’s head relies on working memory, which holds information briefly and updates it as changes arrive ( Baddeley and Hitch, 1974 ; Miyake et al., 2000 ) .
Large language models (LLMs) meet a similar demand when a prompt lists an initial state and a series of updates and asks for the result without written steps ( Nye et al., 2021 ; Wei et al., 2022 , cf.) .
Such state tracking underlies many uses of LLMs, from understanding narratives and instructions to stateful, multi-turn tool use ( Kim and Schuster, 2023 ; Patil et al., 2025 ) .
Much as people write down information they need to remember ( Risko and Gilbert, 2016 ) , a model’s written steps record intermediate states, which removes this demand ( Nye et al., 2021 ) .
Testing this demand therefore requires controlling how prompt wording ( Mizrahi et al., 2024 ) , output budgets, and scoring rules constrain written steps. Existing state-tracking tests share this structure but constrain written steps differently.
Minerva, for example, allows 4,096 output tokens, so models can write out the operations ( Xia et al., 2025 ) .
These tests may therefore not all pose this demand. Figure 1: In a working-memory-inspired probe, LLM accuracy falls as updates accumulate when models answer without written steps. We present a working-memory-inspired probe of cumulative state tracking (Figure 1 ).
Its generator sets the number of updates, and its protocols set how much room is left for written steps.
Under the core protocol, a 16-token budget and the one-integer rule leave little room for written steps. Without written steps, all 18 models answer the core items less accurately than single-step arithmetic, and accuracy falls as sequences grow.
Asking for written steps ( Kojima et al., 2022 ) raises core accuracy from 31.7% to 88.0%, so protocol choices change what is measured.
Core accuracy also correlates positively with public Berkeley Function Calling Leaderboard (BFCL) scores ( Patil et al., 2025 ) (Section 4.6 ). We make three contributions. • A generator with adjustable depth, executable answers, and final-state, intermediate-state, and multi-target queries, released with items, code, and responses. • Same-item comparisons showing substantial protocol effects on measured accuracy, with sensitivity analyses across wordings, seeds, and depths. • Positive BFCL associations across 18 models, compared with those of single-step arithmetic and cancellation controls. 2 Related Work Entity tracking moves objects among boxes ( Kim and Schuster, 2023 ) , and BIG-Bench Hard tracks pairwise object swaps with multiple-choice answers ( Suzgun et al., 2023 ) . Our core instead applies integer updates to three named entities and requests one free-response integer. Minerva’s quantity-state task is closest in content but allows 4,096 output tokens, enough to work through updates in writing ( Xia et al., 2025 ) . GSM-Infinite varies operation counts but requests step-by-step reasoning ( Zhou et al., 2025 ) . Our core allows 16 tokens and scores exactly one integer. Written-steps and large-budget protocols relax these limits on the same items. LLM working-memory studies use match detection on n n -back tasks ( Gong et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2024 ) , and tested models meet or exceed human norms on span tasks ( de Langis et al., 2026 ) . Wang and Sun (2026) ask for the last value of a repeatedly overwritten key. Unlike human operation span ( Conway et al., 2005 ) , our probe keeps every initial value and update visible. Working memory therefore motivates the demand tested here, without establishing equivalence to human span tasks. Representation probes and interventions can distinguish state-tracking algorithms ( Li et al., 2025 ; Tang et al., 2026 ) , but final answers cannot show whether models maintain or recompute states. Figure 2: Probe workflow. (1) Code generates items and computes reference answers. (2) Four response protocols are applied to the same core items. (3) Responses are scored and truncation is flagged. (4) Performance is compared across conditions and related to BFCL scores. The response 28 is Claude Haiku 4.5’s recorded response (Figure 1 a). The other two illustrate the scoring rules. Benchmark scores depend on paraphrased instructions and task-preserving formatting ( Mizrahi et al., 2024 ; Sclar et al., 2024 ) . Output-format restrictions can affect reasoning accuracy ( Tam et al., 2024 ) . Motivated by measurement-validity recommendations ( Bean et al., 2025 ) , we report integer compliance, truncation, and seed and wording sensitivity alongside accuracy. BFCL evaluates function calling, including stateful, multi-step interactions ( Patil et al., 2025 ) , and shares no items with the probe. Comparing the core’s BFCL association with those of simpler controls follows discriminant-validation logic ( Campbell and Fiske, 1959 ) . Appendix H provides additional task and methodological comparisons. 3 Probe and Evaluation Design 3.1 Generating state-tracking tasks Core items initialize three named entities with integers, list K K ordered gains, losses, or transfers between them, and query one entity’s final value (Figure 2 ).
Initial values range from 5 to 20, gains from 1 to 10, losses from 1 to 5, and transfers from 1 to 3.
The reference answer is the queried entity’s initial value plus its signed changes, so update order does not change it. A short program computes it and counts the updates to that entity (median 2 at K = 3 K=3 and 45.5 at K = 100 K=100 ).
A fixed redraw rule excludes core and depth items where that entity is never updated or finishes at its initial value, preventing correct answers from copying the initial value. Each item is one user message.
Although the prompt says the initial state cannot be consulted again, the full sequence remains in context.
The core prompt ends with Respond with ONLY the final number. and allows 16 output tokens.
One complete core prompt reads as follows. You will track a sequence of point updates. You cannot refer back to the initial state after reading it once. Initial state: Leo: 15 points, James: 18 points, Carol: 15 points Operations (apply in order): 1. Carol gives 2 points to Leo. 2. Leo loses 2 points. 3. Leo loses 3 points. After all operations, how many points does Leo have? Respond with ONLY the final number. The reference answer is 12.

Three protocols reuse the core items.
The instruction variant changes user and system instructions, retaining the core budget and scoring. Its system message reads You are a precise arithmetic assistant. You track numerical state changes and report final values. Always respond with only the requested number, no explanation. Large budget retains the core prompt with a 512-token limit.
Written steps uses the same limit but replaces the closing instruction with Think step by step, then give your final answer as a single number on the last line. Their last-integer comparison tests the combined instruction to write steps and place the answer on the last line (Section 4.4 ). Intermediate-state and multi-target items extend the final-state query (Section 4.2 , Appendix G ). 3.3 Response scoring Core, depth, arithmetic, cancellation, wording, instruction-variant, and intermediate-state responses require exactly one extracted integer matching the executable answer (one-integer rule, Figure 2 ). Zero or multiple integers receive zero credit.
Written steps uses the last integer (last-integer rule). Large budget uses both rules.
Multi-target responses must give exactly M M integers matching the reference values in the requested order (Appendix G ).
The parser removes dollar signs and supported thousands separators before extraction but allows surrounding prose, so compliance differs from a bare-number answer.
We report correct, one-integer-wrong, and noncompliant responses.
Truncation, the service’s length finish reason, can overlap any outcome.
All core items remain in the denominator. Assignment and set and count items use two parsers, reported in parallel with no primary parser.
Parser 2 requires one distinct correct vocabulary member for assignment, and one correct integer or exact set equality for sets and counts.
Parser 1 permits more substring, numerical-position, and empty-set matches.

Paired differences (core minus arithmetic and core minus cancellation) assess whether core associations exceed those of the simpler controls.
We report pointwise 95% percentile intervals from 10,000 paired model-bootstrap samples, shared across all associations within a panel, including the instruction variant, large budget, and written steps. A Pearson check repeats core, arithmetic, and cancellation comparisons using correct counts rather than ranks (Table A3 ).
We also leave out each of seven provider groups in turn to assess provider influence. The main analysis retains all 18 models. Under both parsers, we compute associations of sets and counts with the core and three BFCL criteria, and of each domain with the core.
Intermediate-state and multi-target means and paired differences also use 10,000 model-bootstrap samples.
Seed, wording, and depth analyses report descriptive accuracies and rank correlations without significance tests.

With the sequence visible, final answers cannot distinguish state maintenance from recomputation or establish equivalence to human span tasks. Changing instructions altered scores on identical items, with the combined instruction accounting for most of the protocol’s gain (Figure 3 c).
Low core accuracy is therefore not a condition-invariant capacity estimate. Test conditions must accompany scores. Positive BFCL associations connect core performance to an external criterion, but uncertain differences from simpler tasks limit their interpretation (Table 6 ).
The probe is better used to locate answer degradation under reported conditions than as a substitute for tool-use evaluation. 6 Conclusion We introduced WMIP to evaluate cumulative state tracking through programmable tasks, executable reference answers, and protocol comparisons on identical items.
Across 18 LLMs, strong single-step arithmetic performance did not ensure reliable cumulative tracking, and accuracy declined as update sequences grew.
Positive associations with public BFCL scores further connected core performance to an external measure of tool-use performance.
Changing the response protocol substantially altered accuracy on the same problems, with the combined instruction contributing more improvement than the larger output budget.
Together, these findings show that measured tracking performance reflects both cumulative task demands and response conditions.
By making these factors configurable and comparisons reproducible, WMIP provides a framework for investigating where state-tracking accuracy degrades and how output conditions affect performance. Limitations The visible sequence and final answers cannot distinguish state maintenance from recomputation or establish equivalence to human working-memory tasks.
Scores depend on instructions, formatting, and output limits. One-integer responses do not rule out written steps or hidden computation.
Protocol changes are bundled, while depth and control comparisons also vary task content.
The 18-model convenience panel limits generalization. Model-bootstrap intervals omit item-sampling uncertainty and provider dependence and are not adjusted for multiplicity.
BFCL is the sole external criterion, based on a historical snapshot with partly documented settings. Gains draw from 1 to 10. Losses draw from 1 to 5 subject to leaving at least one point. Transfers draw from 1 to 3 subject to the giver retaining at least one point.
The implementation substitutes a one-point gain or a no-transfer operation when the corresponding sampled update is unavailable.

Each depth has four seed cells, each retaining five nondegenerate items. Replacement indices advance within the same cell.

Section 3.2 gives its system message and the written-steps instruction.

These items use gains and losses only and were not redrawn. In 7 of the 30, no update involves the queried entity, so the answer equals its initial value.
All five templates share each item’s initial values, updates, query, and reference answer.

Appendix B Scoring Specifications The numerical parser removes dollar signs and supported three-digit thousands separators, then extracts integer substrings matching the regular expression -?\d+ . The core rule requires exactly one match equal to gold, while the written-steps protocol uses the final match.
The bare-number diagnostic additionally removes supported markdown markers and a final period and requires the remaining string to be a single integer.
Scoring uses the complete recorded response, including one ending at the length limit. Parsers for the assignment items. Parser 1 removes complete <think> segments and lowercases the response, accepting gold as a substring or as the normalized first word.
Parser 2 removes asterisks/backticks, surrounding space, and a final period, then requires exactly one distinct vocabulary member, found at word boundaries, equal to gold.
Repetitions of that member and text outside the vocabulary can still pass. Parsers for the set and count items. For counts, Parser 1 removes complete <think> segments and accepts gold if it equals the first or last unsigned digit string, while Parser 2 uses the one-integer rule.
For sets, Parser 1 lowercases, splits on commas, and deletes standalone no permissions or nothing .
Empty-set gold also passes if the response contains no or nothing .
Parser 2 normalizes markup, case, surrounding space, and final periods, splits on commas or the independent word and , and requires exact set equality.
Only standalone no permissions , nothing , or none denotes an empty set. An empty response fails.

Appendix H Extended Related Work This appendix supplements Section 2 with task settings, findings, and methodological comparisons. Behavioral state tracking. For object movement, insertion, and removal, Kim and Schuster (2023) report accuracy by the number of operations affecting the queried box and test generalization to new wording and longer sequences.
On BIG-Bench Hard’s object-swap task, three 2022 models achieved between 53% and 85% accuracy with chain-of-thought prompting, up from near chance. Scores were averaged over three, five, and seven swaps ( Suzgun et al., 2023 ) .
Other tasks apply up to ten swaps, where chain-of-thought prompting also raises accuracy ( Rezaee et al., 2025 ) . Chen et al. (2024) ask models to add a list of integers without writing steps.
They report that the strongest models directly output sums of more than ten addends and that accuracy falls as the number of addends increases.
Their task keeps one running total, whereas our core items distribute updates across three entities. La Malfa et al. (2024) ask for one variable’s final value after short programs of additions, subtractions, and assignments.
They note that most errors occurred when a model output only the final result, not the execution trace. Programmable memory tests. Beyond quantity updates, Minerva includes set updates and composite state tracking. Its main quantity-state evaluation uses 200 operations, with an ablation over operation count ( Xia et al., 2025 ) .
GSM-Infinite reports a sigmoid decline in accuracy as operation count grows ( Zhou et al., 2025 ) .
Michelangelo’s Latent List task pads long contexts with statements that leave the list unchanged, including blocks of operations that cancel each other ( Vodrahalli et al., 2024 ) .
It sets complexity by the number of operations that change the answer.
Our cancellation control instead scores items built only from adjacent updates that cancel. Working-memory task differences. Working-memory research separates holding information from using it during ongoing processing ( Baddeley and Hitch, 1974 ; Engle et al., 1999 ) .
Operation span interleaves arithmetic judgments with words recalled later ( Conway et al., 2005 ) . Gong et al. (2024) give ChatGPT verbal and spatial n n -back tasks and score match detection with d ′ d^{\prime} . Zhang et al. (2024) apply the spatial version to models of different sizes. Hong et al. (2025) place several demanding subtasks before a question and find that answer quality falls as subtasks are added. Haznitrama et al. (2026) adapt neuropsychological tests and keep the move history of their spatial working-memory task in the prompt, which they argue removes the demand to maintain it.
In adults, the same arithmetic chains become much harder when operands must come from a memorized list rather than the screen ( Oberauer et al., 2001 ) . Behavior and internal mechanisms. Huang et al. (2026) study whether models keep an unexpressed state, such as a hidden sequence of integers, whereas our core items state the initial values.
On the boxes task of Kim and Schuster (2023) , which lacks integer updates, probes and interventions on three open models indicate that they gather the relevant information at the query token rather than update the state step by step ( Tang et al., 2026 ) . Prompt and protocol sensitivity. Prompt-template choices also affect comparisons of in-context learning methods ( Voronov et al., 2024 ) .
Heuristic scoring can inflate measured prompt sensitivity compared with judgments by a language model ( Hua et al., 2025 ) .
Restricting output to formats such as JSON generally lowered accuracy on reasoning tasks but not on classification tasks ( Tam et al., 2024 ) .
Evaluations with more auxiliary task demands, such as producing rather than choosing an answer, can mask abilities that lower-demand evaluations reveal ( Hu and Frank, 2024 ) .
Prompts that elicit written reasoning raise accuracy on multi-step problems ( Wei et al., 2022 ; Kojima et al., 2022 ) . Sprague et al. (2025) combine published comparisons with new experiments and find that these gains concentrate on mathematical and symbolic tasks.
Their follow-up experiments attribute the gains mainly to carrying out the computation.
Reasoning models that write long traces still collapse beyond a model-specific problem complexity on planning puzzles ( Shojaee et al., 2025 ) . Measurement validity. Construct-validity accounts relate a measure’s target phenomenon to its tasks and scoring ( Cronbach and Meehl, 1955 ; Messick, 1989 ; Jacobs and Wallach, 2021 ) .
For LLM benchmarks, Bean et al. (2025) additionally emphasize explicit operational definitions, analysis of unrelated task demands, and validation of automated parsing. External tool-use evaluation. Momentè et al. (2025) report a rank correlation of 0.72 between accuracy on a verbal 3-back task and dialogue-game performance across nine open models and call such links speculative.
Scores on standard benchmarks lie close to a low-dimensional capability space that also predicts aggregate scores on agent benchmarks ( Ruan et al., 2024 ) .
