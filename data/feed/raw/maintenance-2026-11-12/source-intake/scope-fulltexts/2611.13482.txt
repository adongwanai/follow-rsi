I Introduction Robot policies trained on demonstration datasets have enabled increasingly complex and generalizable behaviors across a wide range of robotic control tasks [ 57 , 1 ] . A key technique shared by many of these policies is action chunking [ 70 ] . Rather than predicting a single action at each query, action-chunked policies predict a sequence of actions and execute them open-loop, which can help capture non-Markovian structure in demonstrations [ 32 , 39 ] and reduce compounding errors [ 32 , 68 ] . A common choice in recent robot policies is to predict long action chunks , often exceeding 20 steps and approaching 50, as in vision-language-action (VLA) models such as π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} [ 8 ] and π 0 \pi_{0} -FAST [ 48 ] , and world action models such as DreamZero [ 65 ] . By producing many actions per model query, long chunks enable high-frequency control (e.g., 50 Hz) for dexterous tasks such as laundry folding [ 9 ] , while reducing inference pauses for smoother, faster execution. These long-chunk robot policies, while performant, are rarely optimal when deployed in the real world due to limited demonstration coverage and imperfect generalization [ 55 ] . Reinforcement learning (RL) provides a direct way to adapt these policies from online experience. In other domains such as language modeling, online RL has been shown to be quite effective for adapting models pretrained on offline datasets [ 45 , 24 , 21 ] . For robotic control, however, online data collection in the real world is often slow, costly, and sometimes unsafe, making sample efficiency a central requirement. Yet we observe that long action chunks introduce a major bottleneck for sample-efficient adaptation (Figure , left). RL methods that optimize entire action chunks or their latent variables face an increasingly high-dimensional optimization space as the chunk length grows. This substantially increases the amount of online experience required for adaptation. This motivates us to seek an adaptation interface whose dimensionality does not grow with the chunk length. We observe that action-chunked policies naturally expose such an interface, namely, how long to execute a predicted chunk before querying the policy again. Although execution length is a simple scalar decision, adapting it to the current state and predicted action chunk can substantially alter policy behavior by controlling the trade-off between longer open-loop execution, which better exploits the benefits of action chunking, and shorter execution, which improves responsiveness to unexpected state changes or dynamics mismatch [ 39 ] . This makes execution length a meaningful variable for policy improvement, while being particularly attractive for long chunks: as the chunk size grows, the number of possible execution lengths—and hence the expressivity—increases, whereas the optimization itself remains a low-dimensional discrete problem. We instantiate this idea as Execution at the Right Length (ExRL), a sample-efficient online RL method for adapting long-chunk policies.
It adds a lightweight execution-length policy that selects how many actions from each predicted chunk to execute before replanning, and trains it using standard off-policy RL algorithms. Because this adapter can be learned without modifying the base policy itself, ExRL provides a policy-agnostic interface that can be applied to virtually any class of action-chunked policies. Execution-length control is ultimately bounded by the capabilities of the underlying base policy. To go beyond this limitation, we can further optionally distill successful online trajectories back into the base policy during RL using simple imitation loss, allowing the base policy itself to improve alongside the adapter. Finally, ExRL can also leverage general offline data, such as human demonstrations, by reinterpreting arbitrary trajectory segments as off-policy experience under different execution lengths, further improving online sample efficiency. We empirically demonstrate the effectiveness of ExRL across simulated and real-world manipulation tasks, showing state-of-the-art online adaptation performance across a range of long-chunk policies, largely without task-specific hyperparameter tuning. These include standard action-chunked policies, different classes of pretrained VLA models such as π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} [ 8 ] and π 0 \pi_{0} -FAST [ 48 ] , as well as a world action model, Cosmos Policy [ 27 ] . On real-world tasks spanning precise manipulation, long-horizon coordination, and clothing folding, ExRL efficiently adapts frozen π 0.5 \pi_{0.5} policies, approaching perfect success on cube stacking with about ten minutes of physical interaction. II Related Work Imitation learning in robotics. Imitation learning, particularly behavior cloning, has been widely used to train data-driven policies for robotic control [ 5 , 51 , 69 , 18 , 52 ] . Prior work has explored a wide range of action parameterizations and policy architectures for imitation learning. Earlier methods often represent actions with Gaussian or GMM policies [ 25 , 41 ] , while more recent approaches use expressive generative modeling techniques, including VAEs [ 70 ] , diffusion or flow models [ 14 , 44 , 9 , 7 ] , and autoregressive next-token prediction models [ 72 , 28 , 48 ] . With the growing scale of human demonstration datasets and policy models [ 60 , 26 , 11 , 46 ] , imitation learning has further become a pretraining paradigm for generalist robot policies, absorbing broad prior knowledge into a single model that can be adapted for real-world deployment [ 44 , 28 , 9 ] . Our work provides an efficient method to adapt these diverse policy classes from online experience. Adaptive execution of action chunks. Action chunking, as used in ACT [ 70 ] , has become a de facto design choice in robot policies learned with imitation learning. Recognizing that the execution length of action chunks strongly affects policy behavior [ 54 , 68 , 39 ] , several concurrent works have explored adaptive execution strategies. Earlier RL methods such as Dynamic Action Repetition [ 31 ] and TempoRL [ 6 ] adapt how long a primitive action is repeated, rather than how much of a predicted action chunk is executed.
AAC [ 36 ] selects the execution length of vision-language-action models using action entropy, while FFDC [ 61 ] selects the execution length for world action models using a learned verifier to check whether predicted future observations remain consistent with the real rollout. ExRL instead learns execution-length decisions directly from task rewards through online RL.
TAS [ 63 ] uses online RL to select among actions cached from overlapping chunks at every timestep, rather than explicitly learning a chunk-level execution length.
Two other recent works, ACSAC [ 13 ] and ACH [ 53 ] , learn value-based adaptive chunk-size selection for offline-to-online RL. However, these methods train and evaluate specific chunked policies within offline and offline-to-online RL frameworks, and are not designed as plug-in adapters for arbitrary pretrained action-chunked policies. The closest work to ours is [ 71 ] , a concurrent study that also adapts action-chunked policies through reinforcement learning over execution length. However, it relies on PPO for optimization; our comparison in Appendix B shows that off-policy learning can adapt execution lengths with substantially fewer online samples. Reinforcement learning for robot policy adaptation. On-policy methods such as PPO and GRPO have been used to fine-tune pretrained robot policies [ 34 , 40 , 12 , 50 ] , but often require many online rollouts, limiting their practicality on real robots. Off-policy RL algorithms such as SAC [ 22 ] and TD3 [ 20 ] can use data more efficiently, but their standard implementations typically use deterministic or Gaussian actors, making direct adaptation of more complex generative policies nontrivial. Policy-specific approaches address this challenge in different ways: DSRL [ 59 ] and ZPRL [ 66 ] optimize diffusion/flow policies in latent spaces. OGPO [ 47 ] uses off-policy critics to guide optimization of the diffusion or flow generation process, while RL-100 [ 33 ] applies PPO-style updates across diffusion denoising steps. These methods can improve sample efficiency, but remain tied to particular policy classes. Residual RL instead learns additive corrections to the base actions [ 67 , 4 , 3 , 16 , 56 ] . While policy-agnostic and lightweight, residual RL still requires continuous action optimization; correcting an entire chunk makes this space grow with chunk length. RL Token [ 64 ] learns a lightweight actor conditioned on VLA representations and reference chunks, with regularization toward the base actions. PA-RL [ 42 ] achieves policy-agnostic adaptation by casting RL as action optimization followed by supervised policy learning. RECAP [ 2 ] builds an offline-to-online pipeline for training advantage-conditioned policies, which can incorporate batch online RL [ 17 ] for efficient adaptation. In contrast to these action-modifying approaches, ExRL adapts the execution length of each predicted chunk, reducing online adaptation to a compact discrete decision space and enabling sample-efficient, policy-agnostic adaptation. ExRL + online behavior cloning (BC). ExRL can improve policies without changing the actions proposed within each chunk, but its capability is ultimately bounded by the underlying base policy. How can we go beyond this limitation? We note that, by replanning at adaptive times, ExRL can stitch together prefixes from different predicted chunks and produce successful trajectories that would not arise from simply executing the original long chunks open-loop. We can therefore distill these improved behaviors back into the base policy during RL (Figure 2 ). Specifically, from each successful ExRL rollout, we construct H H -step action-chunk targets from the primitive actions actually executed, even when a target spans multiple replanning decisions, and update π base \pi_{\mathrm{base}} using the same behavior cloning objective as in pretraining. In this way, behaviors discovered through execution-length adaptation become direct supervision for the base policy, improving its chunk predictions and in turn providing a stronger basis for further adaptation. Leveraging offline data. ExRL can also reuse general offline trajectories, such as human demonstrations, even if they were collected without action chunking. The key observation is that any sequence of recorded primitive actions can be retrospectively treated as a virtual action chunk (Figure 3 ). Its prefixes of different lengths then provide real experience of executing the same action sequence for different lengths. Concretely, before online training, we enumerate trajectory segments of all lengths k ≤ H k\leq H from offline trajectories and add the corresponding execution-length transitions to the replay buffer. For each transition, the offline trajectory provides the accumulated rewards and resulting state for the executed prefix; from that state onward, the value target bootstraps with the current base policy and execution-length strategy.
This anchors the value of each virtual chunk to the expected return under ExRL’s actual execution strategy, rather than to the behavior that generated the offline trajectory. When online behavior cloning is enabled, demonstration data can also be mixed with successful online trajectories to jointly update the base policy. Remark. One may ask why we do not simply use a short fixed execution length for previous RL methods to avoid high-dimensional optimization, or first select the optimal fixed execution length and then optimize on top of it. Always using a short execution length for RL, however, can be problematic for several reasons: it may yield very poor initial performance on some tasks, making RL optimization difficult; it requires more frequent policy queries, increasing training cost and inference overhead while potentially introducing inference–execution synchronization issues; and, on long-horizon tasks, it substantially increases the effective decision horizon, further complicating RL. Alternatively, preselecting an optimal fixed execution length instead requires extensive real-world evaluation, potentially hundreds of rollouts, which is already comparable to the interaction budget of a typical real-world online RL experiment. ( Q2 ) Can updating the base policy with successful online trajectories and demonstrations further improve adaptation? ( Q3 ) What execution-length patterns does ExRL learn? V-B Baselines and Setup We compare ExRL against execution-length baselines and several online adaptation baselines, with a focus on policy-agnostic methods for action-chunked policies. Our baselines include: Execution-length baselines. (1) Random- k k : randomly samples an execution length from the feasible set at each policy query;
(2) Optimal fixed- k k : sweeps fixed execution lengths and reports the best-performing one. AAC [ 36 ] : A training-free adaptive execution method that selects the execution length using action entropy. Online BC : An ablation that directly distills successful online rollouts back into the base policy using behavior cloning without RL. Policy Decorator [ 67 ] : An off-policy residual RL method that freezes the base policy and learns additive corrections to its predicted actions, with bounded residuals and controlled exploration for stable online learning. DSRL [ 59 ] : A latent steering method for diffusion- and flow-based policies that keeps the base policy frozen and applies RL in its initial latent-noise space rather than directly modifying the generated actions. PA-RL [ 42 ] : A policy-agnostic RL finetuning method that uses a learned critic to improve action candidates through re-ranking and local optimization, and then distills the improved actions back into the base policy. OGPO [ 47 ] : An off-policy full-finetuning method for diffusion- and flow-based policies. It learns off-policy critics for data reuse and uses their values to optimize the full diffusion or flow generation process through a modified PPO objective. ResFiT [ 3 ] : A demo-augmented residual RL method that keeps the base policy frozen, constrains residual magnitude for stable exploration, and jointly trains on demonstration and online data; we adapt its original per-step residual formulation to operate over action chunks. DICE-RL [ 56 ] : Also a demo-augmented residual RL method that combines value-guided action selection with selective behavior regularization to reinforce high-value behaviors while remaining close to the pretrained policy. Simulation benchmarks and setup. We consider three simulation benchmarks: Robomimic [ 41 ] , spanning five tabletop manipulation tasks from simple tasks to challenging long-horizon tasks such as Tool Hang and Transport ; Adroit [ 49 ] , spanning four high-frequency dexterous hand manipulation tasks ( H = 32 H=32 ); and a LIBERO [ 38 ] task following the DSRL setup, except that we use H = 50 H=50 instead of H = 10 H=10 . On Robomimic , the main experiments use H = 50 H=50 for DP and ACT; Appendix Figure 13 reports additional H = 32 H=32 results for both policies. Following prior work [ 59 , 47 ] , we use base-policy checkpoints that leave meaningful headroom for RL improvement. Our goal on these benchmarks is not to maximize base-policy performance, but to use them as a controlled testbed for evaluating long-chunk policy adaptation. For each variant of ExRL, we keep most hyperparameters fixed across tasks and make only a few task-level adjustments when necessary, such as the discount factor γ \gamma and network width. Appendix A provides task details, policy architectures, baseline adaptations, and hyperparameter settings. For each run in the plot, we report the average success rate and 95% confidence intervals over three seeds. V-E Ablation Studies Accounting for the selected execution length in reward accumulation and discounting improves learning over a TD target that treats each decision as a single step (Figure 8 , leftmost panel). The predicted chunk is also essential to the decision: conditioning on both the observation and the chunk raises success from around 30 % 30\% to nearly 90 % 90\% compared with observations alone (second panel). Thus, the critic benefits from knowing what the base policy proposes, beyond the current state. Allowing k = 0 k=0 offers little additional benefit over positive execution lengths (third panel), while similar learning curves across network widths from 128 128 to 2048 2048 suggest that ExRL does not require precise tuning of network capacity (rightmost panel). V-F What Execution Length Patterns Does ExRL Learn? ExRL improves a policy without changing its predicted actions, raising the question of how execution-length decisions reshape its behavior. Figure 9 (a) reveals a consistent pattern: across Lift , Can , Square , and real-world insertion, ExRL selects shorter executions near the object or target than during free motion. The mean length roughly halves in simulation and falls to about a quarter on real-world insertion. ExRL therefore concentrates replanning near interaction, while allowing longer motions elsewhere. Short executions, however, need not serve the same purpose. Two Square rollouts illustrate how ExRL decides when to continue with a longer chunk. In Figure 9 (b), it repeatedly truncates unsuitable predictions with little physical motion, then accepts a chunk that predicts a useful grasp-and-lift motion. Here, frequent replanning provides new candidate motions from nearly the same state. In Figure 9 (c), short executions instead change the state through small corrections, bringing the nut closer to the peg before ExRL switches to longer execution. The distinction is between waiting for a useful prediction and moving into a state from which a longer prediction becomes useful. Both behaviors depend on the current state and the proposed chunk, rather than a fixed preference for short execution. Figure 17 in the appendix illustrates how this balance unfolds across four real-world tasks. Further analysis shows that shuffling the same execution lengths across states and chunks reduces success (Appendix D-B ). It also suggests a complementary reason to preserve longer execution: under feature aliasing , locally similar policy features can repeatedly produce similar first actions, so frequent replanning may interrupt useful progression through a chunk (Appendix D-C ). VI Conclusion We introduced ExRL, which shifts online adaptation of long-chunk policies from action generation to execution-length control. Conditioning this scalar decision on the state and predicted chunk provides substantial behavioral flexibility, while keeping optimization low-dimensional as chunk size grows. Across simulation and real-world tasks, ExRL enables sample-efficient improvement of diverse frozen policies, often surpassing their best fixed execution length. Online imitation learning and offline demonstrations further extend these gains and support continued base-policy improvement during RL. These findings show that learning how to execute a policy’s predictions can unlock capabilities already present in the policy, making execution length a practical interface for adapting long-chunk robot policies with limited online interaction. VII Limitations Our real-world experiments keep the base policy frozen and do not yet incorporate online BC. Performance therefore remains constrained by the actions the base policy can propose, and several tasks still fall short of 100 % 100\% success. Extending online BC to these experiments may help address the remaining failures. We also adapt each task separately and have not evaluated joint multi-task adaptation or generalization to unseen tasks. Recent work demonstrates that sufficiently diverse multi-task experience and value-based RL can improve performance on unseen scenes and objects in simulation [ 29 ] ; whether execution-length adaptation offers similar benefits remains open. Finally, we have not tested ExRL together with asynchronous inference methods such as real-time chunking (RTC) [ 10 ] . The initial insertion and organization experiments revealed a practical distinction between learning in fewer robot actions and completing a rollout quickly. Early in RL, the adapter often favored short execution lengths, requiring frequent queries to the large base policy and slowing each rollout. The original rewards did not directly penalize these inference pauses: insertion received − 1 -1 per primitive timestep before success and 0 0 at success, while organization received + 1 +1 for each completed stage and zero otherwise. This observation motivated the elapsed-time objective used in our later stacking and folding experiments. Both the reward and discount now account for physical time, including inference within a rollout, so the adapter must balance the benefit of replanning against its time cost. For a decision lasting Δ \Delta seconds, the bootstrap discount is γ Δ / 0.05 \gamma^{\Delta/0.05} , and the reward accumulates a discounted cost over the same interval. Stacking incurs unit cost per 50 50 -ms reference interval until success; folding weights this cost by the number of unfinished stages. The latter rewards both stage completion and timely progress through the remaining fold. Successful rollouts terminate, while ordinary time limits retain bootstrapping. Appendix D Additional Execution-Length Analysis The behaviors in Section V-F suggest that ExRL benefits from choosing when to seek a new prediction and when to continue an existing one. Here we examine this interpretation beyond the main-text examples. We first establish that the execution patterns recur across tasks, then separate the effect of choosing suitable lengths from choosing them at suitable moments. Finally, we examine why frequent replanning can sometimes interrupt progress. The Robomimic analyses use frozen base policies with H = 32 H=32 ; the real-world examples use H = 50 H=50 . D-A Execution Patterns across Tasks The tendency to shorten execution near interaction is consistent across tasks and random seeds. Figure 9 (a) compares free motion with the approach to an object or insertion target, using the same trajectories for both phases. ExRL selects shorter lengths near interaction in all nine simulation task–seed pairs as well as on the real robot. This pattern persists when the distance threshold is changed from 8 8 cm to 6 6 or 10 10 cm, suggesting that it reflects a recurring behavioral preference rather than a particular choice of phase boundary. Fig. 17: ExRL varies execution length within a rollout to balance local adjustments and sustained motion. Representative frames and execution-length decisions across four real-world tasks. For example, stacking and folding use short executions near grasping, followed by longer motions to lift and transport the object or fabric; insertion concentrates short executions near contact, whereas parts organization sustains longer executions across much of the pick-and-place sequence. The real-world examples in Figure 17 show how this preference fits into a complete behavior. Stacking and folding use short adjustments around grasping before continuing with longer motions, while insertion concentrates replanning near contact. Parts organization, by contrast, sustains relatively long executions across much of its pick-and-place sequence. Repeated object interactions therefore do not necessarily require uniformly short execution: the appropriate length also depends on how effectively the predicted chunk carries the task forward. For the phase comparison, we collect 50 50 rollouts per simulation task and seed, and 25 25 real insertion rollouts. We retain trajectories that reach interaction and contain both phases: 86 86 for Lift , 145 145 for Can , 121 121 for Square , and 21 21 for insertion. Queries before the first grasp or insertion are split by their starting distance to the object or target. We average selected lengths within each phase and then across trajectories; confidence intervals bootstrap trajectories. D-B Matching Lengths to States and Chunks A favorable mixture of short and long executions might explain some of ExRL’s gains even without adapting to the current situation. We isolate this possibility on Square by keeping the selected lengths unchanged but shuffling their assignment across environments at each synchronized query. The control receives exactly the same multiset of lengths as ExRL-Q, while losing the correspondence between each length and the state–chunk pair for which it was chosen. This reassignment reduces success by 38 38 – 58 58 percentage points across DP and ACT (Table VIII ). The benefit therefore lies not only in which lengths are used, but in when they are used. This result supports the interpretation of the main-text examples: short corrections, rejection of unsuitable chunks, and longer execution are useful when matched to the current state and prediction. D-C Feature Aliasing and Repeated Replanning Short executions help when a new prediction provides a useful correction or alternative motion. But a new prediction can also restart the same behavior. Each query returns to the first action of a newly predicted chunk; if successive states produce similar policy inputs or internal features, these first actions may remain nearly unchanged. We refer to this local ambiguity as feature aliasing . In such situations, continuing through an existing chunk can preserve a progression that repeated replanning fails to recover. This connection appears across both policy architectures. In the shaded interval, replanning after every action moves the end effector by 19.4 19.4 mm, yet successive arm commands change by only 3.6 % 3.6\% as much as those along the original chunk. The robot moves, but the repeated predictions do not recover the later actions already available in that chunk. This example helps explain why allowing a useful prediction to unfold can be beneficial even when fresh observations are available. Related analysis by Lazzati et al. [32] shows that conditioning on past observations can represent non-Markovian demonstrator behavior and mitigate compounding error, with these benefits also obtainable through delayed policies. Our observations provide a complementary perspective: while current features remain locally ambiguous, continuing a chunk may preserve behavioral progression that newly predicted first actions fail to provide. Preserving this progression still requires choosing how long to continue.
