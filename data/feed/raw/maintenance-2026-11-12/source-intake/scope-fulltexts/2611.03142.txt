Project page: https://github.com/jianke0604/DepthGraft Abstract Scaling depth is a key route to stronger large language models, but Pre-LN architectures often suffer from depth-wise information dilution, limiting the effective use of deep layers.
While cross-layer connectivity has proven effective at mitigating this problem, existing designs usually introduce additional computation or memory access.
We revisit cross-layer KV sharing as an efficient form of such connectivity.
Beyond cache compression, each KV reuse edge creates a direct gradient path from deep layers to source KV projections, turning KV sharing into an implicit structural regularizer for KV representation geometry.
We provide a systematic analysis of this mechanism, showing that proper sharing topologies sharpen key retrieval directions while enriching the value content subspace, whereas existing adjacent or global-anchor designs do not fully exploit this effect.
Motivated by this, we propose DepthGraft, a hierarchical KV-sharing architecture that reconstructs deep-layer KV from stride-aligned shallow–middle source pairs. DepthGraft distributes non-local gradient flow across source layers, while preserving prefill early completion and reducing KV cache by roughly one third.
Experiments on dense and MoE models from 0.5B to 16B parameters show consistent downstream improvements under both GQA and MLA.
Notably, on the 16B MoE model, DepthGraft achieves a 7.0% absolute improvement on MMLU-Pro, along with gains of 6.6% on CMMLU and 6.4% on C3.
These results validate the potential of DepthGraft as a scalable design for large-scale pretraining.

Right two: DepthGraft reduces prefill time (TTFT) and KV cache usage by approximately one-third, while incurring almost no loss in decoding speed. 1 Introduction Scaling depth has been a primary lever for improving Transformer-based large language models (LLMs) [ 42 , 32 , 21 ] .
In modern Pre-Layer-Normalization Transformers [ 40 ] , however, adding layers does not always yield the expected improvements [ 11 , 22 ] .
The residual stream [ 12 ] aggregates all preceding computations through a single hidden-state trajectory with fixed additive updates; as the stack grows deeper, features formed in early layers can be progressively diluted and become difficult for later layers to retrieve.
This depth-wise information dilution limits the effective use of additional layers, making depth scaling less efficient than its representational capacity would suggest [ 30 , 9 ] . Recent work has validated cross-layer connectivity as an effective remedy for information dilution along depth.
Instead of forcing all depth history through the residual stream, recent designs allow deep layers to directly read earlier representations: Attention Residuals [ 33 ] reweights preceding hidden states to replace fixed residual accumulation, while Mixture-of-Depths Attention (MoDA) [ 48 ] augments attention with key–value pairs from previous layers.
Such connections give deep layers direct access to earlier features and create shorter gradient paths to shallow layers.
However, dense cross-layer access also introduces additional computation, memory traffic, and system complexity, requiring careful performance–efficiency trade-offs and sophisticated systems-level engineering. We note that cross-layer KV sharing can also be viewed as a form of such connectivity:
it provides deep layers with a channel that bypasses information dilution in the residual stream by granting direct access to shallow-layer memory representations,
while gradients flow from deep layers back through the shared KV to shallow layers,
supplying shallow-layer KV with optimization signals driven by deep-layer task demands—thus holding the potential to improve depth utilization.
Although cross-layer KV sharing has been explored in prior work [ 4 , 49 , 31 ] , existing methods predominantly approach it from the perspective of cache compression and inference acceleration.
No prior work has systematically studied the cross-layer gradient dynamics of KV sharing, especially how such connections reshape KV representation geometry during pretraining and how the sharing topology determines this effect. In this paper, we propose that KV sharing is not merely a cache-saving mechanism, but also a form of cross-layer gradient regularization .
When a source layer’s KV is reused by a distant deep layer, the source KV receives training signals from multiple query spaces.
This reshapes KV representation geometry in an asymmetric way: keys are encouraged to concentrate around shared discriminative retrieval directions, while values are pushed to cover a richer content subspace.
With a proper sharing topology, this specialization sharpens memory retrieval while increasing the expressive capacity of the value content channel, yielding a useful structural bias for training.
We use this lens to revisit existing KV-sharing methods and find that their topologies expose complementary limitations.
Adjacent-layer sharing provides too little query-space separation, whereas global-anchor sharing concentrates heterogeneous deep-layer gradients on a few sources, preventing either design from fully exploiting the regularization effect. Motivated by this analysis, we propose DepthGraft , a simple hierarchical KV-sharing design derived from the cross-layer regularization view.
DepthGraft partitions the network into shallow, middle, and deep stages.
The shallow and middle stages compute KV normally, while each deep layer reconstructs its KV from a stride-aligned shallow–middle source pair using learnable channel-wise coefficients.
This construction creates non-local source–reuse connections with sufficient query-space separation, distributes cross-layer gradients across many source layers rather than a few global anchors, and gives each deep layer access to memory bases at two abstraction levels.
As a result, DepthGraft preserves the efficiency benefits of KV sharing, including prefill early completion and KV-cache reduction, while turning the memory pathway into an effective regularized cross-layer connection.
Training-dynamics analyses further support our theory, showing that DepthGraft reshapes KV representation geometry as predicted, and experiments across dense and MoE models demonstrate consistent performance gains at multiple scales. Our main contributions are as follows. 1). KV sharing as cross-layer gradient regularization. We identify KV sharing as a form of cross-layer connectivity and provide a systematic analysis of how its gradient paths reshape KV geometry.
Our analysis shows that proper KV sharing sharpens key retrieval directions while enriching the value content subspace, and that this effect is governed by the sharing topology. 2). Hierarchical cross-layer KV reconstruction. We propose DepthGraft, which uses a hierarchical stage layout to reconstruct deep-layer KV from stride-aligned shallow–middle source pairs.
This yields a distributed non-local connectivity pattern with two abstraction-level memory bases, avoiding the concentrated-anchor structure of prior KV-sharing methods. 3). Efficient and scalable implementation. DepthGraft removes independent KV materialization from the deep stage, reducing KV cache by roughly 1 3 \frac{1}{3} and allowing KV construction to finish before the final stage during prefill.
Its stride-aligned source assignment is engineering-friendly and naturally compatible with PP/VPP schedules, making the design suitable for large-scale distributed training. 4). Consistent empirical gains. We validate DepthGraft on both dense and MoE models, covering model scales from 0.5B to 16B total parameters.
Across scales and attention variants, DepthGraft consistently improves downstream performance while preserving inference efficiency. 2 Related Work Cross-layer connectivity. A broad line of work enriches depth-wise information flow by giving each layer direct access to earlier representations [ 15 , 17 , 18 ] .
DenseNet [ 15 ] concatenates all preceding feature maps, establishing a canonical dense cross-layer design.
In Transformers, DenseFormer [ 23 ] and ANCRe [ 45 ] introduce learned layer-wise connection weights, while Value Residual Learning [ 47 ] adds targeted residual access to earlier value states.
Attention Residuals [ 33 ] replaces fixed residual accumulation with learned reweighting over preceding hidden states, and MoDA [ 48 ] augments sequence attention with key–value memories from previous layers.
Together, these works show that cross-layer connectivity is an effective mechanism for improving deep Transformer architectures [ 44 , 25 ] . KV sharing for efficient inference. KV cache is a major memory bottleneck during autoregressive inference, motivating many methods that reduce cache storage through KV sharing [ 38 , 43 , 37 ] .
Cross-layer KV sharing is an important direction in this line.
CLA [ 4 ] and MLKV [ 49 ] reuse KV within adjacent layer groups, reducing cache but often incurring performance degradation when the reuse constraint becomes too strong.
YOCO [ 31 ] shares a global KV memory for all deeper layers, enabling prefill early completion and strong cache reduction.
FusedKV [ 20 ] further reconstructs deep-layer KV from two global anchors, providing more flexibility than direct global reuse.
Cross-layer KV sharing is becoming an increasingly important architectural choice for large-scale LLM pretraining, as illustrated by its adoption in DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash [ 41 ] and HySparse2 [ 35 ] . Positioning. DepthGraft systematically analyzes how cross-layer KV sharing alters training dynamics, showing that the induced cross-layer gradients can regularize KV representation geometry.
Guided by this analysis, DepthGraft uses stride-aligned hierarchical reconstruction to improve KV representations and model quality while preserving the KV-cache savings of sharing-based inference. 3 Method 3.1 Preliminaries Attention and KV cache. Cross-layer KV sharing as memory routing. KV sharing replaces some of these independent KV tensors with KV tensors produced at other layers.

Adjacent-layer sharing. CLA [ 4 ] and MLKV [ 49 ] partition consecutive layers into groups of size N N .

(3) Because source layers are interleaved throughout depth, this pattern reduces the number of cached KV tensors but does not make the cache complete before the final layer group is processed. Forward reuse induces backward connectivity. Although KV sharing is usually introduced as a cache-saving mechanism, it also changes the training dynamics.
If layer ℓ \ell uses KV derived from a source layer s s , gradients from layer ℓ \ell backpropagate through the reused tensors to the source projections 𝐖 s K \mathbf{W}_{s}^{K} and 𝐖 s V \mathbf{W}_{s}^{V} .
Thus every memory-routing edge is also a direct cross-layer gradient path.

In CLA [ 4 ] and MLKV [ 49 ] , consecutive layers are grouped (typically groups of 2–3), and all layers within a group reuse the KV computed by the first layer.

Only the anchor layers receive direct cross-layer KV gradients from deep layers; the remaining shallow and middle layers may still receive gradients through the residual stream, but they do not participate in the direct KV-sharing path analyzed in Sec. 3.2 .
Fig. 5 in the appendix illustrates this effect for FusedKV: the anchor layer exhibits elevated V effective rank, whereas nearby non-anchor layers show no clear improvement over the baseline. 3.4 DepthGraft: Hierarchical Cross-Layer KV Reconstruction By viewing KV-sharing designs through the lens of cross-layer gradients as structural regularization, the analysis above motivates the following design principles: 1. Sufficient cross-layer distance. Source and reuse layers must be separated by enough depth that their query spaces meaningfully diverge, enabling the gradient interference that drives K/V specialization. Adjacent-layer sharing fails this condition. 2. Distributed gradient flow with full-layer participation. Each source layer should receive gradients from only a small number of reuse layers to avoid gradient congestion, and most layers should participate in the cross-layer connectivity graph so that the regularization benefit is not confined to a few anchors. We instantiate these requirements under an efficiency constraint: all source KV tensors should be produced before the reconstructed layers, preserving prefill early completion.
Based on this, we propose DepthGraft, which introduces a hierarchical stage layout that partitions the L L -layer Transformer into shallow, middle, and deep stages, each containing roughly L / 3 L/3 layers.
The shallow and middle stages compute KV normally, while the deep stage reconstructs its KV from earlier stages.
Rather than using global anchors, DepthGraft adopts stride-aligned source assignment , treating deep-layer memory as a locally reconstructed trajectory over depth.
For each deep layer ℓ \ell , DepthGraft selects one shallow source and one middle source at the same relative depth position: s ⁡ ( ℓ ) = ℓ − 2 ​ L 3 , m ⁡ ( ℓ ) = ℓ − L 3 . s(\ell)=\ell-\tfrac{2L}{3},\qquad m(\ell)=\ell-\tfrac{L}{3}. (10) This gives each deep layer two memory bases at different abstraction levels: shallow memory preserves lower-level lexical and token-local patterns, while middle-layer memory carries more abstract semantic states [ 2 , 46 , 26 ] .
It then reconstructs KV from the corresponding shallow–middle source pair with learnable per-dimension coefficients: 𝐊 ℓ = 𝐚 ℓ , 1 ⊙ 𝐊 s ⁡ ( ℓ ) + 𝐚 ℓ , 2 ⊙ 𝐊 m ⁡ ( ℓ ) , 𝐕 ℓ = 𝐛 ℓ , 1 ⊙ 𝐕 s ⁡ ( ℓ ) + 𝐛 ℓ , 2 ⊙ 𝐕 m ⁡ ( ℓ ) , \mathbf{K}_{\ell}=\mathbf{a}_{\ell,1}\odot\mathbf{K}_{s(\ell)}+\mathbf{a}_{\ell,2}\odot\mathbf{K}_{m(\ell)},\qquad\mathbf{V}_{\ell}=\mathbf{b}_{\ell,1}\odot\mathbf{V}_{s(\ell)}+\mathbf{b}_{\ell,2}\odot\mathbf{V}_{m(\ell)}, (11) where 𝐚 ℓ , i , 𝐛 ℓ , i \mathbf{a}_{\ell,i},\mathbf{b}_{\ell,i} are channel-wise coefficients for each KV group, providing higher flexibility and expressiveness than single-source reuse.
For keys, the coefficients are tied across RoPE rotation pairs, so rotary position encoding and reconstruction commute, which preserves the standard KV-cache storage logic.
The stride-aligned correspondence lets each deep layer receive shallow and middle memory from the same relative depth position, preserving the ordering of representation evolution across depth. Because the deep stage does not materialize independent KV, DepthGraft reduces the KV cache and prefill cost by approximately 1 / 3 1/3 .

The stride-aligned source mapping is simple to implement and naturally compatible with pipeline parallelism (PP) and virtual pipeline parallelism (VPP), avoiding additional cross-stage KV communication under an appropriate placement.
Appendix F.3 provides the corresponding PP/VPP details. (3) Regularized KV geometry. The design satisfies the two principles above: non-local source–target pairs provide query-space separation, and stride alignment distributes cross-layer gradients across source layers.

The effective-rank advantage in Fig. 2 is observed across nearly all layers, rather than being confined to either source or reuse layers. 4 Experiments We evaluate DepthGraft on both dense and sparse MoE Transformers, covering multiple model scales and attention variants.

We further compare DepthGraft with existing KV-sharing methods under the same training and evaluation protocol. Cross-layer gradients are the key source of the gain. Variants (2)–(4) all use the same three-stage layout, allowing us to isolate the effect of the cross-layer gradient path. Shallow-Copy directly reuses the stride-aligned shallow source from S 1 \text{S}_{1} , while Shallow-Copy (detach) stops gradients through the reused KV.
The detached variant does not degrade relative to the GQA baseline (42.86 vs. 42.38 Avg.), indicating redundancy in deep-layer KV.
Once the backward path is enabled, however, the average score increases markedly to 45.30.
This controlled comparison supports our central motivation: KV sharing is useful not only as a forward memory reuse mechanism, but also because cross-layer gradients regularize earlier KV representations through deep-layer training signals. Hierarchical reconstruction is more effective than single-source copy. Variants (3)–(5) compare single-source copy with hierarchical reconstruction under active cross-layer gradients. Shallow-Copy in Variant (3) only connects S 1 \text{S}_{1} to S 3 \text{S}_{3} , leaving the middle stage outside the sharing graph; the 2-stage variant (5) uses full one-to-one copy, but each reuse layer still receives only a single memory source.
DepthGraft instead reconstructs each deep-layer KV from a stride-aligned S 1 \text{S}_{1} – S 2 \text{S}_{2} source pair, improving the average score over both single-source alternatives (47.56 vs. 45.30 and 43.58).
The gains on representative benchmarks such as M-Pro, C3, C-Eval, and GSM8K suggest that the benefit comes from combining broad source participation with a more flexible hierarchical memory basis, rather than from cross-layer gradient flow alone. The three-stage layout gives the best overall trade-off. Variants (5)–(8) compare different stage partitions under full gradient flow.
The reconstructed layers take stride-aligned sources and obtain their KV by either direct copy or hierarchical reconstruction.
The 2-stage variant is too restrictive, while the 4-stage variants test whether using more source stages or more reuse stages is beneficial.
The results suggest that reconstruction sources are not simply “the more the better”: reconstructing the last stage from three source stages underperforms the proposed two-source DepthGraft layout, and the 4-stage two-source variant also remains below the three-stage design.
Thus, a three-stage partition provides a stronger operating point, balancing two complementary source abstraction levels, distributed gradient flow, and the intended final-stage cache-saving region. Adjacent sharing substantially underperforms the baseline, supporting the view that reusing nearby KV reduces the model’s effective depth.
Global-anchor sharing performs better than adjacent sharing and can improve over the baseline, but shows limited gains because the gradient regularization is concentrated on a small set of anchors.

long-context capabilities without storing independent KV at every layer. 5 Conclusion and Future Work We presented DepthGraft, a hierarchical cross-layer KV reconstruction method that turns KV sharing from a cache-saving mechanism into a regularized memory pathway.
Our analysis shows that KV reuse creates direct cross-layer gradient paths that reshape KV representation geometry, and that the effectiveness of this regularization depends on the topology of the sharing graph.
Motivated by this view, DepthGraft reconstructs deep-layer KV from stride-aligned shallow–middle source pairs, preserving the efficiency benefits of KV sharing while distributing non-local gradient flow across source layers.
Experiments on dense and MoE models from 0.5B to 16B show consistent downstream gains, substantial reductions in KV cache and prefill cost, and negligible overhead during decoding.
Future work can develop a more rigorous theoretical understanding of how cross-layer KV sharing improves representation geometry, explore more effective sharing topologies, and further scale this direction to larger pretraining regimes. The shallow and middle stages compute and cache KV normally, while deep-stage KV is reconstructed from stride-aligned shallow–middle source pairs. The deep stage therefore does not materialize independent KV cache, while gradients from deep-layer attention flow back to the source KV projections through the reconstruction paths. RoPE-pair tying for K coefficients. Rotary position embeddings apply a block-diagonal rotation to each two-dimensional channel pair of the query and key vectors.
For the NeoX-style layout used in our implementation, dimensions j j and j + d / 2 j+d/2 form one RoPE pair.
If a channel-wise coefficient assigns different values to the two dimensions in the same pair, then applying RoPE before and after reconstruction would no longer be equivalent.
Consistent with FusedKV [ 20 ] , we therefore tie the K reconstruction coefficients within each RoPE pair.
Concretely, for each source coefficient 𝐚 ℓ , i \mathbf{a}_{\ell,i} , we store a half-sized tensor 𝐚 ℓ , i ∈ ℝ H k × d / 2 \mathbf{a}_{\ell,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{H_{k}\times d/2} and expand it to 𝐚 ~ ℓ , i ∈ ℝ H k × d \tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{\ell,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{H_{k}\times d} by 𝐚 ~ ℓ , i [ h , j ] = 𝐚 ~ ℓ , i [ h , j + d / 2 ] = 𝐚 ℓ , i [ h , j ] , j = 1 , … , d / 2 . \tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{\ell,i}[h,j]=\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{\ell,i}[h,j+d/2]=\mathbf{a}_{\ell,i}[h,j],\qquad j=1,\ldots,d/2. (12) Let 𝐑 p \mathbf{R}_{p} denote the RoPE rotation matrix at position p p .
Because each expanded coefficient is constant within every rotation pair, it commutes with the RoPE rotation: 𝐑 p ​ ( 𝐚 ~ ℓ , i ⊙ 𝐊 ) = 𝐚 ~ ℓ , i ⊙ 𝐑 p ​ ( 𝐊 ) . \mathbf{R}_{p}(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{\ell,i}\odot\mathbf{K})=\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{\ell,i}\odot\mathbf{R}_{p}(\mathbf{K}). (13) Therefore, for the reconstructed key, 𝐑 p ​ ( 𝐚 ~ ℓ , 1 ⊙ 𝐊 s ⁡ ( ℓ ) + 𝐚 ~ ℓ , 2 ⊙ 𝐊 m ⁡ ( ℓ ) ) \displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{p}\!\left(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{\ell,1}\odot\mathbf{K}_{s(\ell)}+\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{\ell,2}\odot\mathbf{K}_{m(\ell)}\right) = 𝐚 ~ ℓ , 1 ⊙ 𝐑 p ​ ( 𝐊 s ⁡ ( ℓ ) ) + 𝐚 ~ ℓ , 2 ⊙ 𝐑 p ​ ( 𝐊 m ⁡ ( ℓ ) ) . \displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{\ell,1}\odot\mathbf{R}_{p}(\mathbf{K}_{s(\ell)})+\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{\ell,2}\odot\mathbf{R}_{p}(\mathbf{K}_{m(\ell)}). Value vectors do not use RoPE, so the V coefficients 𝐛 ℓ , i \mathbf{b}_{\ell,i} are stored at full channel dimension and do not require pair tying. Coefficient initialization. Appendix C Theoretical Analysis A conditional spectral view of cross-layer KV regularization. We provide a local spectral analysis to support the effective-rank diagnostic in Eq. 5 .
For a centered key or value activation matrix 𝐌 \mathbf{M} , denote its feature covariance by 𝐂 𝐌 = 𝐌 ⊤ ​ 𝐌 / N \mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{M}}=\mathbf{M}^{\top}\mathbf{M}/N .
The effective rank is high when the covariance spectrum is close to uniform, and low when activation energy is concentrated in a few feature directions.
Our goal is not to prove that cross-layer KV sharing always changes effective rank in a fixed direction.
Instead, we show that the observed K/V effective-rank trends follow under a natural spectral-alignment condition on the cross-layer update covariance. Lemma 1 (spectral injection and effective rank). Consider a local update of the activation matrix 𝐌 + = 𝐌 + η ​ 𝚫 , \mathbf{M}^{+}=\mathbf{M}+\eta\boldsymbol{\Delta}, (15) where 𝚫 \boldsymbol{\Delta} is the induced activation-space update and η \eta is the learning rate.

Proposition 1 (asymmetric effective-rank pressure from cross-layer KV sharing). Suppose that (i) cross-layer key gradients share a low-dimensional task-relevant retrieval subspace 𝒰 K \mathcal{U}_{K} aligned with the leading eigenspace of 𝐂 𝐊 s \mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{K}_{s}} , and their cross-covariance is coherent inside 𝒰 K \mathcal{U}_{K} but weak outside it; and (ii) value readout gradients from the source and reuse layers contain complementary content directions with non-negligible projection onto trailing or underused eigendirections of 𝐂 𝐕 s \mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{V}_{s}} .
Then, under the spectral approximation in Eqs. 18 – 19 , cross-layer KV sharing locally induces erank ⁡ ( 𝐂 𝐊 s + ) < erank ⁡ ( 𝐂 𝐊 s ) , erank ⁡ ( 𝐂 𝐕 s + ) > erank ⁡ ( 𝐂 𝐕 s ) . \operatorname{erank}(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{K}_{s}^{+}})<\operatorname{erank}(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{K}_{s}}),\qquad\operatorname{erank}(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{V}_{s}^{+}})>\operatorname{erank}(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{V}_{s}}). (28) Proof. For keys, Eq. 26 shows that cross-layer sharing adds covariance terms coupling 𝐆 s ⊤ ​ 𝐐 s \mathbf{G}_{s}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{s} and 𝐆 ℓ ⊤ ​ 𝐐 ℓ \mathbf{G}_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{\ell} .

By Lemma 1, the normalized key covariance spectrum becomes more concentrated, yielding erank ⁡ ( 𝐂 𝐊 s + ) < erank ⁡ ( 𝐂 𝐊 s ) . \operatorname{erank}(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{K}_{s}^{+}})<\operatorname{erank}(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{K}_{s}}). (30) For values, Eq. 27 shows that the reuse layer routes an additional readout gradient 𝐏 ℓ ⊤ ​ 𝐄 ℓ \mathbf{P}_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{E}_{\ell} into the same source value matrix.
Unlike keys, values are read out linearly once the attention pattern is fixed; distinct attention patterns can therefore provide complementary content gradients rather than competing through the softmax logits.
By assumption, these additional content gradients have non-negligible projection onto underused value directions, so μ i V λ i V < μ j V λ j V , i ∈ ℐ lead ​ ( 𝐕 s ) , j ∈ ℐ trail ​ ( 𝐕 s ) . \frac{\mu_{i}^{V}}{\lambda_{i}^{V}}<\frac{\mu_{j}^{V}}{\lambda_{j}^{V}},\qquad i\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{lead}}(\mathbf{V}_{s}),\;j\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{trail}}(\mathbf{V}_{s}). (32) Combining the two parts proves Eq. 28 . □ \square Interpretation and scope. The proposition is conditional: it does not claim that effective rank must change monotonically under arbitrary training dynamics.
It states that the K/V effective-rank trends follow when the induced update covariance is organized as suggested by the attention mechanism: keys receive coherent reinforcement in shared retrieval directions, while values receive complementary content gradients.
The topology of the sharing graph affects whether these assumptions are plausible.
Adjacent sharing may make 𝐐 s ≈ 𝐐 ℓ \mathbf{Q}_{s}\approx\mathbf{Q}_{\ell} and 𝐏 s ≈ 𝐏 ℓ \mathbf{P}_{s}\approx\mathbf{P}_{\ell} , yielding mostly redundant gradients.
Global-anchor sharing can overload a few sources with many heterogeneous objectives.
Stride-aligned non-local sharing is more likely to satisfy the assumptions because it provides sufficient cross-layer distance while distributing the gradient load across source layers. Appendix E Additional Training Dynamics and Representation Analysis E.1 Effective Rank Dynamics: Per-Layer Analysis Sec. 3.3 argues that global-anchor sharing, such as FusedKV, induces structurally uneven regularization: only the designated anchor layers participate in cross-layer gradient flow, while nearby non-anchor layers receive no direct gradient benefit from KV reuse.
We provide per-layer empirical evidence for this effect by tracking the value effective rank erank ⁡ ( 𝐕 ℓ ) \operatorname{erank}(\mathbf{V}_{\ell}) , defined in Eq. 5 , at individual layers throughout training. Figure 5 shows V effective-rank trajectories for layers 0–3 under the 9B GQA baseline, FusedKV, and DepthGraft.
For FusedKV, only layer 0, the bottom anchor, exhibits a clear increase above the baseline.
Layers 1–3 do not serve as anchors and therefore receive no direct cross-layer gradient through reused KV tensors; correspondingly, their V effective ranks show no clear improvement over the baseline.
This supports the topology analysis in Sec. 3.3 : global-anchor sharing confines the representation benefit to a small set of source layers. In contrast, DepthGraft elevates V effective rank across the shown source layers.
Because its stride-aligned source assignment distributes reuse edges across the shallow and middle stages, the gradient-mediated regularization described in Sec. 3.2 is spread over many source layers rather than concentrated on a few anchors.
The per-layer effective-rank measurements therefore provide additional evidence that distributed cross-layer connectivity is important for turning KV sharing into an effective representation regularizer. E.2 Attention Sink Analysis A common pathology in deep Transformers is the attention sink phenomenon, where a disproportionate amount of attention mass is assigned to the first token regardless of its semantic relevance [ 39 ] .
In our setting, this behavior provides an additional diagnostic of key geometry: if a layer develops extreme key directions, the softmax distribution can become dominated by a small number of sink positions. DepthGraft mitigates this tendency through cross-layer gradient regularization.
Each shallow or middle source serves its own attention layer while also being reused by a stride-aligned deep layer, so its KV projections receive optimization signals from multiple query spaces.
This structure can regularize the source key geometry and make it less likely for a layer to develop sink-prone key directions that dominate the softmax.
This is consistent with the analysis in Sec. 3.2 , where useful cross-layer KV sharing makes keys form a more compact retrieval geometry. We verify this empirically by tracking the average attention score assigned to the first token throughout training.
Figure 6 reports this metric for both 9B GQA and 9B MLA models.
In both cases, DepthGraft consistently lowers first-token attention concentration relative to the baseline, indicating reduced attention-sink behavior. (a) 9B GQA model. (b) 9B MLA model. E.3 Training Loss Curves Figure 7 compares the training loss curves of DepthGraft and the corresponding baselines across representative dense and MoE settings.
DepthGraft consistently achieves lower training loss across model scales and attention variants, indicating that the proposed cross-layer KV reconstruction does not introduce optimization instability.
The magnitude of the loss improvement, however, is moderate: the relative loss reduction is roughly 0.5 % 0.5\% . This behavior is consistent with the role of DepthGraft as a structural regularizer.
Unlike cross-layer designs whose primary effect is to substantially alter the residual computation pathway, DepthGraft constrains only the KV memory pathway and even removes independent deep-layer KV projections.
Consequently, its training-loss advantage is not as large as that reported for some broader cross-layer connectivity mechanisms, but it yields much larger downstream gains than the loss difference alone would suggest.
This supports our interpretation that DepthGraft improves the geometry and generalization of KV representations, rather than merely optimizing the pretraining loss more aggressively. (a) 1.5B Dense. Appendix F Efficiency and System Analysis F.1 KV Cache and Prefill Cost DepthGraft partitions the network into shallow, middle, and deep stages of approximately equal depth.
The shallow and middle stages materialize KV normally, while the deep stage reconstructs its KV from stride-aligned shallow–middle sources.
Therefore, no independent KV cache needs to be stored for the deep stage.
For an L L -layer model with identical KV dimensionality across layers, the baseline stores KV for all L L layers, whereas DepthGraft stores KV only for roughly 2 ​ L / 3 2L/3 source layers: KVCache D ​ e ​ p ​ t ​ h ​ G ​ r ​ a ​ f ​ t KVCache baseline ≈ 2 ​ L / 3 L = 2 3 . \frac{\mathrm{KVCache}_{DepthGraft{}}}{\mathrm{KVCache}_{\mathrm{baseline}}}\approx\frac{2L/3}{L}=\frac{2}{3}. (33) This gives an approximately one-third reduction in KV-cache memory during inference. The same structure also enables prefill early completion.
In standard autoregressive inference, the prefill phase processes the full prompt through all layers to produce the KV cache and the hidden state needed to generate the first response token.
In DepthGraft, the purpose of prefill with respect to KV construction is completed once the shallow and middle stages have processed the full prompt, because all KV tensors needed by future decoding steps have already been materialized.
The deep stage does not need to process the entire prompt to create additional KV cache.
Instead, after the shallow and middle KV caches are available, we only forward the final prompt token through the deep stage, using the stored shallow–middle KV caches to compute attention and produce the first response token. Thus, the prefill saving comes from avoiding full-sequence computation in the deep stage, while retaining the necessary computation for the last prompt token that initializes decoding.
As context length and model size increase, the full-prompt prefill computation becomes increasingly dominant, and the cost of forwarding only the final token through the deep stage becomes a smaller fraction of total prefill cost.
This explains the trend in Table 3 : the measured TTFT reduction grows at longer context lengths and approaches the theoretical one-third saving. F.2 Training FLOPs and Parameter Count DepthGraft does not introduce additional training FLOPs in the way that many explicit cross-layer connectivity mechanisms do.
The shallow and middle stages are computed normally, and the deep-stage KV reconstruction only requires element-wise linear combinations of two cached source tensors.
This mixing cost is negligible compared with the matrix multiplications in attention and FFN layers.
Moreover, because the deep stage does not require independent K/V projections, an implementation can skip the corresponding K/V projection computation in reused layers, slightly reducing training FLOPs.
In our large-scale runs, the observed training throughput is essentially identical to the baseline. We also reduce the activated parameter count by removing the K and V projection weights from reused layers.

Thus, DepthGraft obtains its downstream gains while slightly reducing, rather than increasing, the active model capacity. F.3 Parallel Training Compatibility Pipeline parallelism (PP) is essential for training large Transformers whose parameters and activations cannot fit on a single device.
PP partitions the model along depth and assigns different layer blocks to different physical pipeline ranks.
Virtual pipeline parallelism (VPP) further splits each physical stage into smaller virtual chunks, typically at layer granularity, and interleaves their execution to reduce pipeline bubbles and improve hardware utilization.
For cross-layer KV sharing, the main systems concern is whether a reuse layer needs to fetch source KV tensors from a different PP rank; such accesses would introduce additional cross-stage communication and complicate activation lifetime management. DepthGraft is designed to avoid this issue.
Because each deep layer reuses a stride-aligned shallow–middle source pair, the source and reuse layers can be co-located on the same physical PP rank under an appropriate VPP partition.
Table 12 gives a balanced example for a 36-layer model with PP size 4.
Using zero-based layer indices, the network is divided into three 12-layer stages: layers 0–11, 12–23, and 24–35.
Each deep layer i + 24 i+24 reconstructs KV from the stride-aligned pair ( i , i + 12 ) (i,i+12) , and each triple ( i , i + 12 , i + 24 ) (i,i+12,i+24) is placed on the same physical PP rank.
