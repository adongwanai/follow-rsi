Building on this insight, we introduce Market-Conditioned JEPA (MC-JEPA), a joint-embedding predictive architecture for financial time series. A synthetic factor-model study with known parameters shows that conditioning improves the recovery of both market exposure and the asset-specific component. 1 Introduction Portfolio construction and risk management require estimates of an asset’s return, volatility, liquidity, and market exposure, which measures how an asset responds to market-wide movements. Self-supervised learning offers a way to learn a unified representation that supports these diverse tasks. Recent work has developed a large number of time-series encoders and foundation models ( Nie et al., 2023 ; Yue et al., 2022 ; Goswami et al., 2024 ; Das et al., 2024 ; Ansari et al., 2025 ) , including models pretrained specifically on financial data ( Shi et al., 2025 ) . However, these methods largely learn from individual time series, overlooking the common-factor structure of financial markets. † † footnotetext: Code is available at https://github.com/OpenFinMind/MC-JEPA . Under this structure, much of an asset’s variation is driven by market-wide shocks ( Sharpe, 1964 ; Fama and French, 1993 ) , which are difficult to predict from an asset’s own history. Therefore, a per-asset prediction objective treats the unpredictable component of these shocks as irreducible noise. This has two costs. First, the optimal predictor need only recover the asset’s conditional mean, without identifying how the asset responds to market movements. This provides limited supervision for market exposure. Second, the asset-specific signal that the predictor learns is observed against this market noise. Existing approaches that explicitly model cross-asset relationships ( Xu et al., 2021 ; Li et al., 2024 ; Gu et al., 2021 ) are largely designed for supervised return prediction and often require cross-sectional information at inference. FactorVAE ( Duan et al., 2022 ) is closely related to our approach, as it uses future cross-sectional returns to infer latent factors during training. However, it is a supervised return prediction model built on engineered daily features, and its factor predictor still aggregates information across stocks at inference. In contrast, MC-JEPA learns self-supervised representations by conditioning on a market factor constructed from other assets’ future target embeddings. The resulting encoder operates independently on each asset at inference. Our key observation is that unpredictable market movements are observable during training through the realized futures of other assets. Conditioning prediction on these movements turns them from noise into supervision, shifting the learning objective from forecasting market shocks to capturing how each asset responds to them. We formalize this in a latent factor model, showing that market-conditioned prediction makes exposure necessary for optimal prediction and removes the market component from the noise against which the asset-specific signal is learned. Building on this observation, we introduce Market-Conditioned JEPA (MC-JEPA) , a joint-embedding predictive architecture ( Assran et al., 2023 ) for financial time series. A per-asset encoder embeds each asset’s recent history to a representation, and a predictor estimates its future target embeddings. During training, we construct a leave-one-out market condition by aggregating the future target embeddings of other assets in the same panel. We then introduce a factor-structured predictor that incorporates this condition through loadings derived from the asset’s own representation, following the structure of a single-index factor model ( Sharpe, 1964 ) . The market condition therefore cannot explain the target by itself. It helps only to the extent that the representation encodes the asset’s exposure. The market condition is privileged information ( Duan et al., 2022 ) available only during training. At inference, the encoder is a standard per-asset model that requires no cross-sectional information. We train MC-JEPA on minute-level candlesticks of the full cross-section of Chinese A-share stocks from 2015 to 2022 and evaluate the frozen representations with ridge probes on a held-out 2023–2025 period. It improves on per-asset JEPA across nine targets spanning returns, market exposure, risk, and liquidity, with the largest and most significant improvements in exposure, risk, and liquidity. It achieves the highest RankIC on all liquidity targets and on returns from two hours to five days, outperforming time-series foundation models and self-supervised baselines. In a historical trading backtest, it achieves the highest excess return and Sharpe ratio and the lowest maximum drawdown. Ablations show that the gains in return and exposure prediction depend on conditioning on the realized market movement over the same target period. Neither panel training alone nor conditioning on a different date reproduces these gains. The factor loadings of the predictor are also interpretable. Rescaled by the price volatility that per-asset normalization removes, they rank stocks by market beta nearly as well as a supervised probe, without any beta labels. A synthetic factor-model study with known parameters further shows that conditioning improves the recovery of both the true market exposure and the asset-specific component. Our contributions are as follows: • We show under a latent factor model that market conditioning makes exposure necessary for optimal prediction and reduces noise in learning asset-specific signals. • We introduce MC-JEPA, which uses market conditioning during training to learn standalone per-asset representations. • We demonstrate improvements across financial prediction tasks and a trading backtest, and show that the predictor’s factor loadings encode market exposure without labels. 2 Related Work Figure 1 : MC-JEPA. During training, each asset’s future embedding is predicted both unconditionally and conditioned on a market factor derived from the future target embeddings of other assets. The factor enters through loadings computed from the asset’s own representation. At inference, only the per-asset context encoder f θ f_{\theta} is retained. Self-supervised learning for time series. Self-supervised learning for time series has three main directions. Contrastive methods learn representations by aligning augmented views or temporally related windows of a series ( van den Oord et al., 2019 ; Tonekaboni et al., 2021 ; Ennadir et al., 2025 ; Woo et al., 2022 ; Liu and Chen, 2024 ) . Masked-reconstruction methods recover hidden input patches from their surrounding context ( Nie et al., 2023 ; Dong et al., 2023 ; Goswami et al., 2024 ) . More recently, large-scale pretraining has produced foundation models that forecast across diverse time-series corpora ( Das et al., 2024 ; Rasul et al., 2024 ; Liu et al., 2025 ; Ansari et al., 2025 ; He et al., 2025 ; HE et al., 2026 ) , as well as general-purpose encoders that learn transferable representations ( Lin et al., 2024 ; Fu and Hu, 2025 ) . Joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPA) ( Assran et al., 2023 ) instead predict representations produced by a slowly updated target encoder, avoiding the need to reconstruct unpredictable details in input space. JEPA objectives have been adapted to time series for control ( Girgis et al., 2025 ) , in-context forecasting ( Verdenius et al., 2024 ) , general representation learning ( Ennadir et al., 2025 ) and anomaly prediction ( He et al., 2026 ) , and recently to equity modeling ( Luo et al., 2026 ; Merchant et al., 2026 ) . Finance-specific foundation models such as Kronos ( Shi et al., 2025 ) pretrain on large-scale candlestick data, yet recent evaluations find that both generic and finance-pretrained models give limited gains on financial returns ( Rahimikia et al., 2025 ; Alonso and Franklin, 2026 ) . A common limitation of these approaches is that they encode each asset independently and predict its future from its own history. However, in financial markets, much of an asset’s future variation is driven by market-wide shocks that cannot be inferred from its history alone. Consequently, such objectives provide little supervision for learning how an asset responds to these shocks. Our method addresses this limitation by retaining a per-asset JEPA encoder while conditioning its predictor on a market factor constructed from other assets’ future embeddings during training, which turns unpredictable market variation into a learning signal for market exposure. Cross-asset dependencies in financial markets. Asset pricing has long modeled returns through asset-specific exposures to common factors, from the CAPM ( Sharpe, 1964 ) to multi-factor models ( Fama and French, 1993 ) . Such commonality extends beyond returns to order flow and liquidity ( Hasbrouck and Seppi, 2001 ) . Two lines of work are particularly relevant to ours. First, factor-based neural models learn asset-specific exposures to shared factors. Conditional autoencoders ( Gu et al., 2021 ) model exposures as nonlinear functions of asset characteristics and combine them with factors estimated from contemporaneous realized returns. FactorVAE ( Duan et al., 2022 ) uses future cross-sectional returns as privileged information to infer latent factors during training. Both are supervised return-prediction models built on engineered daily features. In contrast, we bring the same factor exposure structure into self-supervised learning from raw time series. Asset-specific exposures are learned from each asset’s representation, and the market factor is constructed from other assets’ future target embeddings. Second, cross-asset neural models explicitly incorporate information from other assets through stock graphs and shared concepts ( Xu et al., 2021 ) , inter-stock attention with market gating ( Li et al., 2024 ) , spatiotemporal bootstrapping over correlated series ( Wang et al., 2023 ) , or correlation-aware adapters and group attention in foundation models ( Cheng et al., 2026 ; Ansari et al., 2025 ) . These approaches generally rely on cross-sectional context at inference, while channel-mixing architectures can be less robust to distribution shift than channel-independent ones ( Han et al., 2023 ) . Our method instead uses the market factor only to condition the predictor during training, leaving the deployed encoder fully per-asset and independent of cross-sectional inputs. 3 Method We first describe a per-asset JEPA baseline (section 3.1 ), then show that its objective treats unpredictable market variation as noise (section 3.2 ). Part (a) shows that unconditional prediction requires only the conditional mean u ¯ ​ ( x i ) \bar{u}(x_{i}) , not the exposure B i B_{i} separately. The unpredictable component B i ​ ( f h − m ) B_{i}(f_{h}-m) contributes to irreducible error without providing additional supervision for exposure. Although market factors may be partially predictable in practice, their unpredictable variation remains uninformative for learning exposure under unconditional prediction. Part (b) shows how conditioning changes this. This complements exposure learning by improving beta and risk prediction through market exposure and benefiting return and volume prediction through cleaner asset-specific representations. MC-JEPA thus incorporates cross-asset supervision during training while retaining the inference interface and cost of a standard per-asset encoder. 4 Experiments 4.1 Experimental Setup Datasets and Baselines. We use 1-minute OHLCV bars of Chinese A-share stocks from 2015 to 2025. All splits are chronological. Pretraining uses decision times before July 2022. For evaluation we freeze each encoder and embed one window per stock and trading day, ending at the target’s decision time. Daily targets are decided at the 15:00 close. Minute targets are decided at the 11:30 morning close. We fit ridge probes on 2019-01 to 2022-06, select the ridge penalty and the hyperparameters of MC-JEPA on 2022-07 to 2022-12, and report results on 2023-01 to 2025-12. We compare against three groups of baselines. (i) Finance-specific baselines. We use the Alpha158 daily factors ( Yang et al., 2020 ) . Like the encoders, it is read out with a ridge probe. (ii) Pretrained time-series foundation models. We use frozen Kronos ( Shi et al., 2025 ) , TimesFM-2.5 ( Das et al., 2024 ) , Chronos-2 ( Ansari et al., 2025 ) and MOMENT ( Goswami et al., 2024 ) . (iii) Self-supervised objectives. We compare objectives under the same encoder architecture, training data and compute budget. These include masked patch reconstruction (Masked recon.) ( Nie et al., 2023 ; Dong et al., 2023 ) , next-patch autoregression (Next-patch AR), TS2Vec ( Yue et al., 2022 ) , CPC ( van den Oord et al., 2019 ) , and per-asset JEPA (section 3.1 ). Ablations that isolate each component of our method are in section 4.3 . Each encoder is read out from the [CLS] state if its objective trains it (JEPA variants and CPC), and from the mean of its patch states otherwise. For every baseline we checked that this is the better of the two readouts. Every encoder we train is run with three seeds, and we report the mean and standard deviation. Evaluation metrics. We evaluate nine downstream targets spanning returns, market exposure, risk, and liquidity. These include future returns over horizons from 120 minutes to 20 days, forward 20-day market beta, realized volatility, relative volume, and the fraction of next-day volume traded at the open. Our primary metric is RankIC, computed as the Spearman correlation between predictions and realized targets across stocks on each test day, averaged over the test period. We further assess economic value through a historical long-only backtest using the Ret 20d predictions, reporting annualized excess return (AER), Sharpe ratio, information ratio (IR), and maximum drawdown (MDD). Appendix D provides detailed definitions of all targets and metrics. Implementation details. Inputs are 1-minute OHLCV bars with a context of L = 960 L=960 bars, split into 96 patches of p = 10 p=10 bars. The encoder is a 12-layer Transformer with width D = 256 D=256 (about 10M parameters). Targets are 30-bar blocks at horizons ℋ = { 1 , 60 , 240 } \mathcal{H}=\{1,60,240\} bars, spanning the next half hour to the next trading day. Each step samples 128 decision times, each with a condition pool of | ℳ t | = 32 |\mathcal{M}_{t}|=32 assets of which | 𝒮 t | = 8 |\mathcal{S}_{t}|=8 are scored. The pool is drawn uniformly at random from all assets around t t , and the scored assets are a uniform random subset of the pool. Appendix C lists all hyperparameters. The loadings of the factor-structured predictor can be read as an exposure estimate without beta labels. We project each stock’s loading onto the mean loading of its date, and multiply by the coefficient of variation of the stock’s close price over its context window, which is the scale removed by the per-asset normalization. Its RankIC with Beta 20d is 0.368, compared with 0.374 for a supervised ridge probe on the representation and 0.287 for price scale alone. Without the scale, the projection has a RankIC of − 0.06 -0.06 . This is because normalization hides each asset’s volatility, so the loadings capture its correlation with the market rather than its beta. Consistent with this, the projection predicts each stock’s realized correlation with the market over the next 20 days beyond its trailing four-day correlation and volatility, with a partial RankIC of 0.105. A detailed discussion is provided in Appendix F . 4.5 Synthetic Study Real data offers only one level of market predictability and an unknown factor structure. For the exposure B i B_{i} , linear-probe R 2 R^{2} increases from 0.51–0.68 with per-asset JEPA to 0.69–0.80 with MC-JEPA, with less variation across seeds. This second effect is the one relevant to return prediction on real data, where the probe ranks assets without access to the realized market shock. 5 Conclusion We introduced MC-JEPA, a self-supervised method that turns unpredictable market movements into supervision. Conditioning on the realized market factor makes market exposure necessary for optimal prediction and denoises the asset-specific signal. During training, a factor-structured predictor conditions on a leave-one-out market signal constructed from other assets’ future target embeddings, with loadings computed from the asset’s own representation. The condition is used only during training, leaving a per-asset encoder that requires no cross-sectional information at inference. On 11 years of minute-level Chinese A-share data, MC-JEPA improves over per-asset JEPA on all nine targets spanning returns, market exposure, risk, and liquidity, most strongly on exposure, risk, and liquidity. It achieves the highest RankIC on all liquidity targets and on returns from two hours to five days, as well as the best performance in a historical backtest. Ablations show that the gains in return and exposure prediction depend on aligning the market condition with the realized market movement over the target period. A synthetic factor-model study further demonstrates that conditioning improves the recovery of both the true market exposure and the asset-specific component. 6 Limitations All real-data experiments are conducted on Chinese A-share equities at a single bar frequency. Market-specific characteristics, including heavy retail participation, daily price limits, and T+1 settlement, may influence the impact of market-wide shocks on individual stocks. Whether our gains generalize to other markets, asset classes, or frequencies remains untested. Furthermore, Proposition 1 assumes a linear factor model with access to the true factor, whereas MC-JEPA relies on an estimated leave-one-out market condition. Our use of a single factor ( K = 1 K=1 ) also leaves richer cross-sectional structure, such as industry and style factors, to be captured implicitly. Because inputs and targets are standardized per window, the learned loadings capture co-movement with the market rather than beta. Multiplying them by the window’s price volatility recovers a cross-sectional beta ranking. Targets that preserve each asset’s scale could make the loadings a beta directly. D.3 Backtest Metrics The backtest of Table 2 trades the Ret 20d probe. On each signal day t t , a new tranche buys the top 7 or 8 predictions among the point-in-time CSI300 members at the close of t + 1 t+1 and sells them at the close of t + 21 t+21 . Weights are reset daily, and only the net weight change of each stock is traded, at 5 bp on buys and 15 bp on sells. Prices are daily back-adjusted closes from BaoStock. We examine whether these loadings encode market exposure and whether they can be interpreted as beta directly. All results use three MC-JEPA seeds and the 2023–2025 test period. Probed exposure. Table 13 reports ridge probes for Beta 20d on the representation z i z_{i} , on the loadings b ψ ​ ( z i , 240 ) b_{\psi}(z_{i},240) at the longest horizon, and on their concatenation. The loadings nearly match the representation (0.366 versus 0.374), while combining them yields only a small improvement to 0.377. Since the loadings are a function of z i z_{i} , the factor head makes exposure information accessible to a linear probe rather than introducing new information. Historical betas estimated over trailing 20, 60, and 120 days against the equal-weight market are substantially stronger (0.525). Combining them with z i z_{i} improves RankIC to 0.539, consistently across seeds. Therefore, learned representation provides complementary exposure information. We construct a label-free exposure estimate β ^ i = ⟨ b i , b ¯ ⟩ / ∥ b ¯ ∥ 2 \hat{\beta}_{i}=\langle b_{i},\bar{b}\rangle/\lVert\bar{b}\rVert^{2} , where b ¯ \bar{b} is the mean loading across stocks on the same date. The estimate varies substantially across stocks, with a cross-sectional standard deviation of 0.33–0.43, yet its RankIC with realized Beta 20d is only − 0.06 -0.06 . To see what it ranks instead, we compare it with two statistics of each stock’s daily returns over days t + 1 t{+}1 to t + 20 t{+}20 . Its RankIC is 0.18 with the Pearson correlation between the stock and the equal-weight market, and − 0.26 -0.26 with the stock’s volatility, the standard deviation of its returns. The correlation is not carried over from the context window: after regressing out the ranks of the window’s return standard deviation and its trailing four-day correlation with the equal-weight market, computed from 5-minute returns, the partial RankIC is 0.105 ± 0.008 0.105_{\pm 0.008} . This discrepancy is consistent with the per-asset normalization in Section 3.1 . Each asset’s context and future bars are standardized using its own context-window statistics, reducing the information about absolute volatility scales. Since β i = ρ i ​ σ i / σ m \beta_{i}=\rho_{i}\,\sigma_{i}/\sigma_{m} , where ρ i \rho_{i} is the asset-market return correlation and σ i \sigma_{i} and σ m \sigma_{m} are the return volatilities of the asset and market respectively, capturing market co-movement ρ i \rho_{i} alone does not suffice to recover beta. Therefore, the raw loadings should not be read as a beta directly. Restoring the removed scale. The normalization standardizes each feature by the mean and standard deviation of its context window. We restore the removed scale by multiplying β ^ i \hat{\beta}_{i} by the coefficient of variation of the close price over the window, CV i = std ⁡ ( close ) / mean ⁡ ( close ) \mathrm{CV}_{i}=\operatorname{std}(\text{close})/\operatorname{mean}(\text{close}) . RankIC is computed across stocks on each date, so the market volatility σ m \sigma_{m} is a per-date constant and does not need to be estimated. The result uses no labels, no fitted parameters and only the stock’s own bars. Table 14 compares it with alternatives. With this scale, RankIC with Beta 20d rises from − 0.060 -0.060 to 0.368, close to the ridge probe on z i z_{i} in Table 13 , and 0.08 above CV i \mathrm{CV}_{i} alone. Therefore, the loadings add exposure information beyond the window’s scale. The trailing four-day beta against the equal-weight market is stronger, but it requires the returns of all other stocks at inference.
