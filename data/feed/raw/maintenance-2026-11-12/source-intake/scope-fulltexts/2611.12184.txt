Yet AI scientists are typically given as much task information as possible during idea generation, which can anchor generation toward familiar approaches. Inspired by the cognitive framework of Blind Variation and Selective Retention (BVSR), we propose a discovery framework that separates these two roles of information: it generates proposals under deliberately limited task information to broaden exploration, then learns from proposals previously verified on the same task and their outcomes to retain promising candidates for costly empirical evaluation.
We evaluate BVSR across five proposer models and 12 scientific tasks. Limiting task information substantially increases proposal originality and non-obviousness, and produces a larger share of highly creative and high-utility proposals than existing state-of-the-art prompting-based baselines. Learning from prior proposal outcomes on the task further improves candidate selection, discarding half of the candidates while retaining
most top-performing proposals. When incorporated into existing AI-scientist workflows, BVSR achieves stronger discoveries across gene expression analysis, drug response prediction, anomaly detection, scaling law discovery, and brain activity prediction.
These results suggest that effective AI scientists should use information differently across stages of discovery: constrain task-specific information during exploration while exploiting accumulated empirical experience on the task. Existing studies demonstrate that AI agents can outperform the best human-designed solutions across a wide range of tasks, including mathematical proof [ 14 ] , mathematical discovery [ 33 ] , optimization [ 29 ] , and a subset of scientific problems [ 57 ] .
However, strong performance alone does not establish the creativity of the proposed methods. Prior work has identified limitations in the novelty of AI-generated proposals, including a tendency to explore incremental modifications or extensions of existing approaches [ 54 , 15 ] . A key challenge is therefore to develop AI agents that can reliably generate proposals that are creative. Addressing this challenge requires both assessing creativity rigorously and developing methods to improve it. Since the 1950s, psychologists have systematically studied how creativity should be judged in humans [ 44 , 34 ] . Their work identifies three defining properties of creative ideas: novelty, effectiveness, and non-obviousness [ 39 ] . These properties distinguish creativity from performance alone: an idea must work, but its originality and whether its success could have been anticipated also matter. This perspective is captured by a quantitative definition [ 40 ] : Creativity = Originality × Utility × Non-obviousness = ( 1 − p ) ⋅ u ⋅ ( 1 − v ) , \text{Creativity}\;=\;\text{Originality}\times\text{Utility}\times\text{Non-obviousness}\;=\;(1-p)\cdot u\cdot(1-v), (1) where p p is the probability of generating an idea, u u is its tested utility, and v v represents prior knowledge of its effectiveness. Originality captures whether an idea is uncommon rather than something that many others would propose. Utility reflects whether the idea actually works in practice. Non-obviousness concerns whether its effectiveness is known in advance. In this paper, we adopt this definition to assess the creativity of AI-generated proposals. To improve the creativity of AI agents, prior work has relied mainly on prompt engineering, retrieval augmentation, and a variety of search methods: instructing the agent to be novel [ 37 ] , supplying it with literature as inspiration [ 50 ] , or iteratively refining and selecting among many candidates according to search heuristics [ 20 ] . Each of these approaches, however, has serious limitations. Prompt engineering yields diminishing returns on frontier models [ 49 ] . Literature is a valuable reference, but the literature an agent retrieves for itself suffers from the same lack of diversity as its own proposals [ 38 , 13 ] . Heuristic search methods rely on a fine-grained signal, whereas most scientific problems offer at best a coarse metric of how well a finished proposal works [ 52 ] . Motivated by these limitations, we draw on the theory of Blind Variation (BV) and Selective Retention (SR) to guide how AI agents generate and select proposals [ 5 ] . This theory describes creative discovery as a process involving two complementary stages. The first is blind variation: scientists generate diverse ideas without foreknowledge of which will succeed, sometimes drawing inspiration from experiences unrelated to the immediate problem [ 42 , 9 ] . The second is selective retention: based on prior experience, they discard ideas that are clearly unreasonable and retain plausible ones for further investigation. Similarly, we find that in AI agents, providing too much task information during proposal generation causes the model to anchor on familiar approaches. As illustrated in Figure 1 , our method on blind variation first limits the task information available during generation. We then use selective retention to learn, as a scientist does by working on a problem, from proposals already verified on that task and their empirical outcomes to identify promising candidates for further evaluation. We first show that limiting task information during proposal generation can improve creativity. We provide an intuition for its effect on originality under information-induced anchoring, and validate its benefits empirically across five models and 12 scientific tasks. Across these settings, BV outperforms state-of-the-art prompting-based baselines in originality and non-obviousness, while generating a larger share of highly creative and useful proposals. We then develop SR to learn from previously verified proposals and their empirical outcomes, using a pairwise objective that separates high-utility proposals from the rest. SR retains top proposals more effectively than prompting-based methods, in-context learning, and supervised fine-tuning. Finally, we integrate BVSR into SimpleTES [ 56 ] , an existing common adopted AI scientist workflow, where it largely improves discovery performance over the original baselines. 2 Preliminaries An AI scientist attempts to solve an automated scientific discovery task T T by producing n n candidate methods. This substantially reduces the originality and non-obviousness of its proposals and, in turn, limits the utility of its top proposals. Just as scientists develop judgment about a problem by testing ideas on it, we formulate selective retention as learning from experience on the task itself. Each proposal previously verified on the task provides a pair consisting of a candidate and its empirical outcome. From these proposal–outcome pairs, we learn a scoring function that decides which new candidates for that task should be retained for expensive execution. As illustrated in Figure 1 (b), selective retention (SR) uses this learned judgment to reduce the number of candidates requiring empirical verification while preserving promising proposals. 4.1 Motivation Verification can be time-consuming in many scientific tasks: wet-lab experiments may take months, and machine learning experiments may require substantial GPU computation [ 43 ] . A natural approach is to prompt an LLM to inspect candidates. However, as our comparison below shows, these prompting-based approaches offer limited preservation of top candidates. This motivates learning a screener rather than relying exclusively on prompted judgments.
The learning objective must also reflect the purpose of screening. Pointwise supervised fine-tuning can train a model to predict each candidate’s performance, but its regression loss does not explicitly prioritize the preservation of top candidates. Selective retention has a more targeted requirement: high-value candidates should remain above the discard region. Precise prediction and ordering are secondary. We therefore focus on separating the top proposals from the remaining ones.
We first argue that identifying clearly ineffective methods is much easier than identifying the most effective ones. Proposition 4.1 ( Selective Retention Advantage ) . Under the mild Utility-Gap Assumption C.2 , if Δ SR ​ ( d ) > Δ best \Delta_{\mathrm{SR}}(d)>\Delta_{\mathrm{best}} ,
then a comparison across the selective-retention boundary is more accurate than distinguishing the best candidate from its closest competitor. Formally, we have P correct ​ ( Δ SR ​ ( d ) ) > P correct ​ ( Δ best ) . P_{\mathrm{correct}}\!\left(\Delta_{\mathrm{SR}}(d)\right)>P_{\mathrm{correct}}\!\left(\Delta_{\mathrm{best}}\right). We then argue SR reduces the verification time required to achieve the same performance, provided that its probability of discarding top proposals is significantly lower than that of random discarding. The task is to rank genes by how strongly their expression is organized in space on a tissue section.
The program borrows the nudged elastic band method from transition-state theory [ 17 ] , which finds a minimum-energy path by relaxing a chain of images under two projected forces: the true force perpendicular to the path and a spring force along it.
It takes the heat-flow trajectory of each gene’s expression field on the tissue graph, from the observed field to the uniform one, and computes at every image the component of a robust energy gradient perpendicular to the path.
For pure noise, the heat-flow path is already the minimum-energy path, and this component vanishes; spatial patterns bend the path, so the perpendicular force grows with pattern strength and becomes the gene’s score.

The program transplants the nonuniform (FFLO) superconducting state [ 21 ] , in which two species with mismatched Fermi surfaces still pair by giving each pair a nonzero momentum, so that the order parameter oscillates in space.
Its two “species” are two scores that often disagree: marginal tail evidence from empirical distribution functions, and geometric evidence from a robust covariance and a Gaussian mixture of the normal mass.
Instead of averaging them, it pairs them with an oscillating modulation along the direction in which they disagree most, relaxes the recovered pairing signal on a nearest-neighbour graph and adds it to the combined score.
With no trained network it reaches 0.679, against 0.629 for SimpleTES and 0.491 for Isolation Forest. Scaling law via assignment-game prices. The task is to find a formula with at most 26 parameters for the final training loss as a function of learning rate, batch size, data size and model size.
The program draws on the assignment game of Shapley and Shubik [36] , whose optimal matching is a linear program with competitive prices as dual variables.
It solves one fixed, entropy-regularized assignment problem between two grids in the (log learning rate, log batch size) plane and uses the resulting price field as a fixed surface that reshapes the loss basin near the optimal learning rate and batch size. The law adds power laws in data and model size and a convex bowl around a scale-dependent optimum, and fits only two parameters.

The former relies on grouping semantically equivalent proposals to estimate their frequencies, while the latter assesses whether prior knowledge suggests that a proposal will work. Detailed protocols are provided in Appendix F . Originality. Five PhD-level researchers each annotate 30 proposal pairs, yielding 150 pairs in total. The sample is balanced between pairs grouped together and pairs assigned to different groups by the LLM, and covers multiple tasks, proposer models, and generation methods. Evaluators judge whether the proposals express the same underlying mechanism. 84% of pairs grouped together by the LLM are also judged equivalent by humans (95% CI [ 0.74 , 0.92 ] [0.74,0.92] ), while 73% of pairs assigned to different groups are judged distinct (95% CI [ 0.61 , 0.82 ] [0.61,0.82] ). These results support the semantic-equivalence judgments used to estimate originality. Non-obviousness. Five PhD-level researchers evaluate 150 high-quality proposals from BV and Analogical Reasoning (AR) under blinded conditions. All sampled proposals achieve verified scores above the best baseline for their respective tasks. Evaluators see the task name and summaries of the source paper and proposed method. The summaries are rewritten in a uniform style, and method identifiers are removed. Evaluators assess how readily a proposal’s effectiveness could be inferred from the paper and their own knowledge of the literature, using a 1–5 scale with higher scores indicating greater non-obviousness.

AlphaEvolve and SimpleTES organize proposal generation and execution [ 29 , 56 ] .
These systems provide capable discovery workflows, but their effectiveness also depends on the candidates they explore.
Our work studies how task information shapes proposal generation and how empirical outcomes from previous proposals can guide which new candidates are selected for verification. Creativity in Humans. Associative theories explain creativity through connections between previously remote concepts [ 28 , 8 ] .
Studies show that familiar categories and example solutions can constrain subsequent ideas [ 51 , 16 ] .
Research on incubation further suggests that temporarily setting a problem aside can improve creative problem solving [ 41 ] .
Idea selection presents a separate challenge: people often favor feasible and desirable candidates at the expense of originality [ 32 ] .
Together with Campbell’s blind-variation-and-selective-retention theory [ 5 ] , these findings help explain the relationship between information and creativity.
We investigate whether reducing task information during ideation can encourage exploration in AI scientists. Creativity in AI. Computational creativity distinguishes combinational, exploratory, and transformational processes [ 1 ] .
Recent work evaluates LLM creativity through divergent-thinking tasks and the diversity of repeated generations [ 19 , 59 ] .
Beyond text generation, Creative Agents incorporates imagination into embodied planning for open-ended construction tasks [ 4 ] .
Methods for diversifying outputs include verbalized sampling [ 58 ] , which elicits distributions over responses, and analogical reasoning [ 37 ] , which transfers relational structures across scientific domains.
Our approach adds task information level as a distinct control for creative generation. 7 Conclusion We studied how information should be used at different stages of automated scientific discovery. Our results suggest that more information is not uniformly beneficial. During idea generation, detailed task information can anchor language models toward familiar mechanisms and reduce the probability of generating creative proposals. Blind Variation mitigates this effect by limiting task information.
After generation, empirical experience earned on the task can guide which new candidates deserve expensive verification. Selective Retention learns this decision from proposal–outcome data.
Together, these findings suggest a broader design principle for AI scientists: separate the information used for exploration from the experience used for selection. References [1] M. A. Appendix F Human Validation Protocols This section details the human evaluation protocols used in Section 5.4 . F.1 Originality Our originality measure depends on grouping proposals that express the same underlying scientific or algorithmic idea. To validate this semantic-equivalence step, we construct a set of 150 proposal pairs, balanced between pairs grouped together and pairs assigned to different groups by the LLM evaluator. The pairs are sampled across tasks, proposer models, and generation methods. Five PhD-level researchers each evaluate 30 pairs. For each pair, the evaluator judges whether the two proposals express essentially the same underlying mechanism, ignoring differences in wording and implementation details. Pairs marked Unsure (4 in total) are excluded from the agreement analysis. An example evaluation sheet is provided in Appendix G.1 . F.2 Non-obviousness We sample 150 high-quality proposals from BV and AR, defined as proposals whose verified score exceeds the best baseline of their task. We remove all method identifiers and ask five PhD-level researchers to evaluate their non-obviousness. For each proposal, evaluators are shown the task name, a summary of the source paper, and a summary of the proposed method. Both summaries are rewritten by Claude Opus 5 into a uniform style to reduce cues that could reveal the generating method. Evaluators rate non-obviousness on a 1–5 scale, considering how readily the method’s effectiveness on the task could be inferred from the source paper and their own knowledge of the literature. Higher scores indicate greater non-obviousness. Proposals are split into forms of 30, each balanced across tasks and methods and presented in random order. Each evaluator completes one form. An example evaluation sheet is provided in Appendix G.2 . Appendix G Human Evaluation Example Form G.1 Human Evaluation Example Form of Originality Each page gives the task name and the one-sentence mechanism descriptions of two proposals written for that task, followed by one multiple-choice question: do the two proposals express essentially the same underlying scientific or algorithmic mechanism (Yes / No / Unsure)? Both proposals of a pair come from the same task, the method that produced them and whether the grouping placed them together are not shown, and pairs appear in random order. Figure 8 shows the first pair page of one form. Figure 8: Example page of the originality evaluation form. The evaluator sees a task and the mechanism descriptions of two proposals written for it, and judges whether the two express essentially the same underlying mechanism (Yes, No, or Unsure). G.2 Human Evaluation Example Form of Non-Obviousness Evaluators rate proposals in a Google Form. The form opens with a short instruction page and one field for the evaluator’s identifier; it then shows one proposal per page, 30 pages in total. Each page gives the task name, a summary of the paper the proposal draws on, and a summary of the proposed method, followed by a 1–5 question on non-obviousness. The method that produced each proposal is not shown, proposals appear in random order, and summaries are written by Claude Opus 5 in one uniform style so that the wording does not reveal the method. Figure 9 shows the first page of one form. Figure 9: Example page of the non-obviousness evaluation form. The evaluator sees a task, a summary of the paper the proposal draws on, and a summary of the proposed method, and rates how obvious it is that the method would work for the task, from 1 (obvious: the literature already shows it working on this kind of task) to 5 (not obvious at all).
