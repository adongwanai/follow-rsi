Abstract 
​
Protein engineering requires navigating rugged fitness landscapes, where small sequence 
changes can produce large functional differences, but experimental screening can only sample 
a small fraction of sequence space. Thus, a central challenge is to efficiently traverse the large 
combinatorial space of mutations in silico. We introduce ProTEUS, a mutation-resolved world 
model for closed-loop protein evolution. Pretrained on deep mutational scanning assays, 
ProTEUS learns a joint representation of sequence, evolutionary, and structural information 
using MutationFormer, a mutation-biased attention architecture. Variants are represented as 
states in this joint latent space, and mutations define actions whose functional consequences 
are

ProTEUS refines its prediction and understanding of protein activity through closed loop

established models in both zero-shot and supervised fine-tuning mutation prediction tasks. We 
experimentally demonstrate its utility in two closed-loop multi-objective engineering campaigns 
on aldehyde dehydrogenase and levansucrase, yielding variants with >10 fold higher activity 
than wild type. ProTEUS provides a world-model framework that connects virtual evaluation of 
mutational outcomes with experimental learning for efficient protein evolution. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

 
Introduction 
Protein engineering expands the capabilities of biotechnology and medicine by improving a 
desired activity beyond what is offered in nature including catalytic activity, binding affinity, 
stability and expression (1–3). Directed evolution has played a central role in this pursuit, which 
achieves these goals through repeated mutation and selection, but experimental screening, 
including continuous directed evolution techniques, can only sample a small fraction of the full 
sequence space (4, 5). Although deep mutational scanning for all single mutants of a protein is 
possible, epistasis still complicates the route to find the best sequence which may process 
multiple mutations from wild-type (WT) (6–8). A central challenge is thus to build a 
computational model to evaluate a large number of mutations in silico to guide selection of 
sequences for experimental testing and then use experimental feedback to improve activity 
predictions for successive rounds of variant selection toward a desired phenotype (3, 9, 10). 
Various models have been deployed to score mutations in a zero-shot setting to reduce 
the initial experimental workload in a protein engineering campaign before target specific 
measurements are obtained. Sequence-based models, including the ESM family, learn from 
natural proteins and use amino acid likelihoods to estimate substitution effects (11–14). MSA 
Transformer, Tranception, PoET and Protriever incorporate family-specific evolutionary 
information (15–18), whereas inverse folding models like ESM-IF and ProteinMPNN assess 
sequence compatibility with a specified protein backbone (19, 20). Multimodal models such as 
ESM-3, SaProt and ProSST combine sequence and structural information (21–23) and local 
geometry, residue contacts and molecular surfaces could provide further context for enhanced 
prediction (24–26). Lastly, retrieval-augmented models can incorporate homologous sequences 
and evolutionary information to help with mutational effect prediction (27, 28). Together, these 
approaches support virtual screening through estimates of evolutionary plausibility and 
structural compatibility, but so far still exhibit only a moderate level of correlation with 
experimentally measured protein activity especially for proteins that are less represented in 
evolution (3, 29).  
The moderate performance of these zero-shot predictions can be partly attributed to the 
misalignment between evolutionary selection and a particular activity being optimized. Natural 
sequence distributions reflect selection across biological constraints for a particular organism, 
which can differ significantly from the activity targeted in an experiment (30). Favorable 
sequence, structural or evolutionary scores therefore do not guarantee higher protein activity (1, 
3, 30). To circumvent this problem, functional measurements can be integrated to adapt the 
model to an engineering objective (31). In this pursuit, EVOLVEpro uses protein language model 
representations to guide iterative evolution from small experimental datasets (3). MULTI-evolve 
uses a similar approach on higher order mutants (1). However, these two models still suffer from 
low hit rates in selecting a high-activity initial set of mutations for experimental characterization 
and poor generalization to untested mutants, especially higher-order mutants, during active 
learning.  
In light of these challenges, we developed ProTEUS, a world model for closed-loop

are represented as states in a joint embedding space of sequence, evolutionary and structural 

 
information. We pretrained this joint latent representation on 182 deep mutational scanning 
(DMS) assays spanning 161 protein backgrounds with diverse protein properties including 
stability, organismal fitness, activity, expression and binding (29). At its core, MutationFormer 
aligns sequence, structural, and multiple sequence alignment (MSA) features residue by residue 
and integrates them through attention biased toward mutations (Fig. 1D). This design focuses 
the model’s attention on the mutations defining a candidate while allowing their predicted effects 
to depend on the surrounding protein context. To supply physically valid structural inputs for 
large libraries of higher-order candidates, we developed a fast, physics-constrained all-atom 
predictor. The predictor combines two denoising steps with differentiable Gauss–Seidel 
projection to enforce geometric and stereochemical constraints during training and inference 
(32). The resulting structures provide candidate-specific spatial context for the joint 
representation, supporting virtual evaluation of proposed mutations before laboratory testing. 
During pretraining, ProTEUS exhibited data-scaling behavior with respect to protein activity data 
(Fig. 2C), as seen in the training of large language models (LLM) and protein language models 
(PLM) where increase in training input data or model size leads to better performance and 
generalization. This reveals a potentially new axis of scaling for protein engineering foundation 
models to capitalize on more protein activity data of various forms.  
 
ProTEUS guides successive rounds of evolution through a nomination strategy linked to 
experimental feedback (Fig. 1C). Zero-shot scores guide the initial selection of high-fitness 
variants for experimental testing. Measurements from each round update activity predictions, 
and the nomination strategy combines these predictions with accumulated experimental 
evidence to select subsequent higher order candidates. Computational benchmarks show 
improved mutation-effect prediction in both zero-shot and supervised fine-tuning settings relative 
to the evaluated baselines. ProTEUS also improves higher-order fitness prediction and recovery 
of high-fitness variants during simulated evolution compared to EVOLVEpro and MULTI-evolve. 
In laboratory evolution of levansucrase and aldehyde dehydrogenase, ProTEUS can find 
variants with >10 fold higher activity than the starting wild-type protein within an experimental 
budget of 100 sequences. Together, these results establish a world-model framework that links 
predicted mutational consequences to experimental observations, enabling experimental 
learning to guide protein evolution toward desired functions. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

 
Results 
Mutation-resolved learning improves protein fitness prediction 
For scoring mutational effect on protein activity, where most of the benchmarks and datasets 
center on single mutants (29, 33), evaluation requires distinguishing candidate states that share 
most of their sequence but differ substantially in function. Conventionally, pooling residue-level 
features into a single protein representation is used but this can obscure local information 
relevant to prediction (13, 34, 35). The challenge thus is to focus the attention on the few 
substituted residues while preserving the surrounding context that determines their effects. 
Selective attention and gating can emphasize information relevant to a prediction (36–39), while 
controlling anomalous activations can reduce attention and feature-map artifacts (40). These 
principles motivate us to build a mutation-resolved representation in which mutations receive 
explicit attention and are interpreted jointly with their sequence, evolutionary and structural 
context (Fig. 1D). In particular, amino acid identity, MSA and structural features were aligned 
residue by residue, and an explicit mutation mask marked differences from the wild-type (WT) 
sequence. MutationFormer integrated these features through a 12-layer transformer with 
attention biased toward substituted positions, while retaining the surrounding protein as context.  
To establish this joint embedding space, we pretrained ProTEUS on 182 substitution 
DMS assays from ProteinGym (29) to predict measured variant fitness from multimodal inputs 
(Fig. 1E, tables S1-2). The pretraining data includes experimentally measured stability, 
organismal fitness, activity, expression and binding across human, prokaryotic, other eukaryotic 
and viral proteins (Fig. 2A). We screened 217 ProteinGym assays against 38 reference 
sequences from held-out datasets reserved for downstream evaluation to test model 
generalization. After excluding assays that matched these evaluation targets or met the 
sequence-homology exclusion criteria, we retained 182 assays for pretraining. We characterized 
sequence and structural level of homology to make sure there is no aberrant leakage and found 
no hits with ≥ 30% sequence-identity that are either above 50% query-coverage thresholds or 
query-normalized TM-scores ≥0.5 for structural threshold. (fig. S1). Within this pretraining data, 
ProTEUS achieved a Spearman correlation of 0.933 and a mean squared error (MSE) of 0.115 
on the test set. To compare different model architectures’ ability to learn this mutational dataset, 
we adopted a similar training procedure and found that PoET-2, the strongest evaluated 
baseline by rank correlation in this comparison, only achieved 0.861, showing ProTEUS’s ability 
to use a joint latent space to efficiently learn mutational effect on protein activity (41) (Fig. 2B). 
We next quantified how the world model’s predictive accuracy scaled with functional 
pretraining data. With the architecture and evaluation sets held fixed, increasing training data 
80-fold reduced test MSE from 0.262 to 0.115, a 56.3% reduction (Fig. 2C). Across the 
measured range, MSE followed an approximate power-law relationship with training-data size. 
This fit corresponded to an approximately 12.1% reduction in MSE for each doubling of training 
data. Spearman correlation of the held out test set increased concurrently from 0.842 to 0.933, 
showing that additional supervision improved both numerical fitness prediction and variant 
ranking. Although absolute gains diminished as the dataset expanded, both metrics continued to 
improve at the largest training fraction. These results identify experimental activity 

 
measurements as a potential scalable source of supervision for refining the world model’s 
predictions of untested variants. 
Next, we used ablations to assess the contributions of mutation-biased attention and 
multimodal context to fitness prediction (Fig. 2D). Removing mutation bias from an otherwise 
matched model reduced Spearman correlation from 0.840 to 0.606; a bias strength of 10 
performed best among the values tested. In the input-modality comparison, sequence 
representations alone achieved a correlation of 0.807, whereas joint sequence, MSA and 
structural representations achieved 0.938. Redundancy filtering and weighted sampling of 
MSAs produced similar performance (Fig. 2D). Together, these comparisons supported both 
explicit attention to substituted residues and multimodal integration of a protein’s evolutionary 
and structural context for optimal understanding of mutational effect.  
ProTEUS predicts mutation effects and guides iterative variant selection 
Virtual exploration of a new protein requires predicting mutational outcomes before 
target-specific functional measurements are available. We therefore tested whether 
mutation–fitness relationships learned across the pretraining datasets enable generalization to 
proteins excluded from pretraining (fig. S1). Across 19 held-out DMS assays containing single- 
and multiple-substitution variants, ProTEUS achieved the highest macro-averaged zero-shot 
Spearman correlation compared to other models (Fig. 2E; fig. S2A).  
We then tested whether zero-shot predictions supported the selection of high-fitness 
variants, since most protein engineering campaigns rely on an initial set of high activity single 
mutants to build combinatorial libraries. In this pursuit, MULTI-evolve previously reported a 
method by taking an ensemble of four deep learning models’ predictions with high accuracy (1). 
To benchmark ProTEUS against MULTI-evolve, we compared their zero-shot rankings across 
13 held-out single-substitution DMS assays (fig. S2B) and evaluated variant selection under 
matched selection budgets. Among the 10 highest-ranked candidates, 75.4% selected by 
ProTEUS and 64.6% selected by MULTI-evolve had fitness at or above the assay mean (Fig. 
2F), showing ProTEUS’s superiority compared to ensemble methods. These results support the 
use of the pretrained world model to prioritize candidate variants on new protein backgrounds, 
providing a starting point for experimental evolution before target-specific feedback becomes 
available. 
To assess how well the model can leverage experimental measurements on a particular 
activity to further guide engineering, we evaluated supervised fine-tuning on the held-out DMS 
assays using within-assay fivefold cross-validation. On held-out variants, ProTEUS achieved a 
mean Spearman correlation of 0.549, compared with 0.367 for ProteinNPT (34), the next-ranked 
method (Fig. 2G). Lastly, to evaluate whether the model can efficiently use low-N measurements 
to guide engineering in an active learning fashion, we simulated directed evolution on 10 
single-mutant DMS datasets. ProTEUS and EVOLVEpro each selected 30 variants per round 
from the same candidate library over five rounds. Measurements for selected variants were 
revealed and used to update the model before the next round. By round 5, ProTEUS reached a 
mean normalized best fitness of 0.741, compared with 0.555 for EVOLVEpro (Fig. 2H). Overall, 
these computational benchmarks support ProTEUS as a state-of-the-art model for zero-shot 
recommendation of mutations and supervised fine-tuning on target specific activity data.  

 
Efficient physics-constrained model supports multi-mutant screening 
To efficiently sample a large number of mutations in silico, especially for higher-order variants 
that differ in multiple residues, ProTEUS would need to generate high quality structures with 
atomic level precision and minimal computational cost per structure to supply as input (32). 
Current frontier structural prediction models like AlphaFold3 still suffer from poor physical 
validity and a relatively large computational budget due to the use of diffusion modules (42), so 
we developed a fast all-atom predictor that combined two denoising steps with explicit 
geometric constraint enforcement (32). 
The predictor retained the atom-attention encoder, MSA module and PairFormer of 
Boltz-1 (43), which provided conditioning features for a diffusion network initialized from its 
released weights. The network generated provisional all-atom coordinates, and a differentiable 
Gauss–Seidel projection corrected geometric and stereochemical violations (Fig. 3A-B). At 
inference, projection followed the second denoising update. The learned predictor thus 
recovered molecular conformation, while the projection enforced local constraints on the 
resulting coordinates. The projection balanced correction of constraint violations against 
displacement from the predicted structure. Seven constraint families covered nonbonded 
inter-chain separation, tetrahedral chirality, E/Z double-bond stereochemistry, carbon 
double-bond planarity, ligand internal distances, symmetric-chain overlap and inter-chain 
covalent bonds (Fig. 3B; table S3). The solver exploited constraint locality by updating affected 
coordinates in place and using each correction in subsequent updates. Twenty Gauss–Seidel 
sweeps resolved coupled constraints without assembling a large global system (32). Constraints 
without shared atoms were processed in parallel on the GPU to reduce the cost of geometric 
correction. 
We included projection during training so that the predictor learned to generate 
coordinates compatible with the subsequent correction. The Boltz-1-initialized network was 
fine-tuned on approximately 180,000 Protein Data Bank structures, with the denoising loss 
evaluated on projected coordinates. Implicit differentiation propagated gradients through the 
projection without storing the full sequence of solver iterations. In the reported CASP15 ablation 
(32), fine-tuning through projection increased complex LDDT from 0.5889 to 0.6321 relative to 
inference-only projection. Both settings passed all seven validity checks. The fine-tuned 
two-step predictor approached the complex LDDT of 0.6406 obtained with the 200-step Boltz-1 
reference. Training through projection therefore helped retain structural accuracy under a 
reduced denoising budget. 
We evaluated structural accuracy and geometric validity across CASP15, Test, 
PoseBusters, AF3-AB, dsDNA and RNA–protein benchmarks. Global accuracy, measured by 
complex LDDT (44) and TM-score (45), was broadly comparable to the evaluated baselines, 
and all projected structures passed the seven validity checks (Fig. 3C), when baseline 
predictions did not consistently satisfy these checks. The complete inference procedure, 
including denoising and projection, achieved a median 9.4-fold speedup over 200-step Boltz-1 
on CASP15 (32) (Fig. 3D). Across five atom-count bins, AlphaFold 3 mean runtimes were 
7.81–10.81 times higher than those of our predictor. In the representative protein–ligand 
complex PDB 7WCF, AlphaFold 3 predicted a ligand–side-chain separation of 1.41 Å, whereas 
our predictor yielded 4.44 Å, reflecting more accurate resolution of local geometry that is critical 

 
for a variety of downstream tasks including molecular dynamics and protein engineering (Fig. 
3E).  
Higher-order mutation activity prediction  
Virtual exploration of higher-order variants requires predicting functional outcomes beyond the 
mutation order covered by existing measurements. We therefore tested whether ProTEUS could 
generalize from measured lower-order variants to higher-order combinations. In a K-vs-rest 
benchmark across 15 multi-mutant DMS datasets, models were trained on variants carrying up 
to K substitutions and tested on variants carrying more than K (46). For this task, we compared 
ProTEUS directly with MULTI-evolve for higher-order fitness prediction (Fig. 3F). Across 40 
datasets-K comparisons, ProTEUS achieved a mean Spearman correlation of 0.448, compared 
with 0.295 for MULTI-evolve. Among these comparisons, ProTEUS achieved higher correlation 
in 80%, reflecting a more information-rich joint embedding space ProTEUS uses. 
We then evaluated whether predictions updated with new measurements could guide 
successive rounds of higher-order variant selection. We compared ProTEUS with EVOLVEpro 
and MULTI-evolve in simulations of progressive directed evolution on 17 DMS datasets. We 
also included an additive model fitted to accumulated measurements, providing a baseline for 
variant selection based on individual substitution effects without interaction terms (47). Each 
method selected 30 variants per round from the same candidate library over five model-guided 
rounds following an initial batch. Measurements for selected variants were revealed and used to 
update the model before the next round. ProTEUS achieved the highest mean normalized best 
fitness in each round (Fig. 3G), extending its predictive performance to iterative exploration of 
mutation combinations. 
Based on these results, we finalized ProTEUS’s closed-loop evolution strategy by 
prompting ProTEUS to zero-shot rank an initial single-mutant batch. Subsequent nominations 
combined zero-shot scores, updated fitness predictions and fitness ranks derived from all 
available experimental measurements (fig. S4; table S4). The highest-ranked eligible, untested 
variants were selected, with the maximum allowed mutation order retained or increased by one. 
Measurements from each batch updated the model and the experimental evidence available for 
subsequent nominations. 
Agentic Closed-loop evolution of levansucrase with ProTEUS 
To smoothly integrate ProTEUS with wet-lab and the growing capabilities of LLMs, we built an 
agentic workflow to automate the end to end closed-loop protein evolution (Fig. 4A). An agent 
coordinates sequence candidates generation, fitness prediction, mutation nomination, and 
incorporates experimental feedback to update the model for subsequent rounds. Beyond 
workflow automation, the agent supports scientific assessment of the mutations proposed by 
ProTEUS. ProTEUS prioritizes the top-k candidate mutation sites, and the agent combines 
ProTEUS scores with LLM-based analysis of the physicochemical implications of the proposed 
substitutions to generate a rationale and confidence assessment for each nominated variant. 
These assessments accompany the ranked candidates in a human-in-the-loop review step, 
helping scientists evaluate the proposed substitutions and make informed choices about 
mutation sites for experimental testing. As modern LLMs get increasingly knowledgeable about 

 
diverse proteins, these assessments could also be incorporated as part of scoring function in 
the future (48).  
To evaluate ProTEUS-guided evolution, we followed the predefined variant-nomination 
strategy from our computational benchmarks. For the two closed loop engineering campaigns 
shown here, agent-generated rationales and confidence assessments served as interpretive 
annotations, without human adjustment of candidate rankings or batch selection based on these 
assessments. We first deployed agentic ProTEUS to engineer levansucrase (SacB) from 
Bacillus subtilis. Levansucrase transfers fructosyl groups from sucrose to synthesize levan, 
releasing glucose (49). Levan's use as a viscosifier, stabilizer and gelling agent in a variety of 
food applications motivates efforts to improve its enzymatic activity and stability (50). To 
generate high throughput levansucrase activity and expression data, we cloned mutations 
suggested by ProTEUS into a his tagged vector for expression in E. coli and then measured 
glucose production from sucrose breakdown to infer catalytic activity of the enzyme (Fig. 4A). In 
addition, we can measure the expression of the enzyme directly by an anti-His tag ELISA.  
Using this setup, we performed a multi-objective engineering by considering the product 
of expression and catalytic activity as the reward function since mutations that only improve 
activity but compromise expressions and stability are not useful in downstream applications. 
ProTEUS started with an initial zero-shot recommendation of 30 single mutants, and laboratory 
testing revealed that Q34N mutant within this batch had a 4.5-fold higher activity than the WT 
protein, reaffirming the ability to zero-shot identify high fitness candidates as seen in previous 
computational benchmarks (Fig. 4B-C). Subsequently, ProTEUS used this data to suggest 
higher-order mutants in a round wise fashion (Fig. 4B). The next round identified Q34N:G240W 
and Q34N:S359F with activities of 8.94-fold and 8.91-fold WT, respectively (Fig. 4C). Both 
double-mutant parents were carried forward by ProTEUS, allowing proper exploration of two 
backgrounds with similar measured activities. In the third experimental batch, ProTEUS 
suggested 18 triple mutants derived from Q34N:G240W and 12 derived from Q34N:S359F 
(table S5). In the third round, G23A:Q34N:G240W reached 14.03-fold WT activity, 1.57 times 
that of its parent, while Q34N:M328L:S359F reached 12.38-fold WT activity (Fig. 4C, fig. S5A; 
table S10). Q34N:G240W:Y436E also exceeded the preceding round's best variant, reaching 
10.56-fold WT activity. Similar to our computational benchmarks, ProTEUS can properly identify 
synergistic mutations during exploration of higher-order mutations. Overall, ProTEUS was able 
to find variants with >10 fold higher activity than the starting WT with a budget of <100 
experimental tests, highlighting its ability to efficiently leverage experimental data for closed-loop 
evolution.  
To better understand the mutations explored in this closed-loop evolution, we examined 
how the scores used to evaluate candidates relate to measured activity within each 
experimental batch. Initial zero-shot scores had a Spearman correlation of −0.24 with measured 
activity. It is worth noting that even though the initial zero-shot batch of 30 mutants properly 
identified high fitness variants like Q34N, the overall low global spearman correlation still 
highlights the divergence between model inferred protein fitness and the specific enzymatic 
activity and expression being optimized in this campaign. Composite scores that incorporated 
experimental data in the subsequent rounds were positively correlated with activity, with 
correlations of 0.24 in round 1 and 0.38 in round 2, reflecting the model’s ability to leverage 

 
experimental data to align its output to the specific engineering objective (Fig. 4D). Structural 
mapping placed the selected substitutions in several regions of the protein (Fig. 4E). In the WT 
structure, Q34 and G240 were 29.5 Å and 10.5 Å from the superposed sucrose, respectively. 
The alternative branch included S359, 7.6 Å from sucrose, and M328, 19.0 Å away. Similar to 
previously reported, these mutations do not fall in the key catalytic active site of the enzyme and 
thus likely contributed to the overall improvement in activity through other complementary 
mechanisms (3).  
To examine the candidate states explored during evolution, we mapped WT and the 90 
assayed variants onto a shared t-SNE projection containing 366,836 library representations 
(51). The overlaid lineage traced successive additions of Q34N, G240W and G23A in this 
visualization of the model latent space (Fig. 4F). We then assessed how the original 
768-dimensional representation distinguished candidates within each final-round parental 
background. Among pairs differing at two residues, cosine distances ranged from 0.00151 to 
0.03240 on Q34N:G240W and from 0.00310 to 0.03195 on Q34N:S359F (table S9). Within the 
Q34N:G240W background, 6 principal components were enough to capture 90% of the model 
representation variance, compared with 14 for the amino-acid one-hot representations used in 
MULTI-evolve (table S9).  
We next assessed the novelty of the mutations suggested by ProTEUS. In an alignment 
of 2,451 homologous sequences, serine was the consensus residue at position 34, occurring in 
63 of 188 aligned sequences (33.5%). However, Q34S had only 0.56-fold WT activity, whereas 
Q34N reached 4.50-fold WT activity despite asparagine occurring in only 7 of 188 sequences 
(3.72%). This highlights ProTEUS’s ability to deduce novel mutations that are potentially 
beneficial to protein activity, without overrelying on evolutionary information. In the closed-loop 
evolution campaigns, all three substitutions in the final lead introduced non-consensus residues. 
The residues introduced by G23A and G240W occurred in 6 of 184 (3.26%) and 4 of 1,258 
(0.32%) aligned sequences, respectively. The originality ratios for G23A, Q34N and G240W 
were 0.04, 0.11 and 0.01 (Fig. 4G). Across the campaign, the 89 distinct substitutions at 65 
positions had a median introduced-residue frequency of 1.19%, and 22 were unobserved at 
their respective alignment positions. These measurements showed that the search retained 
beneficial substitutions that were uncommon among homologs while pursuing further 
improvements on experimentally supported backgrounds. 
Closed-loop evolution identifies improved ALDH  
To better understand whether ProTEUS’s capability generalizes beyond one enzyme tested, we 
next applied ProTEUS to engineer aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) from Cupriavidus 
pinatubonensis. ALDH catalyzes the oxidation of aldehydes to carboxylic acids. Human genetics 
have shown that mutation in the ALDH gene can lead to build up of acetaldehyde and is 
implicated with increased risk of cardiovascular disease and cancer (52–54). To identify a more 
potent ALDH enzyme, we established a high-throughput assay to measure total ALDH activity in 
E. coli lysates by detecting NADH generated during acetaldehyde oxidation with a coupled 
reaction that converted a colorless probe into a product absorbing at 450 nm. 
We prompted ProTEUS to nominate thirty variants per round for five rounds, totaling 150 
distinct variants (table S11). The search began with zero-shot nomination of single substitutions 

 
and progressed to double, triple, and quadruple mutants (Fig. 5A). Activity measurements were 
returned to ProTEUS between rounds to update the fitness predictor and inform subsequent 
nominations. During this evolution campaign, the initial zero-shot prediction round identified 
L358I, with 2.02 times higher than WT activity (Fig. 5B). Building on L358I, subsequent rounds 
leveraging experimental datapoints identified A116K and H332N, with the triple mutant reaching 
6.08 times WT activity (Fig. 5A-B, fig. S5B). In the final round, we identified 
S103V:A116K:H332N:L358I with 6.85-fold WT activity (Fig. 5B; table S5). Because the 
high-throughput screen measured total activity without normalizing protein abundance, we used 
an anti-His tag ELISA kit to quantify the enzyme concentration per variant, and calculated the 
normalized enzyme catalytic rate fold change. This showed that the best mutant in the final 
round (round 4) had a 7.90 fold improvement of the catalytic rate over WT with other variants 
showing different combinations of enzyme abundance and concentration-normalized activity (fig. 
S5C). Lastly, we purified the WT, L358I and the final quadruple mutant and measured reaction 
time courses using equal amounts of purified protein. The final variant had a higher absorbance 
slope than WT and L358I (Fig. 5C), supporting increased activity with equal amounts of purified 
protein. To better understand ProTEUS’s engineering trajectory, we examined the relationship 
between scores and measured activity within each experimental batch. Similar to the 
levansucrase campaign, zero-shot scores showed little association with activity in the initial 
batch, whereas composite scores were positively correlated with activity in subsequent rounds. 
The composite score reached a Spearman correlation of 0.66 in the final round among the 30 
tested variants (Fig. 5D). We then mapped the top mutations onto the structure of the enzyme 
and again found that all four altered positions were distal to the annotated catalytic site, lying 
more than 13 Å from the annotated catalytic residues (Fig. 5E). 
To examine how the learned state space represented the experimental trajectory, we 
analyzed the organization and measured activities of ALDH variants in the ProTEUS embedding 
(tables S6-7). We projected WT and the 150 assayed variants into a shared t-SNE map 
containing 354,144 candidate representations (Fig. 5F). Initial single-mutant selections occupied 
multiple regions, whereas subsequent higher-order variants localized around selected parental 
backgrounds. Lineage mapping traced successive additions of L358I, A116K and H332N. The 
final two batches explored alternative fourth substitutions on A116K:H332N:L358I, with S103V 
yielding the final lead. We then tested whether neighboring representations were associated 
with similar activities among the 60 four-substitution variants sharing the A116K:H332N:L358I 
background. Each variant was compared with its five nearest neighbors from the same batch in 
the original 768-dimensional space. Mean absolute activity differences were 1.69 between 
embedding neighbors and 1.88 for sequence-distance-matched comparisons, with WT activity 
set to 1. This represented a 10.1% reduction (two-sided permutation P = 0.046; table S6). 
Three- and ten-neighbor analyses showed smaller reductions of 6.7% and 6.0%, respectively, 
with statistical support varying across neighborhood sizes (table S6). Within the 
A116K:H332N:L358I background, neighboring variants in the ProTEUS embedding showed 
smaller activity differences than sequence distance matched comparisons.  
Lastly, to assess the evolutionary prevalence of the substitutions explored during ALDH 
evolution, we analyzed an alignment of 19,704 homologous sequences (table S8). Across 148 
distinct substitutions at 104 positions, the median substituted-residue frequency was 0.59%, and 

 
10 substitutions were unobserved at their respective alignment positions (Fig. 5G). In the final 
lead, two mutations S103V and A116K were rare and novel mutations whereas the original 
L358I introduced in the zero-shot discovery and H332N were frequently observed residues 
during evolution. This again highlights ProTEUS’s ability to integrate multi-modal information 
both from evolution and experimental data to pick up both common and rare beneficial 
mutations.  
Discussion 
Efficiently finding a set of mutations that will push the protein toward a desired phenotype is one 
of the holy grails of molecular engineering. In the vast space of 20L possible mutations that is 
not possible to experimentally characterize, any computational methods that can identify a path 
that leads to desired activity is of great value, especially for activity not explored in nature but 
critical for biotechnology applications. In the age of generative protein design, many models, 
especially large pretrained foundation models, have been developed to rank protein variants in 
a zero-shot fashion. However, this class of method in general suffers from the fact that many 
protein activities are not well represented in evolution. In contrast, active learning methods like 
EVOLVEpro demonstrated that pretrained protein representations can guide protein engineering 
(single mutant) by using relatively little experimental data points to align the model directly to 
experimental activity of interest (3), and MULTI-evolve has further shown that it is possible to 
guide engineering of higher-order variants (1). These two models highlight the utility of active 
learning to align model output with wet lab-measured protein activity, but still suffer from the lack 
of a good unified representation of protein variants to efficiently leverage experimental data and 
guide engineering toward higher order mutations with complex epistasis (47).   
With an ever increasing capability of DNA synthesis, sequencing and more powerful 
deep learning models, we propose a new way to look at this task, a world model that connects 
the virtual evaluation of proposed mutations with experimental learning. In essence, such a 
world model would need a high quality latent space to understand and reason a given 
mutation’s impact on a protein phenotype and leverage experimental measurement in an 
efficient manner to guide a model’s search inside this vast sequence space. To build a prototype 
of this world model, we leveraged a MutationFormer that integrates sequence, evolutionary and 
structural context to learn a joint embedding space capable of understanding sparse mutations’ 
impact on protein activity, with a physics-constrained structural predictor that significantly 
accelerates generation of candidate-specific structural inputs. ProTEUS achieves higher 
accuracy on both zero-shot ranking of protein variants and supervised fine tuning tasks where 
we test whether the model can efficiently learn from experimental data to extrapolate a 
target-specific activity landscape. Our experimental validation on ALDH and levansucrase 
presents preliminary evidence for the utility of such a model to guide protein engineering in a 
closed-loop fashion.  
There are numerous ways ProTEUS could be further improved. First, the pretraining of 
ProTEUS leveraged ProteinGym, which so far hosts around 2.5 million mutational 
measurements (29). In many deep learning models, especially modern day LLMs, more data 
during pretraining generally leads to better model performance and generalization. This scaling 

 
law both in terms of pretraining data and model size has been the driver of breakthroughs in 
areas like natural language processing, computer vision, and is also seen in protein structural 
prediction and protein language model (55–59). The data-scaling analysis showed that 
ProTEUS predicted held-out variant fitness more accurately as the amount of functional 
pretraining data increased (Fig. 2C). Protein mutation datasets typically are very noisy with 
strong variance even for the same protein from different sources due to the sensitive nature of 
different assays and measurement conditions, and thus have far fewer high quality curation 
compared to other domains. However, with initiatives like the ProteinGym (29) and better 
standardization of high throughput protein activity datasets similar to early efforts in the protein 
structural domains that resulted in protein data bank, we should expect an accumulation of high 
quality mutation datasets on diverse protein activities in the coming decade, and 
correspondingly greater model performance and generalization for pretrained models like 
ProTEUS. With the rise of LLMs for text processing, it is also plausible that a comprehensive 
mining of literatures for scattered mutation experiments will lead to collection of activity dataset 
for pretraining. Together, these directions represent exciting new avenues to further test the 
scaling behavior of protein engineering world models.  
Second, we have also explored the axis of active learning size in this study (fig. S3). 
Similar to previous reports, larger amounts of experimental data in a closed-loop evolution 
campaign lead to better performance. The agentic feature of ProTEUS and the rise of wet lab 
automation thus represent a new exciting frontier where autonomous collections of wet lab 
experimental datapoints are guided by the model to continuously update its weight for optimal 
search of the protein sequence landscape (60–62). These data points not only guide a model’s 
current engineering campaign on a specific target phenotype, but also could be used as training 
data in future pre-training, creating a positive feedback loop.  
ProTEUS puts the protein engineering task into a mutation-resolved world-model 
framework for experimental evolution, complementing efforts to learn general representations of 
protein biology (56). In this framework, an informative joint embedding space allows the model 
to reason about a mutation's impact on a desired protein phenotype through understanding 
laboratory experiments. This provides a foundation for protein engineering in which virtual 
exploration and experimental learning advance together toward a desired function, broadly 
enabling the synthetic biology and molecular engineering community to select for protein 
sequences with the desired phenotype efficiently.

Materials and Methods 
Overview of the ProTEUS world model 
ProTEUS represented protein variants in a joint space of sequence, evolutionary and structural 
information. Mutation actions specified substitutions applied to WT or selected parental 
sequences, and the model predicted the fitness of the resulting candidates. Deep mutational 
scanning (DMS) pretraining established the shared representation, while target-specific 
measurements supported supervised fine-tuning or iterative prediction updates. A nomination 
strategy connected these predictions to successive experimental batches, with returned 
measurements informing the next round of candidate selection. 
Deep mutational scanning datasets for pretraining and evaluation 
We assembled the pretraining data from 217 substitution deep mutational scanning (DMS) 
assays in ProteinGym (29). To separate pretraining from downstream evaluation, we compared 
assay wild-type (WT) sequences with those of 38 reference sequences from held-out datasets 
reserved for downstream evaluation (fig. S1A and fig. S1B). Screening used exact-sequence, 
bidirectional-substring and UniProt-identifier matching, followed by MMseqs2 (71) homology 
searches. We excluded pretraining assays with at least 30% sequence identity and 0.5 query 
coverage to an evaluation protein. After removal of 35 assays, the corpus contained 182 assays 
representing 161 distinct WT sequences and 160 UniProt entries. The pretraining assays 
covered proteins of 37 – 3,423 amino acids (median, 224) and contained 63 – 496,137 
measured variants per assay (median, 2,238). The corpus comprised 65 stability, 61 
organismal-fitness, 32 activity, 15 expression and nine binding assays, with 80 human, 41 
prokaryotic, 31 other eukaryotic and 30 viral assays. We oriented experimental scores so that 
larger values indicated better measured performance and normalized them within each assay. 
Variants with unresolved sequences or reported WT residues inconsistent with the reference 
sequence were removed. Variants from the 182 assays were pooled and divided into training, 
validation and test sets in a 3:1:1 ratio. The test set therefore assessed prediction on held-out 
variants from pretraining assays (Fig. 2B). Each architecture comparison used the same 
held-out variants for all models within that comparison (Fig. 2D). Zero-shot prediction and 
target-specific supervised fine-tuning were evaluated on 19 assays excluded from pretraining. 
The zero-shot benchmark retained the available mixture of single- and multi-substitution 
variants in each assay (Fig. 2E); the first-round nomination benchmark used 13 
single-substitution assays (Fig. 2F). 

 
For higher-order analyses, we assembled 17 phenotype-specific DMS datasets covering 12 
proteins. These comprised 14 datasets from CombinGym (46) and combinatorial datasets for 
AAV, GFP and TrpB. Different phenotypes measured for the same protein were analyzed as 
separate datasets. The higher-order extrapolation benchmark used 15 datasets and the iterative 
multi-mutant benchmark used all 17 datasets. 
 
Mutation-resolved multimodal architecture and pretraining 
Each variant state was encoded relative to the WT sequence for its assay using aligned 
sequence, MSA and structural inputs. Inputs contained at most 512 residues. For longer 
proteins, all three streams used a common mutation-containing window and residue offset. The 
mutation mask, Mmut,i, was one at substituted positions and zero elsewhere, where i indexes 
aligned residues. 
For each variant, we extracted residue-level features from sequence, MSA, and structure 
encoders. Sequence features were taken from the final layer of ESM-2 3B (12) after removing 
beginning- and end-of-sequence tokens, yielding a 2,560-dimensional vector at each residue. 
The MSA stream used the 768-dimensional query-row representation from MSA Transformer 
(15). We used ESM3 (21) as the structure encoder. 
For each WT protein, homologous sequences were retrieved and aligned to the target. 
Redundant sequences were removed with hhfilter (72), and each alignment was limited to 128 
sequences for the primary model.

Target-specific learning and single-mutant evolution 
We evaluated target-specific supervised fine-tuning using measured variants from assays 
excluded from pretraining (Fig. 2G). Trainable parameters were initialized from DMS pretraining 
and optimized against target-assay measurements using the same MSE objective. The trainable 
components are specified in table S2. 
Fivefold cross-validation was performed independently for each assay, with four folds used for 
fine-tuning and the remaining fold for evaluation. Fold assignments were identical across 
methods, and metrics were averaged over folds within each assay. 
Single-mutant evolution was evaluated over five rounds on CALM1, GAL4, HRAS, MAPK1, 
TPK1, TPMT, UBE2I, UBI4, beta-lactamase and HaeIII methyltransferase datasets (Fig. 2H). 
ProTEUS and EVOLVEpro (3) each selected 30 variants per round from the same candidate 
library for each dataset. Accumulated measurements were used to update fitness predictions 
before each subsequent round of selection. 
At each round, we calculated the fraction of nominated variants belonging to the top 5%, 10% or 
20% of the complete DMS dataset, with Precision@10 additionally compared across 14 
datasets at batch sizes of 30, 50 and 100 variants per round (fig. S3A–C). The best fitness 
recovered by that round was normalized to the observed fitness range of the dataset. 
Round-wise trajectories report the mean normalized best fitness across datasets.

Closed-loop variant nomination and experimental feedback 
ProTEUS linked candidate generation, virtual fitness evaluation, batch nomination and 
experimental feedback within a single workflow (Fig. 1A–C). Mutation actions generated 
candidate sequences from WT or selected parental backgrounds within the permitted mutation 
depth. The platform prepared sequence, MSA and structural inputs, evaluated candidates and 
nominated variants for laboratory testing. Returned measurements were added to the 
target-specific training data to update fitness predictions before the next round of selection. 
The initial experimental batch contained 30 single mutants selected by zero-shot ranking. Their 
measured activities were used to update fitness predictions for subsequent nominations. The 

 
model-update procedure and its relationship to supervised pretraining and fine-tuning are 
described in table S2. 
Subsequent experimental rounds could retain the current maximum number of substitutions or 
admit variants carrying one additional substitution. Previously tested variants were excluded 
from subsequent batches.

Mutant library construction and expression 
Individual amino acid substitutions were introduced into Bacillus subtilis levansucrase and 
Cupriavidus pinatubonensis ALDH by inverse PCR using Q5 High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase 
(NEB). Back-to-back primers were centered on each target codon, with 15–25 bp template 
homology on each side and melting temperatures of 60–72 °C. Methylated templates were 
digested with DpnI, and products were circularized with NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly Master 
Mix (NEB) at 50 °C for 15–60 min. Assemblies were transformed into NEB Stable competent 
cells. Two to three colonies per mutant were picked and miniprepped. Sequencing confirmed 
the intended substitution and absence of secondary mutations across the coding region. One 
verified clone per mutant was retransformed into NEB Express cells for expression. Mutants 
were processed in batches of approximately 30 in 96-deep-well plates, alongside three WT 
replicates and three empty-vector controls per plate. Starter cultures contained 1 mL LB 
medium with antibiotic and were grown overnight at 37 °C and 250 rpm. Cultures were diluted 
1:30 into 1.5 mL fresh medium and grown to OD₆₀₀ 0.4–0.6; the OD₆₀₀ at induction was recorded 
for each well. Expression was induced with 0.5 mM IPTG, and cultures were shifted to 25 °C for 
4–6 h. Final OD₆₀₀ was recorded before harvest by centrifugation at 3,000–4,000 × g for 15 min 
at 4 °C. 
Activity assays for experimental screening 
Levansucrase activity was measured in CTAB-permeabilized cells. Pellets were resuspended in 
McIlvaine’s buffer (pH 6.0), normalized to the same optical density and treated with 0.1% CTAB 
for 10 min at 900 rpm. Sucrose was added to a final concentration of 100 mM, and reactions 

 
proceeded at 37 °C for 5 min. Reactions were stopped by dilution into 200 mM Tris (pH 8.3) and 
heat inactivation at 96 °C for 5 min. Released glucose was quantified with the Abcam Glucose 
Assay Kit (ab65333) as the activity readout. 
ALDH activity was measured in clarified crude lysate. Pellets were resuspended in ice-cold kit 
assay buffer containing fresh protease inhibitors and sonicated on ice at 20–30% amplitude. 
Sonication comprised three cycles of 10 s on and 10 s off. Lysates were clarified by 
centrifugation at 12,000–15,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Lysate volumes of 10–20 µL were 
adjusted to 50 µL with assay buffer and combined with reaction mix from the Abcam ALDH 
Activity Assay Kit (ab155893). NADH production was monitored using the coupled absorbance 
readout at 450 nm and 37 °C. Activity was quantified from the slope of the initial linear region of 
the kinetic curve recorded over 20–30 min. 
Enzyme quantification and concentration-normalized ALDH activity 
Both enzymes carried a C-terminal His tag. Both enzyme concentrations were quantified using a 
His-tag enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (GenScript, L00436), following the 
manufacturer’s protocol. Standard curves were fitted in GraphPad Prism using a four-parameter 
logistic model, with R² > 0.9 required for acceptance. Variant concentrations were estimated 
from the fitted curves. Measured ALDH activity was divided by enzyme concentration to obtain 
concentration-normalized activity. Enzyme concentration and concentration-normalized activity 
were each expressed relative to the corresponding WT value.  
Data and model implementation 
Pretraining data and similarity analyses 
Variant sequences in the ProteinGym (29) deep mutational scanning (DMS) corpus were 
reconstructed from reported substitutions and the assay-specific wild-type (WT) sequence. 
Sequence alignments from MMseqs2 (71) were summarized by identity, query coverage and 
aligned length. Structural alignments from Foldseek/TMalign (82, 83) were ranked by 
query-normalized TM-score, retaining the best match for each reserved entry. The structural 
analysis included 181 available pretraining structures and 38 reserved structures. Sequence 
and structural summaries are shown in Fig. S1. 
Model training and target-specific updates 
The ESM-2 (12), MSA Transformer (15) and ESM3 (21) source encoders (Table S1) remained 
fixed. DMS pretraining jointly optimized the multimodal representation model and neural fitness 
head (Table S2). Target-specific neural fine-tuning updated the same trainable components. The 
neural fitness head used the final layer-normalized register-token embedding. This learned 
token was prepended to the residue sequence with a mutation-mask value of zero. 
During iterative evolution, the pretrained representation model provided fixed inputs to a 
target-specific random forest. The random forest was refitted after each round using all 
accumulated experimental measurements. 

 
Geometric and stereochemical constraints 
Table S3 specifies the geometric and stereochemical bounds enforced by Gauss–Seidel 
projection (32). The predictor architecture, training procedure and projection equations are 
described in the section “Physics-constrained all-atom structure prediction”.

Laboratory nomination and batch selection 
Both levansucrase and aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) campaigns began with zero-shot 
variant ranking. Subsequent nominations combined zero-shot ranks, updated random-forest 
prediction ranks and fitness ranks derived from all available experimental measurements. Each 
experimental batch contained 30 variants, and round labels begin at zero. Table S5 lists the 
parental backgrounds, substitution counts and best variants for each batch. 

 
For levansucrase, candidate nominations drew on shortlists generated under different weighting 
configurations. The final batch retained two parental backgrounds, comprising 18 triple mutants 
derived from Q34N:G240W and 12 derived from Q34N:S359F. 
For ALDH, five weighting configurations were evaluated for zero-shot ranks and fitness ranks 
derived from experimental measurements. The respective weight pairs were (0, 0.2), (0, 0.4), (0, 
0.6), (0.2, 0.2) and (0.2, 0.4). The random-forest weight was one minus the sum of these two 
weights. Each configuration produced a shortlist of 30 variants. Candidates were prioritized by 
the number of shortlists in which they appeared, with ties ordered by their best rank across the 
shortlists. The top 30 candidates were selected for experimental testing. 
Analysis of laboratory evolution campaigns 
Score–activity associations 
Score–activity analyses used the zero-shot ranking in round 0. In subsequent rounds, 
composite scores combined normalized zero-shot ranks, random-forest prediction ranks and 
fitness ranks derived from all available experimental measurements, with respective weights of 
0.2, 0.6 and 0.2. Scores were rescaled to 0–1 within each round, and Spearman correlations 
included all 30 assayed variants. 
For levansucrase, composite scores were analyzed in rounds 1 and 2. 
For ALDH, the reference sequence was UniProt P46368. All nine nonpositive activity 
measurements were retained in the score–activity analysis. Cumulative best-activity and top-ten 
summaries included all variants tested up to each round. 
Protein representations and geometric analyses 
For levansucrase, sucrose from PDB 1PT2 was transferred to WT SacB (PDB 1OYG) after 
Cα-based structural superposition. Residue–substrate distances were the minimum heavy-atom 
separations between each WT residue and the superposed sucrose. For ALDH, substitutions 
were mapped to WT model AF-P46368-F1. Distances to the catalytic site were the minimum 
heavy-atom separations between each WT residue and either Glu262 or Cys301. 
Quantitative representation analyses used the original 768-dimensional vectors. Candidate 
libraries and assayed variants were visualized using t-distributed stochastic neighbor 
embedding (t-SNE). Parent–child relationships were defined by shared and newly introduced 
substitutions.

Hamming distance two. Principal component analysis (PCA) of mean-centered representations 
determined the number of components explaining 90% of the variance; the comparator encoded 
the final amino acid at each variable position by one-hot encoding (Table S9).

Local activity association 
We compared absolute activity differences between embedding neighbors with those between 
sequence-distance-matched variants. Neighbors were defined by cosine distance in the original 
representation space, excluding the anchor itself. For each directed anchor–neighbor pair, the 
reference set contained all variants in the same analysis group at the same amino acid 
Hamming distance from the anchor, including the selected neighbor. Hamming distance counted 
residue positions that differed between variants. Differences were averaged within anchors 
before aggregation across variants. 
The statistic was the matched-reference mean absolute activity difference (MAD) minus the 
neighbor MAD; relative reduction was one minus the ratio of neighbor MAD to reference MAD. 
Activity labels were permuted 19,999 times within analysis groups, keeping neighborhoods and 
sequence-distance matrices fixed. Two-sided P values were twice the smaller tail probability, 
with a plus-one correction and a maximum of 1. Variants were the permutation units, and all 
recorded activities were retained. 
For levansucrase, the analysis included the 30 triple mutants from the final round, comprising 18 
descendants of Q34N:G240W and 12 of Q34N:S359F. Neighbors, reference sets and label 
permutations were restricted to each parental background. All 30 anchors were weighted 
equally. We evaluated three, five and ten neighbors, using random seed 20261028. 
For ALDH, the analysis included the 60 four-substitution variants from rounds 3 and 4. 
Neighbors, reference sets and label permutations were restricted to each experimental batch. 
Differences were averaged across variants within each batch and then equally across the two 
batches. Five neighbors defined the primary analysis; three and ten neighbors were used for 
sensitivity analyses (Table S6). The random seed was 20261019.

Representation-based predictors were evaluated retrospectively, separately from the

Alignment-based substitution frequencies 
At each substituted position, we counted occurrences of the introduced amino acid among 
homologous sequences. The originality ratio was the introduced-residue count divided by the 
highest amino acid count at the same position. A ratio of 1 denoted a consensus residue, and 0 
denoted a residue not observed at that position in the alignment. Campaign-level summaries 
counted each distinct substitution once. 
For levansucrase, the alignment contained 2,451 homologous sequences. Frequencies used 
the number of sequences aligned without a gap at the respective position as the denominator. 
Summary frequencies covered 89 distinct substitutions. 
For ALDH, the P46368 A3M alignment contained 19,705 records, including the 506-residue 
query. Lowercase insertion characters were removed, and only sequences whose projected 
length matched the query were retained. Excluding the query left 19,704 homologous 
sequences. WT residues matched the query for all 148 substitutions across 104 positions. 
Frequencies reported in Results used all 19,704 homologs as the denominator, including 
sequences with a gap at the position; nongap coverage ranged from 256 to 18,931 sequences 
across the mutated positions. Figure 5G shows originality ratios. Counts for the four 
substitutions in the final ALDH variant are provided in Table S8.

Similarity to the pretraining set 
Structural comparisons identified related folds among held-out targets with low sequence 
identity to the pretraining set. Of the 38 held-out reference sequences, 20 had a closest 
structural match with a query-normalized TM-score of at least 0.5. Sequence identities for these 
matches ranged from 1% to 25% (Fig. S1B). 

 
Zero-shot fitness prediction 
Across the 19 held-out assays, ProTEUS ranked third by NDCG and second by top-10% recall 
(Fig. S2A). In the 13 single-mutant assays, ProTEUS had the highest mean Spearman 
correlation, whereas its NDCG and top-10% recall were slightly lower than those of the 
four-score MULTI-evolve rank-fusion comparator (Fig. S2B). 
Simulated evolution across datasets and experimental budgets 
ProTEUS achieved higher Precision@10 than EVOLVEpro in 10, 9 and 10 of the 14 datasets at 
batch sizes of 30, 50 and 100 variants per round, respectively (Fig. S3). 
With 30 variants selected per round, ProTEUS exceeded the mean EVOLVEpro result in 10 of 
14 datasets by round 5. ProTEUS reached its final best fitness in round 1 for UBE2I, whereas 
GB1 and TrpB improved subsequently. EVOLVEpro recovered higher final best fitness for 
CALM1, GAL4, HRAS and GFP. 
In silico comparison of nomination strategies 
The five nomination strategies differed in their use of zero-shot ranking, updated fitness 
predictions and fitness ranks derived from all available experimental measurements (Fig. S4; 
Table S4). 
Strategy D achieved the highest across-dataset mean normalized cumulative DMS score in 
each evaluated round. By round 5, the mean was 0.886, compared with 0.866 for the updated 
predictor alone and 0.804 for the two-component ranking. The contributing datasets differed 
among rounds, with counts specified in the Fig. S4 legend. 
Exploratory analysis of laboratory activities 
Among final-round levansucrase variants, the five-neighbor mean absolute activity difference 
was 3.524, compared with 3.030 for Hamming-distance-matched references (two-sided 
permutation P = 0.176). Prediction of round 2 from rounds 0–1 gave MAEs of 2.007 for 
embedding ridge regression and 2.992 for embedding random forest, compared with 2.007 for 
the training mean. Thus, these activity-based analyses did not show an advantage for the 
embedding readouts in the final round. 
Prediction from the preceding ALDH batch yielded a Spearman correlation of 0.26 (95% 
confidence interval, −0.10 to 0.59; two-sided permutation P = 0.158). MAE was 1.60 for the 
embedding-neighbor predictor, compared with 1.71 for the training-mean comparator and 1.80 
for the training-median comparator (Table S7). The estimated MAE reduction relative to the 
training mean was 0.12 (95% confidence interval, −0.16 to 0.39). Hamming-distance ties gave 
every training variant equal weight, producing the same predictions as the training-mean 
comparator. The intervals included zero correlation and zero MAE reduction, leaving prediction 
across these batches unresolved for this nearest-neighbor readout.
