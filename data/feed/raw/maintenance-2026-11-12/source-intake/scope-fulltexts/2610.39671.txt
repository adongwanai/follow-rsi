Yet problem solving in unfamiliar domains calls for tightly coupled knowledge acquisition and reasoning, beyond factual retrieval and aggregation or learning solely from a preassembled context.
We study self-learning—autonomously retrieving, studying, and applying knowledge from long-form reference materials on the fly.
To investigate this capability, we formulate a textbook-based mathematical problem-solving task. LLMs must navigate textbook exposition, learn and apply relevant principles, and construct proofs for exercises, with reference solutions withheld.
We introduce a BookNavigator interface to support this human-inspired study process and incentivize self-learning through reinforcement learning (RL) with fine-grained rewards.
Experiments show that our method improves performance across in-domain and out-of-domain textbooks and subjects, raising Intern-S2-Preview-35B’s overall proof accuracy from 51.5% to 79.5% and outperforming RL-trained textbook-free and fixed-retrieval baselines.
These gains extend to complex long-context benchmarks involving different task types, with improvements over the base model of 1.7 and 2.3 percentage points on BrowseComp-Plus and CL-bench, respectively.
Together, these findings suggest that RL can foster a domain-agnostic self-learning strategy, highlighting the potential of self-learning from long-form corpora to strengthen broader problem-solving capabilities on complex, long-context tasks. 1 Introduction Figure 1: Overview of textbook-based self-learning. (a) The policy alternates textbook study and reasoning to solve exercises, while a judge evaluates the final proof against a reference solution withheld from the policy, providing RL feedback. (b) Textbook-based self-learning RL outperforms the base model and textbook-free RL across our evaluation splits and external benchmarks, providing evidence of cross-domain and cross-task generalization. Large language models (LLMs) acquire broad parametric knowledge through pre-training [ 19 ] , while retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and search tools extend their access to external information at inference time [ 14 , 28 ] . Yet solving unfamiliar problems requires more than access to relevant information: models must understand new principles, assess their applicability, and incorporate them into a developing solution. We refer to this capability as self-learning —autonomously learning from reference materials as a task unfolds—which shifts the focus from expanding what LLMs know to improving how they learn to tackle unfamiliar tasks. How to develop this capability through sustained study of long-form reference materials remains underexplored. Existing research addresses related aspects of this challenge: retrieval agents trained with reinforcement learning (RL) interleave search and reasoning for answer generation [ 12 , 4 ] , context-learning studies examine knowledge acquisition from supplied materials [ 6 ] , and meta-RL and test-time adaptation methods learn from environmental observations and action outcomes [ 11 , 15 ] . In our setting, knowledge acquisition and reasoning must continually inform each other: an attempted derivation may reveal a missing prerequisite, while a newly encountered principle may reshape the solution strategy. Rather than relying on a preassembled context or feedback from repeated attempts, the model must autonomously decide what to study from static reference materials and how to integrate that knowledge into a valid derivation. Inspired by how humans study textbooks, we formulate a textbook-based mathematical problem-solving task. Each problem is paired with a structured textbook containing definitions, theorems, and proofs of general results, with the exercise solution withheld; the model must construct its own derivation by connecting relevant concepts that may be distributed across sections. We use RL to incentivize this multi-round study process, with final-proof correctness providing the core outcome signal [ 8 ] . Our goal is to develop a transferable study strategy that enables models to acquire and apply knowledge from unseen textbooks, rather than merely memorize training-book content or adapt to a particular reference environment. Specifically, our end-to-end pipeline parses advanced mathematics textbooks into chapter-indexed text, separates exercises from exposition, and removes exercise solutions from the model-visible material, yielding 5,183 proof problems from 158 textbooks. To support autonomous study, we introduce BookNavigator , an interface for inspecting the table of contents, locating relevant sections, and reading their contents as reasoning unfolds. An LLM judge assesses the final proof against the withheld reference solution. For policy optimization, we augment Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) [ 22 ] with an annealed exploration bonus and round-level tool credit, addressing the complementary challenges of sustaining textbook exploration and avoiding reinforcement of unproductive calls. The bonus maintains an incentive for textbook access, while a frozen copy of the initial policy assesses individual interactions to guide fine-grained credit assignment. We evaluate Self-Learning on held-out problems from training textbooks, unseen textbooks, and unseen subjects. After RL, it achieves the highest accuracy across all three splits and exceeds the strongest RL-trained fixed-retrieval baseline by 6.4 percentage points overall. Reference interventions indicate that self-learning RL improves not only unaided reasoning but also the acquisition and use of external knowledge. Beyond mathematics, the trained policy improves overall performance on BrowseComp-Plus [ 5 ] and CL-bench [ 6 ] under their original inference protocols, without relying on BookNavigator . Ablations further show that correctness-only RL can improve accuracy while suppressing textbook exploration, whereas combining exploration incentives with round-level tool credit yields stronger cross-domain performance without maximizing tool-call frequency. Together, our findings support self-learning from abundant self-supervised corpora, such as textbooks, as a promising means of strengthening LLMs’ general ability to handle complex-context tasks, offering a path toward autonomous evolution. 2 Method Inspired by how humans study, we formalize self-learning in a textbook-based mathematical problem-solving task. We first formulate the task and the self-learning protocol in Section 2.1 . We use an end-to-end pipeline to construct problem–textbook pairs from raw textbooks (Section 2.2 ) and equip models with BookNavigator to navigate the resulting structured reference materials (Section 2.3 ). We refine RL training to encourage exploration and discourage unproductive textbook access (Section 2.4 ). We first parse each textbook into chapter-indexed text sequences, preserving its hierarchical organization for section-level navigation and reading. Exercises and their corresponding solutions are extracted separately from the expository content and linked to their source textbooks. The remaining reference materials retain definitions, theorems, explanatory passages, and proofs of general results. To make the problem statements self-contained, we further exclude exercises that explicitly refer to textbook section numbers.
The resulting tasks demand deliberate, self-directed study rather than simple answer retrieval. The model-visible textbook ℬ \mathcal{B} is represented as an ordered sequence of sections, each consisting of a section identifier ι \iota , a title ℓ \ell , and the retained text x x : ℬ = ( s 1 , … , s K ) , s k = ( ι k , ℓ k , x k ) , \mathcal{B}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{K}),\qquad s_{k}=(\iota_{k},\ell_{k},x_{k}), (4) where K K is the number of sections. 2.3 BookNavigator: Interactive Textbook Study BookNavigator is the sole interface between the model and its paired textbook. It supports three complementary study operations—orienting within the book, locating potentially relevant material, and reading selected sections—through three types of textbook-access actions in 𝒜 \mathcal{A} . get_toc supports global orientation by returning the complete table of contents of ℬ \mathcal{B} , including section identifier–title pairs ( ι k , ℓ k ) (\iota_{k},\ell_{k}) organized according to the book’s hierarchy. This provides an overview of the available material and its organization. search_index accepts a query containing one or more keywords and performs keyword-based retrieval across all textbook sections. It returns a ranked list of section identifiers ι k \iota_{k} together with matching snippets from the corresponding texts x k x_{k} . The query and section texts are normalized and tokenized before relevance scores are computed using weighted term coverage, term proximity, exact-phrase matches, and title relevance. read_section takes a section identifier ι k \iota_{k} and returns the complete retained text x k x_{k} of the corresponding section s k s_{k} , including any definitions, theorems, proofs, and examples it contains. This enables the model to examine material identified through the table of contents or search results in its full section context. Each action returns an observation o t o_{t} , which is appended to the interaction history. The model can therefore interleave mathematical reasoning with textbook access and adapt subsequent actions to both its developing solution and previously retrieved information. 2.4 Reinforcement Learning with Fine-grained Rewards Rewarding the final solution alone provides limited guidance on individual retrieval decisions [ 26 , 21 ] : a successful trajectory may contain redundant retrieval, while improving problem-solving ability may reduce the incentive to consult references. We therefore combine an annealed exploration bonus with round-level tool credit, encouraging textbook access while reducing reinforcement of clearly unproductive calls. Exploration Bonus. Early in reinforcement learning, unfamiliarity with the interaction protocol may discourage textbook access. As training on textbook-based problems improves the model’s ability to solve problems independently, the incentive to consult references may diminish further. To sustain exploration throughout training, we introduce an annealed exploration bonus. For each trajectory τ \tau with a correct final solution, i.e., r ⁡ ( τ ) = 1 r(\tau)=1 , we add an exploration bonus b ⁡ ( τ ) b(\tau) if at least one tool call is invoked successfully.

We anneal β n \beta_{n} using a cosine schedule with a positive floor, providing stronger encouragement for early exploration while retaining a smaller incentive for textbook access later in training. Tool observations provide context but are excluded from the policy loss. 3 Experiments 3.1 Experimental Setup Dataset and Evaluation Splits. We collect 158 undergraduate- and graduate-level mathematics textbooks covering areas such as analysis, algebra, number theory, and geometry, from which we extract 5,183 mathematical problems.
Each textbook contains 56 sections totaling 208k tokens on average, providing a long-form study environment with intricate dependencies across sections. The training set comprises 4,557 problems from 132 textbooks.
To evaluate the transferability of self-learning, we define three mathematical evaluation splits:
(1) In-Domain Book , comprising 205 held-out problems from 4 textbooks included in training;
(2) Out-of-Domain Book , comprising 155 problems from 7 textbooks excluded from training but covering subjects represented in the training set;
and (3) Out-of-Domain Subject , comprising 266 problems from 19 textbooks on probability and statistics, both held out from task-specific training.
We additionally evaluate Out-of-Domain Task generalization on BrowseComp-Plus and CL-bench.
These settings examine generalization to new problems, textbooks, subjects, and task types, respectively, helping distinguish transferable self-learning from adaptation to domain-specific knowledge or task protocols encountered during training.
Detailed statistics and a problem–textbook example are provided in Appendix A . Problem-Solving Protocols. We compare our Self-Learning protocol with No-Book and three fixed-retrieval baselines. (1) Self-Learning interleaves reasoning with textbook access through BookNavigator , with at most eight interaction turns and a total trajectory length capped at 64k tokens. (2) No-Book receives only the problem as input. (3) Top- k k Sections uses the search_index action to retrieve relevant sections before generation and appends up to eight sections to the problem in relevance order, subject to a 64k-token input budget. (4) Long-Context appends sections in their original textbook order within the same 64k-token input budget. (5) Oracle Section appends the source section from which the problem was extracted, as identified by its provenance metadata. All baselines generate a single response without tool interaction during generation, with a maximum output length of 64k tokens. These comparisons assess both the value of textbook access and whether adapting reading to ongoing reasoning offers advantages over providing fixed context in advance. Prompting details and a Self-Learning trajectory example are provided in Appendix B . RL Implementation. All RL runs are initialized from Intern-S2-Preview-35B [ 10 ] and trained with GRPO for 50 rollout steps. GLM-5.2 [ 7 , 29 ] evaluates solution correctness against the reference solution using 8-shot pessimistic verification [ 9 , 13 ] : a proof receives r ⁡ ( τ ) = 1 r(\tau)=1 only if all 8 independent reviews judge it correct, and r ⁡ ( τ ) = − 1 r(\tau)=-1 otherwise. We use a frozen copy of the initial model as the tool judge to keep the assessment consistent across training variants. Tool judgments are mapped to c t = 0.5 c_{t}=0.5 for harmful and c t = 1 c_{t}=1 for useful or neutral , with redistribution strength α = 1 \alpha=1 . Correct trajectories containing at least one successful tool call receive an exploration bonus of b ⁡ ( τ ) = 1 b(\tau)=1 , scaled by β n \beta_{n} , which follows a cosine schedule from 1 to 0.25 over the 50 rollout steps. The exploration bonus and tool credit are used only during RL training under the Self-Learning protocol, whereas baseline RL uses standard GRPO with final-proof correctness as the sole reward. We evaluate all protocols solely on proof accuracy and report the mean accuracy over four independently sampled solutions per problem.

Improved knowledge acquisition and reasoning may therefore not translate directly into more reliable procedural execution. 3.5 RL Ablation We ablate round-level tool credit and the annealed exploration bonus to examine their effects on proof accuracy and textbook-access behavior, as shown in Table 4 . All RL variants use the Self-Learning protocol for both training and evaluation, with identical settings except for reward and advantage computation. Process feedback improves in-domain learning. Adding tool credit to correctness-only RL increases ID-Book accuracy by 3.3 percentage points, from 60.7% to 64.0%. Since the tool judge is a frozen copy of the initial policy, this gain does not require a stronger model for tool-use assessment. Instead, the initial model itself provides useful supervision by evaluating individual study interactions. The result highlights supervision granularity as a source of improvement: feedback about intermediate decisions can complement final-proof correctness even without upgrading the tool judge. Accuracy gains do not ensure exploration. Correctness-only RL raises overall accuracy from 51.5% to 74.9%, while the proportion of tool-using trajectories drops sharply from 36.9% to 6.7% and average calls decrease from 1.5 to 0.3 per trajectory. Tool credit alone does not reverse this tendency, with only 7.0% of trajectories invoking tools. One plausible explanation is that stronger problem-solving ability enables the policy to solve more problems without references, reducing its incentive to consult textbooks. This tendency may limit transfer to unfamiliar problems that require active knowledge acquisition. Consistent with this possibility, tool credit alone changes OOD-Book and OOD-Subject accuracy by only − 0.8 -0.8 and + 1.0 +1.0 points relative to correctness-only RL. Exploration incentives improve cross-domain transfer. The annealed exploration bonus addresses this missing incentive, increasing the proportion of tool-using trajectories to 79.9% and improving OOD-Book and OOD-Subject accuracy by 2.0 and 2.9 percentage points over correctness-only RL. These results support complementary roles for exploration incentives and process-level credit: the combined method achieves stronger generalization without maximizing tool-call frequency. Retrieval-augmented generation grounds predictions in external documents [ 14 ] , while ReAct interleaves reasoning with actions that acquire information [ 28 ] .
Search-R1 and R1-Searcher learn search policies through reinforcement learning with outcome-based rewards [ 12 , 24 ] .
This line extends to coordinating multiple retrieval tools in MARAG-R1 [ 18 ] and searching scientific literature in PaperSearchQA [ 3 ] .
KARL further combines large-scale off-policy RL with synthetic training tasks across diverse search regimes, including cross-document synthesis and procedural reasoning, and studies out-of-distribution generalization [ 4 ] .
Our work instead centers training on textbook-grounded proof construction: exercises and their solutions are removed from the reference exposition, and the agent is trained to select and apply definitions and theorems rather than retrieve an included exercise solution.
The distinction is therefore not retrieval versus reasoning, but coordinating knowledge acquisition with an evolving reasoning process. Meta-Learning and Test-Time Adaptation. In-context learning enables language models to adapt to tasks using exemplars provided in the prompt, without parameter updates [ 2 ] .
Beyond exemplar-based task adaptation, CL-bench evaluates context learning: whether models can acquire and apply unfamiliar knowledge, rules, and procedures from supplied materials [ 6 ] .
For interactive agents, Reflexion carries feedback-derived verbal reflections across attempts without updating model weights [ 23 ] , whereas MCTR combines metacognitive memory with test-time RL updates [ 15 ] .
LaMer explicitly optimizes cross-episode returns so that exploratory attempts benefit subsequent ones, using reflection for in-context adaptation with fixed test-time weights [ 11 ] .
SEAL takes a parameter-updating approach: RL trains a model to generate self-edits, whose utility is measured by downstream performance after fine-tuning on those edits [ 31 ] .
We instead train a policy to acquire and apply knowledge within a single problem-solving episode, using static reference material without correctness feedback from repeated attempts or test-time weight updates.
Our connection to meta-learning is thus the goal of learning an acquisition strategy, rather than a cross-episode meta-RL objective or persistent test-time parameter adaptation. Fine-grained Agentic RL. To supplement sparse outcome rewards, StepSearch introduces step-wise information-gain and redundancy-aware supervision for search [ 26 ] .
SLATE samples truncated single-step alternatives from a shared prefix and uses decomposed LLM-assigned process rewards for reasoning, query formulation, and answer quality [ 21 ] .
IG-Search instead measures the increase in gold-answer likelihood under retrieved documents relative to random-document counterfactuals, feeding this signal to search-query tokens through advantage modulation [ 16 ] .
Beyond retrieval, OpenClaw-RL extracts evaluative and directive supervision from subsequent interaction observations for online policy updates [ 25 ] .
Our training combines a correctness-gated, annealed exploration bonus with round-level tool credit: a frozen judge assesses a call using its interaction context and the following reasoning, without access to the reference solution or final correctness.
Unlike shared-prefix branching or gold-answer likelihood scoring, this credit is computed on sampled trajectories and centered to preserve their token-weighted mean advantage, redistributing credit away from harmful rounds while retaining the trajectory-level outcome signal. 5 Conclusion We study self-learning in LLMs: the ability to autonomously acquire, understand, and apply knowledge to solve unfamiliar tasks.
We introduce a textbook-based framework that interleaves mathematical reasoning with autonomous textbook study and incentivizes self-learning through fine-grained agentic RL.
Extensive experiments provide evidence for a domain-agnostic learning strategy, with learned capabilities transferring to unseen textbooks, subjects, and task types.
We hope this work opens a path towards leveraging long-form corpora as a source of self-supervision to strengthen general problem-solving on complex, long-context tasks through self-learning. References [1] Arash Ahmadian, Chris Cremer, Matthias Gallé, Marzieh Fadaee, Julia

B.2 BookNavigator Prompt The Self-Learning protocol appends the following prompt to the shared solver instructions to guide textbook interaction through BookNavigator . # Book-Navigation Self-Learning Tool You have access to a `BookNavigator`, a textbook-centered retrieval tool. Use it only when textbook evidence can resolve a concrete uncertainty, recover a needed definition or theorem, or verify assumptions. Tool output is study material rather than a substitute for reasoning. The final solution must be self-contained and must not ask the reader to trust a tool, textbook, or hidden work. The ordinary multi-turn conversation retains every earlier tool result, including complete section bodies. Reuse that context and never repeat a deterministic call. Full sections can be large, so retrieve only material tied to a concrete knowledge gap. A successfully executed call is not necessarily a successful retrieval: use the structured status, content state, similarity, and visible snippet to judge whether useful evidence was found. ## BookNavigator actions - `BookNavigator.get_toc`: returns the complete section-ID/title tree for the assigned textbook together with exact model-token counts and content states. It does not return section bodies. It is mandatory once, and only once, before another BookNavigator action whenever retrieval is used. - `BookNavigator.read_section`: strictly resolves an exact ID copied from TOC/search and returns metadata plus the complete, untruncated stored Markdown body. The body can consume substantial context. `truncated=false` means there is no hidden middle portion; it does not guarantee that source extraction was complete. - `BookNavigator.search_index`: searches substantive body passages and returns actual matching snippets, match types, matched terms, score components, section token/state metadata, and absolute lexical similarity. It never substitutes the current section as a fake fallback. `status=no_match` has an empty result list. Examples of the intended policy: - If an exercise is numbered `8.5.24`, use `get_toc`, inspect the 8.5 entry’s state and size, then read that exact section when appropriate. Do not begin with repeated searches for the whole exercise wording. - If the TOC is Chinese, prefer a concise Chinese textbook term over a long English paraphrase when search is still necessary. - If a TOC entry reports `state=heading_only`, do not read it and do not fabricate the absent definition. ## Mandatory TOC-first retrieval controller 1. First decide whether retrieval is needed. If the problem is already clear and you can solve it reliably, answer without calling a tool. 2. If you decide to retrieve anything from the textbook, your first `BookNavigator` call in the session MUST be `BookNavigator.get_toc`. Call it exactly once, by itself, before any `search_index` or `read_section` call. 3. Inspect each TOC entry’s `tokens` and `state`. Entries with `state=container` only organize the hierarchy. Never read `empty` or `heading_only` entries. Avoid `too_long` entries unless the exact section is essential and its full body is worth the context cost. 4. Map the problem to plausible exact section IDs. If the problem names a chapter, section, theorem, definition, or exercise number and the corresponding entry has `state=body`, prefer one direct `read_section` call over searching first. Copy the ID exactly; the environment will not silently redirect a malformed or missing ID to a nearby section. 5. Otherwise call `search_index` with a short, distinctive textbook term, preferably in the same language as the TOC or retrieved text. Do not paste the whole question. Search returns `status`, real matching passages, and an absolute `similarity` in `[0, 1]`: at least `0.80` is a strong lexical match, `0.55` to `0.80` is weak and must be checked carefully, and `status=no_match` is no evidence. 6. Read at most the best-supported section when the returned snippet does not contain enough surrounding definition, theorem hypotheses, or derivation.
