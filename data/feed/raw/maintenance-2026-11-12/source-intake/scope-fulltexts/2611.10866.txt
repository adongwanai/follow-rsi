The Surprising Effectiveness of Deleting Weights in LLM Post-Training Jack Lu * Affiliation: New York University Zhenbang Yang * Affiliation: New York University Mike Lasby Affiliation: University of Calgary Tejas Pote Affiliation: University of Calgary Yani Ioannou Affiliation: University of Calgary Mengye Ren Affiliation: New York University Abstract How little of a pretrained large language model (LLM) has to change for it to acquire new capabilities?
We find that zeroing fewer than 0.05 % 0.05\% of its weights, with no other modification, matches full fine-tuning (FFT) at scale across three LLM post-training regimes: reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, supervised fine-tuning, and on-policy distillation.
Our method, Deletion Tuning , learns a binary mask over the gate projection of transformer blocks.
Two practical advantages follow: the trained mask is hundreds to thousands of times smaller than the corresponding FFT checkpoint, and Deletion Tuning forgets far less, which addresses a central challenge in continual learning. Under reinforcement learning, its prior-task accuracy stays near the base level while FFT, LoRA, and random sparse fine-tuning lose up to 5.4 5.4 points.
To understand why masking alone suffices, we analyze the geometry of the learned weight changes under reinforcement learning using stable rank, spectrum drift, and principal-weight overlap.
Compared with LoRA and random sparse fine-tuning, Deletion Tuning is closest to FFT across the three measures. * Equal contribution. Project page: https://agenticlearning.ai/deleting-weights/ 1 Introduction To acquire new capabilities during LLM post-training, full fine-tuning can alter every parameter, LoRA ( Hu et al.,, 2022 ) learns a low-rank update, and the Multiple Ticket Hypothesis (MTH) ( Adewuyi et al.,, 2026 ) and SHiRA ( Bhardwaj et al.,, 2024 ) optimize values at selected weight positions.
Weight deletion is typically used differently: pruning removes weights while preserving an already-trained model’s capabilities for efficient inference ( Sun et al.,, 2024 ; Frantar and Alistarh,, 2023 ) , and the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis finds sparse subnetworks that can be trained to match dense networks ( Frankle and Carbin,, 2019 ) .
Unlike full fine-tuning, LoRA, and sparse updates, deletion writes no new values and only chooses which pretrained weights to keep. Recent work shows that fine-tuned solutions lie close to the pretrained model. RL fine-tuning changes only a small subnetwork ( Mukherjee et al.,, 2025 ) , and task experts lie densely around pretrained weights ( Gan and Isola,, 2026 ) . We push this observation to its limit across reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and on-policy distillation (OPD), and ask: Can a pretrained LLM acquire new capabilities by learning only which weights to set to zero? The answer is yes across all three post-training regimes.
We propose Deletion Tuning , which learns only a binary mask over the gate-projection weights of transformer blocks.
At deployment, each target weight either retains its pretrained value or is set to zero, so the learned binary mask is the entire adapter.
We test Deletion Tuning with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) ( Shao et al.,, 2024 ) for RLVR, standard SFT, and Self-Distillation Fine-Tuning (SDFT) ( Shenfeld et al., 2026a, ) for OPD.
Our experiments span two model families, eight backbones from 0.5 0.5 B to 30 30 B, and both in-distribution and out-of-distribution evaluation.
At the largest model scale tested in each setting, Deletion Tuning matches full fine-tuning (FFT) across reasoning, domain knowledge, and tool-use tasks. The binary-mask form brings two practical advantages: (i) the adapter is compact , with the trained mask hundreds to thousands of times smaller than the corresponding FFT checkpoint and smaller than even LoRA r = 1 r{=}1 in our main GRPO comparison; and (ii) Deletion Tuning forgets less , with its forward KL remaining low and its prior-task accuracy staying near the base level across all three Llama scales. Under FFT, LoRA, and random sparse fine-tuning, forward KL grows and prior-task accuracy falls (Figure 2 ), consistent with forward KL as a predictor of forgetting ( Shenfeld et al., 2026b, ) . Learning new tasks while keeping old ones makes Deletion Tuning a promising candidate for continual learning. Finally, we examine why Deletion Tuning can match FFT with deletion alone.
We confirm that the gains come from learning the mask rather than the Wanda prior used in its initialization.
We also find that the geometry of Deletion Tuning’s learned weight updates tracks FFT’s more closely than LoRA or random sparse fine-tuning across stable rank, spectrum drift, and principal-weight overlap. Our contributions are as follows: • Deletion alone matches FFT. Deletion Tuning learns which gate-projection weights to delete (Section 4 ). It is on par with FFT at the largest scale we test in GRPO, SFT, and SDFT, up to a 30B mixture-of-experts model (Sections 5.2 and 5.6 ). Under GRPO, it also solves problems that the base model fails in all 128 128 sampled attempts (Section 5.2 ). • Deletion Tuning is compact and forgets less. Its masks are orders of magnitude smaller than FFT checkpoints in all three regimes (Sections 5.2 and 5.6 ), and it keeps prior-task accuracy throughout training (Section 5.4 ). • Deletion Tuning’s learned updates have FFT-like geometry. Its weight updates resemble FFT’s on three geometric measures (Section 5.5 ), which helps explain its accuracy. 2 Related Work LLM post-training. We study three post-training regimes that differ in where the training signal comes from. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) imitates fixed labeled targets ( Wei et al.,, 2022 ; Ouyang et al.,, 2022 ; Taori et al.,, 2023 ) . Reinforcement learning optimizes a reward on the model’s own samples, from PPO-based RLHF ( Schulman et al.,, 2017 ; Ouyang et al.,, 2022 ) to GRPO ( Shao et al.,, 2024 ) with verifiable rewards, the recipe behind DeepSeek-R1 ( Guo et al., 2025a, ) . With verifiable rewards, rule-based checkers or LLM verifiers score each sample ( Lu et al., 2026a, ) . DPO ( Rafailov et al.,, 2023 ) instead turns preferences into a closed-form loss. On-policy distillation trains a student on its own rollouts to match a teacher’s per-token distribution ( Agarwal et al.,, 2024 ; Shenfeld et al., 2026a, ; Zhao et al.,, 2026 ) , and SDFT uses it to reduce catastrophic forgetting ( Kirkpatrick et al.,, 2017 ) . Self-distillation can also teach reasoning behaviors ( Sprague et al.,, 2026 ) . We use all three to test whether deletion works as a general post-training mechanism. Sparsity and selective parameter updates. One-shot LLM pruning methods like Wanda ( Sun et al.,, 2024 ) and SparseGPT ( Frantar and Alistarh,, 2023 ) extend classical magnitude-based pruning ( Han et al.,, 2015 ; Zhu and Gupta,, 2018 ) to LLM scale, while continuous relaxations ( Louizos et al.,, 2018 ; Maddison et al.,, 2017 ; Jang et al.,, 2017 ) make binary masks differentiable. Unstructured pruning can even retain the test-time scaling of reasoning models ( Monjur et al.,, 2026 ) . Earlier mask learning adapted vision backbones and encoder-only language models to downstream tasks such as image and sentence classification ( Mallya et al.,, 2018 ; Zhao et al.,, 2020 ; Radiya-Dixit and Wang,, 2020 ) .
Recent mask methods target semi-structured LLM inference sparsity ( Fang et al.,, 2024 ) , refinement of a fine-tuned LLM ( Zhang et al.,, 2025 ) , or vision-language backbones ( Zheng et al.,, 2023 ) . Deletion Tuning instead makes the mask the entire post-training update of a pretrained LLM across RLVR, SFT, and OPD, and it learns how many weights to delete.
Sparse weight tuning writes float deltas at selected positions ( Guo et al.,, 2021 ; Sung et al.,, 2021 ; Adewuyi et al.,, 2026 ; Liu et al.,, 2025 ; Bhardwaj et al.,, 2024 ) , additive adapters introduce trainable float parameters ( Hu et al.,, 2022 ; Morris et al.,, 2026 ; Sun et al.,, 2025 ; Houlsby et al.,, 2019 ; Lester et al.,, 2021 ; Lu et al., 2026b, ) , and robust fine-tuning methods restrict or regularize movement of pretrained weights under distribution shift ( Lee et al.,, 2023 ; Tian et al.,, 2024 ) . Deletion Tuning instead keeps every retained weight at its pretrained value and learns only which gate-projection weights to delete. Understanding LLM fine-tuning. The classical Lottery Ticket Hypothesis ( Frankle and Carbin,, 2019 ) posits that randomly initialized networks contain sparse subnetworks that train to comparable accuracy in isolation. Recent work characterizes pretrained-model fine-tuning along three axes. Density: task-expert solutions densely populate the neighborhood of pretrained weights ( Gan and Isola,, 2026 ) . Geometry: Mukherjee et al., (2025) report that RL fine-tuning naturally updates only 5 5 to 30 % 30\% of parameters across PPO, GRPO, DPO, and several model families. Zhu et al., (2026) show these updates land off the principal weights of W 0 W_{0} while leaving the spectrum largely intact, where principal weights are the top-magnitude entries of the rank- k k SVD reconstruction of W 0 W_{0} ( Liu et al.,, 2025 ) . Forgetting: Shenfeld et al., 2026b () establish that the forward KL between fine-tuned and base policies on the new task accurately predicts forgetting. Deletion Tuning reproduces the off-principal update pattern and low spectrum drift. Deletion Tuning’s low forward KL also predicts the strong prior-task retention we measure (Section 5.4 ). Compact storage and ease of deployment. The trained adapter is the hard mask 𝟏 [ G > 0 ] \mathbf{1}[G>0] at each masked layer, which takes one bit per masked pretrained weight before compression. We bit-pack the booleans with NumPy and compress the bit stream with zstd ( Collet and Kucherawy,, 2021 ) . Because fewer than 0.05 % 0.05\% of all weights are deleted in every GRPO run of Table 1 , the bit stream is highly redundant, and zstd compresses it to 1.0 1.0 MB at Llama1B and 8.4 8.4 MB at Llama8B. This gives Deletion Tuning a practical deployment advantage in compact storage and cheap adapter switching: changing tasks only loads a compact bitmask and scatters zeros into the base weights, rather than loading a separate full model checkpoint. Connection to MaskLLM. MaskLLM ( Fang et al.,, 2024 ) learns 2 : 4 2{:}4 masks for inference speedup with a Gumbel-Softmax selector over N : M N{:}M candidate patterns. Deletion Tuning has a different goal, expanding model capability, and a different form, with one deterministic logit per weight and no fixed sparsity pattern (Appendix F relates the two formally). Low spectrum drift and sub-random principal-weight overlap are the RLVR signature reported by Zhu et al., (2026) : RLVR leaves the singular values of each weight matrix nearly unchanged and steers its updates away from the principal weights. LoRA caps the rank of its update at r r , and MTH trains weights chosen at random before training. These two freedoms help explain how Deletion Tuning reaches FFT-like geometry even though it never sets a weight to a new non-zero value. 5.6 Generalizing Beyond GRPO: Results on SFT and SDFT Does deletion also work when the training signal is denser or comes from outside the model? GRPO samples from the model itself and rewards whole responses, and RL updates of this kind are known to be sparse ( Mukherjee et al.,, 2025 ) . SFT instead imitates fixed external targets token by token and produces dense updates ( Mukherjee et al.,, 2025 ) , which makes it a harder test for an adapter that can only delete. SDFT sits in between: the student samples its own responses, and a teacher supplies a target distribution at every token. We test Deletion Tuning with SFT on knowledge and reasoning benchmarks (MMLU-Pro and BBEH) and with SDFT on tool use (ToolAlpaca) and science questions (Science), using the setups of Section 5.1 . Under SFT, Deletion Tuning matches FFT at 3B on both MMLU-Pro and BBEH (Table 5 ). Under SDFT, Deletion Tuning matches FFT on both Qwen7B tasks (Table 6 ). We sweep η ∈ { 1 / 2048 , 1 / 4096 , 1 / 8192 } \eta\in\{1/2048,1/4096,1/8192\} , 𝑙𝑟 ∈ { 10 − 4 , 3 × 10 − 4 } \mathit{lr}\in\{10^{-4},3{\times}10^{-4}\} , and λ ∈ { 0 , 10 − 5 } \lambda\in\{0,10^{-5}\} on Llama8B GSM8K and Qwen7B SimpleRL-Zoo (Tables 14 and 14 in Appendix G ). Every setting lands within 1.6 1.6 points of the best run on Llama8B and within 3.2 3.2 points on Qwen7B, and the single configuration we use for all main runs is at or near the best setting at both backbones. Varying the initial sparsity η \eta by 4 × 4{\times} changes accuracy by at most 0.8 0.8 points on Llama8B and 1.7 1.7 points on Qwen7B at fixed 𝑙𝑟 \mathit{lr} and λ \lambda , so the default configuration is a reasonable starting point for new tasks. 6 Conclusion We introduced Deletion Tuning , which fine-tunes a model by learning only a binary mask over its gate projections. At the largest scale we test in each regime (Qwen14B and the Qwen3-30B-A3B mixture-of-experts model under GRPO, Qwen7B under SDFT, and Llama3B under SFT), it matches full fine-tuning. The mask is smaller than a rank- 1 1 LoRA and hundreds to thousands of times smaller than an FFT checkpoint. In our GRPO retention runs, Deletion Tuning keeps prior-task accuracy near the base level throughout training, while FFT, LoRA, and MTH lose up to 5.4 5.4 points. Its weight updates also resemble FFT’s more closely than those of LoRA or MTH across stable rank, spectrum drift, and principal-weight overlap, which helps explain how deletion alone can match FFT. These results suggest that post-training can work by selection alone, keeping nearly every pretrained weight and choosing the few to delete. Limitations and Future Work. Because Deletion Tuning forgets little, a natural next test is continual learning over a sequence of tasks. Training keeps one logit per masked weight, so Deletion Tuning uses less training memory than FFT but more than LoRA. Restricting the logits to a small set of candidate weights could close this gap. The masks are also small and discrete, which aids interpretability: comparing which weights different tasks delete could localize the changes that post-training makes to a model. Masks for different tasks might also be combined, for example by taking their union, to merge skills without further training. Finally, restricting the mask to N : M N{:}M , channel-wise, or block patterns could let one mask both adapt and compress the model. After three structural modifications to Eq. ( 9 ) (binary candidates, no per-group cardinality, no Gumbel noise), the modified MaskLLM forward matches Eq. ( 5 ) algebraically. MaskLLM’s forward. The base weight matrix is partitioned into groups of M M contiguous weights, and each group is handled independently. Within a single group, MaskLLM enumerates C ⁡ ( M , N ) = ( M N ) C(M,N)=\binom{M}{N} candidate binary patterns M ^ c \widehat{M}_{c} with c ∈ { 0 , … , C ⁡ ( M , N ) − 1 } c\in\{0,\dots,C(M,N)-1\} , each keeping exactly N N of M M entries. It places a learnable logit vector π ∈ ℝ C ⁡ ( M , N ) \pi\in\mathbb{R}^{C(M,N)} over the candidates and draws a Gumbel-Softmax sample ( Jang et al.,, 2017 ; Maddison et al.,, 2017 ) : y ~ c = exp ⁡ ( ( κ ​ π c + g c ) / τ ) ∑ c ′ exp ⁡ ( ( κ ​ π c ′ + g c ′ ) / τ ) , g c ∼ iid Gumbel ⁡ ( 0 , 1 ) . \widetilde{y}_{c}\;=\;\frac{\exp\!\big((\kappa\,\pi_{c}+g_{c})/\tau\big)}{\sum_{c^{\prime}}\exp\!\big((\kappa\,\pi_{c^{\prime}}+g_{c^{\prime}})/\tau\big)},\qquad g_{c}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{Gumbel}(0,1). (9) The group’s soft mask is the candidate-pattern-weighted average M ~ = ∑ c y ~ c ​ M ^ c \widetilde{M}=\sum_{c}\widetilde{y}_{c}\widehat{M}_{c} , and the layer forward applies W ⊙ M ~ W\odot\widetilde{M} within the group. At evaluation, the hard mask is M ^ c ⋆ \widehat{M}_{c^{\star}} with c ⋆ = arg ⁡ max c ⁡ π c c^{\star}=\arg\max_{c}\pi_{c} . The temperature τ \tau and logit-scale κ \kappa both follow linear schedules over training. Step 1: C ⁡ ( M , N ) = 2 C(M,N){=}2 collapses the two-candidate softmax to a sigmoid. Set M = 2 , N = 1 M{=}2,N{=}1 , so each group has exactly two candidates: M ^ 0 = ( 1 , 0 ) \widehat{M}_{0}=(1,0) keeps position 0 and M ^ 1 = ( 0 , 1 ) \widehat{M}_{1}=(0,1) keeps position 1. With only two candidates, the Gumbel-Softmax probability on candidate 0 simplifies to a sigmoid of the logit difference: y ~ 0 = e ( κ ​ π 0 + g 0 ) / τ e ( κ ​ π 0 + g 0 ) / τ + e ( κ ​ π 1 + g 1 ) / τ = 1 1 + e − 1 τ ​ [ κ ⁡ ( π 0 − π 1 ) + ( g 0 − g 1 ) ] = σ ⁡ ( 1 τ ​ [ κ ⁡ ( π 0 − π 1 ) + ( g 0 − g 1 ) ] ) , \widetilde{y}_{0}=\frac{e^{(\kappa\pi_{0}+g_{0})/\tau}}{e^{(\kappa\pi_{0}+g_{0})/\tau}+e^{(\kappa\pi_{1}+g_{1})/\tau}}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\frac{1}{\tau}[\kappa(\pi_{0}-\pi_{1})+(g_{0}-g_{1})]}}=\sigma\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{\tau}\big[\kappa\,(\pi_{0}-\pi_{1})+(g_{0}-g_{1})\big]\Big), (10) where the second equality divides numerator and denominator by the first exponential, and the third uses σ ⁡ ( x ) = 1 / ( 1 + e − x ) \sigma(x)=1/(1+e^{-x}) .
The difference of two independent Gumbel ⁡ ( 0 , 1 ) \mathrm{Gumbel}(0,1) variables is Logistic ⁡ ( 0 , 1 ) \mathrm{Logistic}(0,1) , so y ~ 0 = σ ⁡ ( 1 τ ​ [ κ ​ Δ ​ π + ξ ] ) , Δ ​ π := π 0 − π 1 , ξ ∼ Logistic ⁡ ( 0 , 1 ) . \widetilde{y}_{0}\;=\;\sigma\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\tau}[\kappa\,\Delta\pi+\xi]\big),\qquad\Delta\pi:=\pi_{0}-\pi_{1},\ \ \xi\sim\mathrm{Logistic}(0,1). (11) Eq. ( 11 ) is the Binary-Concrete distribution ( Maddison et al.,, 2017 ) . Step 2: drop the per-group cardinality constraint. MaskLLM forces each group of M M weights to keep exactly N N of them. Letting each weight choose its own binary mask value replaces this group categorical with an independent binary masking choice per weight, parameterized by a single logit G i ​ j G_{ij} . Each weight then has its own Binary-Concrete (Eq. ( 11 ) applied per-weight, with G i ​ j G_{ij} in place of Δ ​ π \Delta\pi ): M ~ i ​ j = σ ⁡ ( 1 τ ​ ( κ ​ G i ​ j + ξ i ​ j ) ) , ξ i ​ j ∼ Logistic ⁡ ( 0 , 1 ) . \widetilde{M}_{ij}\;=\;\sigma\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\tau}(\kappa\,G_{ij}+\xi_{ij})\big),\qquad\xi_{ij}\sim\mathrm{Logistic}(0,1). (12) Step 3: drop the noise. Setting the noise ξ i ​ j \xi_{ij} to zero in Step 2’s per-weight Binary-Concrete gives a deterministic soft mask: M ~ i ​ j = σ ⁡ ( κ τ ​ G i ​ j ) . \widetilde{M}_{ij}\;=\;\sigma\!\big(\tfrac{\kappa}{\tau}\,G_{ij}\big). (13) This matches Eq. ( 5 ) with the scalar α ⁡ ( t ) := κ ⁡ ( t ) / τ ⁡ ( t ) \alpha(t):=\kappa(t)/\tau(t) ; in our implementation, we schedule this scalar directly and linearly.
