Recent evaluations cover diverse tasks but often rely on existing benchmarks, where prior exposure to test material can obscure generalization.
Moreover, real-world decision making often involves long contexts, large option spaces, and complex logical dependencies, challenges that existing benchmarks do not adequately capture together.
Thus, we introduce MMDM (Massive Multitask Decision Making), a benchmark of 5,448 high-quality questions across 16 task categories, combining newly constructed tasks with source-grounded recompositions and rewrites for single-select, multi-select, and soft-label decision making in fresh contexts. It covers realistic scenarios such as agent state tracking, tool and skill selection, preference judgments, and rubric scoring, with option spaces ranging from 2 to 1,024 and inputs extending to 64,817 tokens.
With MMDM, we conduct a comprehensive quantitative evaluation of 28 decision models, showing that many perform well on common knowledge yet struggle with interdependent options, constrained decisions, and multi-select tasks. For example, Perplexity Decider-V1.2 reaches 78.75% Hard Single-select Accuracy on world knowledge, but only 53.95% on option logic and 47.95% on constraint planning, while the best Hard Multi-select Accuracy across all models is 36.81%. These results highlight joint reasoning, constraint-aware selection, and complete multi-select recovery as key priorities for decision-model development, with MMDM providing a testbed for measuring this progress. Therefore, a rigorous and comprehensive evaluation of these models must account for the diversity and complexities of decision making in real-world scenarios. Recent benchmarks have assessed decision models across diverse tasks and calibration criteria ( Duan et al., 2026 ; Solka, 2026 ; Standhartinger, 2026 ) . However, many existing evaluations draw heavily on widely circulated public benchmarks, making test-set contamination a central concern for benchmark validity ( Oren et al., 2024 ) . Moreover, real-world decisions often combine long contexts, large option spaces, and complex logical dependencies. For example, a practically useful model needs to track the current agent state, determine whether an earlier approval remains valid, and select an eligible tool from a large catalog with heterogeneous prerequisites. These settings call for an evaluation that combines task breadth with new instances and explicit tests of these capabilities. In this paper, we introduce MMDM (Massive Multitask Decision Making), a benchmark of 5,448 questions for evaluating text-based System-1 Decision Models across realistic decision scenarios. It combines broad task coverage with explicit tests of context understanding, option discrimination, and logical consistency. Table 1 compares its scope with existing decision benchmarks. MMDM combines all four properties with 1,024-way decisions and long inputs (Detailed distribution is shown in Table A1 ). Specifically, MMDM evaluates Hard Single-select , Hard Multi-select , and Soft-label questions, requiring a unique correct choice, the complete valid set, or a prediction matching the reference distribution over outcomes. These answer types capture practical requirements such as choosing the next actions, identifying all eligible tools, and assigning probabilities to rubric scores under uncertainty. Decisions can depend on agent states, action prerequisites, or relationships among options. In benchmark construction, source answers and contextual evidence ground rewrites of states, queries and distractors to reduce memorization-induced score inflation ( Zhu et al., 2024 ; Zhao et al., 2025 ) .
We combine these adaptations with recompositions and newly constructed tasks. The resulting 16 categories span knowledge and comprehension, logic and constraints, agent and workflow decisions, and preference and rubric judgments. With 2–1,024 options and complete inputs of up to 64,817 tokens, MMDM also tests these capabilities across varied context lengths and option spaces. As shown in Figure 1 , our evaluation of 28 decision models reveals substantial differences across capabilities. Models that perform well on world knowledge still struggle with interdependent options, decisions subject to constraints, and complete multi-select recovery. These capabilities matter in agent workflows, where choices interact, actions have prerequisites, and several tools or skills may be valid for the current goal. Specifically, Perplexity Decider-V1.2 ( Perplexity AI, 2026 ) achieves 78.75% Hard Single-select accuracy on world knowledge, compared with 53.95% on option logic and 47.95% on constraint planning. WeMM-JEV 27B ( WeMM-JEV Team, 2026 ) achieves the highest Hard Single-select accuracy of 76.00%, while the highest Hard Multi-select accuracy is 36.81%, highlighting the challenge of recovering all valid options. Model rankings also differ between hard-answer accuracy and soft-distribution error, making both necessary to characterize decision quality. Overall, our contributions are threefold. First, we introduce a multitask decision benchmark that combines fresh decision instances with long contexts, large option spaces, and interdependent options. Second, we provide a unified evaluation protocol for single-select correctness, multi-select completeness, and soft-label distributional fidelity, together with an open dataset and toolkit. Third, our evaluation reveals capability gaps that are not captured by previous decision-model benchmarks, including failures in joint consistency and complete multi-select recovery, providing concrete directions for future model development. 2 Related Work System-1 Decision Models. System-1 Decision Models produce structured probabilities over request-defined options in a single inference step.
Jev introduces a typed decision interface and Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions, making probability quality a central training objective ( TypeSafe AI, 2026 ) .
After this closed-source model, open implementations explore different ways to obtain these decisions from language-model backbones. SemIf reads logits for option tokens directly and supports shared-prefix reuse across queries ( Lee, 2026 ) . OpenJev learns a shared decision head over request-defined options, using supervised learning followed by reinforcement learning ( Shen, 2026 ) . Intern-Decision and H2O-Lightning extend typed decisions to multimodal inputs ( Zhu et al., 2026 ; H2O.ai, 2026 ) . Microsoft Decision-1 post-trains Qwen3.5-9B for single-pass scoring in routing, verification, and workflow control ( Microsoft, 2026 ) .
Together, these approaches develop efficient readout, task adaptation, and probability calibration. Their growing use in software and agent pipelines makes it essential to evaluate whether they preserve decision quality when the relevant evidence, prerequisites, and options interact. Benchmarks for Decision Making. JevBench establishes an early comparison of typed decision quality, calibration, latency, and cost ( Standhartinger, 2026 ) . DecisionBench broadens applied coverage to 43 tasks with runtime-defined options ( Solka, 2026 ) , while ReflexBench separated raw model quality from workflow-policy effects ( Navarro, 2026 ) . DecideBench subsequently introduced 400 contrastive decisions to test sensitivity to answer-changing context ( Yong, 2026 ) . Jev Evaluation examined 37 existing datasets with frozen templates ( Deußer et al., 2026 ) , and JEVal expanded systematic evaluation across 36 datasets and ten domains ( Duan et al., 2026 ) .
These tool kits improve coverage and comparability, but their main evaluations leave complete multi-select decisions underexplored. Additionally, broad source reuse also leaves a need for fresh decision instances Oren et al. (2024) .
Complementary resources target individual capabilities. AgentBench evaluates interactive agents ( Liu et al., 2024 ) . ToolBench evaluates tool-use tasks ( Qin et al., 2024 ) . LongBench and LongBench v2 studies long-context understanding in challenging realistic tasks ( Bai et al., 2024 ; Bai et al., 2025 ) . MuSiQue evaluates multi-hop evidence composition ( Trivedi et al., 2022 ) . They provide valuable capability tests, while a common assessment of option selection and probability allocation across these settings remains necessary.
MMDM addresses this gap by combining fresh decision scenarios, long inputs, and large option spaces with single-select, multi-select, and soft-label objectives. 3 The MMDM Benchmark MMDM evaluates decision capabilities across diverse tasks through a shared state–query–options interface. A known score gives a hard single-select reference; uncertain outcomes give a soft distribution over levels. The expected score summarizes this distribution on the rubric’s numeric scale. Lower values are better for both. 3.2 Data Collection and Construction Collection. We select public datasets and agent trajectories that support a concrete decision with explicit options and a checkable answer. Sources span knowledge, reading, reasoning, preferences, code, and agent interactions. For example, QuALITY ( Pang et al., 2022 ) and LongBench v2 ( Bai et al., 2025 ) supply document-grounded questions, BIRD ( Li et al., 2023 ) supplies database problems, and ComplexFuncBench ( Zhong et al., 2025 ) and ToolTalk ( Farn and Shin, 2023 ) supply interaction histories. Source questions and annotations provide the basis for adaptation and verification. Construction. We combine source-grounded adaptation with semantic recomposition and newly constructed tasks. For rewritten source instances, we retain the original answers and supporting evidence while reformulating the state, query, and distractors. Semantic and option-quality checks select the resulting instances. This strategy builds on evidence that benchmark rewriting can mitigate memorization-induced score inflation ( Zhu et al., 2024 ; Zhao et al., 2025 ) . Recompositions introduce decision questions grounded in source databases or agent histories. Executable mechanisms construct interacting options, constrained plans, state transitions, and rubric outcomes with computable answers. Across all three routes, distractors are designed around identifiable failure modes, such as applying an expired rule, confusing entities, or overlooking the prerequisite. Validation and Selection. We verify answers using source evidence, executable checks, and model review appropriate to each construction route. Five-model majority screening is followed by independent human expert review of borderline cases. Both stages apply the same quality standards for distractors, answer leakage, duplicates, and source overlap. An option-only probe masks the state and query; instances with unresolved shortcuts are excluded. Appendix B details the review procedure and selection rules. 3.3 Task Coverage and Dataset Statistics Figure 2: Task coverage and answer semantics. The colors of the 16 task categories are fixed across figures. Labels show the total count followed by (Hard Single-select / Hard Multi-select / Soft-label) counts. MMDM contains 5,448 questions across 16 task categories, comprising 4,112 Hard Single-select, 633 Hard Multi-select, and 703 Soft-label questions. The 663 Score questions include 35 with hard levels and 628 with soft targets. The collection covers 62 registered source identifiers and 80 category–subcategory combinations.
Option sets range from 2 to 1,024 options, including 94 questions with more than 255 options. Complete inputs include the state, query, option names, and criteria. We measure complete inputs with the Qwen3.5-4B tokenizer from the Qwen family ( Yang et al., 2025 ) . Their median length is 827 tokens, the 95th percentile is 13,318, and the maximum is 64,817. These ranges support evaluation from common short decisions to long-document reasoning and large-catalog selection. The categories connect knowledge and language understanding with decisions about evidence, rules, actions, and assessments. They cover world knowledge and reading comprehension; deductive, quantitative, and option-dependent reasoning; agent state tracking, tool and skill selection, and workflows; and preference, rubric, and emotion judgments. We evaluate 28 System-1 decision models and use Random as a heuristic reference. These models return option probabilities directly through a decision-scoring head or a single decoding step, without generated reasoning or adaptive thinking. They include hosted APIs and locally deployed checkpoints. Each model uses its supported context window and option limit, preserving the capabilities available in its released checkpoint or API. Appendix C.1 details model selection, deployment settings, and the Random reference. Protocol and Metrics. The evaluator preserves the query and all option names and criteria, deterministically removing the middle of the state when the context budget is exceeded. Independent option sets exceeding a model’s option cap are divided into groups that fit the cap. Each request scores all options in a group together. From each group, the highest-probability option advances for single-select, and up to | G | |G| highest-probability options advance for multi-select. This reduction repeats until the retained options fit one final request. Reference cardinality and gold identities are hidden from the model. Option-dependent and distributional tasks keep the entire option set without splitting; inputs exceeding the model’s option cap count as capacity failures. Grouped outputs contribute to hard selection metrics; full-distribution metrics use native responses.
We use the metrics defined in Section 3.1 . Hard accuracies retain every question in the corresponding track, including failed requests. SB-F, Soft Brier, and Score nMAE use native predictions.
Appendix C.2 specifies token budgets, grouping, and failure accounting. 4.2 Overall Performance Similar single-select accuracy can accompany very different probability quality. In Table 2 , WeMM-JEV and KnowLine differ by less than one percentage point in single-select accuracy. Yet KnowLine has nearly half the Soft Brier error and less than one-third the Score nMAE of WeMM-JEV. A model that chooses the right option frequently can therefore still be a poor fit for applications that use its probabilities or expected scores. Complete multi-select identification and probability support remain weak. The highest multi-select accuracy in Table 2 is only 36.81%, and every model has an SB-F below 0.20. This means that current models often fail to recover the complete set of valid options and to assign probability appropriately across them. Thus, improving multi-select decisions requires progress not only in finding all correct options, but also in representing their relative support reliably. Expected-score accuracy and distribution fidelity favor different models. Jev achieves the lowest Soft Brier, while KnowLine achieves the lowest Score nMAE. GPT-6 Luna Decisions also has a lower Score nMAE than Microsoft Decision-1 despite a higher Soft Brier. This difference arises because the expected score compresses an entire probability distribution into a single value, allowing probability errors at different score levels to cancel out. Therefore, reporting both metrics shows whether a model predicts a useful final score and whether the probabilities behind that score are themselves accurate. WeMM-JEV leads Deductive Reasoning in Table 3 , yet scores below 60% on Option Logic, Constraint Planning, and Workflow Decision. KnowLine shows the same contrast, with 86.0% on Deductive Reasoning but 54.6% on Option Logic and 48.5% on Constraint Planning. In these tasks, an option may look correct on its own but conflict with another option, a rule, or an earlier action. MMDM separates these requirements from deduction, exposing a weakness in joint consistency that the broad knowledge-and-reasoning score of JEVal does not isolate. Similar overall accuracy can hide very different strengths across tasks. Table 3 shows that WeMM-JEV performs better on world knowledge and emotion recognition, while KnowLine is stronger on workflow decisions and policy handling. Models with lower overall scores can still lead particular categories. Quyet achieves the highest accuracy on Evidence Validation, while Mapika leads Emotion Recognition. These differences show why category-level results matter when selecting a model for a specific workload, since a higher overall score does not guarantee better performance on the capabilities that workload actually requires. Strong routing performance does not guarantee strong agent decision making. GPT-6 Luna Decisions outperforms Jev and Microsoft Decision-1 on Intent Routing, but performs worse on Tool and Skill Selection, Agent State Tracking, and Workflow Decision. The largest gap appears in Workflow Decision, which requires the model to account for prerequisites and action order. This shows that routing correctly does not necessarily mean the model can also choose the right action from the current agent state or coordinate a multi-step workflow. 4.4 Confidence Calibration Overconfidence appears at different operating ranges. Figure 3 compares five representative models using reliability curves and expected calibration error (ECE), where lower is better. Intern-Decision is overconfident in the middle of its range. OpenJev is closer to the diagonal there, but its highest-confidence bin averages about 0.97 confidence and 0.87 accuracy. Thus, this difference matters in deployment because the same confidence threshold may be reliable for one model but overly aggressive for another one when making decision. Higher selection accuracy does not ensure better calibration. OpenJev has higher single-select accuracy than Intern-Decision in Table 2 , yet Figure 3 gives it a larger ECE. Perplexity and Jev achieve the lowest ECE among the five representatives, while Microsoft Decision-1 has the largest error and remains below the diagonal through much of the moderate-to-high confidence range. The curves and aggregate errors together show why model selection should account for both how often a model is correct and how reliably its confidence reflects that correctness. 4.5 Context and Option-Space Scaling Model rankings change between long inputs and large option sets. The two panels of Figure 4 reveal different scaling profiles. OpenJev outperforms Intern-Decision in every option-count bin, but falls below it once complete inputs exceed 8K tokens. At 8K–16K tokens, their accuracies are 15.5% and 34.5%, respectively; on 129–1,024 options, they are 26.0% and 8.3%. This reversal distinguishes performance on long-input workloads from performance on large-option workloads. MMDM measures both axes because success on one does not predict the model ordering on the other. Large catalogs expose differences hidden by compact choices. In Figure 4 , Jev and Microsoft Decision-1 differ by about seven percentage points on 2–4 options, but by more than 37 points on 129–1,024 options. Perplexity retains the highest accuracy in the largest option bin, although its advantage over Jev is much smaller there than on 9–16 options. Across the released workloads and the protocol in Section 4.1 , the curves reveal both severe losses and changing relative advantages as the option space grows. A single aggregate accuracy would conceal these differences in the scale of decisions each model can handle. 4.6 Case Studies Models can accept a workflow or report that conflicts with other evidence. In Figure 5 (a), Perplexity V1.2 selects a workflow that approves a document before editing it. Publication requires approval of the final revision, so those later edits invalidate the certificate. Jev and Microsoft Decision-1 select a valid ordering. In Figure 5 (b), Hazel’s report is false under the unique consistent interpretation, yet all three models rank it above at least one true report. The two cases expose errors in checking an option against history or other options, even when the relevant conditions are explicit. Models struggle to translate a simple record-level defect into the correct score distribution. In Figure 5 (c), eight of nine records satisfy every rubric criterion, while one record lacks a review stamp. A three-record sample receives the lower score only when it contains that record, which happens with probability one-third. However, Perplexity V1.2 and Jev assign more probability to the lower score, while Microsoft Decision-1 predicts the correct most likely score but still assigns the probabilities incorrectly. This example shows that even a simple scoring rule can lead to errors in both the predicted score and its probability distribution. 5 Conclusion We introduced MMDM, a benchmark of 5,448 questions across 16 task categories for evaluating text-based System-1 Decision Models. With option sets ranging from 2 to 1,024 and inputs reaching approximately 64K tokens, MMDM tests decisions across both familiar requests and complex contexts. Its single-select, multi-select, and soft-label tasks assess whether models choose correctly, identify all valid options, and assign useful probabilities and scores.
Our evaluation of 28 decision models reveals clear gaps between these capabilities and exposes dimensions of decision quality that are not captured by previous benchmarks.
The strongest models vary across answer types and task categories. High single-select accuracy can accompany inaccurate probabilities, while deduction and routing strengths coexist with weaknesses in joint constraints and workflow decisions. Reliability curves and scaling profiles further distinguish confidence quality from accuracy and long-input performance from large-option performance. These findings direct progress toward checking interacting conditions, handling larger decision spaces, and producing probabilities that support reliable choices and scores. We release MMDM and its evaluation toolkit to make progress on these capabilities easy to measure and compare. Limitations MMDM has three main limitations. First, it focuses on text-based decisions defined by a fixed state, query, and option set. Extending MMDM to multimodal inputs and interactive trajectories would broaden its coverage of real-world decision settings.
Second, multi-select questions are concentrated in option logic, constraint planning, workflows, and tool or skill selection, while most soft targets come from rubric scoring. These settings provide clear labels and verifiable target distributions, but broader evaluation of subjective judgments will require references that better capture variation across human assessments.
Third, MMDM combines newly constructed tasks with source-preserving adaptations, recompositions, and rewrites. Although recompositions and rewrites introduce new decision requirements and contexts, prior exposure to the underlying public source material may still occur. Ethics Statement MMDM combines publicly available research data with newly constructed decision tasks. We cite the source datasets and document their contributions in Appendix B . Source materials remain subject to their original terms of use. The same appendix describes model-assisted construction, label verification, and targeted human review, including independent assessment and adjudication of disagreements. Our experiments evaluate model predictions from recorded or simulated inputs; they do not execute the tools or workflows described in the questions. Jev exposes three interfaces for different kinds of requests ( TypeSafe AI, 2026 ) . They specify what the model returns. The answer types in Section 3.1 specify what makes a prediction correct and how it is evaluated. Choice compares named options. A support request might ask whether a ticket belongs to billing, shipping, or returns, with each option’s criterion defining its responsibilities. The response contains the selected option and a probability for every option. In MMDM, this distribution supports evaluation against a single correct option, a set of equally valid options, or a soft reference, according to the task’s answer type. Noul answers a yes/no query, such as whether a message requests a refund. It returns the probability of yes; values near one favor yes, values near zero favor no, and values near one-half express uncertainty between them. The remaining probability belongs to no, giving the two-option distribution used in evaluation. A definite reference answer is hard single-select, while a reference that quantifies uncertainty is soft-label. Score evaluates an input against ordered, descriptive rubric levels. For report completeness, these might range from missing required evidence to meeting every requirement. The response contains a probability for each level and their probability-weighted numeric score. MMDM evaluates both the level distribution and the expected score. Therefore, Scoring questions belong to the hard single-select or soft-label track, depending on whether the reference identifies one level or a distribution. B.2 Original Task Construction We construct new decision instances by specifying a mechanism, computing its reference answer, and rendering its states and conditions as natural language. The mechanism determines which details affect the decision. Distractors implement plausible mistakes, including an omitted prerequisite, an expired authorization, a stale artifact, a confused entity, or a missed exception. The final families and their validation procedures appear in Table A4 . Interdependent Options and Constrained Plans. Logic tasks define propositions whose truth values depend on other propositions. We enumerate satisfying assignments and check them with an independent truth-table implementation. Single-select questions ask for the unique complete interpretation; multi-select questions ask for all true propositions under that interpretation. Global interpretation rules are stated in the state, and interdependent statements appear in option.criteria . We check entity references, quantifier scope, and natural-language statements against the formal propositions.
Planning tasks evaluate complete proposed assignments against eligibility, scheduling, resource, and precedence constraints; all feasible plans remain valid options. State Transitions and Workflows. State tasks reconstruct the current situation from events and tool receipts. Their mechanisms track goal scope, artifact versions, committed or rolled-back updates, evidence readiness, and execution failures. Transactional histories are checked both by folding the event sequence and by independent SQLite replay. Workflow tasks compare complete action sequences under prerequisites and revision-sensitive certificates. A simulator tracks state and content versions, while a separate checker verifies action positions and required orderings. Workflow option sets contain at most 128 sequences. Scenario and Rule Composition. Additional original tasks combine policy conditions, evidence, tools, practical planning, and scoring in concrete scenarios. Compositional rule programs introduce thresholds, exceptions, authority levels, document versions, and joins between pieces of evidence. Natural-language renderings preserve the facts and rules that determine the computed answer. For tasks with finite uncertain outcomes, the state supplies the underlying weights, transition probabilities, or sampling process used to derive the reference distribution. Hard Single-select and Hard Multi-select. A unique correct option y y is encoded as π j = 𝟏 [ j = y ] \pi_{j}=\mathbf{1}[j=y] . B.4 Cleaning, Correction, and Verification We initially collected 683,237 instances from public sources, retaining 669,135 after normalization and deduplication. We subsequently constructed 75,588 additional instances, producing a pool of 744,723 for selection. Representation and Semantic Checks. We check unique item and option identities, complete option references, valid score scales, normalized nonnegative labels, and agreement between answer types and references. Source-parent and request-overlap checks compare related instances with registered training, replay, and evaluation records.
Answers are checked against source evidence, execution, independent formal computation, or blind review. Repairs undergo the corresponding check again; unresolved failures are excluded. Option Quality. We further check whether the options can be distinguished from the information available in the state and query, rather than from superficial cues. Distractors should reflect realistic failure modes, such as overlooking a prerequisite or choosing a tool with similar functionality but incompatible requirements. We also mask the state and query and ask whether the answer can still be guessed from option names or descriptions alone. Options with obvious shortcuts, copied answers, or weak distinctions are removed. For SQL tasks, we additionally check whether different queries are semantically equivalent and whether surface similarity reveals the answer. Multi-model Screening. Candidate instances are screened by DeepSeek ( DeepSeek-AI, 2026 ) , GPT-6-Astra ( OpenAI, 2026b ) with high reasoning effort, Gemini-3.8-Flash ( Google DeepMind, 2026 ) , Qwen3.8-Max ( Qwen Team, 2026b ) , and GLM-5.2 ( Z.ai, 2026 ) . Each model independently judges whether an instance meets the representation, semantic, and option-quality standards above. Four or five accept votes support automatic inclusion; a 3–2 vote sends the instance to human review, and fewer than three accept votes exclude it. This stage accepted 4,715 instances automatically and referred 928 borderline cases for human review. Independent Human Review. Two reviewers independently assessed all 928 borderline cases under a common standard operating procedure (SOP). The SOP applies the representation, semantic, and option-quality standards above, using source evidence, verification traces, and option-only probe results from the earlier checks. Reviewers verify that the state and query support the reference answer, that all valid options are represented, and that distractors reflect plausible errors rather than superficial cues. C.2 Protocol and Metrics Context and Option Handling. For context budget B B , service allowance H H , and output reserve R R , the adapter preserves the query, all options, and their criteria. It keeps approximately equal token spans from the start and end of the state and recounts the serialized request against B − H − R B-H-R . Truncation preserves Unicode boundaries and inserts an omission marker. Runs record the provider or proxy tokenizer. Fixed-field overflow and provider rejection count as failures; grouped calls share the prepared state.
All requests follow the protocol in Section 4.1 . A supported catalog is evaluated in one call. An independent catalog above the option cap is reduced recursively, with each call presenting all options in its group alongside the same prepared state. The highest-probability option in each group advances for hard single-select. For hard multi-select, the evaluator uses N = | G | N=|G| both as the evaluation cutoff and as the maximum number retained from each group; neither N N nor the reference option identities enter model requests. The metric evaluates complete-set ordering at this reference cardinality. Option-dependent and distributional tasks retain the complete option set without splitting. If that set exceeds a model’s option cap, the request is recorded as a capacity failure. Implementation and Averaging. Hard accuracies include all questions and count boundary ties, failed requests, and missing outputs as incorrect. The implementation treats probability differences of at most 10 − 12 10^{-12} as ties. SB-F, Soft Brier, and Score nMAE use valid native outputs over the complete option set. Each Score error is normalized by that item’s rubric range. Confidence Calibration. We also measure how well single-select confidence predicts correctness using expected calibration error (ECE) ( Guo et al., 2017 ) . For L L predictions partitioned into ten equal-width confidence bins ℬ b \mathcal{B}_{b} , ECE = ∑ b = 1 10 | ℬ b | L ​ | a ¯ b − u ¯ b | , \operatorname{ECE}=\sum_{b=1}^{10}\frac{|\mathcal{B}_{b}|}{L}\left|\bar{a}_{b}-\bar{u}_{b}\right|, (A3) where a ¯ b \bar{a}_{b} is mean correctness and u ¯ b \bar{u}_{b} is mean maximum option probability in bin b b ; empty bins contribute zero. Lower ECE indicates closer agreement between confidence and accuracy. Matched calibration analyses use the same single-select questions with valid, native, full-state predictions and unique maxima across the compared models. Unified Model Output. Every decision model returns a probability distribution over the options supplied in its request. The answer-type annotation and reference number of correct options are not included in the model input. Thus, single-select, multi-select, and soft-label questions share the same prediction interface, without an additional prompt specifying the target answer type or set size. A valid top choice satisfies max i ∈ G ⁡ p i > b \max_{i\in G}p_{i}>b , where b b is the largest incorrect-option probability. Thus, the same confidence threshold corresponds to different error rates across tasks.
To capture this variation, we compute ECE separately within each task category using the same ten confidence bins, then weight the results by question count. This prevents overconfidence in one task from cancelling underconfidence in another. ECE rises from 0.054 to 0.106 for Jev, from 0.085 to 0.110 for Intern-Decision, and from 0.092 to 0.116 for OpenJev. We examine 596 sampled-rubric questions using native predictions from all five models on complete inputs. For predictions with nMAE at most 0.05, equivalent to an error of at most 0.2 points on the 0–4 scale, we measure distribution error using total variation, TV ⁡ ( p , π ) = 1 2 ​ ∑ j | p j − π j | \operatorname{TV}(p,\pi)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}|p_{j}-\pi_{j}| . This is the probability mass that must be reassigned to match the reference. Jev meets this nMAE threshold on 190 questions and Intern-Decision on 111. Their mean TV errors remain 0.323 and 0.306, respectively. Thus, a close estimate of the expected score can still misrepresent the probabilities of individual score levels.
Each question scores a random subset of records against four criteria. The expected total score depends on the four individual pass probabilities, while the full score distribution also depends on which criteria pass together. Using the exact pass probabilities, we construct an approximation that treats the criteria as independent. It preserves the expected score but produces mean Brier and TV errors of 0.0127 and 0.0690 across the 596 questions. This illustrates why accurate expected scores and accurate score distributions require different information.
