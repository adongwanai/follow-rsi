Muller Philippe Bich Chiara Boretti Hyun-Min Chang Jiawei Zhuang Lukas Cavigelli Abstract Weight-activation quantization can reduce the memory and compute
cost of neural-network inference, but recent methods often require expensive
optimization and architecture-specific propagation of basis transforms across
layers. We introduce MACaQ, an MXFP4 W4A4 post-training quantization
method that treats every linear layer independently. MACaQ applies a
per-channel scale and a block-Hadamard transform online to each layer’s
activations, while absorbing the corresponding inverse transforms into its
weights. Retaining these transforms online avoids cross-layer dependencies and
enables a simple streaming implementation of the quantization process,
in which only one layer must reside
in memory at a time. To improve over round-to-nearest quantization, we introduce
Flip-Refinement, a Hessian-aware coordinate-descent procedure that greedily
moves individual FP4 codes to adjacent representable values. Despite executing
the activation transforms at inference time, MACaQ adds only 2.3 % 2.3\% end-to-end overhead over pure MXFP4 on Qwen3-32B in vLLM. MACaQ reaches lower perplexity than DuQuant++ and MR-GPTQ, and already matches their best perplexity on Llama-3-8B in 11× less quantization time. We further apply MACaQ without
architecture-specific modifications to an image diffusion model, illustrating
the generality of its layer-local design and show quantization of a 122B parameter
model in 64 minutes, using only 32.5GB GPU RAM. Introduction Perhaps the central idea in recent neural network quantization strategies is the
change of basis. To compute the matrix product between an input and a weight
matrix, we may transform the input by a matrix P P and apply the
corresponding inverse transform to the weights, leaving the product unchanged.
If P P is chosen wisely, the transformed inputs and weights can be
substantially easier to quantize. Figure 1 : Prior work propagates the activation-side change of basis
(labeled P P ) outside the linear layer to avoid runtime overhead.
This incurs an engineering and quantization-time overhead,
where for each layer a suitable absorption target must be found.
MACaQ instead applies a lightweight transform online, preserving complete
layer locality at negligible (ca. 2%) run-time overhead for sufficiently large layers. Two types of transform are widely used to ease quantization. First, activations
and weights may be rotated into a different basis using an orthogonal matrix M rot M_{\mathrm{rot}} . The Hadamard transform is especially popular
because it can be applied in O ⁡ ( N ​ log ⁡ N ) O(N\log N) operations and tends to produce
approximately Gaussian-distributed outputs ( 4 ) . Second, individual channels
may be rescaled using a diagonal matrix M diag M_{\mathrm{diag}} ( 29 , 18 , 21 ) , thereby suppressing outlying channels and
balancing the quantization difficulty of activations and weights. One potential downside of such a change of basis is that, although the
weight-side transform can be absorbed into the weights at quantization time,
the corresponding input transform ostensibly needs to be applied online. Prior
work often avoids this cost by propagating the input transform through the
network and absorbing it into preceding layers. This approach is widely
used because it can eliminate the explicit runtime transform ( 1 ) . However, it also introduces dependencies between
layers: applying the transform to one linear layer may require modifying its
normalization parameters, preceding projections, residual branches, or other
connected operators. In this paper, we argue for tolerating the overhead introduced by online
transforms because keeping them online provides a different benefit: simplicity through layer locality . MACaQ confines every transform and
quantization decision to a single linear layer. No neighboring layer is
modified to compensate for its quantization, and no basis transform needs to be
propagated through the network. Eliminating these inter-layer dependencies
allows each linear layer to be calibrated, transformed, and quantized
independently. This simplifies application to new architectures, enables layers
to be loaded and quantized one at a time, and leads to substantially faster
quantization. Furthermore, all linear layers can benefit from online basis transforms,
where in some architectures not all layers can have absorbed basis transforms. The cost of this design choice is small. MACaQ uses only a per-channel scale and
a block-Hadamard transform online. Their cost grows with the input dimension,
whereas the cost of the subsequent matrix multiplication also grows with the
output dimension. Consequently, their relative overhead diminishes for larger
layers. We measure an end-to-end decode overhead of 2.3 % 2.3\% for Qwen3-32B in
vLLM, while obtaining a better quality-quantization-time trade-off than prior
MXFP4 methods. The main contributions of this paper are: • We propose MACaQ , a strictly per-linear-layer pipeline for W4A4 MXFP4
quantization.
MACaQ achieves higher output quality than
state-of-the-art MXFP4 methods while requiring up to 11 × 11\times less
quantization time, see Fig. 2 . • We show that retaining lightweight basis transforms online incurs
only a small inference overhead while eliminating cross-layer dependencies .
This simplifies engineering, see Fig. 1 , enables layer-at-a-time
quantization with low memory requirements, and allows the same method to
work on widely different model architectures. • We introduce Flip-Refinement , a fast Hessian-aware coordinate-descent
method that improves an initial round-to-nearest quantization assignment by
moving individual FP4 codes to adjacent representable values. • We provide efficient Triton kernels for MACaQ-quantized MXFP4
models in vLLM, achieving up to 3 × 3\times end-to-end speedup over FP16 while
limiting the overhead of online transforms to 2.3 % 2.3\% on
Qwen3-32B. Figure 2 : Quantization time vs. WikiText-2 perplexity on Llama-3-8B (OCP MXFP4 W4A4),

activation channels while simultaneously balancing the weight columns. There
is a natural trade-off: scaling down a given activation channel scales up the
corresponding weight column, and vice versa ( 29 ) . before reaching this budget. Cost of Online Transforms The activation-side pipeline of a MACaQ linear layer is: (i) apply the per-channel scale, (ii) apply the block-Hadamard transform, and (iii) quantize the result to MXFP4. Activation quantization is required
by any W4A4 MXFP4 implementation, so the additional operations introduced by
MACaQ are only the scale and the micro-rotation. For a layer processing T T tokens with input dimension n n and output dimension m m , a block-Hadamard transform of size B B requires approximately T ​ n ​ log 2 ⁡ B Tn\log_{2}B additions, while the scale requires T ​ n Tn multiplications. By
comparison, the matrix multiplication performs approximately 2 ​ T ​ m ​ n 2Tmn arithmetic operations. The ratio is therefore C online C matmul = T ​ n ​ ( log 2 ⁡ B + 1 ) 2 ​ T ​ m ​ n = log 2 ⁡ B + 1 2 ​ m . \frac{C_{\mathrm{online}}}{C_{\mathrm{matmul}}}=\frac{Tn(\log_{2}B+1)}{2Tmn}=\frac{\log_{2}B+1}{2m}. (17) For B = 32 B=32 , this becomes C online C matmul = 3 m . \frac{C_{\mathrm{online}}}{C_{\mathrm{matmul}}}=\frac{3}{m}. (18) Both online transforms are applied once to each input and reused across all m m output channels. Their cost is therefore independent of m m , whereas the
cost of the matrix multiplication grows linearly with m m . For example, the
smallest output dimension among the quantized linear layers of Llama-3-8B is m = 1024 m=1024 , giving an arithmetic-operation ratio below 0.3 % 0.3\% . This estimate describes arithmetic work rather than wall-clock performance.
The measured overhead also depends on kernel fusion, memory traffic, launch

measure the actual kernel-level and end-to-end overhead in
later sections. Consistent with the
analysis above, the relative overhead decreases with layer and model size. Quantization Procedure MACaQ preserves the layer-wise modularity of the underlying network: each
linear layer has its own scaling factors, block rotation, Hessian, block
exponents, and FP4 codes. Quantizing one linear layer never requires modifying a
neighboring normalization layer, projection, or residual branch. Forward hooks accumulate H ℓ = ∑ k X ℓ , k ⊤ ​ X ℓ , k H_{\ell}=\sum_{k}X_{\ell,k}^{\top}X_{\ell,k} (19) for every linear layer ℓ \ell over chunks of the calibration sequence. After
collecting these statistics, each linear layer is independently scaled,
rotated, exponent-screened, and refined. The original layer is then replaced by
its packed MXFP4 implementation. Only the layer currently being processed needs to reside on the
GPU (and for the calibration, outputs of parallel layers).
The remaining model weights remain in host memory. This streaming
procedure reduces peak device memory and allows the same
quantization implementation to be reused across model architectures; all calibration
and quantization logic is attached locally to linear layers. Implementation Details MACaQ offers two levers for trading quantization time against output
quality: the number of calibration tokens and the maximum
number K K of Flip-Refinement iterations. We produce the Pareto curve in Fig. 2 by sweeping these two
parameters. Quality begins to plateau at approximately 2 × 10 6 2\times 10^{6} calibration tokens and K = 512 K=512 Flip-Refinement iterations. For fair comparison we use K = 128 K=128 and 2 × 10 5 2\times 10^{5} calibration tokens for MACaQ in the experimental section (ca. 150s to quantize Llama3-8B). Besides this, our default configuration uses block size 32 32 , exponent offsets { + 1 , 0 , − 1 } \{+1,0,-1\} , scale-balancing parameter α = 0.75 \alpha=0.75 , and ridge coefficient λ = 1.0 \lambda=1.0 . Optimal Brain Damage (OBD) ( 15 ) and Optimal Brain
Surgeon (OBS) ( 12 ) that introduced Hessian-based model compression.
Optimal Brain Compression (OBC) ( 8 ) adapted this framework to
layer-wise compression and introduced Optimal Brain Quantization (OBQ), using
the layer-input Hessian H = X ⊤ ​ X H=X^{\top}X to minimize output reconstruction error.
GPTQ ( 9 ) subsequently made this approach scalable to large language
models by fixing the quantization order, allowing the same Hessian factorization
to be reused across output rows. These methods assign weights sequentially and
compensate the remaining unquantized weights. In contrast, MACaQ begins from a
cheap RTN assignment and then revisits the individual
codes through incremental Hessian-aware updates. Coordinate-Descent Quantization QuantEase ( 2 ) and CDQuant ( 22 ) also formulate
quantization (pure weight-quantization) as coordinate descent and improve over one-shot assignments such
as GPTQ by revisiting quantized weights.
Our Flip-Refinement is designed primarily
for speed in weight and activation quantization.
It keeps each MXFP4 block exponent fixed and considers
only the two E2M1 levels adjacent to the current code, allowing closed-form evaluation of the gain of each move. Basis Transforms Basis transforms have become a central tool for reducing weight and activation
quantization error. QuIP ( 4 ) popularized incoherence processing through
Hadamard transforms for weight quantization, and QuaRot ( 1 ) extended
Hadamard rotations to weight–activation quantization. SmoothQuant ( 29 ) used element-wise scaling to balance errors between
activations and weights. Later work has optimized the
rotation itself ( 19 , 16 ) , general per-layer affine maps, as in FlatQuant ( 28 ) , or an element-wise scale, including weight-only quantization with AWQ ( 18 ) and
calibration-free weight quantization with SINQ ( 21 ) For microscaling formats, block-local rotations are often preferable to
full-channel rotations, e.g. MR-GPTQ ( 6 ) and ( 27 , 17 ) , as they avoid spreading an
outlier across multiple independently scaled quantization blocks. Other work
has studied how these transforms interact: ( 7 ) analyze the
sources of error in weight–activation quantization and recommend combining
rotation and scaling, while ( 26 ) show that reducing
concentrated activation mass makes Hadamard transforms more effective
preconditioners. Limitations The core design decision of MACaQ, keeping everything layer-local, means that cross-layer error compensation is impossible. A secondary pass with cross-layer statistics could be added on top, but would likely incur the slower quantization times of other methods. In our MoE experiments we restricted ourselves to expert quantization only due to practicality considerations. For the smallest model tested (0.6B parameters) the online basis transform overhead climbs to 8 % 8\% , which starts to be relevant for some settings. Conclusion We have introduced MACaQ, a fast and easy to apply MXFP4 quantization
method for models based on linear layers. MACaQ matches or outperforms
state of the art methods in quality and is faster. The key concepts
that enable this are a light-weight Flip-Refinement to replace GPTQ,
a novel Hessian-aware shared-scale optimization, and light-weight online basis transforms that enable the quantization processing to be
local to each linear layer. All these features combined enable us to
quantize a 122B Qwen3.5 model in 64 minutes while using only 32.5GB
