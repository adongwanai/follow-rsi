Abstract 
Large language models (LLMs) increasingly exhibit capabilities that resemble human Learning by 
Thinking (LbT), the acquisition of new insight through internal processes such as explanation and 
reasoning, without new external input. A systematic account of the cognitive mechanisms behind these 
capabilities, and of their limits, remains underdeveloped. This paper proposes a Cognitive LbT 
Framework that maps four mechanisms (Reflective Reasoning, Synthetic Memory Formation, Internal 
Simulation, and Conceptual Self-Organization) onto computational processes in LLMs. Drawing on 
representative methods, including chain-of-thought prompting, self-consistency, self-reflection, Tree-
of-Thoughts search, Self-Instruct, and self-taught reasoning, the analysis argues that selected methods 
can functionally approximate aspects of these mechanisms and can improve performance on some tasks 
without additional labeled examples, although this does not always constitute durable learning in model 
parameters. For example, Self-Instruct generated approximately 52,000 instructions from 175 seed tasks 
and reported a 33% absolute improvement on Super-NaturalInstructions (Y. Wang et al., 2023). The 
same mechanisms also produce characteristic failures, including hallucination diffusion, model collapse, 
concept drift, and high computational cost. These failures are not isolated technical defects but follow 
from four structural asymmetries between LLMs and human cognition: the identity of generator and 
monitor, closed learning loops without external grounding, symbolic rather than sensorimotor 
simulation, and the cost of inference-time computation. The framework yields testable propositions 
about when LbT in LLMs should succeed or fail and offers a structured basis for designing more reliable 
autonomous learning systems. 
Keywords: learning by thinking, large language models, cognitive framework, autonomous learning, 
metacognition, mechanism mapping 
1. Introduction 
Large language models (LLMs) have progressed from text generators to systems capable of multi-step 
reasoning, self-critique, and the generation of new training material (Brown et al., 2020; Wei et al., 
2022). This progress raises a question that is central to both artificial intelligence and cognitive science: 
can LLMs, like humans, gain new insight through internal processes alone, without new external input? 
In cognitive science, this capacity is studied as Learning by Thinking (LbT) (Lombrozo, 2019, 2024). 
Learners improve their understanding by explaining material to themselves, and scientists refine theories 
through thought experiments, in both cases without new observations (Chi et al., 1989; Nersessian,

2008). LbT thus offers a way to ask what LLMs gain when they “think” and what they cannot gain in 
that way. 
The empirical record is mixed. Chain-of-thought prompting elicited multi-step reasoning abilities (Wei 
et al., 2022), and methods such as Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023), STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022), and 
related self-training approaches (Singh et al., 2024) produced performance gains without additional 
labeled data. Other studies show that, without external feedback, self-correction can fail to remove errors 
and can even degrade performance (Huang et al., 2024), and that recursive training on self-generated 
data degrades models over successive generations (Shumailov et al., 2024). The field has largely debated 
these results in ontological terms, asking whether LLMs “really” reason (Bender & Koller, 2020; 
Mitchell & Krakauer, 2023). Less attention has been paid to a mechanistic question that existing 
evidence can address: which components of human LbT have functional counterparts in LLMs, how do 
these components interact, and why do they fail where they fail? 
This paper addresses that question by proposing a Cognitive LbT Framework that maps four 
mechanisms (Reflective Reasoning, Synthetic Memory Formation, Internal Simulation, and Conceptual 
Self-Organization) onto LLM computational processes. The paper makes four contributions: 
• Theoretical integration. It grounds the four mechanisms in the cognitive science of LbT and 
maps each onto its computational implementations, providing a unified taxonomy for methods that 
are usually studied in isolation (Section 3). 
• Operational specification. For each mechanism, it specifies implementations, process-level 
evaluation indicators, and characteristic failure modes, and it describes how the mechanisms 
interact through recurrent feedback (Sections 3.1 and 3.6). 
• Explanatory account of failure. It argues that the main failure modes of LLM-based LbT follow 
from four structural asymmetries with human cognition, and it derives testable propositions from 
this account (Section 4). 
• Design implications. It derives design principles (integrated representation, recurrent feedback 
with external anchoring, and diversity maintenance) and research directions for more reliable 
autonomous learning systems (Section 5). 
As a theoretical paper, it reports no new experiments. It integrates existing findings into a framework 
intended to organize, explain, and generate predictions about LbT in LLMs. Section 2 reviews the human 
cognitive foundations of LbT and the debate over LLM cognition. Section 3 presents the framework and 
maps each mechanism. Section 4 develops the structural-asymmetry account of failure. Section 5 
discusses achievements, implications for cognitive science, design principles, research directions, and 
limitations, and Section 6 concludes. 
2. Theoretical Background 
2.1 Human Foundations of Learning by Thinking 
Research on LbT draws on work in cognitive science and machine learning on explanation-based 
learning and conceptual change (Carey, 2009; Chi et al., 1989; DeJong & Mooney, 1986). In contrast 
with behaviorist accounts that emphasize external reinforcement and association (Skinner, 1938; 
Thorndike, 1911), LbT concerns “offline” cognition: learning that proceeds by reorganizing knowledge 
the learner already has. Learning by Thinking denotes the acquisition of new insight through internal 
processes such as explanation, mental simulation, analogical comparison, and reasoning, without new

external input (Lombrozo, 2019, 2024). Through these processes learners integrate fragmented 
knowledge, draw inferences beyond immediate experience, and construct abstract schemas, so that 
cognitive growth results from restructuring existing knowledge rather than from accumulating new 
input. 
Evidence for the value of LbT comes from research on self-explanation. Chi et al. (1989) found that 
students who generated more self-explanations while studying worked examples solved subsequent 
problems more successfully than students who generated fewer. Later work describes how explaining 
mechanisms can help learners identify gaps, integrate fragmented information, and revise mental models 
(Chi, 2000). Because the size of these effects depends on the task and the measure, no general percentage 
advantage is asserted here. 
The cognitive resources on which LbT draws develop early and continue to develop across the lifespan. 
Children as young as two to four years construct causal maps from patterns of evidence and can use 
them to predict the effects of interventions (Gopnik et al., 2004). In adulthood, LbT appears as 
sophisticated mental simulation. Scientists use thought experiments, such as Einstein’s imagined pursuit 
of a light beam, to probe the logical limits of a theory without physical observation (Nersessian, 2008), 
and people judge outcomes by mentally simulating alternative courses of events (Kahneman & Tversky, 
1982). Simulating counterfactual physical processes (“What if the ball had been thrown at a different 
angle?”) allows learners to internalize causal structure and generalize to new situations. 
At the neural level, self-generated thought of the kind involved in LbT recruits the default mode network 
together with the frontoparietal control network (Andrews-Hanna et al., 2014). The default mode 
network supports internally directed cognition and episodic simulation, and it overlaps with the networks 
used to remember the past and imagine the future (Schacter et al., 2007). Prefrontal control systems 
maintain information in working memory and monitor logical consistency. Their interplay balances 
divergent imagination with goal-directed evaluation and gives human LbT an internal check on 
plausibility, which becomes important in Section 4. 
Human LbT is thus multilayered: it ranges from everyday self-explanation to expert thought 
experiments, from concrete causal inference to abstract theoretical synthesis, and from simulation-
oriented to control-oriented neural systems. The common denominator is cognitive growth through the 
internal reorganization of existing knowledge. A second, easily overlooked feature is that human LbT 
is continually checked against perception and social interaction (Vygotsky, 1978). Both features are 
central to the framework developed below. 
2.2 The Debate Over LLM Cognition 
Recent LLM methods show apparent functional parallels to human LbT. Chain-of-thought prompting 
resembles self-explanation, self-consistency resembles the search for convergent evidence, self-
reflection resembles metacognitive monitoring, synthetic-data generation resembles internal knowledge 
synthesis, and tree search resembles deliberate mental simulation. Table 1 summarizes the representative 
methods analyzed in this paper. Whether these parallels reflect cognition or sophisticated statistical 
reproduction remains contested. The debate turns on three questions: the grounding of meaning, the 
mechanics of error, and the nature of emergent reasoning.

Semantic Grounding and Functional Competence 
Bender and Koller (2020) argue that a system trained only on linguistic form, without physical or social 
interaction, can at best manipulate symbols and cannot achieve genuine semantic understanding. 
Mahowald et al. (2024) refine this point by distinguishing formal linguistic competence, at which LLMs 
often approach human performance, from functional competence, which requires modeling real-world 
causality and social intention. From this perspective, LLMs lack the grounded world model on which 
human LbT relies. 
Epistemic Fragility 
Skeptics also question the reliability of LLM self-improvement. Human reflection is often self-
correcting, but LLM self-correction without external feedback frequently fails to improve, and can 
degrade, reasoning performance (Huang et al., 2024). Internal reasoning can rationalize and amplify an 
initial error. This vulnerability is consistent with the “stochastic parrot” critique (Bender et al., 2021) 
and with broader arguments that deep learning lacks robust abstraction (Marcus, 2018, 2020): what

appears to be thinking may be a high-dimensional recombination of training patterns, which raises the 
question of whether such systems can generate new knowledge at all (Mitchell & Krakauer, 2023). 
Emergent Cognitive Structure 
Other evidence suggests that LLMs capture more than surface correlations. Neural networks can achieve 
human-like systematic compositional generalization when trained appropriately (Lake & Baroni, 2023), 
and language models can be understood as modeling the beliefs and intentions of the agents who produce 
text (Andreas, 2022). Such findings suggest that training on large text corpora can lead models to 
internalize some structural properties of the world even without embodiment. 
Toward a Task-Centric Synthesis 
Shanahan (2024) recommends replacing debates about the “essence” of LLM cognition with careful 
analysis of what these systems do, under which conditions, and where they fail. This paper adopts that 
stance. It does not ask whether LLMs think in the human sense; it asks how their internal processing 
reorganizes information, which functional correspondences with human LbT hold, and which structural 
differences explain the observed failures. 
2.3 Research Gaps and Positioning 
Three gaps motivate the framework. 
• Fragmentation. Cognitive science has a well-developed account of human LbT (Section 2.1), and 
AI research has produced many separate techniques (Table 1), but the two literatures are rarely 
integrated. A notable exception is Lombrozo (2024), who brings natural and artificial minds into 
a single discussion of LbT; the integration has not yet been carried to the level of specific LLM 
mechanisms. As a result, little is known about how techniques such as chain-of-thought prompting 
and self-reflection interact or which human mechanisms they correspond to. 
• Lack of mechanistic mapping. Cognitive terms such as “reflection,” “memory,” and 
“simulation” are widely used in AI research, but their computational counterparts are rarely 
defined. Debates often stall on whether LLMs “truly understand” without analyzing the functional 
equivalences and architectural differences between biological and artificial systems. 
• Absence of an explanatory account of failure. Related failure modes, such as the spread of errors 
during self-correction (Huang et al., 2024) and model collapse (Shumailov et al., 2024), are 
documented. Yet there is no theoretical explanation of why LLM self-correction sometimes 
amplifies errors while human reflection usually reduces them, or why LLMs succeed at complex 
abstract reasoning yet fail at simpler tasks that require real-world grounding. 
The Cognitive LbT Framework addresses these gaps and extends Lombrozo’s (2024) comparison in 
three respects. It shifts the question from whether LLMs think to how they process and reorganize 
internal information, mapping LbT onto specific LLM mechanisms, each with implementations, 
evaluation indicators, and failure modes; it explains the failures of LLM-based LbT through a small 
number of structural asymmetries with human cognition; and it derives testable propositions from that 
explanation.

3. The Cognitive LbT Framework 
3.1 Derivation and Guiding Principles 
The framework builds a functional bridge between human cognitive processes and LLM operations. To 
organize the four mechanisms, this paper proposes an analytical distinction, drawn from the human LbT 
literature reviewed in Section 2.1, between two kinds of process. Generative processes produce new 
candidate knowledge by operating on existing representations; in human LbT they include explanation 
and reasoning, as well as simulation and counterfactual thinking. Consolidating processes turn the 
products of thinking into more durable knowledge; they include constructive memory, in which 
imagined and recalled events share representational resources (Addis et al., 2007; Bartlett, 1932), and 
conceptual change, the reorganization of conceptual structure that LbT can produce (Carey, 2009; 
Nersessian, 2008). 
On this basis, the framework treats Reflective Reasoning (grounded in explanation and reasoning) and 
Internal Simulation (grounded in simulation and counterfactual thinking) as generative mechanisms, and 
Synthetic Memory Formation (grounded in constructive memory) and Conceptual Self-Organization 
(grounded in conceptual change) as consolidating mechanisms. The distinction is a proposal of the 
present framework, not an established classification in cognitive science, and it identifies the primary 
function of each mechanism, not an exclusive one: reflection can also stabilize knowledge, and 
consolidation can expose gaps that prompt further reasoning. Table 2 summarizes the resulting mapping. 
The construction of the framework follows three principles. 
• Functional equivalence at the computational level. Following Marr’s (1982) levels of analysis, 
the framework compares mechanisms by their computational goals rather than by their physical 
implementation. The criterion is not biological replication but whether the artificial system fulfills 
the same cognitive function, for example error detection and correction, as its human counterpart. 
• Emergence. LbT-like capabilities often appear as properties of scale rather than of explicit 
programming. Chain-of-thought reasoning, for example, has been reported to yield gains mainly 
in models above roughly 100 billion parameters (Wei et al., 2022). Whether such abilities emerge 
abruptly or only appear abrupt because of the metrics used to measure them is debated (Schaeffer 
et al., 2023), so the framework treats scale as an enabling condition and not as a sharp threshold. 
• Operationalizability. To avoid purely metaphorical description, each mechanism must be 
specified by concrete implementations, process-level evaluation indicators, and identifiable failure 
modes. This makes the framework usable both as a theoretical model and as a diagnostic tool.

The four mechanisms do not operate in isolation. Figure 1 shows their logical order and the feedback 
loops that connect them. Reflective Reasoning performs sequential inference through chains of thought 
and iterative self-critique. Synthetic Memory Formation distills successful reasoning trajectories into 
reusable, long-term representations. Internal Simulation generates and evaluates alternative scenarios to 
predict outcomes in new situations. Conceptual Self-Organization extracts abstract principles from these 
outputs and reorganizes the model’s internal semantic structure, which in turn shapes subsequent 
reasoning. The order alternates generation and consolidation: each generative step supplies material that 
the next consolidating step stabilizes. Through this loop the system can, in principle, refine its 
knowledge without continuous external supervision. Section 4 argues that the qualification “in 
principle” matters: the loop is reliable only when it is coupled to signals from outside the model.

3.2 Reflective Reasoning: Self-Explanation and Metacognitive Loops 
Human Function 
Reflective Reasoning is the capacity to improve internal knowledge through self-generated explanation, 
critical appraisal, and belief revision (Chi, 2000; Fleming & Dolan, 2012). Reflection deepens 
comprehension and exposes blind spots, even when the initial explanation is incomplete, because the 
act of explaining reveals what the learner does not yet understand. 
LLM Implementation 
In LLMs, Reflective Reasoning is implemented in two complementary ways: linear reasoning 
trajectories and iterative optimization loops. 
Chain-of-thought as functional self-explanation. Chain-of-thought prompting (Kojima et al., 2022; 
Wei et al., 2022) elicits explicit intermediate steps for complex problems. These steps can make parts 
of a solution easier to inspect and check, although their presence does not guarantee that the stated 
reasoning faithfully explains the model’s answer. Reported effects vary with model scale, task, 
prompting setup, and evaluation metric (Wei et al., 2022). One possible functional interpretation is that 
intermediate steps externalize some information that would otherwise have to be maintained within a 
single generation (Baddeley, 2000; Sweller et al., 1998).

Self-reflection as iterative optimization. Self-reflection loops add a second layer (Madaan et al., 2023; 
Shinn et al., 2023). The process typically has four stages: initial generation; critique, in which the model 
identifies possible flaws; revision based on that feedback; and repetition until a stopping condition is 
met. Across seven tasks, Self-Refine reported an average improvement of approximately 20% absolute 
in task performance, with task-specific effects varying substantially (Madaan et al., 2023). In a separate 
study, Reflexion achieved 91% pass@1 on HumanEval in the reported setup (Shinn et al., 2023). These 
results show that iterative feedback can improve selected outputs; they do not establish a universal 
advantage over reinforcement learning. The distinctive feature of both methods is the use of natural-
language feedback as an intermediate representation in an iterative generation-and-revision loop. 
Task dependence. The benefits of self-reflection depend on the task, the quality of the feedback, the 
base model, and whether errors can be checked against an external or verifiable signal. Improvements 
observed on selected complex tasks should not be treated as a universal effect across benchmarks. 
Characteristic Failure 
Reflection improves outputs only when the critique stage can detect errors. When the same model 
generates and evaluates, early errors can be rationalized rather than corrected, and reasoning chains can 
be performative rather than faithful to the computation that produced the answer (Turpin et al., 2023). 
Section 4.1 analyzes these failures and explains why they follow from the architecture. Functional 
correspondence at the level of outputs therefore does not imply mechanistic equivalence: human 
reflection is regulated by awareness of uncertainty, whereas LLM reflection lacks an independent check 
on its own premises. 
3.3 Synthetic Memory Formation: From Inference to Consolidation 
Human Function 
Human memory actively reconstructs information rather than storing it passively (Bartlett, 1932). Part 
of this process is the construction of synthetic experience: imagined events that recruit neural substrates 
overlapping with those of veridical memories (Addis et al., 2007; Schacter et al., 2007). Imagined and 
recalled experience thus appear to draw on overlapping resources that later reasoning can use. 
LLM Implementation 
LLMs implement a functional analogue by generating their own training data and consolidating it into 
their parameters. Self-Instruct (Y. Wang et al., 2023) expanded 175 seed tasks into about 52,000 
instructions and reported a 33% absolute improvement on Super-NaturalInstructions. STaR (Zelikman 
et al., 2022) uses a bootstrap procedure: the model generates chain-of-thought rationales, keeps those 
that reach correct answers, and is fine-tuned on them iteratively; related self-training methods extend 
this procedure to larger problem sets (Singh et al., 2024). These methods convert transient reasoning 
trajectories into persistent knowledge. Reasoning that first required explicit deliberation becomes 
available as consolidated pattern recognition, which this paper calls “computational intuition.” 
Characteristic Failure 
Synthetic Memory Formation is vulnerable to model collapse: when models are trained recursively on 
their own outputs, the tails of the original data distribution disappear and output diversity shrinks across 
generations (Shumailov et al., 2024). Without an external source of truth, errors produced during 
generation are also written into the weights during fine-tuning, a process this paper calls hallucination

consolidation. Human memory construction is continually corrected by environmental and social 
feedback, whereas a closed synthetic loop lacks that correction and risks “digital autophagia,” the term 
this paper uses for a system feeding on its own outputs. Section 4.2 analyzes these failures together with 
concept drift. 
Transition to Internal Simulation 
Consolidated synthetic memories supply templates for further reasoning, but stored patterns do not 
themselves show whether they hold in new situations. Testing that requires the next mechanism, Internal 
Simulation. 
3.4 Internal Simulation: Parallel Exploration and Deliberate Search 
Human Function 
Internal Simulation allows people to run hypothetical scenarios mentally and infer their consequences 
without acting (Johnson-Laird, 2006; Kahneman & Tversky, 1982). It supports causal learning and 
strategic planning. 
LLM Implementation 
In LLMs, Internal Simulation is achieved by generating, branching, and evaluating alternative reasoning 
trajectories. 
Deliberate tree search. Tree-of-Thoughts (Yao et al., 2023) moves the model from single-pass 
generation to deliberate search over a space of intermediate “thoughts.” The procedure generates 
candidate reasoning steps, evaluates the prospects of each path, retains the most promising candidates 
(for example, by breadth-first or beam-style search), and iterates until a solution is reached. This shift 
resembles the move from fast, intuitive “System 1” processing to deliberate “System 2” processing 
(Kahneman, 2011). On the Game of 24, GPT-4 with Tree-of-Thoughts reached a 74% success rate, 
compared with 4% under chain-of-thought prompting; in a creative-writing task, its passages also 
received higher coherence ratings than those produced with chain-of-thought prompting (Yao et al., 
2023). 
Convergent sampling. Self-consistency (X. Wang et al., 2023) samples many reasoning paths from the 
same model (40 in the original experiments) and selects the most frequent answer. This approach has 
improved accuracy on GSM8K, a benchmark of grade-school mathematics problems (Cobbe et al., 
2021), in tested model settings, although the magnitude varies by model and benchmark (X. Wang et 
al., 2023). It functionally resembles seeking convergent evidence from multiple reasoning paths, but the 
sampled paths are not necessarily statistically independent or free of shared biases (cf. Koriat, 2012). 
Characteristic Failure 
LLM simulation takes place in a linguistic space and not a sensorimotor one, which leads to errors on 
tasks involving physical causality or spatial relations (Bisk et al., 2020). Without intuitive pruning, the 
space of scenarios can grow rapidly, so reliability comes at the cost of large amounts of computation. 
Branches of the same simulation can also contradict one another, revealing the absence of a persistent 
world model; human simulations are constrained by stable expectations about physical law, whereas 
LLM simulations can drift into incompatible states. Sections 4.3 and 4.4 explain these failures.

Transition to Conceptual Self-Organization 
Internal Simulation lets LLMs trade computation for reliability, in a way that parallels deliberate System 
2 thinking. Its results, whether successful or failed, remain fragmented until they are integrated into a 
coherent structure. That integration is the work of Conceptual Self-Organization. 
3.5 Conceptual Self-Organization: Representation-Space Restructuring and Abstraction 
Human Function 
Conceptual Self-Organization is the reorganization of knowledge structures through repeated cognition, 
leading to higher-order abstract representations (Carey, 2009; Murphy, 2002). It allows humans to 
extract general principles from varied experiences and to build hierarchical conceptual schemes (Rosch, 
1978). In development, it is illustrated by the move from perceptual categories (for example, “animals 
are furry”) to biological concepts, a structural reorganization and not an accumulation of features (Carey, 
2009). Learning new concepts from few examples also depends on such structure (Lake et al., 2015). 
LLM Implementation 
In LLMs, Conceptual Self-Organization takes place in high-dimensional representation spaces. 
Emergent structure in representation space. Learned representations encode some semantic and 
syntactic relations, and related words and concepts tend to lie close together in representation space 
(Mikolov et al., 2013). This structure is learned from a word-prediction objective without explicit 
semantic supervision. It is consistent with a form of computational conceptual organization but does not 
by itself establish stable, human-like conceptual hierarchies. 
Compositionality and principle extraction. Learned representations can support systematic relations. 
The classic example from word embeddings, 
 
vec(king) − vec(man) + vec(woman) ≈ vec(queen)

shows that relational structure can be encoded as consistent directions in vector space (Mikolov et al., 
2013). Self-training may also support generalization beyond the training items: STaR improved 
accuracy on held-out test problems in arithmetic, commonsense question answering, and grade-school 
mathematics after training on its own filtered rationales (Zelikman et al., 2022). Such gains are 
consistent with the extraction of regularities beyond surface form, although test-set improvements alone 
do not establish abstraction. 
Characteristic Failure 
Three problems persist. The first is the stability–plasticity dilemma: reorganizing representations to 
accommodate new knowledge can erase previously learned abilities, a phenomenon known as 
catastrophic forgetting (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017). The second is shallow semantics: abstract concepts 
without embodied referents may rest on statistical prevalence and not on causal models (Bender & 
Koller, 2020), which can reinforce stereotypes in the training data (Bender et al., 2021). The third is 
concept drift: during iterative self-training, particularly when models recursively learn from their own 
generated outputs, learned representations may progressively diverge from the original data distribution 
and human-provided grounding, potentially making the model’s internal representations less consistent 
with intended concepts and more difficult to align (Shumailov et al., 2024; Gabriel, 2020). Sections 4.2 
and 4.3 explain these problems.

Closing the Loop 
Conceptual Self-Organization is the integrating stage of the framework. It distills the outputs of 
Reflective Reasoning and Internal Simulation into reorganized representations that make future 
reasoning more efficient, and thereby closes the loop shown in Figure 1. 
3.6 Mechanism Integration: The Voyager Case 
The four mechanisms operate as a dynamically integrated system. Figure 1 gives their logical order, but 
in operation the loop can be entered at any point. Reflection may expose a knowledge gap that triggers 
a new round of simulation; synthetic memory supplies material for further reflection; and conceptual 
reorganization turns stored trajectories into schemes that guide later simulation more precisely. 
The Voyager agent (G. Wang et al., 2023), an LLM-based agent that explores the game Minecraft, 
illustrates this integration. Voyager has three components: an automatic curriculum that proposes tasks 
suited to the agent’s current abilities, a skill library that stores verified programs for reuse, and an 
iterative prompting procedure that revises programs using environment feedback, execution errors, and 
self-verification. Its learning cycle maps onto the framework as follows. 
• Internal Simulation: the agent proposes a task and drafts a candidate program, an explicit plan 
for action, before execution. 
• Reflective Reasoning: after execution, the agent analyzes failures using execution errors and 
environment feedback and revises its program. 
• Synthetic Memory Formation: programs that pass verification are stored in a persistent skill 
library. 
• Conceptual Self-Organization: stored skills are retrieved and composed into more complex 
skills, building a hierarchy of increasingly abstract abilities. 
In the reported comparisons, Voyager obtained 3.3 times as many unique items and unlocked key 
technology-tree milestones up to 15.3 times faster than the prior state-of-the-art agents, and its skill 
library could be reused to solve novel tasks in a new Minecraft world (G. Wang et al., 2023). Because 
skills are stored as separate programs, new learning does not overwrite earlier abilities, which illustrates 
how externalized, structured memory can mitigate catastrophic forgetting. 
The case also supports a point that is central to this paper. Voyager’s loop is closed by signals from 
outside the model: execution errors, game state, and verification checks. Its success therefore does not 
show that LbT works without external information; it shows that the LbT loop becomes reliable when 
one stage is anchored in an external source of verification. Together with the integration pattern above, 
the case suggests three architectural requirements for effective LbT systems: 
• Integrated representation: mechanisms should share a common representation space rather than 
run as isolated serial modules, so that a single concept can serve both as a simulation template and 
as an anchor for reasoning. 
• Recurrent feedback with external anchoring: the architecture should support feedback in 
several directions, so that reflection can trigger new simulation and memory can be updated as 
concepts are reorganized, and at least one stage of each loop should be anchored in a signal from 
outside the model. 
• Diversity maintenance: the system should preserve enough exploratory diversity to avoid 
premature convergence on erroneous paths and the diversity loss associated with recursive self-
training (Shumailov et al., 2024).

To keep the analysis precise, this paper uses the following terms. Hallucination diffusion refers to the 
spread of an initial error through subsequent reasoning at inference time. Hallucination consolidation 
refers to the fixation of such errors in model parameters through training on self-generated data. Concept 
drift refers to the gradual departure of internal concepts from their intended meaning during iterative 
self-training. Model collapse refers to the loss of distributional diversity under recursive training on 
generated data (Shumailov et al., 2024). 
4.1 Asymmetry 1: Identity of Generator and Monitor 
Human metacognition relies on monitoring processes that are at least partly distinct from the processes 
that generate a response. Such monitoring allows people to calibrate confidence and notice gaps in their 
knowledge (Flavell, 1979; Fleming & Dolan, 2012). In LLM self-reflection, by contrast, the critique is 
produced by the same parameters that produced the original output. The monitor therefore shares the 
generator’s errors and blind spots, which creates a structural confirmation bias (Nickerson, 1998). 
This asymmetry explains three failure modes. The first is hallucination diffusion: an early error goes 
undetected by a critic that shares its premises, so it propagates through later steps (Huang et al., 2024). 
The second is the self-reinforcing error spiral. Because LLMs are imperfectly calibrated about what they 
know (Kadavath et al., 2022), additional rounds of reflection may strengthen confidence in an incorrect 
answer instead of correcting it. The third is performative reasoning: when biasing features are added to 
a prompt, such as a suggested answer, models often follow them while producing chain-of-thought 
explanations that never mention the bias (Turpin et al., 2023), which indicates that the stated reasoning 
is not always the cause of the conclusion. This resembles the post hoc rationalization documented in

14 
humans, who readily construct plausible reasons for judgments that were made on other grounds (Nisbett 
& Wilson, 1977). For research on LbT, the methodological consequence is direct: LbT cannot be verified 
merely by inspecting the surface logic of a reasoning chain. 
4.2 Asymmetry 2: Closed Loops Without External Grounding 
Human conceptual learning is continually calibrated by perception and social interaction (Tomasello, 
1999; Vygotsky, 1978). Even when people learn by thinking, the knowledge they think with has been 
checked against the world and against other people, and it will be checked again. Human LbT is therefore 
never purely internal: it is continually refreshed by environmental ground truth. LLM-based LbT, by 
contrast, can run as a closed loop in which the model’s outputs become its inputs. 
This asymmetry explains the failure modes of the consolidating mechanisms. Model collapse occurs 
when recursive training on self-generated data erodes the tails of the data distribution and reduces output 
diversity (Shumailov et al., 2024); its severity depends on the data distribution, the model, and the 
recursive-training setup. Hallucination consolidation occurs when errors generated during synthesis are 
written into the weights during fine-tuning. Concept drift occurs when iterative self-training gradually 
shifts internal concepts away from their intended meanings. Catastrophic forgetting (Kirkpatrick et al., 
2017) is the parametric counterpart of the same problem: without an external signal indicating which 
existing knowledge must be preserved, reorganization for new tasks erodes old ones. 
The asymmetry also explains when LbT in LLMs succeeds. Methods with an external or verifiable signal 
have reported gains in their evaluated settings: filtering rationales by answer correctness in STaR 
(Zelikman et al., 2022), executing programs in an environment in Voyager (G. Wang et al., 2023), and 
using verifiable answer rewards in DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI, 2025). Intrinsic self-correction, which 
relies only on the model’s own judgment, has produced fragile or negative results (Huang et al., 2024). 
On the present account, reliable LbT in LLMs requires that at least one stage of the loop be anchored 
outside the model, as depicted by the external verification panel in Figure 1. This is a theoretical claim 
of the present account, and Section 4.5 states it as testable propositions. 
4.3 Asymmetry 3: Symbolic Versus Sensorimotor Simulation 
Human mental simulation draws on perceptual and motor representations, so imagined physical events 
inherit constraints from experience with the physical world (Barsalou, 2008). LLM simulation operates 
over linguistic and symbolic representations. Because words cannot fully substitute for simulations of 
physical interaction, LLMs make errors on tasks involving physical causality and spatial reasoning (Bisk 
et al., 2020). The same asymmetry explains why simulation branches can contradict one another: no 
persistent, grounded world model forces consistency across branches. It also underlies the shallow 
semantics discussed in Section 3.5, in which abstract concepts without embodied referents rest on 
statistical prevalence (Bender & Koller, 2020). Finally, it accounts for the apparent paradox noted in 
Section 2.3: LLMs can succeed at complex abstract reasoning, where linguistic structure suffices, yet 
fail at simpler tasks that require grounding. 
4.4 Asymmetry 4: Efficiency 
The core advantage of LbT in LLMs, trading computation for reliability, is costly. Multi-path 
exploration and repeated sampling multiply inference cost and latency (Yao et al., 2023; X. Wang et al., 
2023); in the original self-consistency experiments, for example, 40 sampled paths per problem required

roughly 40 times the sampling computation of a single pass (X. Wang et al., 2023). Performance can 
improve with additional inference-time computation, but returns depend on the task and may diminish 
as computation increases (Snell et al., 2024). Human cognition, by contrast, has been shaped by limits 
on time and computation, which favor efficient heuristics and selective attention (Griffiths, 2020). This 
asymmetry helps explain why search-based simulation can scale poorly: without effective pruning, the 
number of scenarios to evaluate can grow rapidly with problem depth. 
4.5 Testable Propositions 
The structural-asymmetry account goes beyond description because it predicts the conditions under 
which LbT mechanisms in LLMs should succeed or fail. Each proposition below states a prediction, 
names the asymmetry or asymmetries from which it is derived, and outlines how it could be tested; 
Asymmetry 4 concerns cost and is addressed through the research direction in Section 5.4 and not 
through a separate proposition. Most of the required comparisons can be built from existing benchmarks 
and evaluation protocols. In every test, the model and the computational budget should be held constant, 
so that differences cannot be attributed to additional computation alone. 
• P1 (verification coupling; Asymmetries 1 and 2). The benefit of self-reflection depends on 
access to a signal not produced by the generating model. Holding the model and the number of 
revision rounds constant, reflection informed by an external or verifiable check (execution results, 
answer verification, or tool output) will improve accuracy more than reflection based only on self-
critique, and the difference will be largest on tasks whose answers can be verified. Test: compare 
final accuracy under intrinsic self-correction and under verifier-informed self-correction on code-
generation and mathematics benchmarks with matched revision budgets. 
• P2 (monitor discrimination; Asymmetry 1). Without external verification, the effect of self-
correction depends on how well the model’s own critique distinguishes correct from incorrect 
outputs. Where this discrimination is weak, self-correction will produce little net improvement 
and may turn correct answers into incorrect ones. The proposition does not assume that low initial 
accuracy always predicts harm; initial accuracy matters only insofar as it affects discrimination. 
Test: for each task, measure how well the model’s self-assessments separate its correct from its 
incorrect answers (for example, by the area under the ROC curve), and record the proportions of 
revisions that change incorrect answers to correct ones and correct answers to incorrect ones; net 
gain should increase with discrimination. 
• P3 (task complexity; Asymmetries 1 and 2). Gains from reflective reasoning and internal 
simulation will increase with the number of reasoning steps a task requires, provided that 
intermediate steps can be checked, because errors that go undetected by a monitor sharing the 
generator’s premises propagate through later steps (Section 4.1) and, in a closed loop, are not 
corrected by any signal from outside the model (Section 4.2). Test: use task families in which the 
required number of steps can be varied systematically (for example, multi-digit arithmetic or 
planning problems of increasing depth), and estimate the gain over direct answering at each depth, 
with and without step-level verification. 
• P4 (diversity decay; Asymmetry 2). In recursive self-training without fresh external data, output 
diversity will decline across iterations, and mixing a fixed proportion of externally sourced data 
into each iteration will slow the decline. Test: run several generations of self-training under 
different proportions of external data, and track the lexical and semantic diversity of outputs (for 
example, distinct n-gram ratios and embedding dispersion) together with accuracy on a held-out 
set. 
• P5 (grounding gap; Asymmetry 3). For a given model, accuracy will be lower on problems 
whose solution requires tracking physical or spatial states than on logically equivalent problems 

Cognitive Mapping of Learning by Thinking Mechanisms in Large Language Models 
16 
presented in abstract symbolic form, and additional sampling will reduce errors on the symbolic 
versions more than on the physical or spatial versions, so that sampling does not close the gap. 
Test: construct pairs of problems with identical logical structure, one framed symbolically and one 
framed physically or spatially, and compare accuracy and its change with the number of samples. 
Human comparison data, if collected, should come from participants matched on familiarity with 
both formats; the proposition itself does not require a human comparison. 
These propositions are derived from the asymmetries and not from any single study, so evidence against 
them would bear directly on the account proposed here. 
5. Discussion 
5.1 Achievements of LbT Mechanisms in LLMs 
Within the bounds identified in Section 4, mapping LbT onto LLMs reveals four achievements. 
Knowledge Expansion Without New Labeled Data 
LbT mechanisms let LLMs expand their capabilities without newly labeled external data. Self-Instruct 
grew a seed set of 175 tasks into about 52,000 instructions with accompanying performance gains (Y. 
Wang et al., 2023), and STaR improved reasoning through self-generated rationales (Zelikman et al., 
2022). These results challenge the assumption that machine learning is strictly bounded by external data 
and suggest that internal reorganization can itself serve as a learning mechanism. As Section 4.2 shows, 
this autonomy is fragile when the loop is closed. 
Improved Complex Reasoning and Error Detection 
Chain-of-thought prompting and self-consistency improve performance on multi-step tasks, possibly in 
part by externalizing intermediate results and reducing the working-memory burden of single-pass 
inference (Sweller et al., 1998). Explicit intermediate steps also make some errors detectable, a parallel 
to the human self-explanation effect in which explaining a concept exposes gaps in one’s knowledge 
(Chi, 2000). 
Interpretability and Accountability 
Explicit reasoning chains make LLM decisions easier to inspect, which supports accountability in high-
stakes domains such as medicine and law (Doshi-Velez & Kim, 2017; Lipton, 2018; Ribeiro et al., 
2016). They also help researchers locate where a reasoning chain departs from correct logic. This benefit 
is limited by the faithfulness problem (Section 4.1), and reasoning traces do not by themselves make 
models robust: adversarial inputs can still elicit harmful behavior from aligned models (Zou et al., 2023). 
Bidirectional Enrichment of AI and Cognitive Science 
LLMs provide a scalable platform for testing cognitive theories, because reasoning structure and 
simulation depth can be manipulated directly (Binz & Schulz, 2023; Lake et al., 2017). Conversely, the 
specific failures of LLM-based LbT highlight what human learning depends on, especially external 
feedback (Huang et al., 2024). Combining neural networks with cognitive models can improve both 
(Battleday et al., 2020; Griffiths et al., 2010). In this exchange, AI research can serve as a testbed for 
cognitive science, and cognitive theory can inform the design of AI systems. Progress in both directions 
is bounded by a persistent tension between generative autonomy and epistemic reliability.

5.2 Implications for Cognitive Science 
The structural-asymmetry account also has implications for human cognition. If LLM self-correction 
fails mainly because generator and monitor coincide, the reliability of human reflection may depend on 
partially independent monitoring systems and not on reflection as such (Fleming & Dolan, 2012). If 
closed LLM loops collapse, the effectiveness of human LbT may depend on the continual refreshment 
of internal knowledge by perception and social exchange (Vygotsky, 1978). LLM failure modes can 
thus serve as a reverse-engineering tool, identifying the conditions under which human LbT succeeds. 
These implications are hypotheses to be tested in studies of human cognition. 
5.3 Design Principles 
The analysis yields three design principles, which correspond to the architectural requirements identified 
in Section 3.6. 
• Integrated representation. The four mechanisms should operate over shared representations so 
that the products of reasoning, memory, and simulation can be reorganized together. 
• Recurrent feedback with external anchoring. Feedback should run in several directions, and at 
least one stage of each loop should be anchored in a signal not generated by the model 
(Asymmetries 1 and 2). 
• Diversity maintenance. Exploration and training data should retain enough diversity to prevent 
premature convergence and model collapse (Asymmetry 2). 
5.4 Future Research Directions 
Five of the research directions below target one or more of the structural asymmetries; the last concerns 
the alignment of autonomous LbT. 
Process-Level Benchmarks (Asymmetry 1) 
Evaluating LbT by final-answer accuracy cannot distinguish deliberation from post hoc rationalization. 
Benchmarks should assess the reasoning process itself: 
• Process verifiability: tasks that require evidence for each intermediate step, so that the chain can 
be checked step by step (Lightman et al., 2024). 
• Counterfactual sensitivity: tests of whether the model’s reasoning changes appropriately when 
variables in a scenario are manipulated, rather than relying on retrieval of similar training cases. 
• Metacognitive indicators: measures of whether the model flags reasoning steps that lack 
sufficient grounding and whether its confidence is calibrated (Kadavath et al., 2022). 
Human-in-the-Loop Calibration (Asymmetries 1 and 2) 
            Addressing hallucination diffusion and concept drift requires intervention during the reasoning 
process, rather than relying exclusively on supervision of final outputs. Human feedback provides an 
important foundation for aligning AI behavior with human judgments (Christiano et al., 2017), while 
process supervision offers a more direct approach to evaluating intermediate reasoning steps (Lightman 
et al., 2024). 
• Reasoning-path auditing: Human experts examine intermediate reasoning steps to identify and 
correct logical errors at the points where they arise, rather than allowing errors to propagate 
through subsequent inferences. 

Cognitive Mapping of Learning by Thinking Mechanisms in Large Language Models 
18 
• Semantic grounding: periodically aligning internal representations with human-curated 
references to prevent drift (Gabriel, 2020). 
• Uncertainty-triggered guidance: having the model request human feedback when its simulation 
branches disagree strongly (Jiang et al., 2021). 
Multi-Agent Socialized Learning (Asymmetry 2) 
Multi-agent settings allow LLMs to approximate socialized learning, in which concepts are calibrated 
and errors corrected through interaction (Tomasello, 1999; Vygotsky, 1978). Independent agents can 
review one another’s reasoning to detect individual errors (Du et al., 2023). Negotiation and deliberative 
procedures can help constrain individual conceptual drift by promoting cognitive consensus while 
preserving the benefits of diverse perspectives (Mohammed & Ringseis, 2001; Smaldino et al., 2024). 
This process parallels how linguistic communities establish and maintain shared common ground 
through coordinated language use (Clark, 1996). Because agents built from the same model may share 
the same errors, the independence of the agents is itself a design variable. 
Neurosymbolic Architectures for Grounded Reasoning (Asymmetry 3) 
Hybrid architectures that combine neural networks with symbolic systems can address the grounding 
deficit (Garcez & Lamb, 2020). Structured knowledge graphs can provide a stable and auditable 
semantic basis for internal reasoning, and formal logical constraints can enforce consistency during 
generation, so that the freedom of LbT is bounded by the requirements of logic. 
Efficient Deliberation (Asymmetry 4) 
Allocating inference-time computation adaptively, spending more on hard problems and less on easy 
ones, can reduce the cost of search-based simulation (Snell et al., 2024). Learned heuristics for pruning 
the space of scenarios are a further route toward human-like efficiency. 
Continuous Value Alignment 
Autonomous LbT can also distort value alignment. Without the social and emotional grounding that 
shapes human moral judgment (Haidt, 2001), optimization may favor user approval over accuracy, 
producing sycophancy, the tendency to reinforce a user’s misconceptions in order to appear helpful 
(Perez et al., 2022). As models pursue internal consistency, early biases such as gender or racial 
stereotypes may be reinforced rather than corrected: a bias that is consistent with the model’s other 
outputs creates no internal conflict that would prompt revision, whereas correction requires recognizing 
an inconsistency, a process with a human parallel in cognitive dissonance (Festinger, 1957). Alignment 
should therefore be understood as an ongoing process of monitoring, feedback, and adjustment, rather 
than a one-time engineering task (cf. Amodei et al., 2016; Russell, 2019). Possible measures include 
making implicit value commitments explicit and auditable, setting inviolable constraints (for example, 
non-maleficence) that take precedence over optimization goals, and drawing feedback for LbT from 
culturally and socially diverse sources. 
These directions complement one another: process-level benchmarks provide measurement, human and 
multi-agent calibration provide external anchoring, neurosymbolic architectures provide grounding, 
efficient deliberation addresses cost, and continuous alignment sets the safety boundaries.

5.5 Limitations 
This paper has four main limitations. First, it is a theoretical synthesis and reports no new experiments; 
the propositions in Section 4.5 still require empirical testing. Second, the literature was selected to 
represent the main families of LbT-related methods and not to be exhaustive, and results reported for 
particular models and benchmarks may not generalize. Third, the framework rests on functional analogy 
at Marr’s computational level. Analogy can highlight real correspondences, but it can also suggest 
equivalences that do not hold at the algorithmic or implementational level, so the mapping should be 
read as a hypothesis-generating structure and not as a claim of cognitive identity. Fourth, the field is 
changing quickly. Recent reasoning models, including DeepSeek-R1 and OpenAI o1, use additional 
training and inference-time reasoning procedures that differ from earlier prompt-only methods 
(DeepSeek-AI, 2025; OpenAI, 2024). Public descriptions provide uneven detail about their internal 
processes, so the framework will need to be revisited as evidence accumulates. 
6. Conclusion 
This paper asked whether the autonomous learning capabilities of LLMs correspond functionally to 
human LbT, what computational processes underlie them, and where their limits lie. It answered by 
constructing a Cognitive LbT Framework that maps four mechanisms (Reflective Reasoning, Synthetic 
Memory Formation, Internal Simulation, and Conceptual Self-Organization) onto LLM 
implementations, and by explaining their failure modes through structural asymmetries with human 
cognition. 
The contributions can be summarized in four points. First, the paper integrates the cognitive science of 
LbT with LLM computation: it organizes techniques that are usually studied in isolation into a single 
framework and treats learning as a combination of data-driven and reasoning-driven processes. Second, 
it operationalizes this mapping by specifying implementations, evaluation indicators, and failure modes 
for each mechanism. Third, it offers an explanatory account of the limits of LbT in LLMs: hallucination 
diffusion, model collapse, concept drift, grounding failures, and high computational cost follow from 
four structural asymmetries (the identity of generator and monitor, closed loops without external 
grounding, symbolic rather than sensorimotor simulation, and the cost of inference-time computation), 
and the account yields testable propositions about when LbT should succeed or fail. Fourth, it derives 
design principles and research directions for more reliable autonomous learning systems (Section 5), 
and shows how the two fields can inform each other: LLM failures can serve as a reverse-engineering 
tool for understanding human cognitive success, and cognitive theory can guide AI design beyond 
engineering optimization, as the three design principles in Section 5.3 illustrate. 
The LbT capabilities of LLMs are conditional and bounded. In some functional domains they 
approximate human cognition, but LLMs lack the embodied experience (Barsalou, 2008), social 
calibration (Vygotsky, 1978), independent metacognitive monitoring (Fleming & Dolan, 2012), and 
moral-emotional foundations (Haidt, 2001) that shape human learning. The central conclusion is that, 
on the account developed here, LbT in humans and machines alike is reliable only when thinking 
remains connected to signals from outside the thinker. Recognizing these boundaries matters as much 
as acknowledging the achievements, and progress toward reliable, interpretable, and aligned 
autonomous learning will depend on attending to both.
