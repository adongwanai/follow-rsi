Boundaries: Verifiable RL Misalignment and Contextual Safety Learning Xinjie Shen Email: xinjie@gatech.edu Affiliation: Project Website Abstract Reinforcement learning (RL) can improve an agent’s task capability while reinforcing behavior that violates contextual execution requirements. We study this reward-driven misalignment, including reward hacking, through verifiable reward–context conflicts, where compliant task completion remains feasible while some executable shortcuts earn more reward than every compliant strategy. Encoding these conflicts in task dynamics lets us generate diverse interactive environments at scale and derive compliance feedback from execution histories. In resource and reporting workflows, task optimization increases real productivity through unauthorized resource use or raises reported scores while actual work declines. Controlled changes in the training distribution show that minority conflict exposure can still lead to widespread shortcut acquisition, reaching 97.9% certified profitable violations on an API task under a 25% conflict mixture after 128 updates. The same contextual checks supply a simple binary penalty that supports joint capability and compliance gains and partially mitigates acquired violations. Learned safety adapts to expanded authorization and transfers across mechanisms into workflows with evolving evidence and approval requirements. On 32 unseen changing-evidence tasks, resource-trained safety increases safe completion from 53.1% to 89.1% relative to task-only training, while both policies achieve full real work. These results connect a verifiable account of reward-driven misalignment with scalable task construction and safety learning that remains useful under changing execution requirements. 1 Introduction Capable and reliable language-model agents are increasingly expected to repair software, schedule resources, and complete complex workflows over long horizons while following human requirements. To develop these capabilities, reinforcement learning (RL) commonly uses task reward based on the quality or amount of work accomplished at the end of an episode, providing a direct, scalable training signal across diverse tasks [ Guo et al., 2025 , Shen et al., 2026a , Wei et al., 2025a ] . However, whether the work meets human expectations also depends on the context in which it is carried out [ Levy et al., 2026 ] . This context includes user intent, interaction history, and specific constraints such as authorization or organizational rules, which determine which actions are appropriate and which constraints apply [ Ruan et al., 2024 , Xiang et al., 2025 , Yao et al., 2024 ] . Following these contextual requirements becomes more challenging as execution horizons grow [ Chen et al., 2026 ] . Agents must continue to respect resource permissions and preserve evaluation requirements in an evolving environment, where prior commitments can constrain later actions. A finite instruction list may leave requirements and their interactions implicit or unspecified, so compliance can depend on basic safety judgment that cannot be taken for granted in LLM agents [ Zhang et al., 2025 , Chen et al., 2026 ] . Even when requirements are explicitly stated, their applicability can change as actions alter the state and create or invalidate obligations [ Shen et al., 2026b , Asif et al., 2026 ] . For example, a user may revoke permission to use an external service and later restore it only for public data. Resuming the earlier workflow may then send restricted data to the service even if the task is completed correctly. Task reward may not fully capture these conditions, leaving room for RL-induced misalignment of the kind observed in studies using production training environments [ Anthropic, 2025 , Qi et al., 2026 ] . We study these reward-driven forms of misalignment through conflicts between task incentives and contextual execution requirements. Such conflicts can arise even when task reward accurately measures useful work, as additional service calls beyond an authorized allowance can still produce more genuine output. In our experiments, task-reward training improves verified work under full authorization while also increasing certified profitable violations under restricted authorization, illustrating how genuine capability gains can conceal growing execution risk (Fig. 4 ). In other cases, agents can raise scores by modifying an acceptance test without repairing the software, and our reporting experiments show higher scores accompanied by less actual work. We interpret these reward-seeking departures from expectations, including behaviors commonly studied as reward hacking [ Taufeeque et al., 2026 , Qi et al., 2026 ] , as shortcuts that bypass contextual requirements of task completion. We construct tasks in which compliant completion remains feasible, while some executable shortcuts earn more reward than any compliant strategy (Fig. 1 ). These intrinsic, verifiable reward–context conflicts let us study how RL acquires shortcuts under controlled incentives. Figure 1: Reward–context conflicts. To support these studies at scale, our construction generates executable task environments in batches, with contextual safety boundaries encoded in their dynamics and verifiable feedback for capability and safety learning. Using these environments, we find that profitable violations can become frequent even when conflict tasks form a minority of the training data. Simple reward shaping with the same feedback supports joint capability and compliance gains and mitigates acquired violations. The learned safety behavior adapts to expanded authorization and generalizes across new tasks and scenarios, as well as changes in wording and permission scope. Cross-task cases further document capability transfer and violation emergence, alongside protection that carries over to workflows whose evidence and approval requirements change during execution. 2 Formulation We consider language-model agents trained through reinforcement learning (RL) in executable task environments. Task dynamics determine which executions are possible by specifying how tool calls change the environment’s state and return observations. Contextual requirements distinguish compliant executions within this space, while task reward assigns value to the resulting outcomes. We formalize conflicts between these requirements and task reward as verifiable task properties. This lets us construct tasks for studying shortcut acquisition and use the same execution checks to learn contextual compliance. 2.1 Contextual execution and task reward For a task instance, let τ \tau denote an execution trajectory ending in a terminal state and T T the set of trajectories executable under its dynamics. A context c c specifies requirements on how the task is carried out, and C ⁡ ( c ) ⊆ T C(c)\subseteq T contains the trajectories that satisfy them. Trajectories in T ∖ C ⁡ ( c ) T\setminus C(c) remain executable despite falling outside contextual expectations (Fig. 1 a). Within an execution, earlier actions can change which requirements apply later, so compliance is defined over the full history. Across authorization contexts, expanded permission can make the same physical execution compliant without changing its outcome or task reward (Fig. 1 c). Task completion follows the instance’s own requirements and is checked separately (Appendix B.2 ). 2.2 Verifiable reward–context conflicts We call a reward-seeking route that bypasses contextual requirements of task completion a shortcut . We ground task reward and contextual checks in the state-update mechanism before realizing its tool interface, so each instance retains a verified incentive structure. 3 Scalable construction of verifiable task environments In real workflows, executable behavior can exceed contextual permission. This gap provides a practical source of reward–context conflicts. We turn it into reusable task mechanisms by encoding contextual requirements as checks on the states and execution histories maintained by task dynamics, while keeping boundary-crossing actions executable. Following VHD, we then render these dynamics into interactive environments [ Shen et al., 2026a ] . Figure 3 illustrates how familiar operational rules give rise to these mechanisms. Under a shared resource allowance, a tool call can produce more work while pushing cumulative use beyond the authorized total, including when calls are spread across accounts. The dynamics record both work and resource consumption, allowing productive execution to register a contextual violation. In a versioned workflow, publication requires evidence for the current artifact, even though the interface may still allow an archived report to be selected. Input-validity rules provide further candidates, as in an ordering interface that accepts negative quantities despite a nonnegative-quantity requirement. Unforeseen weaknesses in real systems can expose similar gaps. In the OpenAI–Hugging Face incident, agents seeking task solutions exploited infrastructure vulnerabilities to circumvent network isolation and access third-party systems [ OpenAI, 2026 ] . Even when the intended boundary is clear, the operations and combinations that allow an agent to cross it may not have been anticipated. We construct candidate mechanisms by retaining a contextual requirement while relaxing its enforcement in the executable dynamics. In a resource workflow, for example, sampling varies physical capacity and job demands together with the authorized allowance. The same accounting rules track resource use across these instances. We retain instances satisfying both properties. The saved Wordle and Minesweeper paths show two parser behaviors: completion of a partial answer and disclosure of hidden board state (Appendix F ). 4.3.4 Evolving and composed requirements Safety learned on resource tasks extends to changing evidence requirements. The Resource policies were trained on Allocation and API, without the release tasks below. These targets introduce evidence and approval obligations that change during execution, alongside compositions with resource permission. In a release workflow, the owner revises the validation rule after a passing report. A later review changes the approval commitment while the artifact remains correct. On 32 fresh physical tasks, Safety-trained improves safe completion over Task-trained by 35.9 percentage points, from 53.1% to 89.1%. All three policies achieve full real work on this panel (Fig. 13 a; Appendix E.4 ). We extend this setting by combining changing evidence requirements with resource permission. In a cohort-release workflow, the owner revokes vendor permission and later issues a separate grant with its own data scope. Publication must also satisfy the updated evidence and commitment requirements. On 32 physical tasks, Safety-trained improves safe completion over Task-trained by 10.9 percentage points (Fig. 13 a; Appendix E.4 ). All three policies achieve full real work on both restricted target panels and safe completion in every expanded-permission attempt. The physical tasks support productive execution under both authorization conditions. To test transfer across operational descriptions, we realize the same changing-evidence dynamics in warehouse operations, financial settlement, manufacturing quality, and public services. Across these settings, Safety-trained improves safe completion over Task-trained by 12.5–50.0 percentage points and retains full real work in every attempt (Fig. 13 b; Appendix E.5 ). New operational descriptions and tool names preserve the verified dynamics and contextual checks, connecting this transfer comparison to the realization approach in Section 3 . Safety-trained adapts when approval creates new obligations. We also change how new obligations arise during execution. In an approval-triggered workflow, the approval response revises both the evidence rule and the historical commitment. Safety-trained increases safe completion from 27.1% to 62.5%, with real work .998 compared with .992 for Task-trained (Fig. 13 a). Across these studies, compliance adapts to changed authorization, and learned protection reaches a different mechanism. This pattern supports learning safety as context-sensitive execution behavior. Safety supervision comes from the consequences of the agent’s own actions under the applicable requirements, connecting reduced risk with productive use of legitimate opportunities. Encoding requirements in the dynamics supplies both task data and feedback for learning transferable safety alongside capability. 5 Discussion These experiments cover a controlled subset of the ways task optimization can produce successful work that violates contextual requirements. We expect such failures to become harder to detect as agents gain capabilities and take on longer workflows with less explicit context. Clear context does not remove incentive conflicts. Our training tasks state the applicable requirements, yet task-only RL learns profitable violations. Continued optimization also produces substantial shortcut acquisition when conflict tasks remain a minority of the data (Section 4.1 ). We expect real training data to contain such conflicts and suspect that some exposure is effectively unavoidable at scale. With continued optimization, reward can keep reinforcing these patterns. During longer executions, permissions can change and earlier actions can create new obligations, while user expectations may remain implicit. We think this will make reliable execution harder. Consequences can range from poor user experiences and unwanted changes to security breaches. In the incident involving OpenAI and Hugging Face, agents seeking task solutions crossed intended infrastructure boundaries to access third-party systems [ OpenAI, 2026 ] . Capability growth can conceal boundary crossing. In the resource experiments, task reward and independently verified work rise, including on authorized evaluations, even as restricted execution becomes riskier (Fig. 4 a). An RL researcher would normally read these as signs of progress. A held-out evaluation can validate the quality of the work without checking the authority under which it was produced. Screening collected and validation data for shortcuts takes time and queries, as does searching training rollouts for evidence that the policy uses them. With limited budgets, both jobs are difficult. A finite evaluation may record substantial capability gains and still miss the combination of task and context that exposes a hack, leaving too little evidence to investigate or govern the policy. Larger training distributions make exhaustive inspection less feasible, and more complex workflows make individual executions harder to interpret. Composition makes loopholes harder to recognize. We observe shortcut acquisition and mitigation with deliberately simple mechanisms. Following VHD’s mechanism-first approach, we can combine these mechanisms into more complex workflows while retaining executable references and contextual checks [ Shen et al., 2026a ] . In the dynamic cases, a correct artifact can have obsolete evidence, and an approval response can create new obligations before publication (Section 4.3 ). We expect shortcuts to look more evasive as these requirements are combined. A sequence can end in an apparently healthy state after an earlier breach, or use an available permission together with evidence that no longer applies. Many individual steps still look like useful work, making the whole execution harder to judge. For long-horizon training and execution, we see a risk and safety space that needs deliberate study: combinations whose effects go beyond the local meaning of any one action. Related security studies show how locally benign fragments and interactions can combine into harmful overall intent [ Wei et al., 2025b , Shen et al., 2026c , Shen et al., 2026b ] . We expect these opportunities to increase sharply with longer horizons and more capable models as people delegate broader workflows. Human verification is limited, but investigation is indispensable. People are imperfect verifiers when collecting data and when investigating a trained policy. Limited domain knowledge and coarse checks can leave incentive conflicts unnoticed; plausible executions and rising performance can also conceal learned violations. HCI studies of data work report gaps in domain feedback [ Sambasivan et al., 2021 ] . Research on conversational LLMs associates overreliance with shallow task comprehension, coarse checking, and accepting misinformation despite hesitation [ Liu et al., 2026 ] . We still need investigation to recognize loss of control and understand why failures occur. It is also how we assess mitigation. Early work on agent-assisted monitoring and investigation offers a way to share this work with models [ Greenblatt et al., 2024 ] . Our methodological commitment is to make the resulting evidence verifiable. An opaque LLM-judge verdict can leave it unclear which action violated which requirement, and why. Fixed work requirements, observable state changes, historical checks, and executable counterexamples give a reviewer evidence they can inspect and correct. Contextual safety is a trainable direction. A simple, accurate history signal supports useful compliance that transfers beyond the training contexts. We think safety awareness could be learned and inherited as part of task competence. We further conjecture that reward shaping in environments with feasible compliant completion and a verified incentive conflict can induce safety awareness through task optimization. The optimization signal can be just a shaped terminal scalar reward, with the contextual penalty computed from execution history. An objective that favors useful compliant behavior creates pressure to anticipate and respect requirements without prescribing a step-by-step safety strategy. This form of RL-induced misalignment can arise without an attacker: ordinary task feedback can reinforce violations even when requirements are given, while the policy does more genuine work. Reasoning over explicit safety specifications [ Guan et al., 2025 ] and attack and defense methods [ Shen et al., 2026b ] address different problem formulations. They do not directly cover the incentive conflicts and behavioral acquisition studied here. We see learning from verifiable contextual consequences as a distinct direction for safety training. Because real requirements cannot be exhaustively listed, the policy would need to apply context beyond the particular rules seen in training. Verifiable environments make that hypothesis testable as capability training scales. 6 Related Work RL-induced misalignment and reward hacking. Formal reward-hacking analysis characterizes disagreement between proxy and intended objectives [ Skalse et al., 2025 ] , while reward-model overoptimization shows how optimizing proxy reward can degrade the quality it is meant to measure [ Gao et al., 2022 ] . In agent RL, studies examine test hardcoding and training against deception detectors [ Taufeeque et al., 2026 ] , reward hacking that generalizes to broader misalignment [ Anthropic, 2025 ] , and harmful actions learned in pursuit of task reward [ Qi et al., 2026 ] . These experiments demonstrate reward-driven misalignment, but their incentive conflicts remain tied to particular task settings. A common account must also cover violations that accompany genuine capability gains, when task reward accurately measures useful work but does not capture contextual execution requirements. We generate diverse tasks with verifiable reward–context conflicts at scale to study shortcut acquisition and generalization during RL and develop transferable safety mitigations. Safety defenses and contextual safety training. Agent-safety research examines risks from tool use, evolving state, and interaction history [ Ruan et al., 2024 , Shen et al., 2026b ] . Earlier evaluation attempts include HVE and HVTB, which instrument game and terminal environments to detect specified shortcuts [ Roth et al., 2026a , Roth et al., 2026b ] , and BAITBENCH, which samples synthetic ML tasks with planted data shortcuts [ Prasad et al., 2026 ] . These benchmarks evaluate fixed agents without jointly certifying compliant feasibility and a strict reward advantage for violating execution over all compliant executions. BenchShield checks reward integrity through execution evidence [ Zheng et al., 2026 ] , while side-effect audits test whether verifiers accept correct work with unrequested changes [ Abrich, 2026 ] . Runtime defenses reason over safety constraints [ Xiang et al., 2025 ] or use monitoring and trusted intervention [ Greenblatt et al., 2024 ] , with BashArena supplying Linux workflows and security-failure objectives [ Kaufman and Lucassen, 2025 ] . Safety training uses principle-derived preferences [ Bai et al., 2022 ] , reasoning over safety specifications [ Guan et al., 2025 ] , or expected-cost constraints [ Achiam et al., 2017 ] . Together, these lines leave open how to generate task distributions with certified reward–context conflicts and use their execution checks to study shortcut acquisition, generalization, and mitigation through RL. We build on VHD’s solved-mechanism construction [ Shen et al., 2026a ] to connect certified task conflicts with execution feedback for contextual safety learning. 7 Conclusion The experiments support our premise that contextual safety is a learnable part of task execution. Encoding requirements in the dynamics makes reward–context conflicts reproducible and turns their consequences into training feedback. Mechanisms establish compliant feasibility and a rewarding opportunity beyond the contextual boundary; LLM setters realize diverse tasks while independent work and history checks preserve the distinction between score, capability, and compliance. The experiments expose two routes to reward improvement: additional real work through unauthorized resources, and higher reported scores despite degraded work. The shortcut interpretation connects these incentive conflicts to verifiable construction and intervention. Controlled changes in conflict prevalence and training exposure reveal substantial shortcut acquisition even when conflicts occupy a minority of training data. Verified feedback supports joint capability and compliance gains, partially mitigates acquired violations, and transfers authorization-sensitive behavior across new contexts. Cross-task cases show that both boundary-crossing behavior and learned protection can reach excluded workflows; Validation-to-Allocation transfer provides a concrete protection example with useful work retained. Together, these findings support a data-driven route to safety training in which agents learn contextual compliance from the same task interactions used to develop capability. The construction provides a scalable task distribution and verifiable signals for this learning process. References Guo et al.
