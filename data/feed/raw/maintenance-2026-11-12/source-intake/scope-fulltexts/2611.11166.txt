Existing world-action models often acquire such predictive knowledge through explicit future prediction. We introduce DexForesight, a framework that learns spatial foresight implicitly by injecting dynamics-aware world representations into the latent space of a dexterous policy. During training, DexForesight learns an action-conditioned spatial world model from offline scene depth, kinematic hand geometry, and demonstration trajectories. The world model predicts 3D hand-point motion and visual feature changes, capturing both articulated hand dynamics and scene changes under demonstrated actions, including the motion of fingers occluded from the cameras. We then freeze the world model and align policy queries with its predictive world queries, transferring the learned spatial dynamics into policy latents and using them to condition action generation. At deployment, only the policy is retained, operating on RGB observations, language, and robot state without depth input or explicit future prediction. On 11 DexJoCo simulation tasks, DexForesight improves the overall success rate of the base policy from 49.4% to 60.5%. On three real-robot dexterous manipulation tasks, DexForesight achieves 58.3% success, compared with 50.0% for the strongest baseline. Project page: https://dexforesight.github.io/ 1 Introduction Controlling a dexterous hand requires anticipating how multiple fingers move relative to an object and the surrounding scene. During a grasp, fingers must move around object surfaces, often while part of the hand is hidden from the cameras. Recognizing the current scene alone is therefore insufficient: the policy also needs information about how actions change the hand–scene configuration. We refer to this predictive understanding as spatial foresight, encompassing both articulated hand motion and changes in the surrounding scene. World action models (WAMs) couple future prediction with action generation, offering a natural route to learning such foresight ( Yuan et al., 2026 ; Yang et al., 2026 ) . Applying them to dexterous manipulation, however, raises two challenges. First, the spatial information used for prediction must capture more than visible scene surfaces. Even accurate scene depth leaves occluded fingers unobserved, whereas the robot’s kinematic model and joint states provide hand-link positions independently of camera visibility. These complementary sources can ground predictions in both scene and hand geometry. Yet requiring estimated depth at deployment ( Qu et al., 2025 ; Sun et al., 2025 ) introduces dependence on an online reconstruction pipeline: depth estimation adds computation, and reliable depth videos require temporal consistency as the hand and objects move ( Li et al., 2023 ) . The challenge is to exploit this spatial information for learning without making reconstruction a prerequisite for control. Figure 1 : Learning and transferring spatial foresight. Demonstrations spanning 60 tasks and five spatial categories train an action-conditioned spatial world model to predict visual dynamics and 3D hand flow. The learned spatial foresight is transferred into a low-level policy, enabling action generation without world-model execution or future decoding at deployment. DexForesight outperforms prior world-action models in both simulation and real-robot evaluations. Second, predictive knowledge need not be used through explicit future generation. In WAM designs that imagine the future during inference, the model generates future images, motion, or spatial states alongside, or before, its actions ( Shen et al., 2026 ) . These predictions then guide action selection or planning ( Huang et al., 2026 ) , making future generation an explicit part of the deployed decision process and adding computation at every control step. For dexterous control, however, the key information is how actions change the hand–scene configuration; reconstructing full future observations may additionally require appearance details, such as texture or background, with limited relevance to action selection ( Zheng et al., 2025 ) . Can a policy instead acquire the predictive features needed for spatial foresight during training, without decoding any future outputs at deployment? These challenges motivate transferring spatial foresight into the policy’s latent representations. We introduce DexForesight: Grounding Dexterous Policies with Implicit Spatial Foresight (Figure 1 ), which decouples an action-conditioned spatial world model from the policy. The action-conditioned world model learns hand and scene dynamics from training-time signals: scene depth estimated offline from stereo demonstrations with StereoAnyVideo ( Jing et al., 2025 ) , kinematic hand geometry, and recorded future states. Given demonstrated controls, it jointly predicts displacements of fixed points on the hand, which cover occluded fingers through forward kinematics, and changes in visual features of the observed scene. Both objectives train a shared set of world queries, while depth conditions the backbone on current scene geometry through cross-layer fusion, so no future depth targets are required. We then freeze the world model and transfer its predictive features into the policy through query alignment (Figure 2 ): policy queries read hidden layers of the VLM backbone, match the frozen world queries, and condition the action expert, linking predictive supervision to action learning within the same policy. At deployment, the policy operates only on current RGB observations, language, and robot state, with no world-model execution, online depth reconstruction, or explicit future generation. DexForesight thus acquires spatial foresight of hand and scene dynamics through action-conditioned prediction during training and carries it into dexterous action generation through the policy’s own latent features. Our contributions are: • We introduce DexForesight , which aligns dexterous policy latents with predictive representations from a frozen action-conditioned spatial world model and uses the aligned features to condition action generation, enabling implicit spatial foresight without world-model execution, online depth reconstruction, or explicit future generation at deployment. • We develop an action-conditioned spatial world model using approximately 370 hours of demonstrations across 60 tasks and five categories. Combining offline scene depth with kinematic hand geometry, it jointly predicts 3D hand motion and visual dynamics, providing supervision beyond visible RGB observations. • We evaluate DexForesight on 11 DexJoCo simulation tasks ( Wang et al., 2026a ) and three real-robot tasks: Close Box, Insert Battery, and Screw On Lid, testing hand–object geometry, alignment under object occlusion, and articulated motion under finger occlusion, respectively. DexForesight achieves 60.5% and 58.3% success in simulation and real-robot evaluations, surpassing the strongest respective baselines by 9.6 and 8.3 percentage points. 2 Related Work 2.1 Future Modeling for Robot Policies World-action models couple future prediction with policy learning, either by jointly modeling future observations and actions ( Zhu et al., 2025 ; Shen et al., 2026 ; Li et al., 2026a ) or by incorporating predictive supervision during training without requiring future generation at deployment ( Liang et al., 2026 ; Yuan et al., 2026 ; Luo et al., 2026 ; Zheng et al., 2025 ) .
Beyond image prediction, EgoWAM ( Li et al., 2026b ) explores 3D motion flow as a predictive target for human–robot co-training.
These approaches motivate learning structured predictive representations, while dexterous manipulation calls for supervision that captures the coordinated motion of articulated fingers, including those hidden from the cameras.
DexForesight addresses this requirement by combining scene geometry with kinematic hand representations and jointly predicting action-conditioned 3D hand motion and visual changes.
Persistent hand-point targets preserve correspondence across articulated motion and supervise occluded fingers through forward kinematics.
The resulting predictive representations are transferred into policy latents, without requiring world-model execution or future decoding at deployment. 2.2 Spatially Grounded Policies Spatially grounded robot policies introduce geometric structure either directly into the policy ( Qu et al., 2025 ; Yuan et al., 2025 ; Sun et al., 2025 ) or through representation-level supervision.
ReconVLA ( Song et al., 2026 ) uses auxiliary reconstruction to improve spatial perception, while Spatial Forcing ( Li et al., 2026c ) aligns intermediate VLA features with representations from pretrained 3D foundation models, enabling implicit spatial grounding without explicit 3D inputs at deployment.
These approaches primarily improve representations of scene geometry, rather than explicitly modeling how an articulated dexterous hand evolves under control.
DexForesight instead targets action-conditioned spatial dynamics : it combines offline scene depth with robot kinematics and predicts future 3D displacements of persistent hand points together with visual changes.
This embodiment-aware supervision captures hand–scene evolution beyond visible image geometry, including occluded finger motion, while the deployed policy still requires only RGB, language, and robot state. 3 Method DexForesight uses offline scene depth and hand geometry computed from recorded robot states to train a spatial world model. Depth enters through intermediate fusion blocks. World queries read the resulting hidden states and receive demonstrated controls through an action-conditioned adapter; these queries condition the Hand Flow Expert and the Visual Dynamics Expert. Hand geometry. We sample fixed points on the hand’s URDF links and transform them into the robot base frame through forward kinematics using recorded robot states. Each point retains its link assignment and local coordinates across frames, preserving correspondence as the hand articulates. The resulting point clouds specify the positions of hand surfaces, including those occluded in the camera views. A Point-BERT ( Yu et al., 2021 ) encoder encodes the current point cloud and a short history of hand motion. These hand features enter the backbone alongside RGB, language, and robot-state history. Current and historical hand geometry provide encoder inputs, while future hand-point displacements provide the hand-flow prediction targets. Depth fusion. For real-robot demonstrations, we estimate scene depth offline from recorded stereo RGB sequences using StereoAnyVideo. For simulation, we render depth directly from MuJoCo rollouts.
OmniVGGT ( Peng et al., 2026 ) encodes RGB frames paired with their corresponding depth maps into geometry tokens E t E_{t} . We use cross-attention for condition injection, as in RDT ( Liu et al., 2025 ) . A set of N N learnable world queries attends to hidden states from backbone layers 12, 20, and 28. Before action conditioning, these queries encode the current observations and hand-state history. We express the demonstrated control at each offset relative to the current robot state and encode it with its temporal offset. The query adapter attends to these action tokens to produce Z t w Z_{t}^{\mathrm{w}} . The demonstrated controls disambiguate which future evolution should be modeled. The hand-flow and visual-dynamics objectives described below train Z t w Z_{t}^{\mathrm{w}} to encode how the articulated hand and observed scene evolve under these controls. Thus, actions serve as conditioning inputs, while hand motion and visual changes provide the prediction targets. Both prediction experts receive Z t w Z_{t}^{\mathrm{w}} and the same action tokens, conditioning their predictions on the controls associated with the target future states. Predicting hand motion and visual changes. The Hand Flow Expert predicts displacements of corresponding hand points, while the Visual Dynamics Expert predicts changes in image features. Depth supplies current scene geometry and is not required at future timestamps. Both experts predict noise added to their targets, with an additional Smooth L1 loss on the recovered clean targets. Both prediction losses backpropagate through Z t w Z_{t}^{\mathrm{w}} and the backbone, training the query features that supervise the policy in Section 3.2 . Data and training. For the real-robot setting, we organize approximately 370 hours of demonstrations across 60 tasks into five categories: spatial arrangement, assembly and contact, rotation and articulation, deformable objects, and tool use (Figure 3 ). These tasks cover changes in object placement, precise alignment, joint rotation, object shape, and sustained contact, providing varied examples of hand motion and scene changes for world-model training. We estimate depth offline with StereoAnyVideo for a 100-hour subset covering spatial arrangement, assembly and contact, and rotation and articulation, matching the three categories evaluated on the real robot. Training proceeds in three stages: warmup , dynamics pretraining , and depth injection . During warmup, we jointly optimize the pretrained backbone and the newly introduced prediction modules, allowing the backbone representations to adapt to the hand-motion and visual prediction objectives. We then perform dynamics pretraining on the demonstration corpus, where the model learns action-conditioned interaction dynamics from RGB observations, language instructions, and histories of hand and robot states. These first two stages are conducted without depth input. In the final depth-injection stage, we introduce depth conditioning and continue training on the 100-hour subset. Geometry features are extracted by a frozen encoder and injected into the backbone through trainable fusion modules, while the backbone and prediction modules remain jointly optimized. The hand-motion and visual prediction objectives remain unchanged, allowing depth conditioning to refine the learned dynamics representations under consistent predictive supervision. 3.2 Injecting Spatial Foresight into Policy Latents After world-model training, we freeze its parameters and use the action-conditioned queries Z t w Z_{t}^{\mathrm{w}} to supervise policy queries Z t π Z_{t}^{\pi} , as shown by the query-alignment connection in Figure 2 . Computing these targets requires the world-model backbone and query adapter, without executing either prediction expert. We jointly optimize query alignment and action prediction to train the policy. Reading queries and conditioning actions. We add N N learnable policy queries that attend to selected hidden layers of the policy VLM. A learned projection maps their outputs into the world-query feature space for alignment. These queries receive only the policy’s current inputs. Let C t C_{t} denote the action expert’s original context. A learned projection W act W_{\mathrm{act}} maps the policy queries to the action-context dimension, after which we concatenate the tokens: C ~ t = [ C t ; Z t π ​ W act ] \widetilde{C}_{t}=[C_{t};Z_{t}^{\pi}W_{\mathrm{act}}] . The action expert uses C ~ t \widetilde{C}_{t} to generate A t A_{t} . This gives it access to the query features alongside its original context. Alignment gradients update the query reader and the trainable VLM parameters; action gradients also train how the expert uses the added tokens. Aligning query features. We match corresponding query features and their pairwise similarities. We retain the policy’s original action objective. Since the policy does not observe U t U_{t} , query alignment is an auxiliary learning target, not an exact reconstruction of the teacher’s action-conditioned predictions. Demonstrated controls specify the dynamics used for supervision; the policy learns from the resulting control-dependent visual and hand-motion variation while retaining its standard behavior-cloning objective. Deployment. At inference, the policy reads current RGB images, language, and robot state, computes its queries, and supplies the augmented context to the action expert. It computes this context once per observation and reuses it during action sampling. The world model, depth encoder, hand-geometry encoder, and both future experts are removed. The deployed policy retains the query reader and context projection, with its original action representation and sampling procedure. 4 Experiments Our experiments answer three research questions: • RQ1 (baselines). Transfer versus direct prediction. We next ask whether directly attaching predictive objectives to the policy can provide the same benefit. The upper block of Table 4 compares DexForesight with four direct additions to GR00T: a DINO future-residual head, a hand-flow head, both heads together, and depth with AttnRes. The hand-flow head is the strongest direct-prediction control at 53.1% overall, 3.7 points above GR00T. DexForesight nevertheless reaches 60.5%, exceeding this control by 7.4 points, while combining both prediction heads reaches only 49.5%. Together with the latent probe, these results suggest that policy latents can retain predictive structure useful for control without future decoding. In this setting, query alignment is more effective than directly supervising visual-change or hand-motion prediction. We further examine the teacher target. Pre-action queries encode scene and hand context without explicit conditioning on the demonstrated controls, yielding 52.7% versus 49.4% for the GR00T N1.7 backbone. Their weaker performance is consistent with a gap between observation-level context and the specific manipulation behavior being learned. Conditioning on the demonstrated actions provides control-dependent predictive features, raising success to 60.5%. Replacing hand-motion supervision with a future-action target reaches only 48.4%, suggesting that action prediction alone does not provide an equally effective transfer target. The teacher is trained, including through future-horizon dropout over exposed action prefixes, to model how actions affect future evolution. Shuffling the conditioning actions reduces success to 41.5%, below the GR00T N1.7 backbone.
