First, we reanalyzed trial-level data from a large adult word-extension study ( Lewis & Frank, 2018a ) , computing each test image’s distance to the examples shown in the space of a frozen self-supervised visual encoder (DINOv2). Distance was associated with extension (log odds − - 3.23 per standard deviation; 520 people), and an exploratory post hoc analysis suggested that this association persisted within the taught domain after controlling for taxonomic level and teaching condition. Example composition also mattered: after three examples, adults extended a word to 1.1% of superordinate items when the examples were subordinate and to 77.5% when they were superordinate. Second, in an artificial learner using the same encoder, words joining two categories were learned less accurately the farther apart the categories were (84.7%, 65.4%, and 53.0% naming accuracy for near, mid, and far pairs). The encoder’s distances correlated with human similarity and confusability measures from three datasets ( ρ = .501 \rho=.501 – .795 .795 ), but this representational alignment did not translate into conclusive evidence that human-derived distances predict learner difficulty, and a second encoder trained on one child’s experience reproduced the tier ordering but not the pair-level effect. The secondary human data were analyzed under an internal plan fixed before the confirmatory analyses (not externally preregistered). Visual similarity accounts for part of how generalization is organized in both systems, while the composition of examples contributes what the distance we used does not capture. Keywords: word learning; word extension; suspicious coincidence; visual similarity; self-supervised vision models; secondary data analysis; artificial learners 1 Introduction A word can cover several kinds of objects and still be coherent. Children call cats and dogs doggie long before they call frogs and trucks by a common name ( Rescorla, 1980a ) , and adults told that three Dalmatians are all feps will typically refuse the label for a cow. Two kinds of explanation are usually offered for such patterns. Similarity-based accounts hold that a word spreads to referents that resemble its known instances ( Sloutsky & Fisher, 2004a ; Nosofsky, 1986a ) . Bayesian accounts hold that learners infer the most plausible extension from a structured hypothesis space, so that the composition of the examples, and not only their similarity to a test object, determines how far the word is extended ( Xu & Tenenbaum, 2007a ) . Developmental work shows both influences: children extend novel nouns by shape ( Landau et al., 1988a ) , construe them taxonomically rather than thematically ( Markman & Hutchinson, 1984a ) , and project properties by category membership over appearance ( Gelman & Markman, 1986a ) . This article asks a narrow version of this question: when two objects receive the same name, how much is explained by their visual similarity, and what do the examples used to teach the word contribute? Answering it requires a measure of visual similarity that is defined independently of the word-learning behavior under study and can be applied to the exact images people saw. Self-supervised vision models now provide such a measure. Their embedding spaces are learned without labels and can be queried for any image, and their geometry partly aligns with human similarity judgments ( Hebart et al., 2020a ; Roads & Love, 2021a ) . They are not models of human perception, and human similarity judgments themselves mix perceptual and conceptual information ( Hebart et al., 2020a ; Hebart et al., 2023a ) ; but they give an explicit, reproducible similarity space in which both human and artificial generalization can be described. A growing literature asks how far such representations align with human similarity and how alignment can be increased. Alignment with human similarity judgments varies across networks and depends more on training data and objective than on model scale or architecture ( Muttenthaler et al., 2023a ) . Representations can be made more human-like by fine-tuning on human perceptual similarity judgments ( Fu et al., 2023a ) , which also benefits several downstream vision tasks ( Sundaram et al., 2024a ) , or by transferring the hierarchical structure of human judgments across abstraction levels ( Muttenthaler et al., 2025a ) . We deliberately used a frozen encoder that was not aligned to human data, so that any convergence with human measures is not built in; the partial alignment we report should be read against this choice. We also used only the global (CLS) embedding of the final layer; intermediate layers and patch-level features may relate differently to human similarity and were not examined. We combine two sources of evidence. The first is human: we reanalyzed trial-level data from Lewis & Frank (2018a) , who conducted a large, multi-experiment replication of the suspicious-coincidence paradigm of Xu & Tenenbaum (2007a) with adults and made their data, stimuli, and code public. Because the exact images are available, we could compute, for every test item, its distance in the encoder’s space to the examples a participant had just seen, and ask whether that distance is associated with the participant’s decision to extend the word. The second is artificial: a learner that operates over the same frozen encoder, for which we fix which categories each word designates and measure whether the learner can name new images. The artificial learner is not a model of the adult participant; it is a controlled system in which the representation is fully known, so that the contribution of similarity in that space can be isolated. The two parts address different behaviors. The adult decided how far to extend a word; the learner tried to recover a taught label. We therefore treat the comparison as a test of how far a shared similarity space describes both, not as evidence of a common mechanism. Our contributions are: • A secondary analysis of adult word extension showing that distance in a self-supervised visual space is associated with extension, and that its effect depends on the composition of the teaching examples (exploratory). • A controlled demonstration, in artificial learners, that the learnability of a two-category word decreases with the distance between its categories, with taxonomic relatedness adding no detectable prediction in the sample tested. • Confirmatory tests of two distinct links between the systems: the encoder’s geometry partially aligns with human similarity and confusability measures, but this representational alignment did not translate into conclusive evidence that human-derived distances predict which words the learner finds hard. All human data are secondary: they come from experiments designed and run by other teams to answer other questions. Table 1 : Main Deviations From the Analysis Plan for the Human Data 2 Adults: Visual Distance and the Composition of Examples 2.1 Data Lewis & Frank (2018a) ran 12 online experiments with adults in the word-learning paradigm of Xu & Tenenbaum (2007a) . On each trial a novel word was taught with one subordinate example, three subordinate examples, three basic-level examples, or three superordinate examples (e.g., one Dalmatian; three Dalmatians; three dogs of different breeds; three different animals), and participants selected, from an array of 24 test images, all the objects that the word could also name. Of the 24 test items, 8 belonged to the taught domain (subordinate, basic-level, and superordinate matches); the rest came from other domains. The suspicious-coincidence effect is the reduction in basic-level generalization after three subordinate examples relative to one. Lewis & Frank (2018a) recovered the effect with a large effect size under both sequential and simultaneous presentation, but found it sensitive to trial order: it was absent, or reversed under sequential presentation, when the three-example trials preceded the one-example trial. We used their public, anonymized trial-level data and the original stimulus images. Participant identifiers in that release are assigned across experiments, so the data comprise 600 sessions from 520 distinct people (56 people took part more than once), 7,200 trials, and 172,800 item-level decisions. 2.2 Visual Distance Each image was encoded with the same frozen DINOv2-small encoder used for the artificial learners ( Oquab et al., 2023a ) . For every trial and test item we computed d d , the cosine distance between the item’s L2-normalized embedding and the normalized centroid of the training examples shown on that trial. The distance is defined entirely by the images, independently of participants’ responses. 2.3 Analysis The confirmatory hypothesis (H4 in the analysis plan) was that the probability of selecting a test item decreases with d d , controlling for the item’s taxonomic level and the teaching condition. In an artificial learner the representation is known and the extension of each word can be fixed. We summarize the core results of the artificial-learner study (full details of the original experiments are in Vera (2026a) and in the project repository). 3.1 Method Images from CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 ( Krizhevsky, 2009a ) were encoded once with frozen DINOv2-small ( Oquab et al., 2023a ) (384-dimensional CLS embeddings, L2-normalized). Images were preprocessed with the model’s default Hugging Face image processor: conversion to RGB, bicubic resizing of the shorter side to 256 pixels (the 32 × \times 32 CIFAR images are therefore upsampled), a 224 × \times 224 center crop, rescaling to [0, 1], and normalization with ImageNet means and standard deviations. The same preprocessing was applied to the stimulus images of the adult study. We did not test alternative preprocessing choices (e.g., other interpolation methods or crops). Learners received nonce words (e.g., slithy , mimsy , vorpal ; Carroll, 1871a ) paired with five images each, in 3-way episodes, and were tested on naming held-out images. The main learner represents a word by the normalized mean of its training embeddings (a prototype; equivalent to a prototypical network over a frozen encoder; Snell et al., 2017a ); exemplar ( k k -nearest-neighbor) and logistic-regression learners gave the same pattern. Each word was assigned either one category (native) or two categories (disjunctive). Disjunctive words were grouped into near, mid, and far tiers by the cosine distance between the two categories’ centroids (mean .359, .559, and .694). For example, tromble (cat + dog) and mivor (frog + truck) both join exactly two categories; what differs is the distance between them. Thirty paired episodes per condition were run (seed 42), with 95% bootstrap intervals over episodes. The tiers contain few words (3, 3, and 4 category pairs for near, mid, and far), so these intervals reflect episode-level variability and do not generalize over words; the dissociation study below addresses generalization over pairs. To separate distance in the encoder’s space from taxonomic relatedness, a dissociation study on CIFAR-100 sampled 100 category pairs from the four quadrants formed by median splits of perceptual (DINOv2) distance and semantic distance (one minus WordNet Wu–Palmer similarity; Wu & Palmer, 1994a ; Miller, 1995a ), taught each pair as one word, and regressed naming accuracy on both distances, under decision rules specified in advance in an internal plan. The reported dissociation results use the original, pre-specified mapping of class names to WordNet synsets. That mapping selected an unintended sense for 21 classes (e.g., tiger as a fierce person; 42 of the 100 sampled pairs involve at least one of them); with these senses corrected, the conclusion was unchanged (perceptual β = \beta= - 0.223 0.223 , semantic p = 0.718 p=0.718 , versus - 0.212 0.212 and 0.660 with the original mapping; with quadrants reassigned, median accuracy remained higher in Q4 than in Q3, .863 vs. .838, n = 35 n=35 discordant pairs). Details are in the released materials. 3.2 Results Naming accuracy was 94.3% [92.2, 96.1] for native words and fell to 84.7% [83.2, 86.1], 65.4% [63.7, 67.2], and 53.0% [51.0, 55.0] for near, mid, and far disjunctive words (chance ≈ 33 % \approx 33\% ; Figure 2 ). The ordering was the same for every non-random learner and replicated across five master seeds. In the dissociation study, perceptual distance predicted accuracy ( β = \beta= - 0.212 0.212 , p = 1.87 × 10 − 7 p=1.87\times 10^{-7} , partial R 2 = 0.245 R^{2}=0.245 ), whereas semantic distance did not ( p = 0.660 p=0.660 , partial R 2 = 0.002 R^{2}=0.002 ; Figure 3 ); sharing a CIFAR-100 superclass did not add detectable prediction once perceptual distance was controlled ( β = 0.015 \beta=0.015 , p = 0.528 p=0.528 ). Because the learner has no access to semantic information, this is a sufficiency result: distance in the learner’s own space suffices to produce the gradient; it does not show that semantics is irrelevant for human learners. Pairs within the same superclass were, on average, learned more accurately (91.5% vs. 84.5%), but only 6 of the 100 pairs fell within a superclass. Accuracy follows the perceptual axis. 4 Testing the Link Between the Two Systems The two results above use the same similarity space, but the adult result does not show that this space captures human similarity, and the learner result does not show that human similarity predicts learning. We tested these two distinct relations, alignment between similarity measures (H1) and prediction of learner difficulty from human distances (H2), with public human datasets, under the same analysis plan. 4.1 Data We used three independent human datasets that cover CIFAR categories: odd-one-out similarity judgments for THINGS concepts ( Hebart et al., 2020a ; Hebart et al., 2023a ) , similarity judgments for ImageNet images (ImageNet-HSJ; Roads & Love, 2021a ), and human classification uncertainty for the CIFAR-10 test set (CIFAR-10H; Peterson et al., 2019a ). Each was converted to a distance between category pairs. CIFAR-100 classes were mapped to THINGS concepts by name and WordNet sense, with ambiguous names resolved to the sense shown in the CIFAR images (e.g., mouse as the rodent, tank as the military vehicle); 83 classes were mapped. Three classes that share a single THINGS concept (maple, oak, and willow, all mapped to tree ) kept their mapping, but the pairs among them were excluded, which yields 3,400 pairs; cattle and ray have only hyponym matches in THINGS and were used only in a stricter sensitivity mapping. For ImageNet-HSJ, each CIFAR class was assigned the ImageNet synsets in the hyponym closure of its WordNet synset (synsets claimed by more than one class went to the most specific class, and exact ties were dropped); 68 CIFAR-100 classes received at least one synset, and pairs were retained when at least 10 relevant judgments were available, yielding 1,390 pairs. CIFAR-10H uses the CIFAR-10 classes directly. Robustness checks with alternative mappings are in the released materials. All mappings were fixed before human distances were computed. THINGS and ImageNet-HSJ judgments reflect both perceptual and conceptual properties of objects ( Hebart et al., 2020a ) , and CIFAR-10H measures confusability rather than similarity; none of them is a pure measure of visual similarity. Confirmatory tests (H1 × \times 3, H2 × \times 2, H4) were Holm-corrected as one family. 4.2 Results The encoder’s geometry aligns with human measures (H1; supported). We do not draw conclusions from H5. 5 Discussion We asked how much visual similarity explains when two objects share a name, and what the teaching examples add. In adults, distance in a self-supervised visual space was associated with the decision to extend a novel word across specifications, and, in an exploratory post hoc test, it added to taxonomic level and teaching condition within the taught domain. In artificial learners operating over the same space, words joining more distant categories were harder to learn, and taxonomic relatedness did not predict accuracy beyond perceptual distance in this sample (only 6 of 100 pairs fell within a superclass); because the learner has no access to semantic information, this is a sufficiency result. The encoder’s geometry aligned partially with three independent human measures of similarity and confusability. Taken together, these results suggest that a shared, partly human-aligned similarity space describes part of the organization of generalization in both systems. The limits are as informative as the associations. First, in adults the composition of the examples changed extension dramatically at comparable distances: three superordinate examples licensed broad extension, whereas three subordinate examples suppressed it, and, in an exploratory analysis, the slope of distance itself depended on the teaching condition. This is the kind of evidence-sensitive inference that Bayesian accounts emphasize ( Xu & Tenenbaum, 2007a ) and that Lewis & Frank (2018a) showed to be sensitive to trial order; the distance-to-centroid measure we used does not, by itself, account for these differences between conditions. In our exploratory post hoc models, terms involving experiment-level trial order improved fit, and within superordinate items both condition and distance improved fit given the other; because order is confounded with other experiment-level differences, we do not interpret these terms further. Second, representational alignment did not translate into conclusive evidence that human distances predict learner difficulty: the encoder’s geometry aligned partially with human measures (H1), but human-derived distances did not reliably predict which words the learner found difficult (H2, inconclusive; one dataset showed an effect in the predicted direction that did not survive correction). Correlation between two representational spaces does not guarantee that both predict learning in the same way. Third, the pair-level learner effect did not reappear with an encoder trained on child experience, so it may depend on properties of the specific encoder. And because THINGS and ImageNet-HSJ mix perceptual and conceptual information, the alignment we observe cannot be attributed to perceptual similarity alone. We therefore claim similarity in the organization of generalization, not a shared mechanism. The adult and the learner performed different tasks, measured on different scales (Figure 1 ), and the learner has no access to the pragmatic and sampling considerations that adults bring to the task. The value of the artificial system is that it isolates one factor, similarity in a known space, and shows what that factor alone can and cannot produce. 5.1 Limitations All human data are secondary. The experiments were designed by other teams for other questions, with different stimuli, tasks, and participants, and we could not alter their designs. The adult analysis is correlational: distance and taxonomic level are related by construction in the stimulus sets, and although models control for level and condition, distance in DINOv2 is one of many possible similarity measures. The descriptive contrast between conditions and the interaction model are exploratory. The H2 analyses had small samples (61 and 32 pairs). All learner results rest on frozen encoders and CIFAR images, two-category words, and shallow learners; only the final-layer CLS embedding was used. The human mixed models include random intercepts by image but no by-image random slopes for distance, so heterogeneity across images in sensitivity to distance was not estimated. The CIFAR-10 tier intervals are episode-level and do not separate pair-level variability. Taxonomic relatedness was measured only with WordNet Wu–Palmer similarity, with few within-superclass pairs. The two post hoc analyses of trial order and superordinate items were specified before being run but after the data had been analyzed, and trial order varied between experiments, together with other design features. The previous multi-agent consensus experiments of this project are reported in Vera (2026a) and are not part of this article. 6 Conclusions and Future Work We asked how much visual similarity explains when two objects share a name, and what the teaching examples add. Distance in a frozen self-supervised visual space was associated with adult word extension across confirmatory specifications, and, in an exploratory post hoc analysis, it added to taxonomic level and teaching condition within the taught domain. Distance was not the whole story. At comparable distances, three superordinate examples licensed broad extension to superordinate test items, whereas three subordinate examples suppressed it, and, in an exploratory analysis, the slope of distance itself depended on how the word was taught. The distance-to-centroid measure we used does not, by itself, account for these differences between conditions. In artificial learners operating over the same encoder, words joining more distant categories were harder to learn, and taxonomic relatedness added no detectable prediction beyond perceptual distance in the sample tested. Because the learner has no access to semantic information, this is a sufficiency result, not evidence that semantics is irrelevant for human learners. The encoder’s geometry aligned partially with three independent human measures of similarity and confusability, and that alignment cannot be attributed to perceptual similarity alone. This alignment did not translate into conclusive evidence that human-derived distances predict learner difficulty: one estimate was small and its interval included zero; another was in the predicted direction but did not survive correction; both samples were small. A second encoder, trained on one child’s experience, reproduced the tier ordering but not the pair-level effect, so that effect may depend on properties of the specific encoder. We do not draw conclusions from the age-of-acquisition analysis. We therefore claim similarity in the organization of generalization, not a shared mechanism. The adult and the learner performed different tasks, measured on different scales, and the learner has no access to the pragmatic and sampling considerations that adults bring to the task. All human data are secondary, and the adult analysis is correlational: distance and taxonomic level are related by construction in the stimulus sets. Visual similarity accounts for part of how generalization is organized in both systems, while the composition of examples contributes what the distance we used does not capture. The most direct next step is an experiment with children using the artificial learner’s categories: teaching novel words for near, mid, and far two-category unions, and for single categories with subordinate, basic, or superordinate examples, would test whether the gradient observed in the learner, and the composition effect observed in adults, appear in the same individuals and with the same images. A closely related question is whether the same pattern holds for another vision encoder. The child-experience encoder we examined already shows that the pair-level learner effect need not reappear; tests with language-supervised encoders such as CLIP ( Radford et al., 2021a ) , other self-supervised encoders, intermediate layers, and patch-level features would show how far the present associations, and the partial alignment with human measures, depend on the frozen final-layer CLS embeddings we used. Further robustness checks include by-image random slopes for distance in the adult models; alternative distance aggregations and image preprocessing; pair-level resampling or hierarchical models for the CIFAR-10 tiers; richer semantic controls (human relatedness norms or distributional semantics) with sampling targeted at within-superclass pairs; and greater coverage for the test of human-derived distances against learner difficulty, with more category pairs and additional human datasets. 7 Ethics Statement No new data were collected for this article. The analysis plan specified random effects by participant identifier. Our first implementation grouped random effects by experiment × \times identifier, assuming that identifiers were reused across experiments for different people. An external review of the analyses pointed out that the original authors assigned identifiers after pooling the twelve experiments, so that one identifier is one person; we verified this in their anonymization script and re-estimated all H4 models, including the planned exploratory interaction, with random effects by person. The conclusions did not change. The person-level first-session sensitivity analysis was then redefined by each session’s recorded start time. In the descriptive comparison of conditions, equivalent condition labels spelled differently across experiments were unified after an error was found; the H4 models were not affected. The test of the incremental contribution of distance within the taught domain was added after an internal critical review. The superordinate-only and trial-order models were specified after an automated external review of the manuscript, when the main results were already known; their specifications were written into the deviation log before they were run.
