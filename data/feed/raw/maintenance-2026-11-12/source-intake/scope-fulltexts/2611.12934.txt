Only EarthRecord tests agents on the raw, multi-decadal, multi-sensor record with both controls. 1 Introduction Multimodal agents now open files, write code, and carry out multi-step data analysis with tools [ 6 , 31 , 32 , 35 , 70 ] .
Turning the Earth’s fifty-year satellite record [ 10 , 65 ] into reliable land-change analysis—tracking forest loss, surface water, settlements, and fire [ 27 , 45 , 46 , 47 , 22 ] —is both a central task of Earth observation science and a demanding test of these agents.
In practice, such analysis begins not with a clean RGB image, but with decades of observations distributed across multiple satellite missions, where an analyst must screen clouds and gaps, map transitions from imagery, and harmonize measurements across sensors and across years [ 7 , 41 , 48 , 50 ] .
Whether multimodal agents can carry out these analyses end to end remains unmeasured. Existing benchmarks fall short in both the data they provide and what their scores verify ( Tab. 1 ).
Vision-language [ 5 , 36 , 64 ] and remote-sensing multimodal benchmarks [ 2 , 66 , 68 ] pose questions on curated images, and recent Earth-observation agent benchmarks [ 19 , 33 , 38 , 44 , 51 , 61 , 72 ] still hand agents preprocessed inputs or short-term tasks, or let them fetch data through dedicated tools, leaving out the raw, multi-decadal, multi-sensor archives where land-change analysis begins.
Moreover, scoring the final answer alone does not verify how the agent reached it.
Models often answer about a region from prior knowledge without using the data [ 1 , 5 , 36 , 63 , 67 ] .
Even when an agent computes from the recorded pixels , a sensor measures radiance that depends on the atmosphere, viewing geometry, and instrument as well as on the ground [ 41 , 50 ] .
In the satellite record, sensor shifts have repeatedly created apparent changes on Earth: tropospheric cooling from orbital decay [ 62 ] , vegetation browning from a degrading sensor [ 60 , 75 ] , and an Amazon green-up from viewing geometry [ 42 ] .
Each time the pixel values moved, the question was whether the ground had changed [ 7 , 15 , 18 , 48 ] . We introduce EarthRecord, a benchmark that evaluates whether multimodal agents can turn four decades of raw satellite records into reliable land-change analysis ( Fig. 1 ).
In an offline sandbox with Python, shell, and an image viewer, an agent receives the record of a site as distributed [ 10 ] —unscaled single-band rasters from Landsat, MODIS, VIIRS, and Sentinel-2 spanning 1984–2026 across 56 sites—leaving cloud masking, mapping, and cross-sensor harmonization to the agent.
EarthRecord poses 238 questions across ten analysis tasks (153 computable from the record, 73 asking what is on the ground, and 12 with insufficient evidence), scored by pass 3 across three passes against withheld references from global products, agreement among methods, and visual interpretation.
Two controls test what answers rest on: the closed book removes the record to measure what prior knowledge alone can answer, and instrument pairs alter one satellite’s data while nothing changes on the ground, testing cross-sensor checks. Evaluating twelve multimodal agents on EarthRecord reveals both their end-to-end capability and two fundamental bottlenecks ( Fig. 1 c, d).
First, the four leading agents complete most analyses end to end—scoring 81.0% to 85.4% pass 3 on the 226 computable and recognition questions—but the six leading models drop from 92.0% on questions computed from record variables to 61.1% on those requiring a map from imagery, where runs often abstain early, mostly without viewing an image, or judge inconsistently across passes ( Sec. 3 ).
Second, the controls show that the record moves answers from prior knowledge to the pixels, but not yet to the ground ( Sec. 4 ).
Providing the record lifts all twelve models on computable questions, though regional priors persist on recognition questions.
When one satellite’s data are altered in instrument pairs, however, 53.5% of right answers turn wrong and follow the altered pixels, and few failed runs attribute the break to the instrument. Our contributions are three. • A benchmark on the raw satellite record. EarthRecord pairs four decades of unscaled Landsat, MODIS, VIIRS, and Sentinel-2 archives at 56 sites with 238 land-change questions across ten analysis tasks. • Controls for prior knowledge and sensor shifts. Closed-book removal and altered instrument pairs test whether answers rest on regional priors, unverified pixels, or cross-checked change on the ground. • An evaluation of twelve multimodal agents. We show that leading agents complete most variable-computed analyses end to end but falter at mapping from imagery, and half of right answers follow altered sensors. Contains modified Copernicus Sentinel data 2024. Landsat data courtesy of the U.S. Geological Survey. 2 EarthRecord Each EarthRecord question asks about the land surface at a given site over a given period.
In an offline sandbox ( Sec. 2.4 and Appendix A ), the agent receives the site’s raw record as distributed by its providers—leaving data selection, cloud masking, scaling, mapping, and cross-sensor harmonization to the agent—and submits a structured answer scored against verifiable references under the standard evaluation and two controls. 2.1 Sites and the raw record EarthRecord spans 56 sites: 48 core areas of 10–30 km on a side and 8 administrative regions [ 49 ] .
The core areas cover eight themes, with six areas each, across six continents and all five Köppen climate groups [ 3 ] , selected by screening a global grid for thematic signal and then reviewed manually.
To probe how prior knowledge interacts with the data, they include 8 famous areas, 7 stable areas selected for their lack of significant change, and 33 obscure areas; the agent can read each area’s coordinates. The record combines four decades of observations up to its snapshot date of 30 September 2026 ( Fig. 2 ).
Landsat surface reflectance begins in 1984, spans TM, ETM+, and OLI [ 10 ] , and provides a median of 1,101 scenes per core area; 109 of the 238 questions span two or more of these Landsat sensors ( Fig. 2 a).
MODIS vegetation indices, land surface temperature, and snow cover begin in 2000 on Terra and later Aqua [ 12 , 14 , 11 , 13 , 58 , 59 , 25 , 26 ] , and VIIRS vegetation indices and temperature begin in 2012 [ 57 , 28 ] .
Sentinel-2 surface reflectance enters in 2019 with at most one scene per month [ 16 ] , and processing baselines from 2022 add a radiometric offset [ 9 ] .
ERA5-Land reanalysis [ 8 , 43 ] , global land cover [ 20 , 73 , 74 ] , terrain [ 17 ] , and administrative boundaries complete the record, and the regions have no Landsat or Sentinel-2 data. Apart from the regional boundary mask and the instrument pairs ( Sec. 2.5 ), pixel values are unmodified: scale factors, offsets, and quality layers are not applied, and a README gives the conversions.
Duplicate and at least 90% obscured scenes are removed, and archive issues present as distributed are kept: of the 57,157 Landsat scenes over the core areas, 4,485 are Tier 2 [ 56 ] , with geometric offsets of up to 15.7 km, and quality layers missed cloud in 5 of 200 sampled scenes they rated clear. 2.2 Questions and analysis tasks The benchmark poses 238 questions—207 at core areas across eight themes (forest, greening, agriculture, water, urban, fire, snow, and coast) and 31 at the regions—over periods of 1 to 38 years.
Every question specifies structured answer fields, including a designated conclusion field used for scoring, and every field accepts insufficient evidence .
Each question belongs to one of ten analysis tasks set by its template ( Sec. B.1 ): Quantify (49), Trend (28), Date (26), Compare (24), Regional statistics (21), Attribute (20), Anomaly and climate (19), Detect (16), Season (12), and Recovery (11), counted over the 226 computable and recognition questions. Computable questions (153, including all 31 regional questions) ask for quantities that a formula can derive from the record, such as greenness trends, lake-area trends, or snow-cover days ( Fig. 3 a). Recognition questions (73, of which 50 are in the forest, fire, and urban themes) ask what is on the ground or what happened, such as forest loss, burn scars, built-up expansion, or coastal reclamation (41 with product references and 32 with visual references; Fig. 3 b, c). Insufficient-evidence questions (12) cover periods with too few clear observations to support a conclusion ( Fig. 3 d). 2.3 Reference answers The target quantity determines the source of each reference answer.
First, the 98 computable questions without a global product take references derived from variables in the record: several methods compute each quantity, numeric references take the method median, and categorical and date conclusions require at least 75% of methods to agree.
Reference methods use Tier 1 Landsat scenes with bright-cloud pixels removed, and the 66 questions that rely on Landsat methods reach the same conclusions with and without this step.
Second, 13 questions take their reference from global products included in the record.
Third, 78 questions take their reference from four global products withheld from the record: Hansen forest loss [ 27 ] , JRC surface water [ 45 ] , GHSL built-up surface [ 46 ] , and MCD64A1 burned area [ 22 ] .
To answer these 41 recognition and 37 computable questions (such as the lake-area trend in Fig. 3 a), the agent must map the change from the imagery itself.
Fourth, causes and land-cover types, and cases where products and methods diverge widely, are set by human review of AI-drafted visual interpretations of the imagery (37 questions: 32 recognition and 5 computable).
The 12 insufficient-evidence questions are set by the number of observations and the clear-sky fraction in the queried period. A numeric answer is right when it falls within the reference range , which 159 questions derive from product and method results and which spans at least ± \pm 5% of the reference value.
Decision thresholds are stated in the question text, and questions with results close to a threshold were replaced during construction. 2.4 Protocol and scoring Each run uses a fresh, offline, CPU-only container with 4 CPUs and 24 GB of memory, Python, a shell, geospatial and classical machine-learning libraries, and an image viewer.
Because the record is delivered as single-band raster files and the viewer reads rendered images, an agent must select bands, apply a stretch, and render an image before it can inspect it.
All models share the same system prompt and tools, each run is limited to 3,600 s of working time and 300 messages, and each answer is submitted with a confidence and a method summary with caveats. Every question is run for three passes and scored on its conclusion field by pass 3 [ 71 ] , which is 1 when the conclusion is right in all three passes and 0 otherwise.
Intervals are 95% bootstrap intervals over questions, and the baselines are the majority class and an always-no-change answer, which is the reference answer for 32 questions.
The first model’s run served as a pilot check of the bank: after its results, references were audited and corrected across the entire bank by question-type rules, 11 questions with unstable references were removed, and pass 3 was set as the main score.
Because question texts did not change, that model was rescored rather than rerun, and the other eleven models ran on the frozen bank. 2.5 Controls Two controls test what the conclusions rest on.
Closed-book runs give the model the same question and the site’s bounding box without the record, and measure what prior knowledge alone can answer; the difference from the score with the record is the gain from the record ( Tab. 4 ). Instrument pairs alter one satellite’s data in the record while nothing changes on the ground, and score the answer against the original question and reference answer.
All 22 pairs use computable questions: 21 scale one satellite’s values from 2018 onward, by up to 20% in 20 pairs and by 5% in one, and the remaining pair replaces Landsat 8 scenes from 2013–2017 with Landsat 7 scenes.
Every retained pair is detectable by comparison with concurrent satellites, and the alteration changes the conclusion of a reference method that relies on the affected satellite.
Eight placebo pairs remove 5% of clear scenes at random and measure conclusion variability unrelated to the instrument.
Model–pair cases right on the original in all three passes are sorted by the altered version: moved cases turn wrong, held cases stay right but are already right in the closed book or have no change as the reference, and beyond cases stay right otherwise ( Sec. 4.2 ). The gap holds within tasks: for the same model and task, the odds of a right answer on a mapping question are 0.31 times those on a computed one (95% interval 0.14–0.66, Sec. B.4 ).
Mapping is also where answers are hardest to get right and least stable: 15 of the 18 questions that no model answers right need a map, and the six leading models are right in only one or two passes on 13.7% of mapping questions against 4.6% of computed ones ( Sec. B.5 ). Some agents abstain without attempting to map. On the 41 recognition questions that need a map, GPT-6.1 Sol, GPT-6 Astra, and GPT-6.1 Terra answer insufficient evidence in 46% to 67% of their runs, against 0.9% to 2.7% on the 37 computable questions that need a map and at most 2% on all computable questions ( Tab. 3 and Sec. B.6 ).
Their abstaining runs make a median of two or three tool calls and mostly view no image.
The declined questions are nonetheless answerable: when these three models answer them, they are right in 85% to 98% of runs, and of the questions that GPT-6 Astra declines in every pass, Opus 5.5 answers nearly half right in all three.
Their lower recognition scores therefore reflect early abstention rather than wrong answers. When agents answer, their confidence does not always signal errors. Every model reports lower confidence on mapping questions than on computed ones, but by very different amounts.
GPT-6 Sol lowers its confidence by 0.11 as its accuracy falls by 13.5 points, whereas Gemini 3 Flash lowers its confidence by only 0.03 as its accuracy falls by 25.7 points, and it never declines an answerable question.
Reported confidence is therefore informative for only some agents: it separates right from wrong conclusions with an AUROC of 0.84 for Sonnet 5.5 and of 0.63 to 0.67 for Gemini 3 Flash, Qwen 3.7 Flash, and GPT-6 Luna ( Tab. 3 and Sec. B.7 ).
When an agent neither declines nor lowers its confidence, as with Gemini 3 Flash, its errors are the hardest for a user to notice. 3.3 Where analyses fail The weaker six models often fail at the first step, using the wrong data or leaving the analysis unfinished in half of their failures ( e.g . , Fig. 5 a; Sec. B.8 ).
Such failures are rare for the leading six (7 of 72); instead, their errors arise later, in an analytic judgment: how to map a change, how to sample time, or how to define the quantity the question asks for ( Sec. B.8 ). For example, one question asks whether winters after 2013 have fewer snow days ( Fig. 5 c).
The answer depends on the definition of winter: none of the 10 runs that define it as December to February finds a decrease, whereas all 13 that use October to April or a longer period find one, as the reference does.
Among these judgments, mapping fails most often, in 31 of the 144 failures, for example when harvested fields are classified as burned ( Fig. 5 d). Figure 5 b shows a further case where observation gaps during the polar night are interpolated as snow or counted as snow-free, and Sec. B.8 shows others such as a flood lake interpreted as a reservoir. Moreover, the same agent often judges inconsistently across passes.
On the snow question of Fig. 5 c, GPT-6.1 Sol defines winter as December to February in one pass, which yields a wrong answer, and as a longer period in the other two, which yield the right one.
Such inconsistency is common: of the 347 wrong runs of the six leading models, 121 (35%) are on questions that the same model answers right in another pass.
These agents are thus able to answer such questions but do not apply the right judgment consistently. 4 Prior knowledge, pixels, or the ground? With the record, answers shift from prior knowledge to the pixels.
When the pixels are altered, however, the answers shift with them rather than reflecting change on the ground.
The question in Fig. 1 a illustrates this sequence. Figure 6 : Outcomes on altered instrument pairs. Outcome of each model–pair case, as defined in Tab. 4 , with grey for cases wrong on the original.
Columns are the instrument pairs grouped by the altered satellite, then the placebo pairs. 4.1 From prior knowledge to the pixels In Fig. 1 a, the question asks whether greenness at a stable forest in northern Argentina has changed significantly.
In the closed book, GPT-6 Sol answers decrease, reasoning that clearing in the region usually lowers greenness; with the record, it computes from the pixels and finds no significant trend, in agreement with the reference.
The same shift from prior knowledge to the pixels holds for all twelve models: with the record, they score 4.6 to 60.8 points higher on computable questions ( Tab. 4 ), and far more answers turn from wrong to right than from right to wrong, 40.9% of model–question pairs against 5.8% ( Sec. C.1 ). On recognition questions, by contrast, the record contributes less and prior knowledge persists.
With the record, four models score lower on recognition questions than in the closed book ( Tab. 4 ).
Even for the three leading models, recognition answers barely depend on viewing the imagery: without the image tool, they give almost the same answers ( Sec. C.2 ).
At famous areas, prior knowledge outperforms the record: the closed book scores 55.9 and the record 50.0 ( Sec. C.3 ). 4.2 To the pixels, but not yet to the ground When one satellite’s data change, the answers change with them.
In Fig. 1 a, Terra NDVI is scaled down from 2018 onward while nothing changes on the ground, yet GPT-6 Sol now answers decrease.
The pattern is general: 53.5% of the cases right on the original turn wrong after the alteration (74/102 on Terra NDVI, 20/50 on Landsat 8, and 13/48 on Terra snow), 91% of the changed answers move in the direction of the alteration, and answers on placebo pairs change in only 12% of cases ( Fig. 6 and Sec. C.4 ). Even among the answers that hold, most do not detect the alteration: they are already right in the closed book or have no change as the reference, and only 7.5% of the cases right on the original hold beyond both ( Fig. 1 d and Fig. 6 ).
Nor does a higher score protect an answer: the eight models above 75 on computable questions still lose 33% to 59% of their right answers ( Tab. 4 ). Agents notice the change in the pixels but interpret it as change on the ground.
On the altered pair in Fig. 1 a, one run notes that Aqua “diverges from Terra in recent years and does not show a significant decline on its own,” yet still answers Decrease.
Although every alteration can be found by comparison with a concurrent satellite ( Sec. 2.5 ), most runs do not make even this comparison: of the wrong runs in cases that move, only 21% report the break and 3.5% attribute it to the instrument, while the others that report it read it as clearing, drought, or land-use change, or leave its cause open ( Sec. C.5 ). 5 Related work Remote sensing vision-language models and benchmarks. Remote sensing vision-language models answer questions on single scenes, in dialogues, and over image time series [ 34 , 53 , 29 ] , and their benchmarks use curated imagery [ 2 , 68 , 66 , 21 ] .
Robustness benchmarks test foundation models under corruptions and shifts, including a radiometric gain and a change of sensor [ 37 , 18 , 15 ] .
EarthRecord evaluates decades of raw record at one site instead of a scene. Agents for data analysis and Earth observation. Sandbox agents are scored by their results in software engineering, databases, and data science [ 31 , 35 , 32 , 6 ] , and in vision, models write programs to answer visual questions [ 24 , 54 ] .
Earth observation agents call tools for retrieval, spectral indices, change detection, or disaster assessment [ 33 , 19 , 51 , 44 , 61 , 72 ] , industrial systems combine them with geospatial foundation models [ 4 , 52 ] , and Earth-Agent-Pro [ 38 ] lets them acquire observations through tools, without controls for prior knowledge or sensor shifts.
EarthRecord gives the agent each site’s multi-sensor archive as distributed, with withheld references and three passes per question. Controls for prior knowledge and sensor shifts. Models can answer visual questions without the image [ 30 , 23 , 1 ] , so benchmarks add blind controls [ 5 , 69 , 21 ] or run agents without tools or internet access [ 33 , 19 ] .
Language models hold extractable but biased geographic knowledge [ 40 , 39 ] , and studies measure how they weigh it against external evidence [ 67 , 63 ] .
The closed book of EarthRecord follows this line, and its instrument pairs follow paired counterfactual tests [ 55 , 36 ] and sensor changes [ 18 , 15 ] but alter the data the agent itself processes while the ground stays as it is. 6 Conclusion EarthRecord evaluates whether multimodal agents can turn four decades of raw, multi-sensor satellite records into reliable land-change analysis across 56 sites and 238 questions.
Across twelve agents, the bottleneck lies not in executing code over the archive but in two scientific steps: mapping change from the imagery and verifying whether a change in the pixels reflects change on the ground.
Leading agents complete most variable-computed analyses end to end, yet falter when a question requires a map—some abstaining, mostly without viewing an image, and others judging inconsistently across passes. When one satellite’s data are altered in stress-test instrument pairs, 53.5% of right answers follow the altered sensor while only 3.5% of failed runs blame the instrument.
Because our sandbox provides general geospatial and classical machine-learning libraries on CPU and our instrument pairs test clear-cut breaks, equipping agents with pretrained vision models for mapping and with routine cross-sensor checks for subtle drift are natural next steps.
EarthRecord and its evaluation sandbox will be released. LLM Usage Statement Multimodal agents are the subjects evaluated in this benchmark ( Secs. 3 and 4 ). In addition, large language models (Claude and GPT) were used in three supporting roles under the authors’ verification:

C.5 Reports on instrument pairs An LLM coded the 228 wrong runs in cases that move and the 45 runs in cases beyond both by what the method summary and caveats of each run say about the sensors: the run attributes a break in the altered period to the instrument; reports the break or a divergence from a concurrent satellite but reads it as land change or leaves its cause open; reports the altered and a concurrent satellite separately, with different results, without flagging them; gives only a generic caveat about sensors; or says nothing about them.
At the two sites with a real fire in the question period, reporting the drop caused by the fire answers the question and does not count as noticing the alteration; only a decline reported outside the burned area counts as a break.
Using two keyword passes over all 273 reports to flag candidate statements, the LLM coded each run; the authors then checked 31 runs—all 11 that the LLM marked as uncertain and 20 drawn at random within the five codes—and agreed with all 31.
