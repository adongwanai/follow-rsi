tabular distribution, but are usually evaluated separately. This leaves
unclear which capabilities they share, how their training objectives
interact, and whether one model can perform all three well. We introduce the
One Table Benchmark ( OTBench ), spanning 56 datasets, 46 registered methods,
and 20 capability axes under aligned data splits and explicit task
contracts. We compare task ranks on aligned support, train five backbones under all
seven non-empty objective combinations on five datasets with three seeds,
and contrast fixed-model profiles with specialist portfolios. Rank comparisons connect imputation and
prediction through numerical recovery and dependence preservation, and link
generation to both through predictive utility; distributional fidelity
follows different, sometimes opposing, rankings. Objective interventions
show the largest pooled gains from including the recipient’s own objective. Generation has
the broadest coverage of positive pooled auxiliary effects, while prediction shows
potential to improve imputation and synthetic-data utility, with responses
that vary across backbones. Cross-addition effects reveal that objective
benefits need not add and depend on the objectives already present.
Fixed-model comparisons expose corresponding utility–fidelity trade-offs:
some generalists surpass specialist portfolios on utility, while fidelity
continues to favor specialization. Frozen foundation models show strong
prediction and numerical point imputation, together with promising synthetic-data
utility, highlighting their potential across all three tasks. These findings
motivate unified tabular models that share useful conditional information
while improving categorical reconstruction and faithful joint sampling.

tabular distribution. Generation produces complete records, imputation
recovers missing features from observed information, and prediction infers
a designated target. Each task has its own benchmarks and modeling
traditions ( Yang et al., 2024 ; Khan et al., 2025 ; Erickson et al., 2025 ) , yet their common
statistical foundation suggests opportunities for sharing learned
capabilities. Understanding these relationships matters for building models
that can serve several uses of a table, and for deciding when a task should
benefit from training on another. The conditional-query view makes this connection explicit: generation
queries the joint distribution, while imputation and prediction query
feature and target conditionals. Models increasingly support several of
these interfaces ( Ivanov et al., 2019 ; Jolicoeur-Martineau et al., 2024 ; Zhang and others, 2025 ) , but existing task-wise
evaluations provide limited insight into which capabilities are shared.
Appendix reviews related benchmarks and generalist
models. Similar rankings may reflect useful common information without showing
whether one training objective helps another. Conversely, objectives that
help separately may provide overlapping or competing benefits when combined.
We therefore study three questions: which capabilities co-occur across tasks,
how objective additions and combinations affect a recipient task, and
whether one fixed model can offer a competitive three-task profile. We introduce the One Table Benchmark ( OTBench ), a unified platform for these
comparisons. It covers 56 datasets, 46 registered methods, and 20 semantic
capability axes. A common outer
train–test split aligns the data populations across tasks, while explicit
information-access and missingness contracts preserve each task’s meaning.
Metric analysis separates point accuracy, distributional fidelity,
dependence preservation, predictive quality, and downstream utility, so that
cross-task relationships can be located at the level of capabilities.

code, and configurations. We first compare task ranks on aligned method–dataset support
(Section ). Imputation and prediction agree most
consistently through numerical recovery, dependence preservation, and
predictive utility. Generation connects to both through the predictive
utility of synthetic data, whereas distributional fidelity has different,
sometimes opposing, rank relationships. This suggests that the information
needed for useful conditional queries can be shared even when broader
distributional criteria favor different methods. To examine objective contributions under controlled changes, we train five
backbones under all seven non-empty combinations of generation, imputation,
and prediction on five datasets with three seeds and declared inference
policies (Section ). Single-addition effects (SAE) measure the gain
from enabling one objective; cross-addition effects (CAE) measure whether
two objectives contribute additively. Recipient self-inclusion yields
the largest pooled gains. Generation has broadly positive pooled auxiliary
effects, while prediction shows potential to improve imputation and
synthetic-data utility. These pooled auxiliary responses are descriptive and backbone-dependent.
CAE reveals sub-additivity and context dependence on the benchmark rank scale: useful individual
objectives need not yield additive joint gains. Finally, fixed-model profiles and comparisons with declared specialist
portfolios connect these relationships to practical model choice
(Section ). Fidelity and utility favor different profiles;
no fixed identity leads throughout. Recent tabular foundation
models ( Zhang and others, 2026 ; Qu et al., 2026 ; Grinsztajn and others, 2026 ) achieve strong prediction and numerical
point imputation without single-dataset fine-tuning, and their generation
is competitive on downstream utility. These results reveal potential for
unified foundation models, while motivating better categorical
reconstruction and faithful joint sampling. Our contributions are: • A conditional-query benchmark that aligns datasets and capability
axes across generation, imputation, and prediction while preserving
task-specific information and evaluation contracts; • Complementary rank comparisons, matched objective interventions,
and fixed-model profile experiments that distinguish shared
capabilities, objective interactions, and practical generality; • Empirical findings on utility-centered task relationships,
non-additive objective effects, and the potential of foundation models
to support all three tasks. 2 Preliminaries 2.1 Definition Table 1: The three tabular tasks expressed as probabilistic queries. relative rankings across generation (G), imputation (I), and prediction (P).
Consistent rankings would indicate that the evaluated capabilities covary
across tasks, whereas axis-dependent or conflicting rankings would show that
cross-task performance cannot be adequately summarized by a single score. Training protocol. Cross-task rank analysis requires a method to support multiple tasks. We
therefore select models with benchmark interfaces for at least two of G, I,
and P; Table 7 lists the methods and their
supported tasks. Each model follows its default training or adaptation
protocol, including its prescribed pretrained or in-context route where
applicable. Dataset-specific fitting and adaptation use the training split
of each OTBench dataset, and evaluation uses its held-out test split.
The same outer split is shared across tasks, so task comparisons do not
change the underlying training and test populations.
Appendix gives the model configurations. For each task pair a a and b b , we identify the common method pool ℳ a ​ b \mathcal{M}_{ab} , align metric directions, average scores over seeds or
replicates, and analyze classification and regression separately.

setting ( Rubin, 1976 ) with a missing rate of 0.3 0.3 . Capability-axis scores follow the common-support rank protocol in
Section . For each axis pair c , c c,c and dataset d d , we retain methods in ℳ a ​ b \mathcal{M}_{ab} for which all
declared members of both axes are valid. We orient the metrics and compute percentile
ranks on this aligned method set, then aggregate the member ranks

To assess sensitivity to method-pool composition, we perform leave-one-method-out (LOMO) analysis by removing each method in turn and recomputing the aligned support, within-dataset ranks, axis scores, and pooled correlation.
An axis pair is LOMO-direction-preserving only when every effective deletion preserves
the sign of the full-pool correlation; deletions that change no valid unit are
excluded from this diagnostic.

Across both prediction settings, numerical point accuracy, association, and downstream utility gap have positive, direction-preserving correlations with every prediction axis.
The distributional axes show a different pattern.
Numerical distribution maintains positive agreement with all three regression axes, but neither distribution axis has a direction-preserving relationship with classification performance. Recovering values for individual records and preserving variable relationships can retain information useful for target prediction.
This is consistent with our intuition that learned statistical dependencies can support both imputation and prediction.
Matching marginal value distributions emphasizes a different property of the completed data, which helps explain its less uniform correspondence with prediction.
The connection is more direct for downstream utility gap because it evaluates completion quality through predictive performance.
Together, these results locate the shared performance of imputation and prediction in numerical recovery, dependence preservation, and predictive utility.
For unified models, this suggests a focus on learning relationships that support both missing-feature recovery and target prediction, including when the target itself is unavailable as conditioning information. Generation–imputation. Generation and imputation have the highest proportion of LOMO-direction-preserving relationships among the task pairs, with 36 of the 42 axis pairs preserving direction.
TSTR gap and global-utility compromise show positive, direction-preserving correlations with all six imputation axes.
Privacy-risk diagnostics show negative, direction-preserving correlations with every imputation axis except numerical distribution. TSTR gap measures the preservation of target-specific predictive utility relative to predictors trained on real data.
Its positive rank correlations indicate that generation methods whose synthetic data better preserve this utility also tend to rank higher on imputation.
This is consistent with our intuition that learned variable dependencies can support both useful data generation and missing-value recovery.
Fidelity and C2ST assess marginal and low-order statistical similarity and real–synthetic distinguishability, respectively, and their correlations with imputation axes vary in sign.
Thus, both tasks can use dependencies among table variables, but a method that ranks highly on generation metrics does not necessarily lead on imputation.
Whether its advantage carries over depends on how effectively the learned relationships support recovery of missing values from observed information. Generation–prediction. For generation and prediction, 17 of the 28 classification axis pairs and 9 of the 21 regression axis pairs preserve direction under LOMO.
Fidelity and C2ST show negative, direction-preserving correlations with all four classification axes, whereas TSTR gap and global-utility compromise show positive, direction-preserving correlations with all classification and regression axes. This contrast in correlation signs distinguishes the statistical similarity of synthetic data from their predictive utility.
Prediction relies on conditional information relevant to a specified target, while generation evaluation also examines other distributional properties whose importance to that target can vary.
The shared focus of TSTR gap and the prediction axes on target-related information helps explain their positive rank agreement.
These results connect generation and prediction through the preservation of target-specific predictive utility.

and prediction relate through their capabilities, training objectives, and
fixed-model performance. Task-rank comparisons connect imputation and
prediction through numerical recovery and dependence preservation, and link
generation to both through predictive utility. Distributional fidelity
follows different relationships, so task competence cannot be inferred from
one criterion alone. Matched objective interventions show the largest pooled gains from recipient
self-inclusion and suggest backbone-dependent auxiliary benefits from
generation and prediction. Their
benefits need not add: joint outcomes depend on the recipient and the
objectives already present. Fixed-model profiles further show why utility
and fidelity can favor different generalist or specialist choices. Foundation models already demonstrate strong prediction and numerical point
imputation without dataset-specific fine-tuning, together with promising
synthetic-data utility. Amid rapid progress in this field, these findings
highlight their potential across all three tasks. We hope OTBench encourages
researchers to develop foundation models that excel at generation,
imputation, and prediction, including reliable categorical reconstruction
and faithful joint sampling. 8 Limitations and Future Work Scope and comparability. Our conclusions depend on the capability axes, information contracts, and
available method–dataset support. Rank alignment describes capability
co-occurrence, rather than identifying objective transfer. In particular,
the rank study’s target-observed imputation and the controlled study’s
target-hidden imputation provide complementary evidence under different
contracts. Profile and portfolio comparisons also use different common
cohorts; the categorical profile contains only three datasets. Cross-task
imputation coordinates average twelve settings and do not replace
setting-specific evaluation. Objective effects and their mechanisms. The factorial study covers five backbones and five datasets, with only one
regression dataset and some unavailable evaluations. Auxiliary single-addition effects remain
exploratory and heterogeneous across backbones. SAE and CAE quantify changes
on a normalized rank scale; they do not identify the mechanisms behind those
changes. I/P self-inclusion and some interaction comparisons can also change
the conditioning policy, while fixed training budgets redistribute updates
among objectives. Objective overlap, update allocation, capacity competition,
and gradient interference therefore require separate interventions. Directions for unified models. The frozen foundation-model results establish the capabilities of the
evaluated inference systems, without isolating the contribution of
pretraining, representations, or decoders. Matched pretraining and inference
ablations are needed before attributing categorical or generation gaps to
specific backbone properties. Future work should broaden the dataset and
model populations, separate training from inference effects, and test how
categorical reconstruction and joint sampling can improve alongside
prediction. AI Use Statement We used AI tools to assist with manuscript drafting and editing, figure

Appendix A Dataset Construction Details A.1 Construction Objective and Overall Pipeline The benchmark supports prediction, missing-value imputation, and data generation under a controlled computational budget. Evaluating every candidate dataset would be costly and would overrepresent sources with multiple versions and groups of structurally similar datasets.
We therefore select representative datasets using only method-independent dataset properties and fixed landmarking statistics ( Pfahringer et al., 2000 ) .
All selection rules and configurations are specified before method evaluation begins, so no evaluated method’s performance influences dataset selection.

A.3 Data Parsing and Hard Filtering Column types and target columns. Variable types follow the ARFF header rather than the appearance of the values. Nominal attributes are treated as categorical variables, whereas numeric , real , and integer attributes are treated as numerical variables. A string attribute is treated as categorical only when its cardinality does not exceed min ⁡ ( 50 , 0.2 ​ N i ) \min(50,0.2N_{i}) . Other free-text fields and all date / relational attributes are removed. Thus, P i P_{i} counts usable features after removing target columns and unsupported fields, rather than raw CSV columns. We use the OpenML default_target_attribute field to identify the target whenever available. If this field is missing, the target is inferred in order from common names, including class , label , target , y , and outcome , or from a low-cardinality final column. Such cases are marked by target_inferred . A numerical final column is inferred as a classification target only when it has between 2 and 30 unique values and the sample size is at least 20 times the number of unique values. as CTGAN and TVAE ( Xu et al., 2019 ) , language-model generators such as
GReaT ( Borisov et al., 2023 ) , and diffusion methods such as TabDDPM, TabSyn, and
TabDiff ( Kotelnikov et al., 2023 ; Zhang et al., 2024 ; Shi et al., 2025 ) . Imputation ranges from chained
equations and random forests ( van Buuren and Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011 ; Stekhoven and Bühlmann, 2012 ) to learned conditional
models ( Ivanov et al., 2019 ; Jolicoeur-Martineau et al., 2024 ) . Prediction combines tree ensembles
with neural and pretrained models ( Chen and Guestrin, 2016 ; Prokhorenkova et al., 2018 ; Hollmann et al., 2023 ) .
Benchmark suites including Why Trees, TabZilla, TALENT, and TabArena broaden
systematic comparisons for prediction ( Grinsztajn et al., 2022 ; McElfresh et al., 2023 ; Liu et al., 2025 ; Ye et al., 2024 ; Erickson et al., 2025 ) .
These task-wise evaluations establish performance within each problem,
while leaving open how capability rankings relate across generation,
imputation, and prediction. Generalist and foundation models. Joint-distribution models can support conditional sampling, while
arbitrary-conditioning approaches accommodate varied observation
patterns ( Ivanov et al., 2019 ; Jolicoeur-Martineau et al., 2024 ) . LimiX further motivates multiple
tabular queries within a shared modeling framework ( Zhang and others, 2025 ) .
Recent tabular foundation models, including LimiX-2, TabICLv2, and
TabPFN-3 ( Zhang and others, 2026 ; Qu et al., 2026 ; Grinsztajn and others, 2026 ) , extend the range of pretrained
systems considered in our benchmark. Conditional-query extensions also
expose generation and imputation routes ( Prior Labs, 2026 ) .
These developments motivate distinguishing interface coverage from
empirical quality under fixed model identities and task contracts. OTBench evaluates this distinction through capability-specific scores and
joint task profiles, rather than introducing a new model architecture. Evaluation and task relations. Synthetic-data quality depends on which aspects of fidelity, dependence,
and downstream utility are measured, and metrics can favor conflicting
properties ( Yang et al., 2024 ; Khan et al., 2025 ) . Multi-task learning also
studies cooperation and competition among training objectives when deciding
which tasks to learn together ( Standley et al., 2020 ) . OTBench connects these issues through three complementary experiments.
Task-rank analysis studies capability co-occurrence on aligned support;
SAE and CAE measure objective additions and combinations under declared
training–inference protocols; and fixed-method profiles compare joint task
performance with specialist portfolios. This separates observational task
relationships, controlled objective effects, and practical model choice,
