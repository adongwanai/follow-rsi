Much of the missing information is implicit in the environment’s dynamics, because each state transition can change the conditions for success long before the reward arrives. A world model trained on real transitions can learn to predict what each action changes, but a predicted observation does not by itself show whether success has become more likely. With a binary terminal reward, PPO’s critic makes this judgment by estimating the current policy’s probability of success, but only for states the agent reaches. Our method, WM-PPO, elegantly brings the two together. For each action the policy samples, executed or not, the world model predicts the next observation and the critic values the predicted successor. In the policy channel, comparisons among these values give the policy auxiliary advantages; in the value channel, their mean gives the critic an auxiliary target. A shared gate weights both channels according to how reliably comparisons with similar margins predicted real outcomes earlier in training. The policy, critic and world model all learn from the agent’s own interaction, with no teacher or privileged information, and only the policy is used at inference. With a comparable number of real environment steps, WM-PPO raises success from PPO’s 63.5% to 87.5% on ALFWorld and from 39.3% to 52.9% on ScienceWorld. It matches the strongest PPO refinements we tested on ALFWorld and exceeds the best of them by 6.5 points on ScienceWorld. The trained policy more often completes prerequisite steps before placing an object or committing to an answer. 1 Introduction Agents trained with reinforcement learning on multi-step tasks often receive only sparse terminal rewards, which provide limited feedback for learning. Yet each state transition can change the conditions for success long before the reward arrives. A needed object is found, an operation’s precondition is met, or a command is rejected, and the next observations record all these changes. When tasked with cleaning a plate and putting it in a cabinet, for example, an agent may find the plate and pick it up, but put it away without first cleaning it at the sink. The final reward marks the whole attempt as a failure. It does not say which steps helped, or which other action would have left the agent better placed to succeed. World models learn environment dynamics by predicting how an agent’s actions change the environment. What a predicted change means for success depends on the task and on what the current policy can accomplish from the resulting state. In text environments, an action’s contribution to success is not necessarily reflected in how much the observation text changes. A transition that produces a very different textual observation may leave the chances of success unchanged: opening a cabinet, for example, may reveal a long list of objects unrelated to the goal. Conversely, a small change in the environment may establish a critical prerequisite or make the goal unreachable. Heating a mug, for instance, may produce only a brief confirmation, yet satisfy a necessary condition for a task that requires a hot mug. We therefore evaluate predicted transitions by their effect on eventual success. Fortunately, PPO ( Schulman et al., 2017 ) already learns such a valuation through its critic. The critic estimates the expected cumulative reward from a state under the current policy, and its evaluation evolves as the policy learns. When agents must learn from sparse terminal success rewards, the critic offers feedback before the task outcome is known. Its values estimate the probability of eventual success, and changes across a transition reveal how an action affects that probability. While PPO uses these estimates only through the advantages of executed actions, we apply the critic to the next states predicted by a world model for the policy’s candidate actions (Figure 2 ). These estimates, which we call transition values , let us compare the policy’s candidates through their predicted consequences. Diagnostics on ALFWorld ( Shridhar et al., 2021 ) support this idea: selecting among the policy’s candidates using these values achieves higher success than direct policy sampling (Section 2 ). We therefore introduce WM-PPO, which incorporates this feedback through policy and value channels. At each state along the main training trajectory, the policy samples several candidate actions and executes the first as usual. The policy channel turns candidate comparisons into auxiliary advantages in PPO’s clipped objective, while the value channel uses the mean transition value as an additional one-step target for the critic at the current state. These critic updates also affect the advantages used in subsequent policy updates. Since value estimation in LLM reasoning can be biased and yield unreliable comparisons between candidate steps ( Yuan et al., 2025 ; Kazemnejad et al., 2025 ) , WM-PPO calibrates both channels against real outcomes. For each training task, it collects one main episode and three branches, each trying a different action at a randomly chosen step. A shared gate uses earlier branch outcomes to weight both channels according to the margin between transition values (Section 3 ). The design stays elegant: the world model is the only learned component added to PPO and reuses the real transitions collected during training. Real returns remain the critic’s main supervision. Training requires no teacher model or privileged information, and the policy acts alone at inference. Prior work also draws on signals beyond terminal rewards to improve language agents (Table 3 in Section 6 ). Some methods add observation-prediction losses during RL ( Shrivastava et al., 2026 ; Lu et al., 2026 ; Ran et al., 2026 ) , while others learn environment dynamics before downstream policy training ( Zhang et al., 2026a ; Chen et al., 2026a ; Yu et al., 2026b ) . Distillation methods obtain finer feedback from teacher distributions or from models given hindsight observations or reference solutions ( Agarwal et al., 2024 ; Yang et al., 2026 ; Wu et al., 2026 ) . World models also provide imagined outcomes for action selection and refinement in WMA, ITP, and CoMAP, where they remain part of the deployed agent ( Chae et al., 2025 ; Liu et al., 2026a ; Liu et al., 2026b ) . Internalizing the Future ( Zhang et al., 2026b ) learns from teacher-generated foresight traces. Its policy expresses confidence in an imagined plan before generating an action, and this confidence is calibrated against task outcomes to construct step-level advantages. ProAct’s MC-GRPO ( Yu et al., 2026c ) compares actions sampled at the same state using values estimated from simulator rollouts. WM-PPO instead evaluates candidates through their predicted consequences using a world model and PPO’s existing critic. Both networks learn from the agent’s own interactions, turning predicted state changes into feedback for policy and value learning. We evaluate WM-PPO on the simpler ALFWorld and on the harder ScienceWorld ( Wang et al., 2022 ) , whose tasks often require establishing intermediate conditions before the agent commits to an answer. The comparisons test whether learning from predicted consequences improves success for a comparable amount of real interaction (Section 4 ). They cover PPO, its multi-turn refinements and group-based methods; structural controls that change one component at a time; and auxiliary supervision from observation prediction, agreement and teachers. The main table compares 17 methods and variants, and the interaction-efficiency analysis places WM-PPO among 23 other methods and settings on ALFWorld and 20 on ScienceWorld. We then analyze how the two channels affect learning, and how the trained policy advances a task (Section 5 ). Our contributions are threefold: • Learning from dynamics by valuing predicted consequences. We show that valuing world-model predictions with PPO’s critic turns information implicit in environment dynamics into feedback that supplements sparse terminal rewards. Diagnostics demonstrate that these transition values support both action selection and policy improvement. • Calibrating value-based feedback with real outcomes. To account for noisy critic estimates, we use outcomes of real branches to calibrate a shared gate that weights both channels by how often comparisons with similar margins agreed with real outcomes earlier in training. The critic’s values thus reach both the policy and the critic itself as a stable signal grounded in real outcomes. • WM-PPO: transition values for policy and critic learning. We introduce two complementary learning channels: comparisons among transition values provide auxiliary policy advantages, while their mean supplies an auxiliary critic target. Only the policy is needed at inference. On ALFWorld and ScienceWorld, WM-PPO reaches PPO’s success levels with fewer real interaction steps and achieves higher success at comparable interaction budgets. 2 From Predicted Consequences to Learning Signals We first relate state transitions to changes in the probability of success, then test whether predicted consequences can guide a policy update. Prior work reports noisy and initially biased value estimates that rank candidate next steps near chance ( Kazemnejad et al., 2025 ; Yuan et al., 2025 ; Yue et al., 2025 ) . At randomly chosen states, we executed each distinct candidate and estimated its success probability from three continuations. Of the 195 candidate pairs whose estimates differ, the critic orders 57% correctly, and the median gap between its two values is 0.008. Ranking accuracy increases from 53% in the lower half of the critic’s value gaps to 71% in the top quarter. Among the 12 pairs for which one candidate succeeded in all three continuations and the other in none, the critic ranked 11 correctly (Figure 1 b). Large value gaps identify the comparisons the critic orders most reliably. The reliability gate uses real outcomes to determine how much weight each margin should receive (Section 3.5 ). 2.2 Do predicted state transitions preserve this value? We next test whether learned dynamics make these values available for candidates that are not executed. A world model replaces the one-step execution: a separate LoRA adapter on the same base model, trained with a next-observation loss on transitions that the same policy generated on 1,000 training games. For this diagnostic only, its data also include one-step executions of candidates the policy did not choose (48k transitions, 44% of them for such candidates); the world model of WM-PPO is trained on executed transitions alone. At each step, the world model greedily predicts the next observation of every distinct candidate, and the critic values each predicted next state. The agent selects the candidate with the highest value and executes it in the environment; the other candidates are never executed. Choosing by predicted consequences reaches 66.4%, 9.5 points above majority vote (SE 2.5, p < 0.001 p<0.001 ; Figure 1 a) and close to the 70.4% obtained with the true consequences. With the untrained base model as the world model, selection reaches 61.7%; the trained model is 4.7 points higher. Two controls examine why selection with true consequences performs better. When the true next observation is combined with the action list from before the step, as in the world model’s successor, selection reaches 65.3%. When newly revealed objects are removed from the true observation, it reaches 59.1% (Appendix C ). Both the refreshed action list and the newly revealed content contribute to the gain. The predictions are exact for 99.7% of the held-out observations that follow from what the agent has already seen, but for only 26% of those that reveal objects it has not seen; for the latter, they still give a prior over what will be found (Appendix C ). In this diagnostic, predicted transitions preserve most of the selection gain obtained with real ones. 2.3 Can the policy learn from these values? The value channel trains the critic on the mean, an estimate of how good the state is under the current policy. The policy channel uses the ordering of the deviations to form the gated preferences in Equation ( 8 ); both these deviations and the auxiliary advantages sum to zero over the K K samples. The value channel also affects the policy through the critic. PPO’s advantages are built from critic-value differences along the executed path (Section 3.4 ), so a critic that values states more accurately can give PPO’s own update better advantages. This effect starts from the next iteration, because each iteration’s advantages are computed before its critic update. We call the method with both channels WM-PPO, and the variant without the term of Equation ( 12 ) WM-PPO (policy channel only). 3.4 Learning through real interaction Each iteration uses one batch of branched real rollouts. Actions and returns train the policy and critic, observed transitions train the world model, and paired outcomes calibrate the gate (Figure 2 ; Table 4 in Appendix B ). Episodes and branches. Each iteration draws 64 training tasks. For each task, the policy plays one main episode, the chain , in which every step also records the K K candidates. The policy then plays three branches from the chain. For each branch, we draw a step of the chain uniformly at random. Because the environments are deterministic, replaying the chain’s actions up to that step restores its state. We resample the policy’s first response at this state up to eight times, stopping when its action differs from the chain’s, and then continue the branch to the end. If all eight samples repeat the chain’s action, the branch continues with that action but yields no comparison. The policy proposed a different action within eight samples in 62–67% of the branches on ScienceWorld and in 40–61% on ALFWorld. Each iteration therefore plays 256 episodes. Branching from intermediate states to obtain real outcomes for different actions is common in tree-based RL for language models ( Ji et al., 2026 ; Hou et al., 2025 ) . Here the branches add real interaction to the PPO update and test the candidate comparisons against real outcomes (Figure 2 b; Section 3.5 ). The resampled action enters PPO as an ordinary sample with its unconditioned log-probabilities (Section 7 ). ALFWorld ( Shridhar et al., 2021 ) is the simpler environment. Its six household task types mostly follow the same routine: find an object, heat, cool or clean it when required, and place it at the requested location. The environment lists admissible actions at every step, leaving search for hidden objects as the main difficulty. We train on 3,553 games and pool evaluation over 140 seen-room and 134 unseen-room games. ScienceWorld ( Wang et al., 2022 ) poses harder elementary science tasks. The 18 task types, fixed before training, all have oracle solutions within 40 steps. Eleven are procedural: the agent must first create or find an intermediate state, such as a mixture, a heated substance, a connected circuit or an organism in another room. The other seven require choosing the right object among candidates. The agent must identify the relevant conditions in long room descriptions and express its actions as free text using 25 templates. Unparseable commands consume a step, and focusing on the wrong object ends the task in failure. These dependencies make the value of a state transition less apparent from the next observation alone: the agent must judge whether a change establishes a condition needed for later success. Appendix B describes ScienceWorld’s fixed training and evaluation sets. In both environments, the reward is 1 for completion within 40 actions and 0 otherwise, with no intermediate rewards. ScienceWorld’s reported progress is part of the observation and is also predicted by the world model. Model and common start. The shared PPO warm-up reaches 27.0% success on ALFWorld and 20.8% on ScienceWorld. Its real transitions also train the initial world model. Training protocol and full results: Appendix F.1 . 5.1 Valuing Predicted State Transitions Section 2.1 showed that large value gaps identify comparisons the critic orders more reliably. This pattern persists during online training with predicted successors. Late in ScienceWorld training, the preferred action has the better outcome in 84% of branch pairs with different real outcomes in the largest margin bin, while the smaller bins are close to chance (Figure 5 a; Table 20 ). What a large margin means. A large margin indicates a large estimated difference in the current policy’s chances of success after the candidate actions. Such a difference can arise when an action commits the agent to a choice that prevents later completion. For example, a ScienceWorld task requires focusing on a non-living thing and moving it into a box. Focusing on air leaves the episode running, but air cannot be moved into the box. Across the three runs, the critics value the predicted successor of focusing on air at 0.03–0.34, compared with 0.54–0.80 for going to the hallway. None of the 24 real continuations after focusing on air succeeds, whereas 19 of 24 succeed after going to the hallway. Such decisive choices make up a growing part of the largest bin. Its share of pairs with different real outcomes grows from 14% early in training to 33% later, and its realized gain per pair from +0.08 to +0.22 (Table 19 ), which keeps its gate weight high (Section 5.2 ). Predicting successors improves candidate comparisons. Since a transition value approximates an action value, directly learning a Q network is a natural alternative. The relation from Equation ( 5 ), q ^ t ( a ) ≈ Q π ( s t , a ) = 𝔼 [ V π ( s t + 1 ) | s t , a t = a ] , \hat{q}_{t}(a)\approx Q^{\pi}(s_{t},a)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[V^{\pi}(s_{t+1})\bigm|s_{t},a_{t}=a\bigr], allows either a direct estimate of Q π Q^{\pi} or an estimate through the action’s successor. Q-PPO tests the first route by adding a network Q ω ​ ( s t , y ) Q_{\omega}(s_{t},y) trained on executed actions’ returns. It replaces the world model and critic’s candidate evaluation while retaining the candidates, margins, gate and auxiliary update of WM-PPO (policy channel only). The world model can also learn from the next observation at every executed step, including those in failed episodes. Predicting successors therefore lets the evaluation use supervision about what actions change as well as the returns used to learn value. In ScienceWorld’s largest margin bin, WM-PPO (policy channel only), which differs from Q-PPO only in how candidates are valued, favors the successful action in 85% of training branch pairs with different real outcomes, against 76% for Q-PPO (Table 20 ). On a shared pool of candidates, the comparisons using the world model are also more accurate at large margins (Figure 5 b). ECHO, PaW and DGS-WM use real observations as auxiliary prediction targets during RL, so the policy’s own parameters are also trained to predict what its actions produce ( Shrivastava et al., 2026 ; Lu et al., 2026 ; Ran et al., 2026 ) . DGS-WM keeps the observation fragments that best support the policy’s real next response, which favors decision-relevant content, but this selection score does not estimate eventual success. Other methods learn the dynamics before task-level policy training. Early Experience offers next-state prediction as a warm-up before imitation, and alternatively mixes self-reflection rationales into imitation ( Zhang et al., 2026a ) ; SPA writes true states into the policy’s reasoning before RL ( Chen et al., 2026a ) ; and RWML rewards predictions that are close to the true next state ( Yu et al., 2026b ) . In all of these methods, a prediction is judged by how well it matches the real observation. WM-PPO keeps the world model separate and values each predicted change by its effect on task success. The two uses of a world model could be combined. Predicted outcomes at decision time. WMA already pairs candidate actions with predicted consequences and a value judgment. With the policy frozen, a world model predicts the next observation of each candidate, a value model scores it, and the best candidate is executed at test time ( Chae et al., 2025 ) . WMA also reports that scoring the predicted next state works better than scoring the action alone; Q-PPO makes the corresponding comparison inside training (Section 5.1 ). WebDreamer plans in the same way with an LLM simulator and a prompted judge ( Gu et al., 2025 ) , and QWM selects robot actions by world-model search with a learned Q-function ( Dong et al., 2026 ) . ITP lets the agent reflect on imagined multi-step rollouts before acting, either without training or after an expert warm-up and actor-critic RL that learns the imagination length together with the action; in both variants the world model stays in the deployed agent ( Liu et al., 2026a ) . CoMAP, which is also evaluated on ALFWorld and ScienceWorld, shares several design choices with WM-PPO ( Liu et al., 2026b ) . Its world model is a separately parameterized copy of the same backbone, retrained on the agent’s own transitions; its labels come from real two-branch rollouts; and a gate decides when to refine. The policy drafts an action, reads the predicted next state and revises the draft after reflection. Training is supervised by expert or corrected actions, and the world model also learns from an EMA teacher that sees the real next state. The prediction thus enters the decision itself, and the world model remains part of the deployed agent. WM-PPO instead turns predicted consequences into training signals through PPO’s critic, and only the policy acts at deployment. Learned foresight. Internalizing the Future combines future simulation and success estimation within a single policy ( Zhang et al., 2026b ) . Its mid-training and supervised fine-tuning use foresight traces that DeepSeek V3.1 writes from recorded trajectories, including their future continuations. During RL, the policy generates an imagined plan and a confidence score before reasoning about the next action. A calibration reward trains this score against the terminal outcome, and the scores of corresponding steps, normalized within the group, form an auxiliary step-level advantage in GRPO. The feedback therefore concerns the generated plan rather than a comparison of alternative actions from a shared state. WM-PPO makes that comparison explicitly: the world model predicts the consequences of sampled actions, and PPO’s critic evaluates the resulting states. Both learn from the agent’s own interactions, and their estimates provide feedback for policy and critic updates. At inference, the policy acts without an explicit foresight stage. Teacher and hindsight feedback. Distillation supplies token-level feedback from a stronger or better-informed model. In standard on-policy distillation, the student generates its own sequences and a teacher supervises its token distribution on these prefixes ( Agarwal et al., 2024 ) . OCSD, in its main configuration, uses the agent’s own earlier policy instead: it scores already generated tokens in matched replay contexts with and without the real later observations, and the score difference rescales the token update while keeping the sign of the original advantage ( Yang et al., 2026 ) . Its hindsight also comes from the agent’s own interaction; the difference is that OCSD re-scores executed responses, whereas WM-PPO predicts the consequences of candidates that were not executed. VASPO gives a return-trained critic and a self-teacher access to reference solutions during training and weights tokens by their judgments, on mathematical and logical reasoning ( Wu et al., 2026 ) . Like WM-PPO, it deploys the policy alone, but it predicts no state transitions. Section 4.2 compares WM-PPO with OPD and OCSD. Model-based reinforcement learning. WM-PPO learns dynamics from the agent’s own interactions and values predicted consequences to guide policy and critic learning. Dyna adds simulated transitions to learning ( Sutton, 1991 ) ; World Models and Dreamer learn behavior inside a latent model ( Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018 ; Hafner et al., 2020 ; Hafner et al., 2025 ) ; MBPO branches short model rollouts from real states ( Janner et al., 2019 ) ; MVE and STEVE build the critic’s targets from model rollouts ( Feinberg et al., 2018 ; Buckman et al., 2018 ) ; and DreamGym replaces the environment of text agents with an LLM simulator ( Chen et al., 2026b ) . Value Prediction Networks and MuZero learn models through values ( Oh et al., 2017 ; Schrittwieser et al., 2020 ) , and value equivalence judges a model by the values it induces ( Grimm et al., 2020 ) . In WM-PPO, real rollouts provide every trajectory, the returns that are the critic’s main supervision, and PPO’s advantages. The world model predicts one step for the policy’s candidates, and the critic’s values of these predictions enter learning as candidate comparisons and an auxiliary critic target. This target, the mean transition value, is related to value expansion ( Feinberg et al., 2018 ; Buckman et al., 2018 ) and supplements regression on real returns. The gate checks the comparisons on real outcomes, in the spirit of value equivalence; the world model itself is trained by likelihood. Policy improvement over sampled actions. Valuing actions that were not executed, through a learned critic, is the idea of all-action policy gradients ( Allen et al., 2017 ; Ciosek & Whiteson, 2020 ) . MPO reweights actions sampled from the policy by their estimated values, and AWR reweights the actions in its replay buffer by their estimated advantages ( Abdolmaleki et al., 2018 ; Peng et al., 2019 ) ; Muesli regularizes policy-gradient learning toward sampled actions valued with a learned model ( Hessel et al., 2021 ) ; Sampled and Gumbel MuZero improve the policy over a sampled subset of actions with search ( Hubert et al., 2021 ; Danihelka et al., 2022 ) ; and the MC-GRPO variant of ProAct executes candidates sampled at one state in the simulator and values each by its immediate reward plus Monte-Carlo continuations from a random policy ( Yu et al., 2026c ) . The auxiliary term of WM-PPO belongs to this family: it moves probability toward the best of the policy’s own K K samples. Its choices concern how to value these samples and how much to trust their comparisons. WM-PPO values candidates through their predicted successor states and PPO’s critic, then uses outcomes from real branches to calibrate the reliability weights. Expert Iteration, AlphaZero, Dyna-Mind and the first stage of ProAct instead train the policy toward the output of a search ( Anthony et al., 2017 ; Silver et al., 2018 ; Yu et al., 2026a ; Yu et al., 2026c ) , whereas WM-PPO supplies scalar advantages for the policy’s own samples rather than a target distribution. ProAct’s MC-PPO variant estimates state values through random-policy rollouts in the simulator, with 1,000 rollouts per state in our Sokoban comparison ( Yu et al., 2026c ) . With comparable numbers of real environment steps, WM-PPO reaches 87.0% success versus 77.3% for MC-PPO, a significant gain of 9.7 percentage points (Appendix E.6 ). Value and credit signals for language agents. QLASS guides action selection with a Q-network trained on exploration trees ( Lin et al., 2025 ) ; SWEET-RL turns a privileged turn-level critic’s ranking of sampled actions into preference pairs ( Zhou et al., 2025 ) ; SPA-RL redistributes the final reward into per-step progress rewards ( Wang et al., 2025 ) ; and process reward models score steps from step-level labels ( Lightman et al., 2024 ) . Agent Q trains on sibling pairs whose value gap exceeds a fixed threshold ( Putta et al., 2024 ) , whereas the gate of WM-PPO measures which margins are reliable. VinePPO finds that PPO’s value network ranks candidate next steps near chance and replaces it by Monte-Carlo estimates ( Kazemnejad et al., 2025 ) , consistent with the small-margin comparisons of Sections 2.1 and 5.1 . Group-based methods compare episodes ( Shao et al., 2024 ; Ji et al., 2026 ) or actions taken at the same state or history ( Feng et al., 2025 ; He et al., 2026 ) ; PPO refinements change how advantages and ratios apply within a turn ( Wang et al., 2026 ; Li et al., 2026 ; Yue et al., 2025 ; Yu et al., 2025 ) ; and majority-vote self-training rewards agreement with the policy’s most frequent answer ( Zuo et al., 2025 ; Shafayat et al., 2025 ) . WM-PPO complements these forms of credit assignment with learned comparisons of unexecuted alternatives. 7 Discussion and Limitations The value of a predicted consequence depends on what the current policy can still accomplish from the resulting state. As the policy improves, the same successor can acquire a different value. The world model learns which changes an action produces, and the critic relates those changes to task success using supervision from real returns. Evaluating alternatives in this way lets the policy learn preferences that a few realized outcomes leave unresolved, including between candidates whose observed continuations end with the same reward (Appendix B.2 ). Large margins increasingly identify choices that change what remains achievable (Section 5.1 ). A premature commitment can close off success, while a step that establishes a prerequisite can make it attainable. These are the decisions on which the comparisons are most reliable. The behavioral analyses show the corresponding change in the policy: it more often completes prerequisites before placing an object or committing to an answer. Gains are largest on procedural ScienceWorld tasks and ALFWorld tasks that require heating, cooling or cleaning before placement. They are smaller on forced-choice tasks, where correctness becomes apparent only at the end. What the world model predicts determines which part of a comparison the critic must supply. Predicted endings supply outcomes directly, and comparisons between continuing successors rely on the critic. For the latter comparisons on ScienceWorld, the value channel raises agreement with real outcomes from 55% to 62% (Appendix F.1 ). On ALFWorld, no predicted ending settles a comparison, so every comparison rests on the critic. The gate assigns only partial weights there (Figure 8 ). The value channel improves early candidate comparisons and the critic’s fit to real returns later in training (Figure 7 ; Section 5.2 ). WM-PPO trades additional prediction and evaluation during training for more learning from each real interaction. The benefit is higher success at comparable interaction budgets and fewer steps to reach PPO’s success levels. The extra computation is confined to training; the deployed policy acts alone. Limitations. Our main evaluation covers one 4B base model and two text environments, with episodes limited to 40 steps. Branching and gate calibration require a reproducible prefix, as in our deterministic environments, or a saved state. A branch’s first action is sampled conditional on differing from the chain’s action. The update does not correct for this conditioning. A single greedy prediction of the next observation does not represent uncertainty over possible outcomes. Longer-range effects enter only through the critic. Prediction errors can bias the auxiliary policy advantages and critic targets. Comparisons cover only the policy’s candidates, and many small-margin comparisons receive little or no weight. Extending the benefit to these decision points remains an open problem. 8 Conclusion WM-PPO uses learned dynamics to evaluate candidate actions through their predicted consequences. A world model trained on real transitions predicts each candidate’s next observation, and PPO’s critic, trained on real returns, values the predicted successor according to the current policy’s chance of success. In the policy channel, comparisons between transition values become auxiliary advantages for the policy’s own samples. In the value channel, the mean transition value becomes a one-step target for the critic at the current state. Real branch outcomes calibrate the weights of both channels. On ALFWorld and ScienceWorld, the resulting policy achieves higher success than PPO with comparable real interaction. It matches the strongest PPO refinements we tested on ALFWorld and exceeds the best of them by 6.5 points on ScienceWorld. Unlike distillation, WM-PPO needs neither a teacher model nor privileged information, yet it also surpasses the teacher-based methods we tested. When deployed alone, the policy more often completes prerequisite steps before committing to an answer or placing an object. PPO-160: 160 tasks × \times 1 episode per iteration; PPO-256: 256 tasks × \times 1; Wide-Tree-PPO, Q-PPO and Fixed WM: WM-PPO’s trees (64 tasks × \times (1 chain + 3 branches)); group methods: 20 tasks × \times 8 episodes, also 40 tasks × \times 4 for GRPO and, on ScienceWorld, GiGPO (Tree-GRPO: WM-PPO’s trees or 32 trees of 8 leaves, Table 7 ); PPO refinements and the auxiliary-feedback methods: PPO-160’s sampling. All start from the warm-up checkpoint with the same task order. Methods. Wide-Tree-PPO : WM-PPO (policy channel only) without the auxiliary term, that is, the same trees, tree GAE, critic targets and sibling term, and the same ordinary PPO rows of the executed steps. Q-PPO : a third LoRA adapter with a linear head reads the history followed by a candidate response and is regressed on the return below each executed step, plus the sibling term; it is initialized from the warm-up critic and replaces q ^ \hat{q} in Equation ( 6 ); candidates, margins, gate and auxiliary rows are unchanged. Fixed WM : WM-PPO (policy channel only) with a world model that is never updated after its initialization; the policy and the critic are trained, and the gate and the auxiliary rows computed, as in that variant. In both environments it starts from the same initial world model as WM-PPO, trained on the warm-up’s executed transitions (on ScienceWorld, predictions at iteration 13 are token-identical for 78 of 81 shared state–candidate pairs). CAPO ( Wang et al., 2026 ) : action-level critic and importance ratio. SO-PPO ( Li et al., 2026 ) : one geometric-mean importance ratio per turn with clipping-triggered normalization. Turn-ratio PPO : the GSPO-style turn-level ratio ( Zheng et al., 2025 ) with a narrow clip, as implemented in the CAPO code base ( Wang et al., 2026 ) . VAPO-lite ( Yue et al., 2025 ) : PPO-160 plus a language-model loss (weight 0.1) on responses of successful episodes. GiGPO ( Feng et al., 2025 ) and HGPO ( He et al., 2026 ) : step-level grouping by identical environment states and by history, ported from their released code. Tree-GRPO ( Ji et al., 2026 ) : tree-based group advantages on WM-PPO’s trees. Majority-vote labels : PPO-160 plus four samples per step and auxiliary rows as in Equation ( 8 ) with the unique plurality action as the preferred one, weight 1, no gate; computed on PPO-160’s episodes, their total auxiliary weight is about two to three times that of WM-PPO. Standard OPD ( Agarwal et al., 2024 ) : a frozen Qwen3-32B in non-thinking mode, which shares the student’s vocabulary, computes the log-probabilities of the executed response given exactly the student’s prompt; every token’s advantage is the whitened GAE advantage plus clip ⁡ ( log ⁡ π T − log ⁡ π old , ± 5 ) \operatorname{clip}(\log\pi_{T}-\log\pi_{\mathrm{old}},\pm 5) , the reverse-KL reward of on-policy distillation, with coefficient 1. OCSD ( Yang et al., 2026 ) : the teacher, the policy of the previous iteration (self) or the same Qwen3-32B, computes the log-probability of the executed response with and without the next one or two observations of the episode, but not the current action; the PPO advantage is multiplied by 1 + 0.5 ​ sign ⁡ ( A ) ​ tanh ⁡ ( Δ / 2 ) 1+0.5\,\operatorname{sign}(A)\tanh(\Delta/2) , where Δ \Delta is the difference of the two log-probabilities, on the 20% least likely steps per episode, following the released implementation. All OPD and OCSD variants are built on PPO-160 and use its interaction. PaW ( Lu et al., 2026 ) : PPO-160 plus PaW’s auxiliary next-observation loss on the policy with the published settings: the clipped loss with ρ = 0.2 \rho=0.2 on the tokens of the true next observation, applied to the 75% of transitions with the highest action uncertainty, the reward-adaptive weight following the paper’s formula (with the batch’s mean episode return under PPO), and the paper’s 20 prediction prompts. Unlike the original full fine-tuning at learning rate 10 − 6 10^{-6} , it trains PPO-160’s LoRA at 10 − 5 10^{-5} . Published results. F.1 Branch pairs and the reliability gate Protocol. In each iteration, every branch pair (the chain action and the branch action at the branch point, both played to the end) was valued by the iteration’s pre-update models and assigned to a margin bin with that iteration’s thresholds. The following measurements compare advantages and candidate preferences; Section F.4 reports the critics’ comparisons during training and at evaluation, and Section F.5 reports the policy’s command choices during training. The recomputation uses the critic of training (saved at the end of the preceding iteration) and the training code’s estimator ( γ = 1 \gamma=1 , λ = 0.95 \lambda=0.95 ).
